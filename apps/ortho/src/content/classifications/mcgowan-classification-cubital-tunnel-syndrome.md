## Overview

The McGowan classification grades cubital tunnel syndrome (ulnar nerve compression at the elbow) by severity of sensory and motor deficit, from subjective sensory symptoms alone (Grade I) through severe intrinsic wasting/paralysis and clawing (Grade III). Grade drives both the operative threshold and, in part, the choice between in situ decompression and nerve transposition.

## Types

| Grade | Findings | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Subjective sensory symptoms only, no weakness | Non-operative: splinting, activity modification, nerve gliding |
| IIa | Objective sensory loss, subjective weakness without measurable motor deficit | Trial of non-operative management, reassess with EDX; surgery if no improvement |
| IIb | Measurable intrinsic weakness | Operative decompression favored (in situ vs transposition per surgeon/anatomy) |
| III | Severe intrinsic wasting/paralysis, clawing, established sensory loss | Operative decompression indicated but counsel guarded prognosis for full recovery of intrinsic function |

## Pearls

- Non-operative management (elbow extension or 45-60° flexion night splinting, activity modification avoiding leaning on the elbow or sustained flexion, nerve gliding exercises) is effective mainly in Grade I-IIa; corticosteroid injection is not standard practice given proximity to the nerve and lack of robust benefit data.
- In situ decompression (simple release of Osborne's ligament ± intermuscular septum) is favored first-line surgery for most Grade I-IIb cases without gross instability — multiple RCTs/meta-analyses show equivalent outcomes to transposition with lower morbidity for mild-moderate disease.
- Anterior transposition (subcutaneous or submuscular) is reserved for nerve subluxation/snapping over the medial epicondyle, recurrent/revision cases, significant cubitus valgus or bony deformity, or when in situ release leaves inadequate nerve glide — not chosen by grade alone.
- Sensory recovery precedes motor recovery after decompression; intrinsic (motor) recovery in Grade III cases may remain incomplete regardless of technique or timing of surgery — set expectations accordingly at the severe end of the spectrum.
- On differential diagnosis for 4th/5th finger paresthesias, cubital tunnel syndrome involves dorsal ulnar hand sensation, whereas Guyon's canal (ulnar tunnel) compression spares it (the dorsal cutaneous branch arises proximal to Guyon's canal) — a key discriminator from cervical (C8/T1) causes as well.
- Elbow flexion-compression test (sens ~75%, more sensitive than Tinel's alone), Tinel's at the cubital tunnel, Wartenberg's sign, and Froment's sign support clinical staging; NCS/EMG grade severity and localize compression (across-elbow conduction velocity <50 m/s or >10 m/s drop versus the forearm segment is diagnostic).
