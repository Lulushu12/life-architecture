## Overview

The Mayfield classification describes the progressive, circumferential ligamentous injury pattern around the lunate that produces perilunate and lunate dislocation, from isolated scapholunate dissociation (Stage I) through true lunate dislocation (Stage IV). It represents a single high-energy hyperextension event propagating around the lunate rather than a stepwise chronic process, and stages directly dictate the extent of ligament repair required at surgery.

## Types

| Stage | Structures disrupted | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Scapholunate ligament | SL dissociation only | If isolated and acute: SL repair ± capsulodesis; radiographically may show only SL widening |
| **II** | + Capitolunate joint (lunocapitate) | Perilunate dislocation — capitate dislocates dorsal to lunate | Emergent reduction; open repair of SL and capitolunate capsule |
| **III** | + Lunotriquetral ligament | Progression around ulnar side to LT joint | Add LT repair/pinning to the above |
| **IV** | + Dorsal radiocarpal ligament (lunate dislocates volarly) | True lunate dislocation ("spilled teacup" sign); lunate rotates and displaces into carpal tunnel | Surgical emergency (median nerve compression risk); open reduction and repair of all disrupted ligaments, carpal tunnel release if median nerve symptoms |

**Lesser-arc injuries** = pure ligamentous perilunate dissociation through the above stages. **Greater-arc injuries** = the force passes through bone (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial styloid perilunate fracture-dislocation) rather than purely through the SL/LT ligaments — these require fracture fixation in addition to the ligamentous repair implied by the corresponding stage.

## Pearls

- Up to 25% of perilunate dislocations are missed on the initial ED film — look specifically for disrupted Gilula's arcs, the "spilled teacup" lunate sign, and capitate overlap; a "wrist sprain" with persistent pain and swelling after high-energy trauma should not be dismissed without these being checked.
- The lunate itself, tethered by intact volar radiolunate ligaments, tends to stay reduced relative to the radius while the rest of the carpus displaces around/away from it — until Stage IV, when the lunate itself finally displaces volarly.
- Emergent closed reduction follows the reverse Mayfield sequence (traction-countertraction with thumb pressure on the lunate) to decompress the median nerve and skin; this is the immediate step regardless of eventual definitive surgical plan.
- Greater-arc (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial styloid) variants require fracture fixation in addition to whatever ligament repair the corresponding stage implies — do not treat them as pure ligamentous injuries.
- Even with technically adequate, appropriately timed repair, outcomes literature shows persistent moderate residual disability (grip strength deficits, reduced ROM, later SLAC-pattern arthritis) is common — this should be part of patient counseling regardless of stage or timing.
- Missed/chronic (>6 weeks) perilunate injury becomes progressively harder to reduce openly; if reduction is not achievable or cartilage damage is already present, proceed directly toward a salvage strategy (PRC/4-corner fusion) rather than persisting with reconstruction.
