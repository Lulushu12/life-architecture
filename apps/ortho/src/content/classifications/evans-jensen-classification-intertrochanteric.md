## Overview

The Evans-Jensen classification grades intertrochanteric fractures by mechanical stability rather than pure anatomic pattern, making it a practical complement to AO/OTA 31-A for implant selection. It explicitly captures the reverse-obliquity pattern (Type V), which is the single most important pattern for excluding the sliding hip screw as an option.

## Types

| Type | Description | Stability | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Undisplaced, 2-part | Stable | SHS or CMN; low failure risk with either |
| **II** | Displaced, 2-part, reducible to stable configuration | Stable after reduction | Anatomic reduction restores medial/posterior cortical buttress; SHS or CMN |
| **III** | Comminuted, posteromedial fragment, irreducible to stable position, lesser trochanter detached | Unstable | Fixed-angle/load-sharing device preferred (CMN); anticipate controlled collapse |
| **IV** | Comminuted with subtrochanteric extension | Unstable | Long CMN mandatory to span the subtrochanteric zone |
| **V (reverse obliquity)** | Fracture line runs from proximomedial to distolateral (opposite obliquity) | Highly unstable | SHS biomechanically contraindicated (fragment slides medially, fixation cuts out); CMN or fixed-angle blade plate required |

## Pearls

- The 2023 INSITE trial (large UK pragmatic RCT) found no clinically meaningful 1-year EQ-5D difference and no significant revision-rate difference between CMN and SHS for intertrochanteric fractures overall, with **no interaction by fracture stability** — reinforcing that stable Evans-Jensen I–II patterns can reasonably be treated with either implant.
- Type V (reverse obliquity) is the pattern most likely to be mismanaged: the SHS's sliding mechanism depends on an intact lateral wall to resist medialization of the shaft, and reverse-oblique fixation with SHS has unacceptably high cutout/fixation-failure rates.
- A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis found CMN carries a **higher reoperation and refracture rate** than SHS for intertrochanteric fractures overall, tempering blanket "nail-for-everything" practice — implant choice should follow the stability grade, not default automatically to nailing.
- CMN and SHS show similar surgical-failure and mortality rates overall; CMN carries a modest excess of peri-implant femur fractures with older nail designs (largely mitigated by modern nail geometry), while SHS remains cheaper.
