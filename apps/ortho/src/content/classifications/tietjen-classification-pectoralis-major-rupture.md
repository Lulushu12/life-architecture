## Overview

The Tietjen classification describes pectoralis major ruptures by anatomic location (sternocostal origin/muscle belly, musculotendinous junction, or tendon avulsion from the humerus) and, together with completeness (partial vs. complete), guides which injuries are amenable to anatomic tendon-to-bone repair. Chronicity (time from injury) sits alongside location/completeness as the third major variable driving the acute-repair-versus-reconstruction decision.

## Types

| Location type | Description | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Sternocostal origin/muscle belly injury | Least amenable to anatomic tendon-to-bone repair given the injury is intramuscular/at the origin rather than at a fixable tendon-bone interface |
| II | Musculotendinous junction | Intermediate; repair feasibility depends on tissue quality at the junction |
| III | Tendon avulsion from the humerus | Most common pattern and most amenable to anatomic tendon-to-bone repair (suture anchors, transosseous tunnels, or cortical button fixation) |

Each location type is further graded as **partial** or **complete**, which — combined with chronicity — determines whether acute primary repair or allograft reconstruction is the appropriate operative strategy.

## Pearls

- Time from injury is the single most consistent predictor of outcome, arguably more so than Tietjen type alone: acute repair (<3–4 weeks) reliably restores near-normal strength, while chronic/retracted injuries (>4–6 weeks) or a large tendon gap often require allograft (e.g., Achilles tendon) reconstruction with generally somewhat inferior outcomes and a higher re-rupture rate.
- Document time from injury explicitly at the first visit — this single data point is the detail most likely to be missed if the patient delays presentation, and it is the primary determinant of repair technique.
- Anabolic steroid use is a recognized risk factor for tendon quality compromise and should be asked about directly; it also elevates re-rupture risk after repair.
- The sternal head inserts distal/posterior to the clavicular head and is under the greatest tension in the eccentric-loaded, extended/abducted/externally-rotated position (classically the eccentric phase of a bench press) — this is the mechanism to specifically elicit in the history, along with a sudden anterior axillary "pop," ecchymosis, and a palpable defect.
- When both heads are torn, identify and repair the sternal and clavicular heads separately — repairing only the more visible head under-restores strength.
- Exam pearls: palpable defect, ecchymosis tracking into the arm, loss of the anterior axillary fold contour, and weakness on resisted adduction/internal rotation; compare bilaterally with arms crossed (chest-press position accentuates the defect).
