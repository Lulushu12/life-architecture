## Overview

The Meyers-McKeever classification (with a Zaricznyj type IV addition for comminuted fragments) stages displacement of the tibial spine (intercondylar eminence) avulsion fracture, the tibial insertion of the ACL. In skeletally immature patients the ACL midsubstance is stronger than the incompletely ossified spine, so a valgus/hyperextension or deceleration force that would tear an adult's ACL instead avulses the tibial spine as a sleeve of bone. The classification guides the choice between an extension-cast trial and arthroscopic reduction/fixation.

## Types

| Type | Definition | Management |
|---|---|---|
| I | Nondisplaced or minimally displaced fragment | Immobilize in extension (or slight hyperextension) cylinder/long-leg cast 4–6 weeks; aspirate tense hemarthrosis for comfort and to improve extension |
| II | Anterior 1/3–1/2 of fragment elevated, posterior hinge intact ("beak" appearance on lateral) | Attempt closed reduction with knee extension (reduces the anterior fat pad against the fragment); cast in extension if reduced; arthroscopy if a mechanical block (meniscus, intermeniscal ligament) prevents reduction |
| III | Complete separation of fragment from underlying bone bed; commonly subclassified IIIA (no rotation) vs IIIB (rotated/displaced fragment) | Operative — arthroscopic reduction and fixation (suture through the ACL base vs. screw fixation of the bony fragment, technique chosen by fragment size/comminution) |
| IV (Zaricznyj) | Comminuted fragment | Operative, usually suture fixation (screw fixation often not feasible through comminuted bone); most technically demanding subtype |

## Pearls

- Extension, not flexion, is the reducing position for tibial spine fractures (opposite of the intuitive "flexion relaxes the ACL" reasoning) because the intact anterior horns of the menisci and the intercondylar roof push the fragment down into its bed as the knee extends.
- The anterior horns of the menisci and the intermeniscal ligament can become entrapped beneath an avulsed, flipped fragment and block anatomic reduction — this mechanical block is the most common cause of an irreducible fragment and an indication for arthroscopy even in type II injuries.
- On exam, a positive Lachman with a soft/absent endpoint reflects laxity from the bony avulsion, not a true ACL midsubstance tear (which is usually intact); inability to actively extend the knee reflects an extensor lag from the anterior fat pad/fragment blocking terminal extension.
- MRI shows associated ACL/meniscal injury in up to 40% of cases and is useful for fragment size and meniscal entrapment characterization.
- Comminuted (Zaricznyj IV) fragments are usually not amenable to screw purchase and are managed with suture fixation instead.
