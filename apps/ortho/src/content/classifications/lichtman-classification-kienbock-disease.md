## Overview

The Lichtman classification stages Kienböck disease (avascular necrosis of the lunate) from radiographically occult marrow edema (Stage I) through lunate collapse with fixed carpal malalignment and secondary arthrosis (Stage IV). Stage — combined with ulnar variance and carpal height — drives the choice between joint-leveling/revascularization procedures and salvage surgery.

## Types

| Stage | Radiographic/MRI findings | Carpal height | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Normal or near-normal radiographs; MRI shows marrow edema/signal change, possible linear fracture | Normal | Immobilization/NSAIDs; consider revascularization if symptoms persist; joint leveling if ulnar-negative |
| **II** | Lunate sclerosis on radiographs, no collapse | Normal | Joint leveling (radial shortening if ulnar-negative) or capitate shortening/STT fusion if neutral/positive; ± revascularization |
| **IIIA** | Lunate collapse (loss of height/fragmentation) | Maintained | Joint leveling/limited fusion ± vascularized graft; scaphoid not yet fixed in flexion |
| **IIIB** | Lunate collapse with fixed scaphoid flexion/rotation, proximal capitate migration | Decreased (ratio <0.54) | Requires correction of carpal collapse: STT/scaphocapitate fusion, PRC, radial shortening + capitate shortening, or vascularized graft with structural (corticocancellous) support |
| **IV** | Stage IIIB changes plus radiocarpal and/or midcarpal osteoarthritis | Decreased | Salvage: PRC, limited/total wrist fusion, denervation, or wrist arthroplasty |

## Pearls

- Stage I disease is radiographically occult — plain films stage I–IIIA disease, but MRI is required to detect Stage I changes and to assess lunate marrow viability before committing to a revascularization procedure.
- Ulnar variance determines the joint-leveling strategy independent of stage: radial shortening osteotomy is effective only in ulnar-negative wrists (shortening to neutral or 1–2 mm negative unloads the lunate); in ulnar-neutral or -positive wrists it is biomechanically ineffective, and capitate shortening/STT fusion is used instead.
- The lunate's intraosseous vascular pattern (Gelberman) is a key etiologic factor: a single dorsal or single volar vessel without intraosseous anastomosis carries the highest risk, while dual dorsal+volar supply with anastomoses is protective. A Type I lunate-hamate morphology (flat proximal hamate facet, single-facet articulation) further concentrates load and is also implicated.
- Carpal height ratio (normal ~0.54) is the key radiographic threshold separating IIIA (maintained) from IIIB (decreased, ratio <0.54) — this distinction changes management from joint leveling alone to procedures that also correct fixed carpal collapse.
- Lunate excision with tendon/prosthetic interposition is largely historical given poor long-term results; modern pyrocarbon lunate prosthetic arthroplasty has renewed interest for unreconstructable Stage IIIB/IV collapse without established pancarpal arthritis, though it is not first-line and evidence quality remains limited.
