## Overview

The Mayo (location-based) system describes scaphoid fractures by where along the bone they occur — distal tubercle, distal third, waist, proximal third, or proximal pole. Because the scaphoid's dominant blood supply enters distally and runs retrograde, location is the single strongest predictor of nonunion and avascular necrosis (AVN) risk, and is used alongside fracture-line orientation (Russe classification) to set the treatment ladder.

## Types

| Category | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Distal tubercle** | Extra-articular, distal to STT joint | Very stable, casts reliably |
| **Distal third** | Distal to the waist | High union with cast; short immobilization |
| **Waist** | ~70–80% of fractures | Cast vs screw per SWIFFT if undisplaced; screw if displaced |
| **Proximal third** | Proximal to waist, still >1/3 pole remaining | Elevated nonunion/AVN risk; fixation favored |
| **Proximal pole** | Small proximal fragment | Highest AVN/nonunion risk; fragment-specific fixation, consider primary vascularized graft in delayed presentation |

## Pearls

- The scaphoid's dominant supply is the dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery, entering dorsally at the distal pole/waist ridge and running retrograde to supply 70–80% of the bone, including the entire proximal pole; a separate volar branch supplies only the distal 20–30% (tubercle). This watershed, retrograde pattern is the anatomic basis for the location-risk gradient in this table.
- There is essentially no periosteal blood supply over 80% of the scaphoid surface (cartilage-covered), so revascularization from surrounding soft tissue is not possible without a dedicated vascular pedicle — the rationale for vascularized grafting specifically in proximal-pole/AVN nonunion.
- Roughly 70–80% of scaphoid fractures occur at the waist, where hyperextension + radial deviation + intercarpal supination puts the volar cortex in tension and the dorsal cortex in compression; untreated waist fractures can collapse into the classic humpback deformity as the volar cortex resorbs.
- Proximal pole fractures have an untreated/delayed nonunion risk up to 30–40% — meaningfully higher than waist fractures — which is why fixation (and sometimes primary vascularized grafting) is favored even for minimally displaced proximal-pole injuries.
- Location and orientation (Russe) are complementary, not competing, systems: a waist fracture's management is further refined by whether it is horizontal-oblique, transverse, or vertical-oblique.
