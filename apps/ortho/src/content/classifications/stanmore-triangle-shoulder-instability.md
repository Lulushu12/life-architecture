## Overview

The Stanmore Triangle is a conceptual, non-numerical classification of shoulder instability that frames each patient along a spectrum across three "polar" types rather than assigning a rigid grade. It is used to distinguish structural (Bankart-type) instability from muscle-patterning (non-structural) instability, and thereby to guide whether treatment philosophy should be primarily structural/surgical or primarily rehabilitation-led.

## Types

| Type/Grade | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Type I — Traumatic structural** | Classic traumatic unidirectional instability with a structural (Bankart) lesion, normal muscle patterning | Structural/surgical repair (Bankart, ± bone augmentation) is primary treatment |
| **Type II — Atraumatic structural** | Structural abnormality (capsular redundancy, labral pathology) in the absence of a single traumatic event, normal muscle patterning | May need structural correction (capsular plication) but usually after a rehab trial |
| **Type III — Muscle patterning (non-structural)** | Instability driven by abnormal muscle recruitment/patterning (overactive prime movers, underactive stabilizers) with no true structural lesion; classically atraumatic, often multidirectional/bilateral | Physiotherapy-led rehabilitation is first-line and often sufficient; surgery is high-risk for failure and reserved for refractory cases after confirming no missed structural lesion |

## Pearls

- Patients can migrate along the triangle — e.g., a Type III muscle-patterning shoulder that develops a structural lesion after a traumatic episode becomes a polar/intermediate Type I–III. This dynamic migration is the classification's key clinical utility: it forces re-evaluation rather than a one-time label.
- Screen for voluntary/psychogenic instability (patients who can demonstrate subluxation at will without pain, often with underlying psychological factors) before committing a patient to a Type III rehabilitation pathway or to any surgery — surgical outcomes in voluntary instability are poor.
- A positive sulcus sign that does not reduce with external rotation is a hallmark of rotator interval laxity relevant to Type II/III presentations and to MDI surgical planning (rotator interval closure).
- Because it is non-numerical, the Stanmore Triangle is best used to frame treatment philosophy (structural vs. muscle-patterning problem) rather than as a scoring or prognostic tool — it does not replace ISIS or glenoid-track assessment when a structural lesion is present.
