## Overview

Kanavel's four cardinal signs are the classic clinical criteria for diagnosing pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis (flexor sheath infection) of the digit. Because this condition is a surgical emergency — delayed treatment risks tendon necrosis, stiffness, and amputation — recognizing these signs, individually or in combination, should trigger urgent surgical evaluation rather than a period of observation.

## Types

| Sign | Description | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fusiform ("sausage") swelling | Uniform swelling of the entire digit | Any combination of 2+ signs (classically all 4, though sensitivity of the full tetrad is imperfect early) with clinical suspicion → urgent surgical washout |
| 2. Flexed posture at rest | Digit held in slight flexion to minimize sheath pressure/pain | Same |
| 3. Tenderness along flexor tendon sheath | Pain to palpation from palm to fingertip along the sheath | Same |
| 4. Pain with passive extension | Disproportionate pain on passive digit extension (most sensitive early sign) | Same — this sign often precedes the full tetrad and should trigger urgent evaluation alone |

## Pearls

- Pain with passive extension is the most sensitive early sign and can precede the other three; do not wait for the full tetrad to act — it should trigger urgent evaluation on its own.
- On differential diagnosis, Kanavel signs localize specifically to the flexor tendon sheath; joint aspiration distinguishes septic MCP/PIP arthritis, and cellulitis lacks pain on passive tendon excursion — useful discriminators in a painful, swollen, flexed digit after a minor puncture.
- Imaging should never delay debridement of a clinically clear flexor sheath infection; early surgical consultation is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Radiographs are still obtained to exclude retained foreign body or osteomyelitis, and labs (CRP/WBC) and blood cultures are drawn for systemic signs.
- Delay beyond 24-48 hours from clear Kanavel signs worsens outcomes (tendon necrosis, stiffness, amputation risk); surgical drainage remains the standard of care once the signs are established.
- Recent literature (Sawyer et al. 2024; Hennessy et al. 2026) notes emerging interest in selective non-operative management (IV antibiotics + close monitoring) for early/mild presentations without frank sheath distension, but this evidence is still preliminary and lacks consensus guidelines — it does not change the standard of urgent surgical drainage once Kanavel signs are clearly established.
- As a red flag, Kanavel's signs mandate OR within hours, not days.
