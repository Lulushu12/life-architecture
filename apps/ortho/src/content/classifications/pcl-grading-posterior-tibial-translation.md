## Overview

This supplementary grading system stages isolated PCL injury by the amount of posterior tibial translation on posterior drawer testing at 90° flexion (or on stress radiographs), assessed relative to the position of the tibial plateau against the femoral condyles. It is used alongside the Schenck and Fanelli classifications to guide the non-operative vs. operative threshold in PCL injury, particularly when combined ligament involvement is suspected.

## Types

| Grade | Posterior tibial translation | Tibial plateau position vs. femoral condyles | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | 0–5 mm | Plateau remains anterior to condyles | Non-operative first-line in essentially all cases |
| **II** | 5–10 mm | Plateau flush with condyles | Non-operative first-line; brace + quad rehab, reassess at 3 months |
| **III** | >10 mm | Plateau posterior to condyles | Non-operative trial reasonable if isolated and low-demand; strongly consider reconstruction if combined with PLC/other ligament injury, bony avulsion, or persistent functional instability |

## Pearls

- Posterior drawer is the most sensitive PCL exam test (~90% sensitivity); posterior sag (Godfrey test) is a passive test with high specificity that avoids quadriceps-guarding confound; the quadriceps active test confirms reduction of posterior sag with active quad contraction at 90° flexion.
- Stress radiographs (Telos device or manual stress views) quantify PCL laxity via side-to-side posterior translation and are increasingly used to objectively grade combined instability and guide the operative vs. non-operative decision, rather than relying on manual exam grading alone.
- A reverse pivot-shift on exam indicates combined PLC injury, not isolated PCL laxity — always pair PCL grading with a dial test at 30° and 90° to detect combined PLC involvement before committing to a non-operative pathway.
- Isolated PCL injury is comparatively uncommon and frequently under-recognized, because patients often present with a relatively well-functioning knee and subtle exam findings — a large proportion of "isolated" PCL tears on casual exam are in fact missed low-grade combined injuries, making careful grading and associated-structure testing important even in apparently mild presentations.
- Long-term physiotherapy-led brace/exercise cohorts show durable improvement in patient-reported outcomes at 5 years for non-operatively managed Grade I–III isolated PCL injury, with need for delayed reconstruction (when it occurs) concentrated in the first 2 years.
