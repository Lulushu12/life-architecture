## Overview

This is a complementary directional/stability framework for elbow dislocation, used alongside the fracture-specific classifications (AO/OTA, Mason, Mayo, Bado, O'Driscoll) to describe whether a dislocation is simple or complex and which ligamentous instability pattern is present. It is essential for deciding between closed reduction with early motion versus operative stabilization.

## Types

| Category | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple dislocation** | No associated fracture; ulnohumeral and radiocapitellar joints dislocate together (>90% are posterior/posterolateral) | Closed reduction, brief splint, early protected active motion |
| **Complex dislocation** | Dislocation with associated fracture (radial head, coronoid, or both = terrible triad; or with olecranon = transolecranon variant) | Operative stabilization per fracture-specific and terrible-triad protocols |
| **Posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI)** | LUCL-deficient pattern; forearm subluxates/rotates around an intact MCL as the elbow extends and supinates | LUCL repair/reconstruction; the "pivot-shift" test is the clinical correlate |
| **Varus posteromedial rotatory instability (VPMI)** | Anteromedial facet coronoid fracture + LCL injury; forearm subluxates in varus/internal rotation | Anteromedial facet buttress plating (medial approach) is essential — LCL repair alone is inadequate |
| **Valgus posterolateral instability** | MCL-dominant pattern, often from a valgus-overload mechanism (e.g., overhead throwing), distinct from the acute dislocation spectrum | Usually managed non-operatively acutely; chronic valgus instability in overhead athletes (UCL reconstruction) is a separate entity outside the acute fracture-dislocation cluster |

## Pearls

- Redislocation risk after a "simple" elbow dislocation is easy to underestimate: always recheck concentric reduction on a true lateral radiograph — >50% widening of the ulnohumeral joint on AP or lateral indicates instability, not a truly "simple" injury.
- Stability testing (varus/valgus stress, forearm rotation with the elbow at 30° flexion for PLRI/"pivot-shift" apprehension) is best performed under anesthesia intraoperatively for complex dislocations, not relied upon in the awake ED patient where guarding limits accuracy.
- Prolonged immobilization (>3 weeks) after a truly simple, concentric, stable dislocation is associated with worse ROM and higher rates of chronic stiffness without improving stability — early protected motion (within 3–5 days) is now the standard of care.
- VPMI (Type 2 coronoid + LCL injury) is a distinct pattern from terrible triad and is easy to under-treat if managed with LCL repair alone; it requires anteromedial facet buttress plating via a separate medial approach.
