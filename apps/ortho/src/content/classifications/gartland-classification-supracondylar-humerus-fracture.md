## Overview

The Gartland classification grades **extension-type** pediatric supracondylar humerus fractures by displacement and integrity of the posterior cortex, with a Wilkins modification adding rotational subtypes to type II and a Leitch addition describing multidirectionally unstable type IV injuries. It is the primary decision tool for choosing nonoperative casting versus closed reduction versus urgent closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP), and correlates with neurovascular injury risk. Flexion-type fractures (~2–5% of supracondylar fractures) are a much rarer separate pattern with the highest ulnar nerve risk.

## Types

| Type | Definition | Posterior cortex | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Nondisplaced or minimally displaced; posterior fat pad sign may be only finding | Intact | Long-arm cast/splint 3 weeks, no reduction |
| II | Displaced with intact posterior cortex (hinge); anterior humeral line may still intersect capitellum abnormally | Intact, acts as a hinge | Closed reduction; cast if stable and no rotational malalignment; CRPP if any instability, IIA with rotation, or comminution ("IIA" = angulated only; "IIB" per some schemes = angulated + rotated, treated as unstable) |
| III | Completely displaced, no cortical contact; subdivided by direction (posteromedial vs posterolateral) | Disrupted | CRPP, urgent (within 24h; emergent if vascular compromise) |
| IV (Leitch/multidirectional) | Periosteal hinge disrupted circumferentially — fracture is unstable/multidirectional under fluoroscopy in both flexion and extension, often only recognized intraoperatively | Disrupted, no stabilizing hinge in any plane | CRPP with careful fluoroscopic assessment in multiple positions; higher threshold for open reduction if reduction not maintainable percutaneously |

## Pearls

- Posteromedial type III fractures carry the highest risk of AIN injury and, less commonly, brachial artery injury (the proximal fragment's spike points anteromedially toward the neurovascular bundle); posterolateral fractures carry higher radial nerve risk.
- Type IV is characterized by circumferential periosteal hinge disruption and is often only recognized **intraoperatively** under fluoroscopy in multiple positions, not on preoperative films alone.
- Many centers now pin most type II fractures given a measurable, if modest, rate of loss of reduction with casting alone — this is a genuine practice-pattern variation, not a single correct answer.
- Baumann angle (64–81°, side-to-side difference <5°) and the anterior humeral line (should pass through the middle third of the capitellum) are the coronal- and sagittal-plane reduction checks used alongside the Gartland grade.
- Cubitus varus ("gunstock deformity") is the classic late complication of inadequately corrected coronal-plane malunion; remodeling of coronal-plane elbow malalignment is essentially absent, so intraoperative Baumann angle/rotational control — not a "fix later" approach — is the prevention strategy.
