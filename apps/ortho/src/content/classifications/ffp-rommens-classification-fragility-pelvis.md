## Overview

The FFP (Fragility Fractures of the Pelvis) / Rommens classification grades low-energy osteoporotic pelvic ring injuries by the degree of **posterior ring involvement and displacement**, since posterior involvement (often occult on plain radiographs) is what predicts failure of conservative treatment and materially higher 1-year mortality. It is used to stratify elderly patients with apparently "simple" pubic rami fractures into conservative versus operative pathways.

## Types

| Type/Grade | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Ia / Ib** | Anterior ring only: unilateral (Ia) or bilateral (Ib) pubic rami fracture(s), no posterior involvement | Conservative: analgesia, early mobilization, osteoporosis workup |
| **IIa** | Nondisplaced posterior compression fracture of the sacral ala (impaction) + anterior ring fracture | Trial conservative treatment first; percutaneous fixation if pain refractory or mobilization fails |
| **IIb** | Minimally displaced (<10 mm) unilateral sacral fracture (incomplete) + anterior ring | As IIa; lower threshold for fixation if instability on standing films |
| **IIc** | Bilateral nondisplaced posterior lesions + anterior ring | As IIa/b, closer monitoring for progression |
| **IIIa/b/c** | Displaced unilateral posterior lesion (iliac fracture, SI joint dislocation, or sacral fracture) + anterior ring | Surgical: percutaneous posterior fixation (SI screw/TITS, often cement-augmented) |
| **IVa** | Bilateral displaced posterior lesions + anterior ring | Surgical: bilateral percutaneous posterior fixation |
| **IVb** | Spinopelvic dissociation (bilateral vertical sacral fractures + transverse component — "H" or "U" pattern) | Surgical: TITS screw(s) or lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation; consider decompression if neurologic deficit |

## Pearls

- CT or MRI upstages roughly 15–40% of apparently isolated "pubic rami fractures" to a combined anterior-posterior FFP pattern — plain radiographs alone are unreliable for staging. Any elderly patient with disproportionate pain or failure to mobilize after an apparently simple rami fracture warrants cross-sectional imaging of the posterior ring.
- **Practical caveat (2024–2025 literature)**: recent series challenge the strict Rommens algorithm — some FFP II patients do well with fixation up front (avoiding a failed conservative trial in a frail patient who cannot tolerate re-hospitalization), while select FFP III/IV patients without significant displacement or intractable pain have been managed successfully nonoperatively. Individualize based on frailty, pain trajectory, mobilization capacity, and displacement on serial imaging rather than applying the type strictly.
- Only 40–60% of FFP patients regain their pre-fracture mobility level at 1 year, and posterior involvement carries 1-year mortality comparable to hip fracture (roughly 10–20% in several series) — this underscores a low threshold for early fixation in patients failing conservative trials, and the value of orthogeriatric co-management regardless of type.
- Screw loosening/cutout is common in the osteoporotic bone typical of FFP patients, driving increasing use of cement-augmented ("screw-in-cement") constructs for operative types.
