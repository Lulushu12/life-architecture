## Overview

The Loder classification grades SCFE by clinical weight-bearing stability rather than radiographic severity — a stable slip can walk (with or without crutches); an unstable slip cannot bear weight at all. It is the single most important classification for management and prognosis in SCFE, since stability, not slip magnitude, drives surgical urgency and avascular necrosis (AVN) risk.

## Types

| Type | Definition | AVN risk | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stable | Patient can walk, with or without crutches, weight-bearing on the affected limb | ~0–5% | In-situ pinning, generally can be scheduled semi-urgently (within 24–48h) rather than as a middle-of-the-night emergency |
| Unstable | Patient cannot walk or bear weight on the affected limb at all, even with crutches | Historically reported 20–50%+ | Surgical urgency (same-day/urgent); gentle positional reduction with in-situ pinning is the most widely practiced approach; modified Dunn procedure is an alternative at high-volume centers |

## Pearls

- Assess weight-bearing ability with and without assistance at every evaluation — this single clinical determination is more prognostically important than any radiographic measurement (e.g. Southwick angle) in SCFE.
- Unstable SCFE is an orthopedic urgency: risk of osteonecrosis is high and rises further with delay or with attempted closed reduction/manipulation before fixation — do not attempt forceful reduction maneuvers beyond gentle positioning on the fracture table.
- Acute-on-chronic presentations (background chronic symptoms with an acute worsening, often after minor trauma) behave like unstable/acute slips regarding AVN risk and should be triaged accordingly.
- The in-situ pinning versus modified Dunn debate is specifically about how to treat unstable/severe slips — stable slips are essentially uniformly treated with in-situ single-screw fixation, so the Loder type itself substantially narrows the treatment discussion before considering slip severity (Southwick angle).
