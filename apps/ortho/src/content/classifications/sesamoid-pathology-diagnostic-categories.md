## Overview

This diagnostic framework distinguishes the four sesamoid pathology categories — asymptomatic bipartite variant, acute fracture, sesamoiditis/stress reaction, and osteonecrosis — by radiographic appearance and confirmatory imaging. The distinction matters clinically because bipartite sesamoids are common (roughly 10–33% of the population, often bilateral) and are easily mistaken for an acute fracture if the imaging pattern is not recognized.

## Types

| Entity | Radiographic clue | Confirmatory imaging | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bipartite sesamoid (asymptomatic anatomic variant) | Smooth, rounded, sclerotic (corticated) margins; often bilateral | Comparison contralateral-foot views; no marrow edema on MRI | No treatment; do not mistake for fracture |
| Acute sesamoid fracture | Sharp, jagged, non-sclerotic fracture line; single event/trauma history | MRI/bone scan shows acute marrow edema | Immobilization first; ORIF/bone graft only for symptomatic nonunion |
| Sesamoiditis / stress reaction | Often normal or subtle radiographs | MRI marrow edema without discrete fracture line | Offloading orthotic, activity modification, boot trial |
| Sesamoid osteonecrosis (AVN) | Increased density/fragmentation over time | MRI low signal, patchy or diffuse marrow change; bone scan may show decreased or increased uptake depending on stage | Prolonged offloading; partial sesamoidectomy if refractory |

## Pearls

- Obtain a dedicated **sesamoid axial (skyline) view** in addition to AP and lateral radiographs.
- Bipartite sesamoids have smooth, rounded, sclerotic margins versus the sharp, jagged, non-sclerotic margins of an acute fracture — this margin appearance is the key differentiator on plain film.
- Because ~10–33% of the population has a bipartite tibial sesamoid, frequently bilateral, **comparison views of the contralateral foot** are a practical, low-cost way to resolve equivocal cases before escalating to MRI/bone scan.
- The tibial sesamoid is far more frequently symptomatic than the fibular sesamoid, given greater axial load transmission through it during push-off.
- When surgery is needed, **partial** (not complete) sesamoidectomy is strongly preferred: complete tibial sesamoidectomy risks iatrogenic hallux valgus, and complete fibular sesamoidectomy risks iatrogenic hallux varus, from loss of the respective static/dynamic restraint.
