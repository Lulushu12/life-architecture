## Overview

The Pfirrmann classification grades lumbar disc degeneration on sagittal T2-weighted MRI, from a bright, well-hydrated normal disc (grade I) to a collapsed, black disc with no distinguishable nucleus (grade V). It is used as a common descriptive language for disc degeneration severity and to exclude gross disc pathology, but it correlates only weakly and inconsistently with symptom severity and is not a stand-alone surgical indication.

## Types

| Grade | MRI appearance | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Bright, homogeneous nucleus, normal height, clear nucleus/annulus distinction | Normal disc; not a pain generator per se |
| II | Bright, inhomogeneous nucleus ± horizontal grey bands, normal height, clear distinction | Early degeneration; conservative care if symptomatic |
| III | Grey, inhomogeneous nucleus, indistinct nucleus/annulus border, mild-moderate height loss | Correlates with early discogenic back pain; still primarily non-operative |
| IV | Grey/black inhomogeneous nucleus, lost nucleus/annulus distinction, moderate-severe height loss | May correlate with clinical discogenic pain but is a poor stand-alone surgical predictor |
| V | Black, collapsed disc space, no distinguishable nucleus | End-stage; if fusion contemplated for axial pain, correlate rigorously with provocative discography/clinical exam given weak isolated predictive value |

## Pearls

- Pfirrmann grade correlates weakly and inconsistently with symptom severity — its principal clinical use is as a common language for disc degeneration severity and for excluding gross disc pathology, not as a stand-alone surgical indication.
- Degenerative MRI findings, including advanced disc degeneration, are near-ubiquitous with age and frequently asymptomatic: disc bulge prevalence on MRI in asymptomatic people rises from ~30% in the 20s to >80% by the 80s (Brinjikji et al., AJNR 2015). Always establish clinical-imaging concordance rather than treating the grade in isolation.
- For isolated axial pain attributed to degenerative disc disease (Pfirrmann IV–V without instability or radiculopathy), fusion has the weakest evidence base of any diagnosis in this cluster; discography as a stand-alone "confirmatory" gatekeeper for fusion is not supported by current guideline bodies given its meaningful false-positive rate and potential to accelerate degeneration at the injected level.
