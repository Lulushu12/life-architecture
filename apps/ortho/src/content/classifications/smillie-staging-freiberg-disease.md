## Overview

Smillie staging grades the radiographic progression of Freiberg disease (osteonecrosis of a lesser metatarsal head, overwhelmingly the 2nd, classically in adolescent/young adult females) from an early subchondral fracture through to flattened, arthritic articular collapse. It determines whether the joint can still be preserved with a joint-sparing osteotomy or whether salvage debridement/arthroplasty is required.

## Types

| Stage | Findings | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Subchondral fracture of the metatarsal head, no collapse | Offloading/immobilization; usually resolves |
| II | Central depression of the articular surface | Continued conservative management, or joint debridement if refractory |
| III | Further central collapse with a viable dorsal or plantar hinge of articular cartilage | **Dorsiflexion closing-wedge osteotomy (Gauthier/Kinnard technique)** — rotates viable plantar articular cartilage dorsally into the load-bearing zone; joint-preserving procedure of choice at this stage |
| IV | Loose intra-articular body/fragmentation | Debridement, loose body excision ± osteotomy |
| V | Flattened, arthritic collapse of the metatarsal head | Joint debridement, interpositional arthroplasty, or implant/resection arthroplasty as salvage |

## Pearls

- **MRI is helpful early**, showing bone marrow edema and subchondral collapse before radiographic changes are visible, and is used to assess how much viable articular cartilage remains for joint-preserving osteotomy planning.
- **Stage III is the key inflection point**: a viable dorsal or plantar cartilage hinge is what makes the Gauthier/Kinnard dorsiflexion closing-wedge osteotomy feasible — once cartilage viability is lost (stage IV–V), the procedure shifts to debridement/salvage arthroplasty rather than joint preservation.
- Etiology is attributed to repetitive microtrauma to a vulnerable epiphyseal blood supply during rapid growth, combined with mechanical overload of a relatively long 2nd metatarsal — this is why the disease classically presents in adolescent/young adult females.
- Early-stage (Smillie I–II) disease is managed nonoperatively: activity modification, stiff-soled/rocker-bottom shoe, and a period of offloading (CAM boot, 4–6 weeks) to allow the fracture/collapse to stabilize.
