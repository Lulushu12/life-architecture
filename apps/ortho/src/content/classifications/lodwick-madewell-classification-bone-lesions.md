## Overview

The Lodwick–Madewell classification grades the plain-radiographic pattern of bone destruction — geographic, moth-eaten, or permeative — as a surrogate for growth rate and biological aggressiveness. It is the radiographic backbone of the referral rule in bone tumor evaluation: it determines whether a lesion can be observed or requires urgent MRI and referral to a treating center before any biopsy or intervention.

## Types

| Grade | Pattern | Zone of transition | Typical biological behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| IA | Geographic, with a sclerotic rim | Narrow, well-defined | Slow-growing, usually benign (e.g., latent bone cyst, non-ossifying fibroma) |
| IB | Geographic, well-defined but no sclerotic rim | Narrow-moderate | Usually benign but higher activity than IA (e.g., active UBC, enchondroma) |
| IC | Geographic, ill-defined margin | Wide | More aggressive — needs further workup (e.g., aggressive GCT, low-grade sarcoma) |
| II | Moth-eaten — multiple 0.5–1.5 cm coalescing lytic holes | Wide | Aggressive — infection, myeloma, lymphoma, metastasis, higher-grade sarcoma in differential |
| III | Permeative — numerous tiny (<1 mm) lytic foci through cortex | Wide, indistinct | Highly aggressive, rapid growth — osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma, infection |

## Pearls

- Grades II and III, and any IC lesion with correlating clinical red flags, mandate urgent MRI and referral to a treating center before any intervention — do not biopsy, inject, or curette these locally.
- Read periosteal reaction pattern alongside the Lodwick grade: a solid, uninterrupted periosteal reaction supports a slow/benign process, while lamellated ("onion-skin"), spiculated ("sunburst"/"hair-on-end"), or an interrupted reaction with a Codman triangle all support an aggressive process (classically osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma).
- This grading is a starting heuristic to shape pretest probability and urgency, not a substitute for tissue diagnosis — infection (Brodie abscess, osteomyelitis) frequently mimics an aggressive Lodwick pattern radiographically, so ESR/CRP should be checked when infection is in the differential.
- Plain radiographs (orthogonal views of the full bone) remain the single most informative study for characterizing growth rate via zone of transition and periosteal reaction pattern, and should always be obtained first, before MRI or other advanced imaging.
