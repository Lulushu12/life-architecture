## Overview

The O'Driscoll classification categorizes coronoid fractures by fragment location (tip, anteromedial facet, or base) and size, and is the key determinant of surgical approach — lateral vs. medial — because different fragment types capture different soft-tissue attachments critical to elbow stability. CT is required to apply this classification reliably, since coronoid fragment size and comminution are frequently underestimated on plain radiographs.

## Types

| Type | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Type 1 — Tip fracture** | Subtype 1: <2mm height; Subtype 2: ≥2mm height | Small tip fragments (<2mm) are often left alone if the elbow is stable after LCL/radial head fixation; larger tip fragments (≥2mm) get suture fixation through drill holes to capture the anterior capsule attachment even when the bone fragment itself is unfixable |
| **Type 2 — Anteromedial facet fracture** | Subtype 1: rim only; Subtype 2: rim + tip; Subtype 3: rim + sublime tubercle | Associated with varus posteromedial rotatory instability (VPMI), a distinct pattern from terrible triad; often requires buttress plating of the anteromedial facet (medial approach) because this fragment is critical for varus stability and is NOT adequately addressed by LCL repair alone |
| **Type 3 — Basal fracture** | Subtype 1: coronoid body/base, no involvement of olecranon; Subtype 2: transolecranon basal fracture (extends into and through the proximal ulna/olecranon) | Large structural fragment requiring formal fixation (screws antegrade/retrograde, or plate) — essential for anterior stability; Type 3.2 (transolecranon) requires olecranon fixation that indirectly captures the coronoid fragment |

## Pearls

- **Clinical use:** the type dictates surgical approach. Type 1 and most Type 2 subtype-1 injuries are addressed through the lateral approach as part of terrible-triad sequencing; anteromedial facet fractures (Type 2, especially subtype 2/3) and large basal fractures (Type 3) usually require a separate medial approach for direct buttress fixation, because lateral-only fixation cannot adequately capture these fragments.
- Fragments below the O'Driscoll "tip" level are often not visible on a standard lateral radiograph but are critical to the anterior capsular/brachialis attachment — CT is essential whenever fragment size or comminution will change the fixation plan.
- Loss of coronoid height, even a small tip fragment, disproportionately destabilizes the elbow because it removes both the anterior bony block and the soft-tissue sleeve attached to it.
- Type 2 (anteromedial facet) injuries produce a distinct instability pattern (VPMI) from the classic terrible triad and are easy to under-treat if only the lateral side is addressed.
