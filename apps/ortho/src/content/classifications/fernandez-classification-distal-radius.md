## Overview

The Fernandez classification organizes distal radius fractures by injury mechanism (bending, shearing, compression, avulsion, combined/high-energy) rather than by pure morphology. It is favored by many surgeons for surgical decision-making at the bedside because it explicitly links mechanism and soft-tissue implication to fixation strategy — buttress vs bridging vs combined — unlike AO/OTA's purely morphologic scheme.

## Types

| Type | Mechanism | Pattern | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Bending (metaphyseal) | Extra-articular fracture (Colles/Smith) | Closed reduction/cast if stable; percutaneous pinning or plating if unstable |
| II | Shearing | Articular fracture of the joint surface (Barton, radial styloid/Chauffeur) | Inherently unstable — ORIF (buttress plating) essentially always required |
| III | Compression | Articular impaction/die-punch, comminuted central depression | Requires elevation/bone graft or substitute, fragment-specific or dorsal plating; arthroscopic-assisted reduction useful |
| IV | Avulsion | Radiocarpal fracture-dislocation with ligamentous avulsion (styloid processes) | High association with DRUJ/carpal ligament instability — fix bony fragments and reassess/treat ligamentous stability |
| V | Combined/high-energy | Combination of above + severe soft tissue injury (high-velocity, e.g., MVC, fall from height) | Often needs staged management, external fixation, soft tissue coverage planning; highest complication risk |

## Pearls

- Reach for Fernandez specifically when deciding fixation strategy at the bedside — it is mechanism-based and maps directly to buttress vs bridging vs combined fixation, unlike a purely morphologic scheme.
- Type II (shearing) patterns are inherently unstable and essentially always require ORIF — closed management is not a reasonable option for this mechanism.
- Type III (compression/die-punch) is the pattern that drives the decision toward fragment-specific or dorsal plating and arthroscopic-assisted reduction.
- Type V combined/high-energy injuries carry the highest complication risk and often require staged management rather than single-stage definitive fixation.
