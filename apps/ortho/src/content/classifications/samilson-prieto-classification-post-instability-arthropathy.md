## Overview

The Samilson-Prieto classification radiographically grades degenerative changes ("dislocation arthropathy") that develop after recurrent shoulder instability or after instability surgery, based on the size of the humeral head/glenoid osteophyte on an AP radiograph. It is used to stage post-instability arthropathy severity and to guide the choice between observation, arthroscopic debridement, and arthroplasty.

## Types

| Type/Grade | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild (I)** | Inferior humeral head osteophyte ≤3 mm | Observation; optimize activity, NSAIDs; continue instability surveillance |
| **Moderate (II)** | Osteophyte 3–7 mm, with or without mild joint space narrowing | Symptom-directed conservative care; consider arthroscopic debridement/capsular release for mechanical symptoms; counsel on arthroplasty timeline |
| **Severe (III)** | Osteophyte >7 mm with glenohumeral joint space narrowing/irregularity | Consider arthroplasty (TSA if glenoid bone stock and cuff intact, or reverse TSA if cuff-deficient/glenoid-deficient/younger-patient bone-block hardware complicates anatomy); avoid further soft-tissue-only stabilization surgery |

## Pearls

- Instability surgery itself is a risk factor for this arthropathy — historically higher after non-anatomic procedures such as Putti-Platt or Magnuson-Stack, and after intra-articular hardware prominence.
- Anatomic, extra-articular hardware placement (as taught in modern Latarjet technique, avoiding lateral screw overhang) is intended to mitigate this progression risk.
- Rapid glenohumeral arthropathy after stabilization surgery — especially with prominent hardware/staples — should raise concern for Samilson-Prieto progression and prompt hardware assessment on CT.
- Long-term risk of this arthropathy is higher with repeated dislocation events before surgery, reinforcing the rationale for earlier, well-selected stabilization in high-risk patients.
