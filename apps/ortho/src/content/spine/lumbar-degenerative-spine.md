## Quick Reference

**Red flags mandating same-day imaging / escalation**
- **Cauda equina syndrome (CES):** saddle/perianal anaesthesia, new bladder dysfunction (retention with overflow incontinence is the most sensitive sign; loss of urge to void), faecal incontinence/loss of anal tone, bilateral leg pain/weakness, sexual dysfunction. **Any one of these → emergency whole-spine MRI within 4 hours of request (UK GIRFT National CES Pathway 2023), decompression as soon as safely possible** — do not wait for a fixed 24/48h "window"; earlier decompression trends toward better bladder/bowel/sexual recovery even though the historical 24–48h cutoff for motor/sensory recovery is not sharply supported by modern series.
- Screen for these at **every** visit for a patient with low back and/or leg pain, not only the first — CES can develop in a patient previously managed conservatively for a known herniation or stenosis, and delayed recognition on a return visit is a recurring theme in medicolegal case reviews.

Other red flags: age <20 or >55 with new back pain, history of malignancy, unexplained weight loss, fever/IV drug use/immunosuppression (infection), significant trauma, progressive neurological deficit, thoracic pain, structural deformity, systemic steroid use (fracture risk). Each of these should prompt consideration of an alternative or coexisting non-degenerative diagnosis (infection, malignancy, fracture) rather than a default degenerative work-up.

**Diagnosis-specific treatment ladder**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical indication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Disc herniation w/ radiculopathy | Activity modification, NSAIDs, PT (McKenzie/directional preference, nerve glides), 6 wk trial | Transforaminal ESI (short-term relief, NNT ~4 for pain) | Persistent/disabling sciatica >6–12 wk despite conservative care, progressive motor deficit, or CES (emergency) |
| Spinal stenosis (neurogenic claudication) | Flexion-biased exercise, weight loss, walking-pole gait training, NSAIDs | ESI (modest, short-term disability benefit only), lumbar orthosis | Progressive claudication limiting function despite 3–6 mo conservative care; decompression ± fusion per stability criteria |
| Degenerative spondylolisthesis | As per stenosis | ESI | Decompression alone if stable (no gross instability, ≤ Meyerding II, minimal listhesis progression on flex-ex); add fusion if dynamic instability, prior laminectomy at level, facet sagittalization, or predominant mechanical back pain |
| Isthmic spondylolisthesis | Core stabilization, activity modification, bracing (adolescents) | ESI/pars injection (diagnostic ± therapeutic) | Persistent radiculopathy or high-grade (Meyerding III–V)/progressive slip → decompression + instrumented fusion ± reduction |
| Degenerative disc disease (axial, no instability) | Exercise therapy, weight control, CBT for pain, activity pacing | Discography is **not** recommended as a stand-alone indication for fusion (poor predictive value) | Fusion reserved for highly selected cases after exhaustive conservative care; evidence for isolated single-level fusion in DDD is weak |
| Cauda equina syndrome | N/A — emergency | N/A | Emergency MRI + decompression ASAP, regardless of time of day |
| Facet arthropathy | NSAIDs, PT (extension avoidance if pain provoked by extension), weight loss | Intra-articular facet or medial branch block (diagnostic, ≥75–80% relief on 2 blocks) → radiofrequency ablation if positive | Fusion only if facet disease coexists with structural instability |
| Non-specific LBP | Reassurance, stay active, exercise programme (NICE strong recommendation), avoid bed rest | Manual therapy/spinal manipulation as part of a package; NSAIDs short course | Surgery **not** indicated; imaging not routinely indicated without red flags |

**Classification quick-scan (full detail in Classification section below)**

| Classification | Used for | Grades | One-line treatment takeaway |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meyerding | Spondylolisthesis severity | I–V | Grade I–II usually decompression ± conservative; III–V usually needs instrumented fusion, high-grade reduction is nerve-root risk |
| Pfirrmann | Disc degeneration (T2 MRI) | I–V | Weak stand-alone predictor of pain; supports diagnosis, not surgical indication alone |
| Modic | Vertebral endplate marrow change | 1–3 | Type 1 = most pain-associated and highest pseudarthrosis risk at fusion; Types 2–3 more mechanically stable |
| Lee zones | Herniation location (axial/sagittal) | Zone 1–4 | Determines surgical trajectory — Zone 3–4 needs a more lateral/far-lateral or transforaminal endoscopic approach, standard laminotomy will miss it |
| Schizas | Central stenosis morphology (axial T2) | A–D | C/D grades correlate best with symptomatic, surgery-warranting stenosis when clinically concordant |

**Key numbers**
- CES emergency MRI: **within 4 hours** of request (GIRFT 2023).
- Meyerding grading: measured on lateral radiograph, slip % of AP vertebral body width, 5 grades (I–V, V = spondyloptosis).
- Pfirrmann grading: 5 grades on T2 sagittal MRI, grade V = collapsed disc.
- Facet block diagnostic threshold: ≥75–80% pain relief on dual comparative blocks before RFA.
- SPORT trial (disc herniation): surgery advantage over nonoperative maintained to **8 years**; ~10-point greater improvement in SF-36/ODI; **15% reoperation by 8 years**.
- Degenerative spondylolisthesis decompression-alone vs fusion: **Nordsten-DS at 5 years — decompression alone non-inferior** to decompression+fusion (BMJ 2024); Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study (Försth) similarly showed no advantage to fusion; Ghogawala (SLIP) trial showed fusion superior on SF-36 and reoperation at 4 years — evidence is genuinely mixed, hence AO Spine 2025 recommendations for individualized selection.
- ESI NNT for radicular pain: **~4** (short-term pain), **~6** (short-term disability), **~9** (long-term disability) per 2024–2025 AAN systematic review.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The lumbar functional spinal unit (FSU) comprises two adjacent vertebrae, the intervertebral disc, paired facet (zygapophyseal) joints, and the surrounding ligamentous complex (anterior/posterior longitudinal ligaments, ligamentum flavum, interspinous/supraspinous ligaments). The disc is a three-joint complex with the facets; degeneration of one component accelerates degeneration of the others ("degenerative cascade" of Kirkaldy-Willis: dysfunction → instability → restabilization/stenosis).

**Disc degeneration** begins with biochemical changes in the nucleus pulposus — loss of proteoglycan/water content, decreased type II collagen, increased type I collagen — leading to loss of disc height, annular fissuring, and reduced hydrostatic load distribution. This shifts load onto the annulus and facets, promoting facet arthropathy, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy (in response to abnormal segmental motion), and osteophyte formation, which together narrow the central canal, lateral recess, and neuroforamen — the substrate of degenerative spinal stenosis.

**Disc herniation** occurs when nuclear/annular material displaces beyond the normal disc margin, most often posterolaterally due to the relative thinness of the PLL laterally. Sciatica results from a combination of mechanical nerve root compression and a chemical radiculitis driven by nucleus pulposus-derived inflammatory mediators (phospholipase A2, TNF-alpha, IL-6, nitric oxide) — this dual mechanism explains why radicular pain can occur without severe compression, and why the natural history favors spontaneous resorption in a majority of cases (extruded/sequestered fragments resorb at higher rates than protrusions, via macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and neovascularization).

**Degenerative spondylolisthesis** is anterior (rarely lateral or posterior) translation of one vertebra on another through degenerated, incompetent facet joints and disc, occurring **without a pars defect**, classically at L4-L5 in women >60, associated with sagittally-oriented facets and generalized ligamentous laxity. **Isthmic spondylolisthesis** results from a pars interarticularis defect (spondylolysis) — a fatigue/stress fracture typically in adolescent athletes performing repetitive extension/rotation (gymnastics, fast bowling, weightlifting) — most common at L5-S1, and can progress to slip during the adolescent growth spurt or remain stable into adulthood, later presenting with degenerative disc/foraminal changes at the slip level.

**Facet arthropathy** is osteoarthritis of the synovial zygapophyseal joints, contributing to axial mechanical back pain (often worse with extension/rotation, relieved by flexion) and, through hypertrophy, to lateral recess and foraminal stenosis.

**Facet-mediated axial pain** arises from the richly innervated facet capsule and synovium (innervated by the medial branch of the dorsal ramus at the same level and the level above), which becomes a pain generator as cartilage thins, subchondral bone sclereses, and capsular laxity/hypertrophy develops — the same process that narrows the lateral recess and contributes to foraminal stenosis when combined with disc height loss.

**Cauda equina syndrome** results from compression of the lumbosacral nerve roots distal to the conus medullaris (typically ends L1-L2 in adults) — most commonly from a large central/paracentral disc extrusion, but also from spinal stenosis, epidural hematoma/abscess, or tumor. Because the cauda equina is a collection of peripheral nerve roots bathed in CSF rather than a single cord structure, compression produces a variable, often asymmetric mix of motor, sensory, and autonomic (bladder/bowel/sexual) deficits — hence the emphasis on bladder function and perianal sensation as the most reliable clinical discriminators.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

### General principles
Degenerative imaging findings are near-ubiquitous with age and are frequently asymptomatic — the diagnostic task is establishing **concordance** between the clinical pattern and the imaging finding, not simply reporting the most abnormal-looking level. Treat every new patient encounter with leg-dominant pain or bladder/bowel change as a CES screen, regardless of the presumed primary diagnosis.

**History:** pain distribution (axial vs radicular — dermatomal leg pain typically dominates over back pain in true radiculopathy), aggravating/relieving positions (flexion relief + extension provocation suggests stenosis/facet; the reverse suggests discogenic), neurogenic claudication pattern (bilateral, vague, relieved by flexion/sitting, worsened by standing/extension — distinguish from vascular claudication which is relieved by simply stopping, unaffected by posture, with diminished pulses), bladder/bowel/saddle symptoms (screen every patient with low back pain and leg symptoms), constitutional symptoms, prior spine surgery, oncologic history, IV drug use/fever.

**Examination:** straight leg raise (SLR, sensitive ~90% but poor specificity for L5/S1 herniation; positive 30–70°), crossed SLR (less sensitive, ~25–30%, but highly specific ~90% for herniation), femoral stretch test (L2-L4 roots), myotomal strength (L4 = tibialis anterior/quadriceps, L5 = EHL/gluteus medius, S1 = gastrocnemius/peroneals), dermatomal sensation, reflexes (L4 = patellar, S1 = Achilles), gait (heel/toe walk), and — mandatory whenever CES is on the differential — perianal sensation and digital rectal tone/exam, post-void residual bladder scan.

**Imaging:**
- Plain radiographs (AP/lateral, flexion-extension) remain the first study for suspected instability/spondylolisthesis — flexion-extension views detect dynamic translation (>4 mm or >10–15° angular motion suggests instability) that static MRI misses.
- MRI is the study of choice for disc herniation, stenosis, and CES; obtain **emergently** for suspected CES.
- CT is preferred for pars defects not clearly seen on MRI, bony foraminal stenosis, and pre-operative instrumentation planning, or when MRI is contraindicated.
- CT myelogram is reserved for patients with MRI contraindications (pacemaker, severe claustrophobia) or extensive hardware artifact.
- Correlate imaging findings with clinical picture always: degenerative MRI findings (disc bulges, Modic change, mild stenosis) are extremely common in **asymptomatic** adults — prevalence of disc bulge on MRI in asymptomatic people rises from ~30% in the 20s to >80% by the 80s (Brinjikji et al., AJNR 2015) — so imaging must never substitute for a concordant clinical exam.

### Quantitative imaging thresholds worth knowing
- Central canal cross-sectional area <100 mm² (or anteroposterior canal diameter <10 mm) is a commonly cited threshold for "severe" central stenosis, though morphological grading (Schizas) correlates better with symptoms than absolute measurements alone.
- Foraminal height <15 mm or foraminal area <40 mm² are commonly used thresholds for radiographic foraminal stenosis, best assessed on parasagittal MRI.
- Disc height loss and facet effusion >1.5 mm on supine MRI have both been proposed as adjunctive radiographic markers of segmental instability, though flexion-extension radiographs remain the clinical reference standard for dynamic instability.

### Per-diagnosis evaluation notes

- **Disc herniation:** leg pain > back pain, dermatomal pattern, positive SLR/crossed SLR, weakness in the corresponding myotome; MRI confirms level/side/zone (see Lee classification below) — order it once conservative care has failed or if there is a progressive deficit, not routinely at first presentation of uncomplicated sciatica.
- **Spinal stenosis:** the "shopping cart sign" (relief leaning on a cart) and bicycle test (pain-free cycling despite limited walking tolerance) are useful bedside discriminators from vascular claudication; examine peripheral pulses and consider ankle-brachial index if vascular disease is also plausible (common comorbidity in the same age group).
- **Degenerative spondylolisthesis:** palpable "step-off" is unreliable; flexion-extension radiographs are mandatory before any surgical planning to assess dynamic instability, which static MRI cannot show.
- **Isthmic spondylolisthesis:** in adolescents/young adults with extension-provoked back pain, single-leg hyperextension ("stork") test raises suspicion for pars pathology; oblique radiographs ("Scotty dog" fracture sign) or thin-cut CT confirm the pars defect when SPECT/MRI edema suggests an active lesion.
- **Cauda equina syndrome:** perianal sensation and post-void residual bladder scan are mandatory in any patient with bilateral leg symptoms or reported bladder change — do not rely on the patient volunteering bladder/bowel symptoms; ask directly, as embarrassment commonly suppresses spontaneous reporting.
- **Facet arthropathy:** pain reproduced with lumbar extension and rotation, paraspinal tenderness over the facet joint line, relief with flexion; imaging (facet hypertrophy, effusion, or T2 signal change on MRI) is supportive but not diagnostic alone — the reference standard remains a positive diagnostic block.
- **Non-specific LBP:** the evaluation goal is primarily red-flag exclusion; a focused history and exam without imaging is appropriate for the great majority of first presentations per NICE and ACP guidance.

## Classification

### Meyerding grading (spondylolisthesis severity)

| Grade | Slip (% of caudal vertebral AP diameter) | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | 0–25% | Usually managed non-operatively unless symptomatic and refractory; low fusion-instability risk |
| II | 25–50% | Most degenerative and many isthmic slips fall here; decompression alone often adequate if degenerative and stable; isthmic slips more often benefit from fusion given the pars defect (inherent posterior element incompetence) |
| III | 50–75% | Usually warrants instrumented fusion; consider reduction (partial) in isthmic cases, monitor L5 nerve root during reduction |
| IV | 75–100% | High-grade — fusion (often circumferential/interbody), controversial reduction vs in-situ fusion given L5 root traction risk with aggressive reduction |
| V (spondyloptosis) | >100%, complete translation | Rare; complex reconstruction, often staged, high neurologic risk; specialist deformity referral |

Note: Meyerding is a **static** severity grade — it does not capture instability. Always pair it with flexion-extension films for degenerative spondylolisthesis surgical planning.

### Pfirrmann grading (disc degeneration on T2 MRI)

| Grade | MRI appearance | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Bright, homogeneous nucleus, normal height, clear nucleus/annulus distinction | Normal disc; not a pain generator per se |
| II | Bright, inhomogeneous nucleus ± horizontal grey bands, normal height, clear distinction | Early degeneration; conservative care if symptomatic |
| III | Grey, inhomogeneous nucleus, indistinct nucleus/annulus border, mild-moderate height loss | Correlates with early discogenic back pain; still primarily non-operative |
| IV | Grey/black inhomogeneous nucleus, lost nucleus/annulus distinction, moderate-severe height loss | May correlate with clinical discogenic pain but poor stand-alone surgical predictor |
| V | Black, collapsed disc space, no distinguishable nucleus | End-stage; if fusion contemplated for axial pain, correlate rigorously with provocative discography/clinical exam given weak isolated predictive value |

Pfirrmann grade correlates weakly and inconsistently with symptom severity — its principal clinical use is as a common language for disc degeneration severity and for excluding gross disc pathology, not as a stand-alone surgical indication.

### Modic changes (vertebral endplate/marrow signal changes on MRI)

| Type | MRI signal | Histology | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type 1 | T1 hypointense, T2 hyperintense | Vascularized fibrous marrow replacement, inflammatory/edematous | Most strongly associated with active discogenic low back pain and Modic-related pain; may respond to antibiotics in a subset attributed to low-virulence *Cutibacterium acnes* infection (Birkenmaier/AIM trial data — still debated, not standard of care); higher fusion nonunion risk if endplate inflamed at surgery |
| Type 2 | T1 hyperintense, T2 iso/hyperintense | Fatty marrow replacement | More stable, chronic; weaker/inconsistent pain association than Type 1 |
| Type 3 | T1 hypointense, T2 hypointense | Subchondral bony sclerosis | End-stage, mechanically stiff segment; least inflammatory |

Modic 1 changes adjacent to a planned fusion level raise concern for higher pseudarthrosis risk and can prompt consideration of extending instrumentation or optimizing endplate preparation; Modic changes are not in themselves an operative indication.

### Lee classification (disc herniation zone, axial + sagittal)

Zone system standardizing herniation location for surgical approach planning:

| Zone | Location | Approach implication |
|---|---|---|
| Zone 1 (central) | Central canal | Standard interlaminar approach; larger fragments may need bilateral or wider laminotomy |
| Zone 2 (subarticular/lateral recess) | Beneath the facet, lateral to thecal sac | Most common radiculopathy-producing zone; interlaminar approach with medial facetectomy as needed |
| Zone 3 (foraminal) | Within the neural foramen | Requires more lateral trajectory; risk of missing the fragment with a standard interlaminar window — consider far-lateral/foraminal or transforaminal endoscopic approach |
| Zone 4 (extraforaminal/far-lateral) | Lateral to the foramen | Requires a far-lateral (Wiltse/paraspinal) approach or extraforaminal endoscopic technique; standard laminotomy will miss it entirely |

The sagittal component (disc level vs migration cranial/caudal, "high-grade up/down migration") further refines approach — significantly migrated fragments (superiorly behind the pedicle, or far caudally) change the trajectory and extent of bony resection needed and are a common cause of missed fragments at revision surgery.

### Schizas classification (morphological grading of lumbar central stenosis on axial T2 MRI)

| Grade | Description (rootlet/CSF pattern) | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| A (A1–A4) | CSF clearly visible, rootlets recognizable, occupying various proportions of the canal | Mild-moderate stenosis; conservative management typically appropriate first |
| B | No CSF visible but rootlets still distinguishable, occupying most of the canal | Moderate-severe; surgical candidacy driven by symptoms/function, not grade alone |
| C | Rootlets not distinguishable, forming a homogeneous grey mass, canal fully occupied but epidural fat still visible | Severe stenosis; strong morphological correlate of symptomatic canal compromise |
| D | No epidural fat visible at all, rootlets not distinguishable from surrounding structures | Most severe grade; when concordant with clinical claudication/radiculopathy, favors operative decompression |

Schizas grades C and D correlate best with symptomatic, functionally limiting stenosis and are the most useful morphological grades for surgical decision support, but as with all classifications here, surgery is offered for the *symptom-imaging concordance*, not the grade in isolation.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Non-specific LBP:** NICE NG59 (most recently updated 2025–2026) is the benchmark guideline: promote self-management and staying active; offer a **group exercise programme** as first-line (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body, or a combination); consider manual therapy (spinal manipulation, mobilisation, or soft tissue techniques) **only as part of a treatment package** including exercise; **do not offer opioids**; NSAIDs are the pharmacological option of choice at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time; do **not** routinely offer imaging in primary/secondary care without red flags or unless it will change management; psychological therapy (CBT) and combined physical-psychological programmes were **withdrawn** from the guideline in the 2026 update pending updated evidence review — do not cite them as current NICE recommendations. The American College of Physicians (ACP) guideline (2017, still the reference US position) concurs: nonpharmacologic first-line (exercise, multidisciplinary rehab, spinal manipulation, CBT, mindfulness), NSAIDs/muscle relaxants second-line, opioids last-resort only.
- **Radiculopathy from disc herniation:** structured PT emphasizing directional preference exercises (McKenzie method — extension-biased for most posterolateral herniations), nerve mobility/gliding, activity modification avoiding prolonged flexion/sitting; NSAIDs; short course of oral corticosteroids is of unproven benefit and not routinely recommended; a 6-week trial of conservative care is standard before considering intervention in the absence of progressive deficit.
- **Neurogenic claudication/stenosis:** flexion-biased exercise programs, stationary cycling (tolerated better than walking due to flexed posture), lumbar stabilization/core strengthening, weight loss, and a trial (3–6 months) before surgical referral in the absence of progressive deficit; AAOS guidance supports supervised exercise and multimodal non-surgical care as reasonable options, while acknowledging limited high-quality evidence for any single non-surgical modality's superiority.
- **Degenerative disc disease (axial pain, no red flags/instability):** the mainstay is exercise therapy, weight optimization, ergonomic/activity modification, and pain self-management (CBT-based approaches); passive modalities (heat, TENS) have limited evidence and are adjunctive only.
- **Isthmic spondylolisthesis (low-grade, adolescent):** activity restriction from provocative extension/impact sports, core stabilization, and bracing (e.g., Boston brace/TLSO) for symptomatic low-grade slips in skeletally immature patients, with serial radiographs to monitor progression through the growth spurt; most low-grade isthmic slips (Meyerding I) remain stable and are managed non-operatively long-term.
- **Facet arthropathy:** weight loss, activity modification away from extension/rotation-provoking positions, NSAIDs, core/extensor strengthening.

**Pharmacotherapy notes for non-specific LBP and radiculopathy**

| Agent class | Role per current guidance |
|---|---|
| NSAIDs | First-line pharmacological option (NICE/ACP) at lowest effective dose, shortest duration; account for GI/renal/cardiovascular risk in older patients |
| Paracetamol (as monotherapy) | Not recommended as a stand-alone analgesic for LBP by NICE — limited efficacy over placebo in trial evidence |
| Muscle relaxants | Modest short-term benefit in acute non-specific LBP per ACP; sedation limits use, particularly in older/working patients |
| Oral corticosteroids | Not routinely recommended for radiculopathy — trial evidence shows no meaningful functional benefit over placebo despite occasional short-term symptomatic effect |
| Gabapentinoids | Not supported by current evidence for acute radicular pain; occasionally used off-label in chronic neuropathic-pattern pain with modest, inconsistent benefit |
| Opioids | **Not recommended** by NICE for LBP/sciatica; ACP reserves as last-resort only after other options fail, given addiction/overdose risk |

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Transforaminal/interlaminar epidural steroid injection (ESI):** best evidence is for **short-term** relief of radicular pain from disc herniation — 2024–2025 AAN systematic review reports **NNT ≈4 for short-term pain reduction, ≈6 for short-term disability reduction, ≈9 for long-term disability**; evidence for long-term pain reduction is insufficient. For neurogenic claudication from **stenosis**, ESI has weaker evidence — possibly effective for short- and long-term *disability* but **not** short-term pain; ESI does not reduce the eventual likelihood of surgery compared with non-steroid injectate. Safe to perform in severe multilevel stenosis. No significant difference in outcomes between interlaminar, transforaminal, or caudal routes or between corticosteroid formulations in comparative trials.
- **Diagnostic facet (zygapophyseal) or medial branch blocks:** used to select candidates for radiofrequency ablation; a **≥75–80% pain relief on two separate comparative blocks (placebo-controlled or comparative local anesthetic)** is the accepted threshold before proceeding to RFA, given the noted high false-positive rate of single blocks.
- **Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) of medial branch nerves for confirmed facet-mediated pain:** Level II evidence for pain and function improvement (2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of placebo-controlled RCTs); effect on quality-of-life and global impression less clear; typical duration of relief 6–12 months, may be repeated; three-tined/multi-tined electrode techniques are an emerging refinement aiming to increase lesion size and durability, but long-term comparative data are still maturing.
- **Interspinous process spacers / minimally invasive indirect decompression devices:** an option for mild-moderate stenosis with preserved extension tolerance in patients who are poor candidates for formal decompression; effect sizes are modest and reoperation rates are non-trivial — not first-line over standard decompression in operative candidates.
- **Regenerative/biologic injections (PRP, stem cell, intradiscal biologics) for discogenic pain:** remain investigational; insufficient high-quality evidence to recommend routine use outside of trials.

### Operative

**Lumbar disc herniation — microdiscectomy / discectomy**
- Indications: persistent disabling radiculopathy despite 6–12 weeks of conservative care, progressive/severe motor deficit, or CES (emergent).
- Standard: open or tubular **microdiscectomy** via interlaminar window with partial hemilaminotomy/flavectomy, medial facetectomy as needed for lateral recess exposure, nerve root retraction, and fragment excision; aggressive versus limited (fragmentectomy-only) discectomy is a long-standing trade-off — fragmentectomy alone reduces same-level recurrence risk less than aggressive annular/nucleus removal, but aggressive removal increases disc height loss and theoretically accelerates degeneration; most surgeons perform a fragment excision plus loose/free intradiscal material removal without complete nuclear evacuation.
- **Endoscopic discectomy (full-endoscopic interlaminar or transforaminal / percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy, PELD):** rising adoption worldwide; 2024–2025 comparative cohorts and RCT-adjacent data show endoscopic approaches are **non-inferior** to open/tubular microdiscectomy for leg pain, ODI, and JOA outcomes, with **shorter length of stay (often <2 days vs standard admission), faster return to work, and comparable or lower complication/dural tear/reoperation rates** in experienced hands; the transforaminal route is particularly suited to foraminal/far-lateral (Lee Zone 3–4) herniations. Learning curve is real — outcomes in early-adopter series lag behind high-volume centers; case selection (avoid highly migrated fragments and significant bony lateral recess stenosis in low-volume settings) remains important.
- Approach selection by Lee zone: central/subarticular (Zone 1–2) — standard interlaminar micro/endoscopic; foraminal (Zone 3) — more lateral trajectory, consider transforaminal endoscopic or extended facetectomy; far-lateral/extraforaminal (Zone 4) — Wiltse paraspinal approach or extraforaminal endoscopic technique; standard midline laminotomy will miss these.
- Pitfalls: missed fragment (especially migrated or Zone 3-4), dural tear (~2–3% open, generally lower reported in endoscopic series), incidental durotomy management (primary repair, fibrin sealant, or accept for small CSF leaks with endoscopic technique given different fluid dynamics), same-level recurrent herniation (~5–15% lifetime risk, higher with large annular defects), and epidural hematoma.

**Lumbar spinal stenosis — decompression**
- Techniques: laminectomy (classic, wide exposure, greater facet/interspinous ligament disruption risk of iatrogenic instability), laminotomy/hemilaminectomy, bilateral decompression via unilateral laminotomy (**"over-the-top" technique**, preserves midline posterior tension band and spinous process, reduces iatrogenic instability while achieving bilateral canal/lateral recess decompression), and minimally invasive tubular decompression.
- Preserve as much of the pars interarticularis, facet joint capsule, and midline structures as possible to minimize iatrogenic instability, particularly when not fusing.
- Add fusion when: pre-existing spondylolisthesis with dynamic instability, iatrogenic destabilization anticipated from extensive facetectomy (>50%), same-level revision decompression, adjacent scoliosis/deformity requiring correction, or predominant mechanical back pain.

**Degenerative spondylolisthesis — decompression alone vs decompression + fusion**
- This is one of the most actively debated areas in spine surgery; the practicing surgeon should be conversant with the trial landscape:
  - **SLIP trial (Ghogawala et al., NEJM 2016):** for single-level stable degenerative spondylolisthesis with stenosis, fusion + decompression showed better SF-36 physical component scores and a lower reoperation rate at 4 years compared to decompression alone — the traditional basis for routine fusion.
  - **Swedish Spinal Stenosis Study (Försth et al., NEJM 2016):** in a broader stenosis population including those with spondylolisthesis, adding fusion conferred **no advantage** in any outcome measure or reoperation rate at 2 years (and this held at longer follow-up).
  - **Austevoll/NORDSTEN-DS trial (Norway):** decompression alone was **non-inferior** to decompression+fusion for ODI improvement at 2 years, confirmed at **5-year follow-up (BMJ, August 2024)** — the most robust and longest RCT follow-up currently available, reinforcing that routine fusion is not required for most stable degenerative spondylolisthesis.
  - A 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis of randomized trials similarly found no consistent advantage for added fusion, while fusion increased operative morbidity, blood loss, and cost.
  - **AO Spine Clinical Practice Recommendations (2025, Global Spine Journal):** synthesize this evidence into individualized guidance — favor **decompression alone** for degenerative spondylolisthesis without gross dynamic instability (no significant motion on flexion-extension, no prior same-level decompression, minimal slip progression), and reserve **fusion** for cases with radiographic instability, facet sagittalization/effusion suggesting a hypermobile segment, revision surgery at the same level, or predominant axial back pain out of proportion to leg symptoms.
  - Practical synthesis for the practicing surgeon: default to decompression alone (with a bilateral-via-unilateral or facet-sparing technique) for straightforward stable Meyerding I–II degenerative spondylolisthesis; add instrumented posterolateral or interbody fusion when instability criteria are met or when revision/high clinical suspicion of a "pain generator" at the disc/facet exists.
- Fusion technique options when fusion is indicated: posterolateral instrumented fusion (pedicle screws + posterolateral bone graft), transforaminal/posterior lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF/PLIF) adding anterior column support and indirect foraminal height restoration, oblique/lateral lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF/LLIF) for indirect decompression with less direct neural manipulation in appropriately selected anatomy, and minimally invasive percutaneous screw techniques to reduce approach-related morbidity in appropriately selected patients.

**Interbody fusion approach selection**

| Approach | Access | Advantages | Trade-offs / selection caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| PLIF (posterior lumbar interbody fusion) | Posterior, bilateral facet/nerve root retraction | Direct decompression + fusion in one posterior exposure | Greater nerve root/thecal sac retraction, higher dural tear risk than TLIF |
| TLIF (transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion) | Posterior, unilateral facetectomy | Less neural retraction than PLIF, workhorse for single/two-level degenerative disease with stenosis | Unilateral cage placement can under-restore contralateral foraminal height if not carefully executed |
| OLIF/ALIF (oblique/anterior lumbar interbody fusion) | Anterior or antero-oblique, retroperitoneal, no direct canal exposure | Large footprint cage restores disc height/lordosis well, indirect decompression, avoids posterior muscle/facet disruption | Vascular injury risk (great vessels), typically combined with posterior percutaneous fixation, not suitable when direct central/lateral recess decompression is required |
| LLIF/XLIF (lateral lumbar interbody fusion) | Transpsoas lateral, retroperitoneal | Large cage, strong indirect decompression and deformity correction capacity, muscle-sparing posteriorly | Lumbar plexus/femoral nerve injury risk (higher at L4-5), limited access at L5-S1 due to iliac crest, thigh numbness/weakness (transient in most) |

Practical selection: TLIF is the default workhorse when direct decompression and fusion are both needed at one or two levels; ALIF/OLIF/LLIF are favored when indirect decompression suffices and greater segmental lordosis/disc height restoration is prioritized (e.g., adjacent segment disease revision, higher-grade slips needing more correction), often as part of a combined anterior-posterior construct.

**Isthmic spondylolisthesis**
- Low-grade (I–II), symptomatic and refractory to conservative care: decompression (if radiculopathy from foraminal stenosis at the pars defect/L5-S1) plus instrumented posterolateral or interbody (TLIF/ALIF) fusion; direct pars repair (Buck's, Scott wiring, pedicle screw-hook constructs) is an option in young patients with an isolated symptomatic pars defect, minimal disc degeneration, and no appreciable slip, preserving motion.
- High-grade (III–V): instrumented fusion, typically L4/L5 to S1 (with iliac fixation considered for long constructs or poor sacral bone quality); the reduction-versus-in-situ-fusion debate persists — partial reduction to improve sagittal balance and fusion surface area is favored by many, but aggressive full reduction carries a recognized risk of L5 nerve root traction injury and should be undertaken with intraoperative neuromonitoring.

**Degenerative disc disease (isolated axial pain, no stenosis/instability)**
- The weakest evidence base for surgery of any diagnosis in this cluster. Isolated single- or two-level fusion for "discogenic" back pain without radiculopathy, stenosis, or instability has produced inconsistent results in randomized comparisons against structured non-operative/rehabilitation programs; several trials (e.g., Swedish Lumbar Spine Study, SPORT-adjacent degenerative disc cohorts) show fusion outcomes only modestly better than intensive non-operative rehabilitation, with considerably higher cost and complication burden.
- Provocative discography to "confirm" a symptomatic level is controversial — it has a meaningful false-positive rate, may itself accelerate disc degeneration at the injected level, and its use as a stand-alone gatekeeper for fusion is not supported by current guideline bodies.
- Practical stance: reserve fusion for isolated DDD only after exhaustive, well-documented conservative management (including a structured exercise/CBT-based program) has failed, and counsel patients explicitly about the modest and inconsistent evidence base compared to other indications in this cluster (e.g., degenerative spondylolisthesis or stenosis).

**Facet arthropathy (isolated)**
- Rarely an isolated surgical target; addressed as part of decompression/fusion when facet hypertrophy contributes to stenosis, or as the presumed pain generator justifying fusion at a level with confirmed dynamic instability. Isolated facet-mediated axial pain without instability is managed with RFA, not fusion.

**Cauda equina syndrome**
- Emergency decompression (laminectomy/discectomy addressing the causative pathology) as soon as safely and logistically possible after diagnosis is confirmed on emergent MRI; do not delay for a fixed time window once CES is confirmed — **earlier decompression is associated with better recovery of bladder, bowel, and sexual function**, even though the historical "24-hour rule" for motor/sensory recovery specifically has weaker supporting evidence in modern systematic reviews. Full, meticulous informed consent documentation regarding residual bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction risk is essential given the recognized high medicolegal profile of this diagnosis.

### Special populations and shared decision-making
- **Elderly/frail patients:** age alone is not a contraindication to decompression (outcomes for stenosis decompression in octogenarians can be favorable), but comorbidity burden, bone quality, and functional/frailty status (e.g., modified frailty index) should drive construct choice (favor decompression alone or facet-sparing technique over fusion where reasonable) and realistic goal-setting around walking tolerance rather than pain elimination.
- **Osteoporosis:** screen with DEXA/FRAX in postmenopausal women and older men being considered for fusion; consider pre-operative anti-osteoporotic therapy (teriparatide has the best supporting evidence for improving fusion-construct bone purchase) when surgery can be safely delayed, and intra-operative cement augmentation or larger-footprint interbody devices when it cannot.
- **Smoking:** strongly associated with pseudarthrosis and wound complications; structured cessation counseling before elective fusion is a standard, evidence-based intervention and should be documented as part of informed consent and surgical planning.
- **Obesity:** associated with higher wound complication and reoperation rates; does not preclude minimally invasive/endoscopic technique selection, which may in fact reduce approach-related morbidity in this group.
- **Shared decision-making for degenerative spondylolisthesis:** given the genuinely mixed trial landscape (SLIP favoring fusion vs Försth/NORDSTEN-DS favoring decompression alone), this is a paradigm case for structured shared decision-making — present both options with their respective trial evidence, patient priorities (avoiding reoperation vs minimizing surgical morbidity/cost), and individualized instability findings, rather than defaulting to a single institutional practice pattern.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Microdiscectomy / endoscopic discectomy**
- **Weeks 0–2:** wound care, avoid prolonged sitting (>30 min) and bending/lifting >2–4 kg; walking encouraged from day 1; no formal brace typically required.
- **Weeks 2–6:** gradual increase in walking tolerance, initiate core-stabilization/lumbar stabilization exercises avoiding end-range flexion under load; driving typically permitted once off opioids and reaction time/comfort allow (~1–2 weeks); desk-based work return often possible by 2–4 weeks.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progressive strengthening, return to low-impact aerobic conditioning; manual/heavy labor return typically 6–12 weeks depending on job demands and surgeon assessment; return to non-contact sport generally 6–8 weeks, contact/axial-loading sport (weightlifting, contact sports) individualized, often 3 months.
- Endoscopic patients often track faster through early milestones (walking, sitting tolerance, return to sedentary work) given the smaller access corridor, but strengthening progression timelines converge with open microdiscectomy by 6–12 weeks.

**Decompression (laminectomy/laminotomy) without fusion**
- **Weeks 0–2:** mobilize day 0–1, no brace required routinely; avoid heavy lifting/twisting.
- **Weeks 2–6:** progressive walking program (particularly valuable given the claudication indication), core stabilization initiated.
- **Weeks 6–12:** strengthening progression, return to full activity/work by 6–12 weeks depending on occupation; claudication-related walking tolerance is typically reassessed as the primary functional outcome.

**Instrumented fusion (posterolateral/TLIF/PLIF/OLIF)**
- **Weeks 0–2:** early mobilization day 1, brace per surgeon preference (many surgeons use a soft lumbar corset primarily for proprioceptive feedback/pain control rather than mechanical necessity with rigid pedicle screw fixation); avoid BLT (bending, lifting >4–5 kg, twisting).
- **Weeks 2–6:** walking program advanced, gentle isometric core activation, no formal resistance training; radiographic check at 6 weeks.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progressive core/paraspinal strengthening once early fusion mass consolidation is presumed underway; sedentary return to work often 4–6 weeks, manual labor typically deferred to 3–4 months.
- **Months 3–6:** advance to functional strengthening and conditioning; return to high-demand manual labor or axial-loading sport generally not before **4–6 months**, guided by clinical exam and radiographic evidence of fusion progression (bridging bone, absence of screw lucency/instrumentation failure) — flexion-extension films or CT at 6–12 months if fusion status is uncertain, especially before clearing heavy labor/contact sport.
- Union rates: instrumented posterolateral/interbody fusion in degenerative lumbar disease reported in the **90–97%** range in modern series with pedicle screw instrumentation and interbody support; smoking, diabetes, osteoporosis, and multilevel constructs reduce union rates and should prompt more conservative RTW/RTS timelines and consideration of bone health optimization (vitamin D, address osteoporosis per FRAX/DEXA) pre-operatively.

**Return-to-work / return-to-sport general criteria (all procedures):** pain-free or well-controlled with non-opioid analgesia, no ongoing neurological deficit progression, adequate core/paraspinal endurance on functional testing, and (for fusion) radiographic evidence favoring fusion progression before clearance for high-axial-load occupations or sport.

**Physiotherapy content commonly prescribed across this cluster** (tailored by diagnosis and phase): directional-preference/McKenzie-based exercises for discogenic and herniation-related pain; flexion-biased and cycling-based conditioning for neurogenic claudication; segmental stabilization/motor control training (transversus abdominis, multifidus activation) for instability-prone or post-fusion patients; graded exposure and pain-education/CBT-informed coaching for patients with high fear-avoidance or central sensitization features; general aerobic conditioning (walking, cycling, aquatic therapy) throughout, scaled to the operative phase.

**Non-operative rehabilitation (all diagnoses managed conservatively)**
- Structured exercise programs (NICE-endorsed) typically run **6–8 weeks**, combining biomechanical (core/motor control), aerobic, and mind-body components; reassess function and pain at completion before considering escalation to injections or surgical referral.
- For neurogenic claudication, a walking-tolerance diary and a graded walking program (with poles/flexed posture as needed) is a useful objective marker of treatment response and surgical timing decision-making.
- For adolescents with a low-grade isthmic pars stress lesion managed in a brace, typical bracing duration is **8–12 weeks** with serial clinical reassessment and return to provocative sport only after a pain-free period and, where imaging was used to confirm healing (SPECT/CT or MRI), radiological evidence of lesion consolidation.

**Cauda equina syndrome — post-decompression rehabilitation**
- Bladder/bowel function recovery is often the slowest domain and may lag motor/sensory recovery by months; early urology involvement (post-void residual monitoring, intermittent self-catheterization teaching if needed) is standard.
- Physiotherapy follows the same general decompression timeline above, but return-to-work/sport timing is individualized entirely around neurological and sphincter recovery rather than a fixed schedule; a meaningful minority of patients have permanent bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction despite timely surgery, and this should be reflected in rehabilitation goal-setting and patient counseling from the outset.

## Complications

- **Dural tear/CSF leak:** ~2–3% open discectomy/decompression, historically reported lower in some endoscopic series though technique-dependent; primary repair or sealant, flat bed rest 24–48h if repaired, watch for pseudomeningocele/headache.
- **Same-level recurrent disc herniation:** ~5–15% lifetime risk after discectomy; higher with larger annular defects; revision discectomy is generally effective, though may carry a modestly higher complication profile than primary surgery.
- **Epidural hematoma:** rare but a leading cause of early post-operative neurological deterioration — low threshold for emergent MRI and re-exploration if new/progressive deficit in the immediate post-op period.
- **Nerve root injury:** direct retraction/traction injury, or traction injury during aggressive spondylolisthesis reduction (particularly L5 root in high-grade isthmic slip reduction) — mitigate with intraoperative neuromonitoring in higher-risk reductions.
- **Wrong-level surgery:** mitigated by intraoperative localization imaging (fluoroscopy/spot film) and a formal surgical time-out with level confirmation, particularly important in obese patients and transitional anatomy.
- **Surgical site infection:** ~1–3% for decompression, higher (up to ~5%) for instrumented fusion, particularly with diabetes, obesity, smoking, and prolonged operative time; use of intrawound vancomycin powder remains a debated but common adjunct.
- **Pseudarthrosis:** incidence inversely related to construct rigidity, number of levels, bone quality, and smoking status; smoking cessation counseling is a standard, evidence-supported pre-operative intervention for fusion candidates.
- **Adjacent segment degeneration/disease:** progressive degeneration at levels adjacent to a fusion, radiographically common (up to ~30% at long-term follow-up) though clinically symptomatic ASD requiring reoperation is less frequent (~2–3% per year cumulative); a key argument favoring fusion avoidance when decompression alone is a reasonable option.
- **Iatrogenic instability after decompression:** risk rises with facetectomy >50%, complete bilateral pars resection, or destabilization of an already borderline-unstable segment — an argument for facet/pars-preserving techniques (over-the-top, tubular) whenever feasible.
- **Instrumentation failure (screw loosening/pullout, rod fracture):** more frequent in osteoporotic bone, long constructs, and at the caudal-most level of long fusions; consider cement augmentation or expandable screws in osteoporotic patients, and pre-operative bone health optimization when time permits.
- **Vascular injury:** specific to anterior/lateral approaches (ALIF/OLIF/LLIF) — great vessel injury during anterior exposure, or segmental vessel/lumbar plexus injury during transpsoas access; mitigated by careful approach-surgeon co-operation, neuromonitoring (transpsoas), and thorough pre-operative vascular anatomy review on axial imaging.
- **Persistent/failed back surgery syndrome:** multifactorial — consider missed pathology (wrong level, missed fragment, foraminal stenosis not addressed), recurrent herniation, epidural fibrosis, adjacent segment disease, or a primarily non-structural (central sensitization/psychosocial) pain generator before offering further surgery; a structured, MRI/CT-correlated re-evaluation is mandatory before any revision decision.

**Typical post-operative imaging follow-up**

| Procedure | Routine imaging | Trigger for earlier/additional imaging |
|---|---|---|
| Discectomy (open/endoscopic) | None routinely if clinical course is favorable | New/worsening neurological deficit, suspected recurrent herniation or epidural hematoma |
| Decompression alone | Standing radiographs at 6–12 weeks if instability was a pre-operative concern | Progressive deformity, new mechanical back pain |
| Instrumented fusion | AP/lateral radiographs at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6–12 months; flexion-extension or CT if fusion status uncertain before high-demand RTW/RTS clearance | Hardware-related pain, suspected pseudarthrosis or instrumentation failure |

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- [Decompression alone or with fusion for degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis (NORDSTEN-DS): five-year follow-up RCT (BMJ, Aug 2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39111800/) — confirms at 5 years that decompression alone remains **non-inferior** to decompression+fusion for ODI outcomes in stable degenerative spondylolisthesis; the longest RCT follow-up to date and the strongest current argument against routine fusion in stable cases.
- [AO Spine Clinical Practice Recommendations for the Surgical Management of Degenerative Spondylolisthesis: When to Decompress Alone and When to Fuse (Global Spine Journal, 2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12158961/) — synthesizes SLIP, Försth, and NORDSTEN-DS trial data into individualized selection criteria (favor decompression alone absent instability markers; fuse for dynamic instability, revision surgery, or facet sagittalization) — the current best consensus framework for this decision.
- [Decompression alone versus decompression with instrumented fusion for lumbar degenerative spondylolisthesis: systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised trials (2023)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10359551/) — pooled RCT data show no consistent outcome advantage for added fusion, with higher morbidity in the fusion arm.
- [Surgeon Recommendation and Outcomes of Decompression With vs Without Fusion in Patients With Degenerative Spondylolisthesis (JAMA Network, Jan 2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39777439/) — following the treating surgeon's pre-randomization fusion-vs-decompression preference was **not** associated with better 2-year outcomes than the assigned (potentially discordant) treatment, further undercutting routine surgeon-preference-driven fusion.
- [Epidural Steroids for Cervical and Lumbar Radicular Pain and Spinal Stenosis — AAN Guidelines Subcommittee Systematic Review Summary (Neurology, approved through Dec 2024, published 2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39938000/) — establishes quantified NNTs for ESI in radiculopathy (≈4 for short-term pain, ≈6 for short-term disability, ≈9 for long-term disability) and clarifies weaker, disability-only benefit in stenosis; reaffirms no evidence ESI reduces subsequent surgery rates.
- [Epidural steroid injections in lumbar disc herniation: evidence synthesis from 72 RCTs, 7,701 patients (2024–2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11979942/) — large-scale synthesis reinforcing short-term efficacy with no significant difference across injectate/technique (interlaminar vs transforaminal vs caudal).
- [Comparison of outcomes in open and full endoscopic lumbar discectomies for treating lumbar radiculopathy — Australian cohort (Journal of Spine Surgery, Mar 2025)](https://jss.amegroups.org/article/view/6932/html) — endoscopic discectomy non-inferior to open, with shorter length of stay, supporting continued uptake of endoscopic technique outside its original East Asian/European strongholds.
- [Effectiveness of Radiofrequency Ablation in Managing Chronic Axial Spinal Pain of Facet Joint Origin — systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38512629/) — confirms Level II evidence for RFA improving pain/function in confirmed facet-mediated pain, while quality-of-life/global-impression outcomes remain less certain, reinforcing the need for rigorous diagnostic block confirmation before RFA.
- [Getting It Right First Time (GIRFT) National Cauda Equina Syndrome Pathway (2023) and subsequent systematic review of international CES guidelines (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40000448/) — codifies the UK 4-hour emergency MRI standard and documents ongoing international variability in the exact red-flag symptom set and surgical timing thresholds used across guidelines — useful benchmark even outside the UK for setting institutional CES pathways.
- [Low back pain and sciatica in over 16s: assessment and management, NICE NG59 (last updated 2025–2026)](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng59) — the reference guideline for non-specific LBP/sciatica management (exercise-first, no opioids, no routine imaging without red flags); note the 2026 update **withdrew** the psychological-therapy and combined physical-psychological programme recommendations pending evidence re-review — do not cite these as current NICE positions going forward.
- [Open Versus Minimally Invasive Spine Surgery in the Treatment of Single-Level Degenerative Lumbar Spondylolisthesis: AO Spine Global Cross-Sectional Study (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40211513/) — international practice-pattern survey highlighting substantial global variation in open-vs-MIS approach selection for degenerative spondylolisthesis, underscoring that technique choice remains surgeon/resource-driven rather than dictated by high-grade comparative evidence.
