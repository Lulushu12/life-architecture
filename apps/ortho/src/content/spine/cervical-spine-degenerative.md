## Quick Reference

**Core distinction driving everything downstream**: radiculopathy = nerve root compression → pain/dermatomal deficit, mostly self-limited, conservative-first. Myelopathy (DCM) = spinal cord compression → progressive, often irreversible if untreated, lower threshold to operate. A patient can have both (myeloradiculopathy) — treat as myelopathy.

### Diagnosis-specific treatment ladders

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cervical radiculopathy** (soft disc/foraminal stenosis) | Activity modification, NSAIDs/short course oral steroid, structured PT (isometrics → isotonic strengthening), patient education, 4–6 wk trial | Selective/transforaminal cervical ESI (image-guided), medial branch/facet blocks if axial component | Progressive motor deficit, failure of ≥6–12 wk conservative care with concordant imaging, intractable pain refractory to injections |
| **Cervical disc herniation** (acute, radicular) | Same as radiculopathy pathway; most resolve or improve within 4–8 wk | ESI for pain bridging while awaiting natural resolution | Progressive/severe motor deficit (≤ grade 3), myelopathic signs, failed 6–12 wk conservative care |
| **Cervical spondylosis (axial pain only, no cord/root compromise)** | PT, ergonomic/postural correction, NSAIDs, activity modification | Facet-mediated pain: medial branch block → RFA if diagnostic block positive | Surgery rarely indicated for axial pain alone; reserve for confirmed instability or concomitant radiculopathy/myelopathy |
| **Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM)** | Mild (mJOA 15–17): surgery OR structured rehabilitation with close surveillance — shared decision-making | N/A — this is not a "step up" disease; nonoperative failure = deterioration, not lack of response | Moderate (mJOA 12–14) and severe (mJOA <12): surgery recommended, not merely offered. Any progression on observation = proceed to surgery |
| **OPLL** | Same tiering as DCM by mJOA; asymptomatic OPLL with cord compression on imaging → counsel on deterioration risk, favor surgery if canal-occupying ratio high | — | Myelopathic signs, canal-occupying ratio ≥60%, K-line negative morphology, or progression |

### Red flags (any diagnosis → urgent MRI + specialist referral)

- New or progressive **gait disturbance**, hand clumsiness/fine motor loss, bladder/bowel dysfunction (myelopathy until proven otherwise)
- **Hoffmann sign, inverted radial reflex, clonus, hyperreflexia, wide-based gait** — signs of cord involvement, not just root
- Progressive motor weakness ≤ MRC 3/5 in a myotomal distribution
- Bilateral symptoms, especially with lower-extremity involvement
- Signs of instability (trauma superimposed on spondylosis, os odontoideum-type concerns) or infection (fever, night sweats, IVDU)

### Key numbers

| Parameter | Threshold/value | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Torg-Pavlov ratio** | <0.8 | Suggests developmental cervical canal stenosis (screening tool only — poor specificity, not diagnostic alone) |
| **Canal-occupying ratio (OPLL)** | ≥60% | Predicts worse surgical outcomes with laminoplasty; favors laminectomy + fusion or anterior decompression in appropriate candidates |
| **mJOA cutoffs** | 18 = normal; 15–17 mild; 12–14 moderate; <12 severe | Drives surgery vs. observation decision in DCM/OPLL |
| **Sagittal canal diameter (absolute)** | <13 mm relative stenosis; <10 mm often symptomatic | Correlates with myelopathy risk, but MRI cord signal/compression matters more than isolated bony measurement |
| **Cord compression ratio (CCR)** | <0.4 | Associated with higher myelopathy risk on MRI |
| **T2 cord signal change** | Present | Worse prognostic factor if focal/high-intensity with low-intensity T1 correlate; not an absolute contraindication to surgery |
| **Natural history of untreated moderate/severe DCM** | Progressive in ~20–60% within 3–6 yr (historic surgical-era cohorts vary) | Do not "watch" moderate/severe disease |
| **ESI NNT for short-term pain relief (radiculopathy, pooled cervical+lumbar data)** | NNT ≈ 4 | AAN 2025 systematic review — modest, time-limited benefit |
| **Conservative trial duration before surgical workup, radiculopathy** | 6–12 weeks (sooner if progressive deficit) | Most acute radiculopathy improves spontaneously |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The cervical spine's degenerative cascade begins at the **disc**: loss of hydration and height (Kirkaldy-Willis model) shifts load to the facet joints and uncovertebral joints (**joints of Luschka**, C3–C7), producing osteophyte formation that narrows both the central canal and the neural foramina. Foraminal narrowing is compounded anteriorly by uncovertebral hypertrophy and posteriorly by facet hypertrophy and ligamentum flavum infolding — the latter is not true hypertrophy but buckling secondary to loss of disc height and segmental settling.

**Radiculopathy** results from mechanical compression and/or chemical (inflammatory, via TNF-α, IL-6, phospholipase A2 from nucleus pulposus material) irritation of a nerve root, most commonly at the neural foramen. C6 and C7 roots are most frequently affected (C5-6 and C6-7 are the most mobile, highest-stress segments). Foraminal stenosis from uncovertebral/facet osteophyte is the dominant chronic mechanism; acute soft disc herniation (posterolateral, paracentral) is the dominant mechanism in younger patients.

**Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM)** is a mechanical + vascular injury to the spinal cord. Static compression (spondylotic bars, disc-osteophyte complexes, OPLL, congenital canal narrowing) is compounded by dynamic factors — repetitive flexion-extension microtrauma stretches/compresses the cord over osteophytic ridges, and segmental instability adds a shear component. Chronic compression causes cord ischemia (watershed zones especially at the anterior gray matter and lateral corticospinal tracts), blood-cord barrier disruption, neuronal apoptosis, and demyelination — mechanisms that explain why chronicity and severity correlate with irreversibility, and why the term "myelopathy" already implies established cord damage rather than a purely reversible functional state. DCM is now understood as a common, underdiagnosed cause of non-traumatic spinal cord injury and adult spinal cord dysfunction worldwide.

**Cervical disc herniation** follows the same disc degeneration cascade but with an acute or subacute annular tear allowing nucleus pulposus extrusion; posterolateral herniations compress the exiting/traversing root (radiculopathy), central herniations can compress the cord directly (acute or chronic myelopathy), particularly dangerous in a congenitally narrow canal.

**OPLL (ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament)** is a distinct pathologic process — heterotopic ossification of the PLL, strongly associated with East Asian ethnicity (prevalence ~2–4% in Japan/Korea vs. <1% in Western populations), genetic loci (COL6A1, and others), and metabolic factors (diabetes, obesity, calcium-phosphate metabolism abnormalities). Segmental, continuous, mixed, and localized morphologic types exist; continuous and mixed types carry higher canal-occupying ratios and worse prognosis. OPLL causes myelopathy through pure static (and eventually dynamic) anterior cord compression that is unyielding — unlike a soft disc, the ossified mass does not "shrink," which materially changes surgical decision-making (decompression must be adequate on the first attempt).

**Cervical spondylosis** is the umbrella radiographic term for degenerative disc, facet, and uncovertebral changes; it becomes clinically named radiculopathy or myelopathy only when neural structures are compromised. Axial neck pain from spondylosis alone (facet arthropathy, discogenic pain) is a separate clinical problem from the compressive syndromes and should not automatically be treated as a surgical disease. Radiographic spondylotic change is near-universal with age (present in the large majority of asymptomatic adults over 60), which is precisely why imaging findings must be interpreted in the context of a corroborating clinical syndrome rather than treated as diagnostic in isolation — an incidental disc-osteophyte complex on an MRI obtained for an unrelated indication is not, by itself, an indication for referral or intervention.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: distinguish radicular arm pain (dermatomal, often worse with neck extension/ipsilateral rotation — Spurling maneuver) from myelopathic complaints (hand clumsiness — dropping objects, difficulty with buttons/handwriting, gait unsteadiness, "stepping on cotton wool," urinary urgency/frequency). Ask specifically about falls and fine motor tasks — patients underreport gait change as "just getting older."

**Exam — radiculopathy**: dermatomal sensory change, myotomal weakness (C5: deltoid/biceps; C6: wrist extensors, brachioradialis reflex; C7: triceps, triceps reflex; C8: finger flexors; T1: intrinsics), diminished reflexes, positive Spurling test (high specificity ~90–95%, low sensitivity), relief with shoulder abduction (relief sign), reduced/negative upper limb tension test helps rule out.

**Exam — myelopathy**: hyperreflexia, **Hoffmann sign**, inverted radial reflex (brachioradialis reflex triggers finger flexion — indicates pathology at C5-6), clonus, **Babinski sign**, wide-based/spastic gait, **Lhermitte sign** (electric shock with neck flexion), impaired tandem gait, hand grip-and-release test (>20 in 10 s is normal; myelopathic patients slow and struggle to fully open the hand — "myelopathy hand" with intrinsic wasting/finger escape sign).

**Imaging**:
- **MRI cervical spine** is the study of choice for all three syndromes — assesses disc, canal diameter, cord signal (T2 hyperintensity = edema/gliosis; T1 hypointensity paired with T2 hyperintensity = worse prognostic marker for poorer surgical recovery), foraminal compromise, and OPLL (though CT is better for ossification morphology).
- **CT cervical spine** essential for OPLL characterization (continuous/segmental/mixed/localized), canal-occupying ratio calculation, and bony foraminal anatomy for surgical planning; also useful pre-op to assess facet/uncovertebral osteophyte for anterior vs posterior planning.
- **Flexion-extension radiographs**: assess dynamic instability, measure the **K-line** in neutral/flexion/extension for OPLL surgical planning, evaluate cervical lordosis (kyphotic alignment worsens laminoplasty candidacy).
- **EMG/NCS**: adjunct for radiculopathy when diagnosis is unclear or to differentiate from peripheral entrapment (e.g., carpal tunnel mimicking C6 radiculopathy); not routinely needed when MRI and exam concur.
- **Somatosensory/motor evoked potentials**: selectively used intraoperatively for high-risk decompressions (severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery) rather than for diagnosis.
- **Timing of EMG/NCS in radiculopathy**: denervation changes (fibrillations, positive sharp waves) take approximately 2–3 weeks to appear after acute nerve root injury — an EMG performed too early can be falsely reassuring; if used, time it to at least 3 weeks from symptom onset unless the study is being used purely to exclude a peripheral entrapment mimic (which does not depend on this latency).

Imaging findings must always be correlated with the clinical picture — degenerative changes and even cord signal change are common incidental findings; **asymptomatic cord compression** without clinical myelopathy (sometimes termed "non-myelopathic cord compression," NMCC) is managed differently (see Classification/Treatment below) — surveillance rather than automatic surgery, though patients should be counseled about deterioration risk, particularly with trauma.

**Sagittal alignment parameters** (measured on standing lateral or flexion-extension radiographs, essential for operative planning): **C2–C7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA)** — normal <40 mm; increasing SVA correlates with worse HRQOL and is a key driver toward fusion/realignment rather than motion-preserving surgery. **C2–C7 lordosis (Cobb)** — loss of lordosis or frank kyphosis excludes laminoplasty and favors anterior or posterior fusion with correction. **T1 slope** — high T1 slope with insufficient compensatory lordosis predicts postoperative sagittal malalignment and worse outcomes, particularly after multilevel posterior surgery.

### Differential diagnosis

Myelopathic and radicular presentations overlap with several non-compressive conditions; misattributing these to cervical spondylosis delays correct treatment and risks unnecessary surgery.

| Mimic | Distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| **Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)** | Combination of upper AND lower motor neuron signs across multiple myotomes/regions not explained by a single compressive level; fasciculations, bulbar signs, normal/discordant imaging; EMG shows widespread denervation beyond a single root/cord level |
| **Multiple sclerosis** | Younger patient, optic neuritis history, other CNS white-matter lesions on brain MRI, relapsing-remitting course, cord lesion often does not correlate with a compressive level |
| **Vitamin B12 deficiency (subacute combined degeneration)** | Dorsal column-predominant (proprioception/vibration loss), often with peripheral neuropathy and macrocytic anemia; low B12, elevated methylmalonic acid |
| **Peripheral entrapment neuropathy** (carpal tunnel, cubital tunnel) | Distribution respects peripheral nerve territory, not myotome/dermatome; positive Tinel/Phalen, confirmatory EMG/NCS; can coexist with radiculopathy ("double crush") |
| **Brachial plexopathy / thoracic outlet syndrome** | Multi-root/trunk distribution, provocative maneuvers (Roos, Adson) positive, normal cervical imaging at symptomatic levels |
| **Syringomyelia** | Cape-like suspended sensory loss, dissociated sensory loss (pain/temperature affected, proprioception spared), associated Chiari malformation; diagnosis on MRI |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** | Gait apraxia (magnetic gait) plus cognitive decline and urinary incontinence triad, ventriculomegaly on brain imaging, no cervical cord compression |
| **Rotator cuff pathology / shoulder pathology** | Pain reproduced with shoulder-isolated maneuvers (Neer, Hawkins), preserved reflexes/dermatomal sensation, normal Spurling test |

## Classification

### mJOA (modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association) severity score

The primary clinical severity and treatment-stratification tool for DCM/OPLL. Score out of 18, combining upper extremity motor function, lower extremity motor function, sensation, and sphincter function.

| Domain | Points | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Upper extremity motor | 0–5 | 0 = unable to feed self; 5 = normal |
| Lower extremity motor | 0–7 | 0 = unable to walk; 7 = normal |
| Sensation (UE) | 0–3 | 0 = complete loss; 3 = normal |
| Sphincter function | 0–3 | 0 = urinary retention; 3 = normal |

| mJOA total | Severity | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **15–17** | Mild | Either surgery or a structured, supervised trial of rehabilitation with close follow-up is acceptable (shared decision-making); progress to surgery if deterioration or non-response |
| **12–14** | Moderate | Surgery recommended as standard of care |
| **<12** | Severe | Surgery recommended, generally urgently; higher risk of incomplete recovery, but decompression still indicated to halt progression and salvage function |
| **18** | No myelopathy (normal) | Not a surgical indication by itself even if imaging shows compression — see NMCC discussion above |

mJOA also anchors **recovery ratio** = (postop mJOA − preop mJOA) / (18 − preop mJOA) × 100%, the standard outcome metric in DCM surgical literature.

### Nurick grade

Older, gait-centric myelopathy grading — simpler, less granular than mJOA, but still widely reported for historical comparison and quick bedside communication.

| Grade | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Signs/symptoms of root involvement only, no cord disease | Radiculopathy pathway |
| **1** | Signs of cord involvement, normal gait | Early myelopathy — surgical candidate per DCM pathway if confirmed |
| **2** | Slight gait difficulty, does not interfere with employment | Still functional — surgery reduces progression risk |
| **3** | Gait difficulty prevents full-time employment / activities of daily living, but not requiring assistance to walk | Moderate — clear surgical indication |
| **4** | Able to walk only with assistance (frame, cane, or another person) | Severe — surgical indication, expect incomplete recovery |
| **5** | Chair-bound or bedridden | End-stage — surgery may halt progression; functional recovery limited |

Nurick correlates roughly with mJOA lower-extremity subscore but does not capture hand function or sphincter involvement — always report both when available.

### Torg-Pavlov ratio

Radiographic screening measure of developmental (congenital) cervical canal stenosis, calculated on a lateral cervical radiograph as the ratio of sagittal canal diameter to sagittal vertebral body diameter at the same level.

| Ratio | Interpretation | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **≥1.0** | Normal canal | No increased baseline myelopathy risk from canal geometry |
| **0.8–1.0** | Borderline | Watch; correlate with MRI cross-sectional canal/cord measurements |
| **<0.8** | Developmental stenosis | Historically used (especially in athletes, "cervical cord neurapraxia"/stinger literature) as a screening flag for reduced functional reserve; poor specificity — high false-positive rate in muscular athletes due to large vertebral body size, so **never used in isolation** to determine return-to-play or surgical candidacy; always confirm with MRI canal/cord measurements (space available for cord, cord compression ratio) |

Clinical use today is largely historical/adjunctive; MRI-based canal and cord measurements have superseded it for individual surgical decision-making, but it remains a quick screening tool and is still cited in sports-medicine return-to-play algorithms after cervical cord neurapraxia.

### K-line (OPLL)

Described by Fujiyoshi et al., the K-line is drawn on a lateral cervical radiograph or midsagittal CT/MRI connecting the midpoints of the spinal canal at C2 and C7. It determines whether posterior decompression alone (laminoplasty) can achieve adequate indirect decompression of an anteriorly-based OPLL mass.

| K-line status | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **K-line positive** | The OPLL mass does not cross (stays posterior to/does not exceed) the K-line | Laminoplasty (posterior decompression relying on cord drift-back) is a viable option — cord can adequately migrate away from the ossified mass after canal expansion |
| **K-line negative** | The OPLL mass crosses anterior to the K-line (bulk of ossification too large/too far posterior) | Laminoplasty alone likely inadequate — favor anterior decompression (if feasible) or laminectomy with instrumented fusion ± anterior decompression; posterior-only surgery in K-line-negative patients has historically shown inferior neurological recovery |

Important nuance from recent literature (2023–2025): K-line status alone has shown inconsistent predictive power in some multicenter cohorts once combined with **canal-occupying ratio (COR)** — patients with K-line negative but low COR may still do reasonably with laminoplasty, while K-line negative + high COR (≥60%) does poorly with posterior-only surgery. **Dynamic K-line** (assessed in flexion, not just neutral) further refines this — a K-line that becomes negative in flexion identifies patients who appear favorable in neutral imaging but are not. Practical takeaway: use K-line as one input alongside COR, cervical alignment (lordosis vs. kyphosis), and OPLL morphologic type, not as a stand-alone algorithm.

### OPLL morphologic type (adjunct classification)

Not one of the four headline classifications above, but essential companion information whenever K-line and canal-occupying ratio are being interpreted for OPLL surgical planning.

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Segmental** | Ossification confined behind individual vertebral bodies, disc spaces spared | Generally lower canal-occupying ratio; more amenable to either anterior or posterior surgery; segmental instability at disc-level gaps should be assessed |
| **Continuous** | Ossification extends uninterrupted across multiple vertebral bodies and disc spaces | Higher canal-occupying ratio, higher rate of dural ossification; posterior indirect decompression favored if K-line positive to avoid anterior dural adhesion risk |
| **Mixed** | Combination of segmental and continuous morphology | Behaves like continuous type at the involved continuous segments — highest complexity for surgical planning, often the group requiring combined anterior-posterior strategies |
| **Localized (circumscribed)** | Focal ossified mass, often at a single disc level, may bulge posteriorly independent of vertebral body margins | Can behave more like a large calcified disc herniation; anterior direct resection more feasible if limited to 1–2 levels |

Continuous and mixed types carry the highest canal-occupying ratios and the highest rates of dural ossification (reported in a meaningful minority of these subtypes), which is the principal reason many surgeons default to posterior indirect decompression whenever K-line status allows it, reserving anterior direct resection for K-line-negative or focal/localized disease.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Cervical radiculopathy / acute disc herniation with radiculopathy**:
- First-line for the large majority of patients — most acute radiculopathy from soft disc herniation improves substantially within 4–8 weeks regardless of intervention.
- **Structured physical therapy**: initially pain-modulating and cervical retraction/traction-based, progressing to deep neck flexor and scapular stabilizer strengthening; cervical traction has mixed evidence but is reasonable in foraminal-stenosis-predominant cases.
- **Short course NSAIDs**; a short oral corticosteroid taper is reasonable for severe acute pain, though evidence quality is modest.
- Activity modification (avoid prolonged extension/rotation positions that narrow the symptomatic foramen), ergonomic correction, patient education on expected natural history.
- **Duration of conservative trial**: 6–12 weeks is the conventional threshold before considering surgery in the absence of progressive/severe deficit — this aligns with NASS evidence-based guideline positioning of conservative care as first-line.

**Cervical spondylosis with axial pain only**: PT (postural correction, isometric strengthening), NSAIDs, activity modification; no role for early surgery. Facet-mediated pain confirmed by diagnostic medial branch block responds to radiofrequency ablation (see Injections below) rather than decompressive/fusion surgery.

**Mild DCM (mJOA 15–17) and asymptomatic/non-myelopathic cord compression**: This is the area of greatest controversy and the area where 2023–2025 evidence has actively reshaped practice.
- AO Spine/CSRS 2017 and the 2019 WFNS recommendations both allow **either surgery or a supervised, structured trial of rehabilitation with close interval follow-up** for mild DCM — this is not a "do nothing" pathway; it requires scheduled reassessment (clinical + often repeat mJOA) and low threshold to convert to surgery with any deterioration or lack of improvement.
- For patients with **radiographic cord compression without clinical myelopathy** (NMCC), current guidance favors surveillance over prophylactic surgery in the absence of myelopathic signs, but patients should be counseled explicitly about the risk of deterioration (particularly after even minor trauma/hyperextension injury) and the shared decision should be documented.
- Nonoperative management is **never appropriate** for moderate or severe DCM (mJOA <15) — evidence consistently favors surgical decompression to halt progression, and delay worsens achievable recovery.

**Surveillance protocol for mild DCM / NMCC managed nonoperatively**: clinical review with formal mJOA and/or Nurick reassessment at approximately 3–6 months, then every 6–12 months if stable; patient education to self-report acute change (new falls, hand clumsiness, bladder symptoms) rather than waiting for the next scheduled visit; explicit counseling to avoid high-risk activities for cervical hyperextension/impact (some clinicians restrict contact sport and advise caution with chiropractic cervical manipulation) given the risk of acute decompensation after even minor trauma; repeat MRI is not mandated on a fixed schedule but should be obtained promptly with any clinical deterioration.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Transforaminal/interlaminar cervical epidural steroid injection (ESI)**: reasonable second-line option for radiculopathy pain not controlled by initial conservative measures, used as a bridge to natural resolution or to avoid/delay surgery. The 2023 AAN systematic review (published 2025) pooling cervical+lumbar radiculopathy data found ESIs **probably reduce short-term pain** (NNT ≈ 4) and disability (NNT ≈ 6), with **insufficient evidence for long-term pain benefit**; cervical-specific data remain sparser than lumbar. Cervical transforaminal ESI carries a small but serious risk profile (vertebral/radicular artery injury, spinal cord infarction) — use image guidance (fluoroscopy or ultrasound), non-particulate steroid preferred by many practitioners for transforaminal approach, and interlaminar approach is an alternative with a different (lower vascular, higher dural puncture) risk profile.
- **Selective nerve root block**: diagnostic (confirms symptomatic level when imaging is equivocal/multi-level) and therapeutic.
- **Medial branch block → radiofrequency ablation**: for facet-mediated axial spondylotic pain confirmed by diagnostic block (typically requiring ≥50–80% relief on at least one, ideally two, controlled blocks) before proceeding to RFA.
- No meaningful injection-based adjunct exists for DCM/OPLL — these are surgical decompressive diseases once symptomatic at moderate/severe grade; injections have no role in altering the compressive/cord-injury pathophysiology.

### Operative

**General principles**: The anterior vs. posterior approach decision is driven by (1) number and location of compressive levels, (2) sagittal alignment (lordosis vs. kyphosis), (3) predominant compression location (anterior disc-osteophyte/OPLL vs. posterior ligamentum flavum/facet), (4) patient factors (smoking status affecting fusion, prior anterior neck surgery/radiation affecting soft tissue, swallowing risk), and (5) OPLL-specific factors (K-line, canal-occupying ratio).

**Radiculopathy — surgical options** (after failed conservative care or progressive deficit):
- **ACDF (anterior cervical discectomy and fusion)**: workhorse for 1–2 level radiculopathy, especially with concomitant kyphosis or central/paracentral pathology requiring direct anterior decompression. Excellent, durable results for radicular symptom relief (>90% good/excellent outcomes in large series). Technical pearls: complete uncovertebral decompression for foraminal stenosis, generous foraminotomy under microscope/loupes when uncal spurs contribute, avoid over-distraction (risk of adjacent segment strain), meticulous endplate preparation for fusion.
- **Cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA/artificial disc replacement)**: appropriate alternative for 1–2 level radiculopathy in younger patients with preserved disc height, minimal facet arthropathy, no significant kyphosis, and no instability — preserves motion and is designed to reduce adjacent segment degeneration risk. **10-year comparative data** (2023–2024 meta-analyses of pooled RCT/cohort data): CDA shows statistically superior rates of overall clinical success, lower subsequent surgery (reoperation/revision) rates, and fewer adverse events than ACDF at 10 years; NDI and VAS differences favor CDA but do not consistently reach minimal clinically important difference thresholds — i.e., the durability/reoperation advantage is more clinically meaningful than the pain-score advantage. Contraindications to CDA: significant facet arthropathy, kyphotic segmental alignment, ossification/bridging osteophyte, prior fusion at the level, osteoporosis, active infection.
- **Posterior foraminotomy (Frykholm/keyhole)**: excellent option for pure foraminal soft disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte causing unilateral single-level radiculopathy without central stenosis or kyphosis — avoids anterior approach morbidity (dysphagia, RLN injury) and preserves motion without an implant. Best suited to posterolateral, not central, pathology.

### Approach selection at a glance

| Procedure | Best-fit scenario | Avoid when |
|---|---|---|
| **ACDF** | 1–2 level radiculopathy or myelopathy, anterior-predominant compression, kyphosis needing correction | ≥4 levels (rising nonunion/dysphagia risk without corpectomy strategy), severe osteoporosis without augmentation plan |
| **Cervical disc arthroplasty** | 1–2 level radiculopathy/soft disc herniation, young patient, preserved disc height and facets, neutral/lordotic alignment | Facet arthropathy, kyphosis, prior fusion at level, OPLL/bridging ossification, instability, osteoporosis |
| **Posterior foraminotomy** | Single-level unilateral foraminal soft disc or osteophyte, no central stenosis | Central/bilateral pathology, myelopathy, significant kyphosis |
| **Laminoplasty** | Multilevel (≥3 level) myelopathy/OPLL, preserved or lordotic alignment, lower canal-occupying ratio, K-line positive | Kyphotic alignment, significant instability, high canal-occupying ratio with K-line negative |
| **Laminectomy + instrumented fusion** | Multilevel myelopathy/OPLL with kyphosis, instability, or K-line-negative/high-COR OPLL requiring lordosis restoration | Preserved lordosis with low complication tolerance (laminoplasty typically preferred instead given lower morbidity) |
| **Anterior corpectomy ± strut/cage** | Focal high-grade anterior compression (localized OPLL, large disc-osteophyte complex) over 1–2 vertebral levels requiring direct decompression | Long-segment (≥3 corpectomy levels — very high graft/plate failure rate); consider hybrid ACDF-corpectomy instead |

**Degenerative cervical myelopathy / OPLL — surgical decision framework**:

- **Anterior approaches** (ACDF, corpectomy ± strut/cage reconstruction, or hybrid constructs): favored for 1–3 level disease, especially with predominant anterior (disc-osteophyte or OPLL) compression, kyphotic or straight alignment (posterior decompression relies on cord drift-back toward a lordotic curve — it fails in kyphosis), and when direct decompression of the compressive pathology is desired. Corpectomy provides wider decompression per level but carries higher graft/plate-related complication rates over multilevel constructs; hybrid ACDF-corpectomy constructs balance decompression adequacy against construct stability.
- **Posterior approaches**:
  - **Laminoplasty** (open-door or French-door/double-door): motion-preserving canal expansion, ideal for multilevel (≥3 level) disease with preserved or lordotic alignment and predominantly posterior or multisegmental compression. Avoids fusion-related morbidity and adjacent segment disease. Requires adequate lordosis for cord drift-back to work; contraindicated in kyphotic alignment. C5 palsy (typically transient, deltoid/biceps weakness) is a well-recognized complication (~5–10%), thought related to nerve root tethering during cord drift-back/decompression at C4-5.
  - **Laminectomy with instrumented posterior fusion**: preferred over laminoplasty when there is pre-existing kyphosis (fusion allows correction/stabilization not achievable with laminoplasty), significant instability, or where laminoplasty has failed/is contraindicated. Better maintenance of/ability to correct cervical lordosis than laminoplasty, but higher blood loss, longer OR time, higher C5 palsy rate in some series, and loss of motion with attendant adjacent segment risk.
- **Comparative evidence (2024–2025 meta-analyses)**: laminoplasty shows shorter operative time, less blood loss, and lower overall complication/C5 palsy rates than laminectomy-fusion; laminectomy-fusion shows better lordosis maintenance/correction and, specifically in OPLL, better control of OPLL progression (ossification tends to continue growing under a mobile laminoplasty construct more than under a fused segment). Net practical synthesis: **laminoplasty for multilevel disease with preserved lordosis and lower canal-occupying ratio; laminectomy-fusion for kyphotic alignment, instability, or high canal-occupying ratio/K-line-negative OPLL.**
- **OPLL-specific technical pearls**: never attempt to fully resect/"float" a densely adherent OPLL mass without assessing dural ossification risk (dural ossification is present in a meaningful minority of continuous/mixed-type OPLL and carries high CSF leak risk with anterior floating techniques) — many surgeons favor posterior indirect decompression (when K-line positive) precisely to avoid this anterior dural ossification hazard. For K-line-negative, high-COR OPLL where posterior indirect decompression is predicted to be inadequate, combined anterior-posterior surgery or anterior corpectomy with direct decompression (in centers with that expertise) is considered, accepting higher CSF leak/complication risk for more complete decompression.
- **Timing**: moderate/severe DCM and symptomatic OPLL — decompress without prolonged delay; outcomes correlate with preoperative duration/severity of symptoms. Do not "trial" conservative care in moderate/severe disease.

**Perioperative considerations common to all cervical decompression/fusion surgery**:
- **Preoperative swallow/voice risk stratification**: consider baseline ENT/laryngoscopy assessment in revision anterior surgery, prior contralateral approach, or pre-existing dysphonia, given the cumulative risk to the recurrent laryngeal nerve with repeat anterior exposure.
- **Intraoperative neuromonitoring (SSEP/MEP, and free-running or triggered EMG for nerve root-at-risk cases)**: selectively used for severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery, or any case with a deformity-correction component; a significant signal change should prompt an immediate wake-up test or intraoperative checklist (blood pressure augmentation, re-check hardware/positioning, consider halting decompression) before proceeding.
- **Positioning**: avoid excessive neck extension in patients with severe stenosis or myelopathy during intubation and prone/supine positioning — awake fiberoptic intubation is reasonable in severe myelopathy with unstable or high-grade stenosis to avoid a positioning-related cord injury.
- **VTE prophylaxis**: mechanical prophylaxis (sequential compression devices) intraoperatively/postoperatively is standard; chemoprophylaxis timing is individualized given epidural hematoma risk, generally initiated once hemostasis is confirmed satisfactory, per institutional protocol.
- **Mean arterial pressure goals**: in acute/severe myelopathic presentations some centers extrapolate MAP augmentation (>85 mmHg) practice from acute traumatic spinal cord injury literature for the immediate perioperative period, though the evidence base specific to degenerative myelopathy is weaker than for trauma.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Post-ACDF / anterior cervical fusion (1–3 level)**:
- **Weeks 0–2**: soft cervical collar for comfort (not universally required — surgeon preference; many use collar only for comfort/proprioceptive reminder, not rigid immobilization for single/two-level constructs with plate). Avoid heavy lifting (>2–4 kg), driving until off narcotics and collar comfortable, no BLT (bending/lifting/twisting). Swallowing precautions/soft diet if dysphagia present (common, usually transient, 1–4 weeks, occasionally longer).
- **Weeks 2–6**: gentle active ROM as tolerated, gradual collar weaning, begin isometric neck exercises. Wound check, assess for delayed hematoma/dysphagia progression.
- **Weeks 6–12**: formal PT initiated once early fusion stability presumed — isometric progressing to isotonic strengthening, scapular stabilization, postural retraining. Radiographic flexion-extension views around 8–12 weeks in select cases to assess early fusion progress.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive strengthening, return to most activities; confirm fusion (static + dynamic radiographs, occasionally CT if fusion status uncertain and symptomatic) before clearing for high-risk activities.
- **Return to sedentary work**: typically 1–2 weeks. **Return to manual labor**: typically 6–12 weeks, guided by radiographic fusion progress and symptom resolution. **Return to contact/high-risk sport**: individualized, generally not before solid radiographic fusion (often ≥4–6 months), and controversial/relatively contraindicated in some contact sports even after solid fusion given adjacent segment biomechanics — case-by-case with imaging confirmation and often flexion-extension stress views.
- **Fusion rates**: single-level ACDF with plate ~95–97%+; rates decline with increasing levels (multilevel ACDF fusion rates meaningfully lower without plate/allograft optimization) and with smoking (smoking cessation should be mandated pre- and post-operatively — nonunion risk is substantially elevated in smokers).

**Post-cervical disc arthroplasty**:
- Faster early mobilization than fusion — no fusion to protect. Soft collar optional/brief (days), early ROM encouraged (this is part of the point of the procedure — preserve segmental motion).
- Return to sedentary work often within 1–2 weeks, manual labor 4–8 weeks, guided by symptom resolution rather than a fusion-healing timeline.
- PT focus: early active ROM, cervical/scapular strengthening, avoid prolonged immobilization that could promote heterotopic ossification.

**Post-laminoplasty / laminectomy-fusion (DCM/OPLL)**:
- **Weeks 0–2**: collar for comfort/support (soft, not rigid, in most laminoplasty; more consistent use in multilevel laminectomy-fusion constructs), inpatient mobilization with PT/OT from day 1 given baseline myelopathic gait/balance impairment — fall risk is elevated in this population and early mobility with assistance is a priority, not an afterthought.
- **Weeks 2–6**: gait training progression (myelopathic gait recovery lags neurological recovery), gentle ROM, monitor for delayed C5 palsy (can present days to ~2 weeks post-op).
- **Weeks 6–12**: formal outpatient PT — proprioceptive/balance training is disproportionately important in this population (spastic/ataxic gait improvement is often the slowest-recovering domain), strengthening, hand dexterity/fine motor rehabilitation (occupational therapy referral valuable for hand-intensive deficits).
- **Months 3–12+**: neurological recovery in DCM/OPLL is gradual and can continue for 12–24 months post-decompression; set expectations accordingly — mJOA recovery ratio is typically assessed at 1 year but continued gains are documented beyond that in a subset.
- **Return to work**: sedentary 2–6 weeks; manual labor individualized and often delayed 3+ months given fusion/bone healing (laminectomy-fusion) or until neurological/functional recovery plateau (laminoplasty). Return to contact sport is generally discouraged after laminectomy/laminoplasty for myelopathy given the underlying cord vulnerability, regardless of construct healing.

### Return-to-work / return-to-sport summary

| Procedure | Sedentary work | Manual labor | Contact/collision sport |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACDF (1–2 level) | 1–2 weeks | 6–12 weeks | Individualized, not before solid fusion (≥4–6 months); many advise against return to high-risk contact sport even after fusion |
| Cervical disc arthroplasty | 1–2 weeks | 4–8 weeks | Individualized; motion preservation does not remove risk to adjacent unprotected levels — case-by-case |
| Laminoplasty | 2–4 weeks | 8–12+ weeks, individualized | Generally discouraged given underlying cord vulnerability |
| Laminectomy + fusion | 2–6 weeks | 12+ weeks, pending fusion | Generally discouraged; construct + cord vulnerability both argue against |
| Posterior foraminotomy | 1–2 weeks | 4–6 weeks | Individualized; no fusion to protect, lowest-morbidity option among the operative choices |

## Complications

**Anterior approach (ACDF/corpectomy/CDA) — general**:
- **Dysphagia**: most common early complication, 20–60% short-term incidence depending on definition, usually self-limited by 6–12 weeks; multilevel constructs and prolonged retraction increase risk.
- **Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy/hoarseness**: usually transient (traction neurapraxia); permanent injury rare (<1%) but under-recognized without formal voice assessment.
- **Adjacent segment disease**: cumulative incidence after ACDF reported roughly 2–3%/year, driving long-term reoperation rates; a principal rationale for CDA in appropriate candidates, supported by the 10-year comparative reoperation data above.
- **Pseudarthrosis/nonunion**: increases with levels fused and smoking; may require revision (anterior or posterior supplemental fixation).
- **Hardware failure/graft subsidence**: more relevant in multilevel/corpectomy constructs.
- **Epidural hematoma, CSF leak, vertebral artery injury, esophageal injury**: rare but serious; esophageal perforation especially feared with prominent anterior hardware or revision surgery.
- **C5 palsy**: also occurs after anterior corpectomy, not exclusively posterior surgery, though more classically described posteriorly.

**Posterior approach (laminoplasty/laminectomy-fusion)**:
- **C5 palsy**: ~5–10% (range reported 4–12% across series), typically deltoid/biceps weakness, usually improves over weeks to months but can be permanent in a minority; mechanism debated (nerve root tethering during cord drift-back, direct foraminal nerve root injury during decompression).
- **Axial neck pain**: more prominent after laminoplasty than anterior surgery in some series, related to posterior muscle/ligament disruption; muscle-sparing laminoplasty techniques aim to reduce this.
- **Loss of cervical lordosis / progressive kyphosis**: risk after laminectomy without fusion (hence current preference for laminectomy + fusion over laminectomy alone); laminoplasty preserves alignment better if baseline lordosis is adequate.
- **Wound infection, epidural hematoma**: standard posterior spine surgery risks; hematoma is particularly concerning in this population given baseline cord compromise — low threshold for urgent imaging with any post-op neurological decline.
- **OPLL-specific**: dural ossification and associated CSF leak risk with attempted direct anterior resection; continued OPLL progression under laminoplasty (mobile, non-fused segment) vs. better growth control under fusion.

**Both approaches**:
- **Incomplete neurological recovery**: expected in advanced/long-duration myelopathy — surgery's primary goal in severe/chronic disease is often to halt progression rather than guarantee full recovery; set expectations pre-operatively.
- **Postoperative neurological deterioration**: rare but devastating; higher risk in severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery — rationale for selective intraoperative neuromonitoring in high-risk cases.

**Cervical disc arthroplasty-specific**:
- **Heterotopic ossification (HO)**: reported in a meaningful minority of levels at long-term follow-up (rates vary substantially by implant design and follow-up duration across series), can progressively restrict segmental motion and, if severe, functionally converts the arthroplasty into an ankylosed segment; higher-grade HO more common with longer follow-up.
- **Implant subsidence/migration**: uncommon but a recognized reason for revision; meticulous endplate preparation and correct implant sizing/positioning are the primary technical safeguards.
- **Adjacent segment disease**: occurs at a lower rate than after ACDF per the pooled long-term comparative data above, but is not eliminated entirely — motion preservation reduces, rather than removes, adjacent segment risk.

| Complication | Approximate incidence | Approach |

## Recent Evidence

- **2025 — AO Spine Clinical Practice Recommendations update (evidence-based decision-making review)**: A 2025 review of cutting-edge literature reaffirms the 2017 AO Spine/CSRS DCM guideline framework while incorporating newer data, and continues to note that the 2017 recommendations remain the most authoritative comprehensive guideline despite not having been formally re-issued — practice updates are being layered on via focused recommendation papers rather than a full guideline rewrite. [AO Spine Clinical Practice Recommendations for DCM (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40257837/)
- **2025 — WFNS Spine Committee recommendations on posterior surgical techniques for cervical spondylotic myelopathy**: Consensus-based recommendations specifically addressing laminoplasty vs. laminectomy-fusion selection criteria, refining when each posterior technique should be chosen based on alignment and pathology distribution — directly informs the operative decision framework in this monograph. [WFNS Spine Committee Recommendations — Posterior Surgical Techniques for CSM](https://www.e-neurospine.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.14245%2Fns.1938274.137)
- **2025 (reporting 2024 data) — 10-year CDA vs. ACDF meta-analysis**: Systematic review with meta-analysis of pooled RCT/cohort 10-year outcomes confirms CDA's durability advantage (lower reoperation, fewer adverse events, superior overall clinical success) persists at a full decade of follow-up, while pain/disability score differences remain statistically but not always clinically significant — the strongest long-term comparative evidence yet for arthroplasty in appropriately selected 1–2 level degenerative disc disease/radiculopathy patients. [Ten-Year Outcomes of Cervical Disc Arthroplasty Versus ACDF: Systematic Review With Meta-Analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38018778/)
- **2025 — Prospective RCT 10-year data from 3 US sites (IJSS)**: Independent confirmation from a dedicated prospective randomized trial cohort of CDA vs. ACDF at 10 years, reinforcing the meta-analytic findings above with trial-level (not just pooled observational) data. [Cervical Disc Arthroplasty vs ACDF at 10 Years: Prospective RCT, 3 Sites](https://www.ijssurgery.com/content/17/2/230)
- **2025 — Long-term single-center CDA cohort, minimum 10-year follow-up (J Neurosurg Spine)**: Real-world single-center data extending arthroplasty durability evidence beyond trial settings, useful for counseling patients on long-term expectations outside idealized RCT populations. [Long-term results of cervical disc arthroplasty: single-center ≥10-year follow-up](https://thejns.org/spine/view/journals/j-neurosurg-spine/43/2/article-p195.xml)
- **2024 — Laminoplasty vs. laminectomy-fusion meta-analysis (22 studies, 2,128 patients)**: Confirms laminoplasty's advantages in operative time, blood loss, and lower overall complication/C5 palsy rates, while laminectomy-fusion better maintains lordosis — the evidentiary basis for the alignment-driven selection algorithm in this monograph's Operative section. [Laminoplasty Versus Laminectomy and Posterior Fusion for Cervical Myelopathy: Meta-Analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38764362/)
- **2025 — Dynamic/flexion K-line and canal-occupying-ratio refinement of OPLL surgical selection**: Multiple 2024–2025 multicenter studies show static neutral K-line alone has inconsistent predictive power; combining K-line with canal-occupying ratio and dynamic (flexion) K-line status substantially improves prediction of which OPLL patients will do poorly with posterior-only decompression — changes practice from a binary K-line rule to a multifactorial risk assessment. [Dynamic K-Line Status and Surgical Outcomes in Multilevel Cervical OPLL: Multicenter Study](https://doi.org/10.3390/jcm15020520); [Flexion K-Line Status Predicts Surgical Strategy in Multilevel OPLL](https://www.e-neurospine.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.14245%2Fns.2551108.554); [K-Line Conversion Predicts Outcomes: Laminectomy-Fusion vs. Laminoplasty in K-line-negative OPLL](https://www.thespinejournalonline.com/article/S1529-9430(25)00244-X/abstract)
- **2025 — AAN Guidelines Subcommittee systematic review on epidural steroids for cervical/lumbar radicular pain and stenosis**: Formal evidence grading confirms ESIs "probably" reduce short-term pain (NNT ≈ 4) and disability (NNT ≈ 6) in radiculopathy, with insufficient evidence for long-term pain benefit and a persisting evidence gap specific to cervical (as opposed to lumbar) radiculopathy — reinforces using ESI as a time-limited bridge, not a long-term solution, and flags that much of the injection evidence base is extrapolated from lumbar data. [Epidural Steroids for Cervical and Lumbar Radicular Pain and Spinal Stenosis — AAN Systematic Review Summary](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000213361)
- **2023 (Spinal Cord) — Modeled UK prevalence of DCM shows major underdiagnosis**: Population modeling estimates DCM prevalence around 2% of UK adults (rising to ~4% in those ≥79), with most cases undiagnosed, particularly in the elderly — reinforces the case for a lower threshold to suspect and image for DCM in older patients presenting with vague gait/hand-function complaints. [Most degenerative cervical myelopathy remains undiagnosed, particularly amongst the elderly](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36056204/)
- **2024 (JMIR Formative Research) — UK national GP survey on DCM awareness**: Cross-sectional survey (responses collected Sept 2022–Jul 2023) documents low primary-care awareness of DCM as a diagnosis, contributing to the well-documented ~2-year average diagnostic delay — relevant for any surgeon involved in referral-pathway or primary-care education efforts. [Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy Awareness in Primary Care: UK National GP Survey](https://formative.jmir.org/2024/1/e58802)
- **2025 (Lancet Neurology) — Editorial call to raise DCM awareness**: A high-profile 2025 editorial explicitly frames DCM as an underrecognized, common, treatable cause of progressive disability and non-traumatic spinal cord injury, adding weight to ongoing RECODE-DCM and AO Spine advocacy efforts to standardize research outcomes and public/professional awareness. [Raising awareness of degenerative cervical myelopathy — The Lancet Neurology](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(25)00074-2/fulltext)
- **2023 (Nature/Spinal Cord) — International survey on management of mild DCM and asymptomatic cord compression**: Documents ongoing global practice variation and uncertainty in managing mild DCM and non-myelopathic cord compression, underscoring that the mild-DCM "surgery vs. structured rehabilitation" recommendation remains a genuine equipoise area in real-world practice, not just guideline text. [Management of mild DCM and asymptomatic spinal cord compression: an international survey](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41393-023-00945-8)
- **2025 (single-center service evaluation) — Real-world outcomes after implementing AO Spine International DCM guidelines**: Service evaluation demonstrates measurable practice change and outcome tracking when a center formally adopts the AO Spine DCM guideline pathway, supporting guideline-concordant care as a quality-improvement target rather than a purely theoretical recommendation. [Outcomes for DCM Following Implementation of the AO Spine International Guidelines: Single-Centre Service Evaluation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11561916/)
