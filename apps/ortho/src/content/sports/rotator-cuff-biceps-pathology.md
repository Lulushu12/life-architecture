## Quick Reference

**Red flags** — do not treat as routine subacromial pain syndrome:
- Acute traumatic tear in a patient <65 y with weakness (pseudoparalysis, positive drop-arm/lag signs) → early MRI, consider repair within 2–6 weeks (retraction and fatty infiltration progress).
- Sudden **anterior axillary "pop"** during bench press/eccentric load with ecchymosis and a palpable defect → **pectoralis major rupture**, surgical window best <3–4 weeks (acute repair outcomes clearly superior to chronic/delayed).
- Fever, night pain out of proportion, rapid destructive change → rule out septic arthritis / neoplasm before attributing to cuff disease.
- Acute anterior shoulder dislocation in patient >40 y with persistent weakness → suspect concomitant cuff tear (up to 30–40% incidence), not just axillary nerve palsy.

**Key numbers**
- Critical shoulder angle (CSA) >35° → associated with higher cuff-tear risk and higher retear risk after repair; CSA <30° associated with glenohumeral OA risk.
- Acromiohumeral distance (AHD) <7 mm on plain radiograph → suggests full-thickness tear; <5–6 mm often signals irreparability.
- Tangent sign positive (supraspinatus muscle belly below the line drawn from coracoid tip to superior scapular spine) → predicts poor repairability/high retear risk, correlates with Goutallier ≥3.
- Barbotage/needling for calcific deposits: best results for Gärtner type I–II (dense, well-circumscribed); type III (fluffy/dissolving) often resorbs spontaneously and may not need intervention.
- Massive cuff tear defined as >5 cm in any dimension or ≥2 full-thickness tendon tears (Cofield classification).
- Nonoperative trial for atraumatic degenerative cuff tears: typically 3–6 months of structured physiotherapy before considering surgery in low-demand/older patients; shorter (4–6 weeks) trial acceptable before proceeding to surgery in active/younger patients with high-grade partial or full tears.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The rotator cuff comprises **supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, and subscapularis**, forming a musculotendinous cuff that centers the humeral head in the glenoid and generates the force couple balancing the deltoid. The supraspinatus footprint averages ~25 mm mediolateral × 12–16 mm anteroposterior; most degenerative tears begin in the **"critical zone"** ~1–2 cm proximal to the insertion, a relatively hypovascular watershed area (Codman).

**Pathophysiology of degenerative tears** is multifactorial: intrinsic tendon degeneration (age-related collagen disorganization, hypovascularity), extrinsic mechanical factors (subacromial outlet morphology, acromial shape — Bigliani types I–III flat/curved/hooked, though causality with impingement is debated), and biomechanical overload. The **critical shoulder angle (CSA)**, measured on true AP radiographs as the angle between a line from the inferior glenoid to the lateral acromion edge and a line from the inferior to superior glenoid, correlates tear risk (CSA >35°) versus osteoarthritis risk (CSA <30°) — a useful conceptual anchor even though its independent predictive value is debated.

**Subacromial pain syndrome (SAPS)** — the preferred umbrella term replacing "impingement syndrome" — encompasses subacromial bursitis, cuff tendinopathy, and partial-thickness tears without invoking a single mechanical cause; it reflects the shift away from the Neer mechanical-impingement model toward a multifactorial pain model, given that arthroscopic decompression has not outperformed sham surgery (see Recent Evidence).

**Calcific tendinopathy** is an active, cell-mediated process (fibrocartilaginous metaplasia → calcium hydroxyapatite deposition → resorptive phase with vascular ingrowth and macrophage/osteoclast-like activity) occurring in tendon substance, most commonly supraspinatus, in patients typically 30–60 years old; distinct from dystrophic calcification of degenerate cuff tissue. The resorptive phase is when pain is most acute (hyperemic, "hot" deposit) but also when spontaneous resolution is most likely.

**Massive irreparable cuff tears** progress through a well-described natural history: tendon retraction (Patte stage), muscle atrophy, and **fatty infiltration** (Goutallier), which is largely irreversible once advanced (stage ≥3) and is the single strongest predictor against successful repair and re-tear risk.

**SLAP lesions** (superior labrum anterior-to-posterior) involve the superior labrum and biceps anchor; pathomechanics include peel-back (in throwers, with the arm in abduction-external rotation causing torsional force on the posterosuperior labrum) and traction/compression from falls on an outstretched arm. Internal impingement and glenohumeral internal rotation deficit (GIRD) are commonly co-existing in overhead athletes.

**Long head of biceps (LHB) tendon** pathology (tendinopathy, subluxation/instability, partial tearing) frequently coexists with subscapularis tears (the biceps pulley is formed by the coracohumeral ligament, superior glenohumeral ligament, and subscapularis fibers) and with SLAP lesions, given the shared biceps anchor. LHB is a well-established pain generator independent of cuff status.

**Pectoralis major** rupture typically occurs at the **musculotendinous junction or tendinous insertion onto the humerus** (the sternal head inserts distal/posterior to the clavicular head, and is under greatest tension in the eccentric-loaded, extended/abducted/externally-rotated position — classically the eccentric phase of a bench press). Anabolic steroid use is a recognized risk factor for tendon quality compromise. Tietjen classification distinguishes location (I sternocostal origin/muscle belly, II musculotendinous junction, III tendon avulsion from humerus — most common and most amenable to anatomic repair) and completeness (partial vs complete), which together with chronicity drive the acute-repair-versus-reconstruction decision.

**Subscapularis tears** deserve separate mention within the cuff-tear spectrum: they are frequently missed on standard exam (isolated belly-press/lift-off testing required) and on imaging unless the axial sequences are specifically scrutinized; they are commonly associated with biceps pulley lesions and medial biceps subluxation/dislocation. The **Lafosse classification** (types 1–5 by tear extent from partial upper-third to complete tear with lesser tuberosity involvement) guides whether an isolated upper-border tear (type 1–2, often amenable to arthroscopic repair with concomitant biceps tenodesis) or a complete tear with retraction (type 4–5, may need interval slides or is occasionally irreparable) is present.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (atraumatic/degenerative vs acute traumatic), dominant arm, occupation/sport and demand level, night pain, weakness vs pain-limited weakness, prior injections/surgery, smoking (impairs tendon healing and increases retear risk), diabetes.

**Exam battery**:
- **Cuff-specific**: Jobe/empty-can (supraspinatus), external rotation lag sign and drop-sign (infraspinatus/teres minor — high specificity for large/massive posterosuperior tears), belly-press and lift-off (subscapularis), Hornblower's sign (teres minor, predicts poor outcome after isolated repair without transfer).
- **Impingement provocative tests**: Neer and Hawkins-Kennedy — sensitive but nonspecific; positive predictive value for SAPS alone is limited, use as part of a cluster.
- **Biceps/SLAP**: Speed's test, O'Brien's active compression test, biceps load II, dynamic labral shear — individually modest accuracy; cluster testing improves diagnostic performance. No single test is definitive for SLAP; MR arthrography remains the diagnostic anchor.
- **Instability/pseudoparalysis**: active vs passive forward elevation discrepancy — inability to actively elevate above ~90° despite full passive ROM with pain controlled suggests either massive tear with loss of force couple or nerve injury (check axillary/suprascapular nerve).
- **Pectoralis major**: palpable defect, ecchymosis tracking to the arm, loss of the anterior axillary fold contour, weakness on resisted adduction/internal rotation; compare bilaterally with arms crossed (chest-press position accentuates defect).

**Imaging**:
- **Plain radiographs** (true AP/Grashey, axillary, outlet/scapular-Y): AHD, CSA, glenohumeral OA, calcific deposits (best initial modality for calcific tendinopathy — characterizes Gärtner type), proximal humeral migration (chronic massive tear).
- **Ultrasound**: dynamic, cheap, operator-dependent; excellent for full-thickness tears and effusion/bursitis; useful for image-guided injection and barbotage.
- **MRI**: gold standard for tear size, retraction (Patte), fatty infiltration (Goutallier), muscle atrophy (occupation ratio/tangent sign), and SLAP/labral pathology.
- **MR arthrography**: preferred for suspected labral/SLAP pathology and partial-thickness articular-sided tears in young athletes — improves sensitivity/specificity over conventional MRI.
- **CT**: useful for glenoid/humeral bone stock planning before arthroplasty or when MRI contraindicated.

### Diagnosis-specific evaluation notes

**Rotator cuff tear**: correlate exam findings with imaging before committing to a size/reparability plan — clinical strength does not always match structural tear size, especially in chronic compensated tears. Document baseline active/passive ROM in all planes and a validated outcome score (ASES, Constant, OSS) for longitudinal tracking.

**Subacromial pain syndrome**: this is a diagnosis of exclusion within the differential of shoulder pain — actively rule out cervical radiculopathy (Spurling's), adhesive capsulitis (globally restricted passive ROM), glenohumeral OA, and acromioclavicular joint pathology (cross-body adduction test, direct AC tenderness) before labeling pain as purely subacromial.

**Calcific tendinopathy**: correlate radiographic Gärtner type with symptom acuity — a "hot," acutely painful shoulder with a fluffy (type III) deposit on X-ray is often in active resorption and may resolve within days to weeks; a chronic, dull ache with a dense type I deposit is less likely to self-resolve.

**Massive irreparable cuff tear**: assess for **pseudoparalysis** (active elevation <90° with full passive ROM, in the absence of nerve palsy or pain-limited effort under intra-articular local anesthetic) versus **pseudoparesis** (weak but present active elevation) — true pseudoparalysis with a positive Hornblower's sign and advanced fatty infiltration steers strongly toward rTSA over joint-preserving reconstruction, especially in patients >65–70 years.

**SLAP tear**: assess concomitant GIRD (measure internal rotation at 90° abduction bilaterally; >20° side-to-side deficit is significant) and scapular dyskinesis (scapular assistance/retraction tests) — untreated kinetic-chain deficits predict recurrence/failure of isolated labral treatment in throwers.

**Biceps tendinopathy**: bicipital groove tenderness with a positive Speed's test and normal cuff strength suggests isolated LHB pathology; always examine the subscapularis specifically, since pulley lesions/subscapularis tears are a common co-driver of biceps instability that will not resolve with tenodesis alone if unaddressed.

**Pectoralis major rupture**: document time from injury explicitly at first visit — this single data point (</> 3–4 weeks) is the primary determinant of repair technique and is often the detail most likely to be missed if the patient delays presentation.

### Key differential diagnoses to actively exclude

| Presentation | Consider instead of primary cuff/biceps pathology |
|---|---|
| Diffuse shoulder pain with globally restricted passive ROM | Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder) |
| Pain radiating below the elbow, neck pain, positive Spurling's | Cervical radiculopathy |
| Point tenderness over AC joint, pain with cross-body adduction | Acromioclavicular joint arthropathy/separation |
| Weakness with normal strength on isolated testing, sensory changes | Suprascapular or axillary neuropathy (e.g., paralabral cyst, quadrilateral space syndrome) |
| Young athlete, instability sensation, apprehension | Glenohumeral instability (Bankart, multidirectional instability) with secondary cuff/labral injury |
| Older patient, stiffness plus crepitus, joint-line pain | Glenohumeral osteoarthritis or cuff tear arthropathy |
| Acute atraumatic severe pain, fever, systemic symptoms | Septic arthritis or crystal arthropathy (calcific tendinopathy can mimic this in its acute resorptive phase) |

## Classification

### Cofield classification — tear size (full-thickness cuff tears)

| Grade | Size | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Small | <1 cm | Often amenable to single-row repair or margin convalescence; good healing potential |
| Medium | 1–3 cm | Single- or double-row repair per surgeon preference; good prognosis |
| Large | 3–5 cm | Double-row/transosseous-equivalent preferred (biomechanically superior); assess retraction/mobility |
| Massive | >5 cm or ≥2 tendons involved | Evaluate reparability (Patte, Goutallier, tangent sign) before committing to repair vs salvage procedure |

### Goutallier classification (modified by Fuchs) — fatty infiltration on sagittal oblique MRI/CT

| Stage | Finding | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Normal muscle, no fat | Excellent healing potential |
| 1 | Some fatty streaks | Good healing potential; proceed with repair |
| 2 | Fat < muscle (less fat than muscle) | Repair reasonable but retear risk rises; consider augmentation |
| 3 | Fat = muscle (equal amounts) | Threshold for irreparability in many algorithms; repair often still attempted in supraspinatus if infraspinatus better preserved, but retear risk high |
| 4 | Fat > muscle | Considered irreparable/non-functional muscle; favor salvage (SCR, tendon transfer, partial repair, rTSA if arthritic) over primary repair |

Goutallier ≥3 in 2 or more cuff muscles is a strong predictor of poor outcome after attempted repair and a key criterion steering toward massive-tear salvage strategies.

### Patte classification — tendon retraction (intraoperative or MRI, coronal oblique)

| Stage | Retraction | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tendon stump near its anatomic footprint | Direct repair straightforward |
| 2 | Retracted to the level of the humeral head | Repair feasible, may need mobilization (interval slides, releases) |
| 3 | Retracted to the glenoid rim | Poor tendon mobility/quality; repair difficult, higher retear risk; consider partial repair, margin convalescence, or salvage if combined with high Goutallier stage |

### Snyder classification — SLAP lesions

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Degenerative fraying of superior labrum, biceps anchor intact | Debridement only; no repair needed |
| II | Superior labrum + biceps anchor detached from glenoid (most common; subtypes IIA anterior, IIB posterior, IIC combined) | Repair (suture anchors) in young/high-demand with peel-back mechanism confirmed; consider tenodesis if >35–40 y or revision |
| III | Bucket-handle tear of labrum, biceps anchor intact | Excise the bucket-handle fragment; anchor stable so repair of anchor not needed |
| IV | Bucket-handle tear extending into the biceps tendon | Repair or tenodesis depending on extent of biceps tendon involvement and patient age/demand |

Extended classification (V–X, Maffet/Morgan) describes SLAP combined with Bankart, anterior-inferior, or anterior-posterior labral extension — relevant when instability coexists; management follows the combined-pathology repair principle (address both labral zones).

**Epidemiology note relevant to counseling**: asymptomatic full-thickness cuff tears are common and increase steeply with age — population imaging studies report prevalence rising from roughly 5–10% in the fifth decade to >50% by the eighth decade. This underlies the rationale for treating the patient's symptoms and functional demand, not the MRI in isolation, and for a genuinely permissive nonoperative pathway in asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic incidental tears found on imaging for unrelated reasons.

### Ellman classification — partial-thickness cuff tears (by depth and location)

| Grade | Depth | Location subtype | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | <25% tendon thickness (low grade) | Articular (A) / Bursal (B) / Intratendinous (C) | Debridement ± PT trial; rarely needs repair |
| II | 25–50% thickness (moderate) | A / B / C | PT trial first; if failing and symptomatic, debridement or repair depending on tear behavior and fiber quality |
| III | >50% thickness (high grade) | A / B / C | Generally treated as functional full-thickness — take-down and repair (in-situ repair or completion + repair), especially bursal-sided (worse prognosis than articular-sided at equivalent depth) |

Bursal-sided partial tears tend to progress faster and have worse natural history than articular-sided tears of similar grade — lower threshold for surgical treatment.

### Gärtner classification — calcific tendinopathy (radiographic deposit morphology)

| Type | Appearance | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Dense, well-circumscribed, sharply demarcated ("formative"/resting phase) | Often chronic, less likely to resorb spontaneously; barbotage/needling more technically feasible and effective (firm deposit yields more calcium on aspiration) |
| II | Dense with a partially defined/fluffy contour (mixed) | Intermediate behavior; barbotage still generally effective |
| III | Fluffy, cloud-like, poorly defined ("resorptive" phase) | Deposit often actively resorbing — high spontaneous resolution rate; needling technically harder (soft, toothpaste-like) but symptoms often self-limit within weeks; favor watchful waiting/NSAIDs over intervention unless pain is severe |

Some series subdivide further (Molé classification integrates deposit type with tear presence), but Gärtner type is the most widely used shorthand and directly informs whether to counsel watchful waiting (type III) versus offer barbotage (type I/II).

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Subacromial pain syndrome / partial tears / low-demand degenerative full tears**: structured physiotherapy is first-line — scapular stabilizer strengthening, rotator cuff strengthening (eccentric-biased), posterior capsule stretching, activity modification. A supervised program of at least 6–12 weeks is reasonable before reassessing; degenerative asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic full-thickness tears in older/low-demand patients can be managed nonoperatively indefinitely with periodic reassessment, since many remain stable or only slowly enlarge and function can remain acceptable. **AAOS 2025 CPG** frames early mobilization vs delayed mobilization (up to 8 weeks) after repair as producing similar outcomes for small-medium tears, and explicitly does not support routine acromioplasty as a therapeutic adjunct beyond repair itself for small-medium tears.

**Calcific tendinopathy**: NSAIDs, activity modification, and PT are reasonable first steps, particularly for Gärtner III deposits given high spontaneous resorption rates. Radial or focused extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT) has good evidence for pain and function improvement and deposit fragmentation, particularly high-energy focused ESWT.

**SLAP tears**: PT addressing scapular dyskinesis, posterior capsule tightness/GIRD, and kinetic-chain deficits is first-line for the majority of patients, including many overhead athletes, before considering surgery — a meaningful proportion improve without surgery, and surgical SLAP repair has a nontrivial reoperation/failure rate in throwers.

**Pectoralis major rupture**: reserved for low-demand, sedentary, or partial-thickness (intramuscular) injuries where residual strength deficit is functionally acceptable; young athletic patients and anyone seeking return to heavy pressing/contact sport should be counseled toward surgical repair given the consistently demonstrated strength/endurance deficits (bench press, adduction, internal rotation strength) with nonoperative management.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Subacromial corticosteroid injection**: effective for short-term (weeks) pain relief in SAPS; limit total number (commonly ≤3 per year, spaced ≥3 months) given concerns about tendon quality/healing if surgery is subsequently needed — avoid injecting shortly (<3 months, ideally longer) before planned cuff repair.
- **US-guided barbotage/needling (percutaneous needle aspiration/lavage) for calcific tendinopathy**: single or two-needle technique under US guidance to fragment and aspirate the deposit, typically combined with corticosteroid instillation. Meta-analyses (2023–2024) confirm barbotage produces meaningful short-term pain reduction (notably by ~2 months) comparable to or better than ESWT in the short-term, but effect size attenuates over longer follow-up, and a substantial minority of patients require repeat intervention (further injection, repeat barbotage, or surgery). Best candidate: Gärtner I–II, well-circumscribed deposit, failed initial NSAID/PT trial.
- **ESWT**: alternative/adjunct to barbotage, particularly where image-guided needling access is difficult or patient preference favors a non-invasive option; network meta-analyses place high-energy focused ESWT and barbotage as generally the most effective nonoperative modalities, both outperforming sham and low-energy ESWT.
- **LHB tendon sheath injection**: useful diagnostically and therapeutically for isolated biceps tendinopathy without significant structural tear.
- **PRP/biologic augmentation** for cuff repair: an area of active investigation and the focus of much of the AAOS 2025 CPG's emphasis on "optimizing the healing environment"; evidence remains mixed/heterogeneous and it is not yet a standard-of-care recommendation — discuss as an adjunct, not a substitute for sound mechanical repair.

### Operative

**Rotator cuff repair — general indications**: full-thickness tears in symptomatic patients who fail an adequate nonoperative trial (or who present with acute traumatic tears with significant weakness — earlier surgery favored, ideally within ~6 weeks before retraction/atrophy progress), progressive tears on imaging, and most tears in young/high-demand patients regardless of size once symptomatic.

**Operative vs conservative decision matrix by tear type and age** (general framework, individualize to demand/comorbidity):

| Scenario | <50 y | 50–65 y | >65–70 y |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute traumatic full-thickness tear, weakness | Early repair (within ~2–6 wk) | Early repair unless significant comorbidity | Repair if active/good tissue quality; nonoperative if low-demand/frail |
| Chronic degenerative partial-thickness (Ellman I–II) | PT trial first; repair if failing and high-demand | PT trial first | PT trial, favor nonoperative unless high grade/progressive |
| Chronic degenerative full-thickness, small-medium | PT trial (4–6 wk), low threshold to repair if active | PT trial (6–12 wk), repair if symptomatic/failing | PT trial; repair only if failing conservative care and good functional demand |
| Chronic degenerative full-thickness, large-massive, reparable | Repair favored (progression risk high) | Repair favored if tissue quality permits | Individualize — PT trial reasonable; repair if active and healthy tissue |
| Massive irreparable, no arthritis, no pseudoparalysis | Joint-preserving salvage (SCR/LTT) | Joint-preserving salvage or partial repair | Partial repair/debridement; rTSA if pseudoparalysis develops |
| Massive irreparable + pseudoparalysis or arthropathy | rTSA (rare at this age — confirm irreparability first) | rTSA if arthropathy/pseudoparalysis present | rTSA is workhorse solution |

**Technique selection**:
- **Single-row repair**: simpler, faster, lower cost, adequate for small-medium tears (<3 cm) where meta-analyses show no significant functional or retear-rate difference versus double-row.
- **Double-row / transosseous-equivalent (suture-bridge) repair**: restores footprint contact area and pressure more anatomically; meta-analyses (2023–2024) consistently show **lower retear rates and better functional scores for large/massive tears (≥3 cm)** compared with single-row, with no clear benefit for small tears. Knotted vs knotless double-row constructs show no significant difference in retear rate — construct choice can follow surgeon preference/cost.
- **Margin convalescence / partial repair**: for tears not fully reducible to footprint — repair what is mobile, accept a smaller residual defect; improves function even without complete footprint restoration.
- **Concomitant acromioplasty**: AAOS 2025 CPG does not support routine subacromial decompression/acromioplasty as adding therapeutic benefit over repair alone for small-medium tears — reserve for cases with a clearly hooked/impinging acromion morphology or bursal pathology requiring debridement for visualization, not as a routine adjunct.
- **Biceps tenodesis or tenotomy** is commonly performed concomitantly when LHB pathology (fraying, subluxation, partial tear, or SLAP-associated instability) is present at the time of cuff repair.

**Massive irreparable cuff tear — options when repair is not feasible (Goutallier ≥3, Patte 3, tangent sign positive) but glenohumeral joint is preserved and pseudoparalysis is absent or "correctable":**

| Option | Best candidate | Key evidence/pearls |
|---|---|---|
| Partial repair / debridement ± biceps tenotomy | Older, lower-demand, pain-predominant without pseudoparalysis | Reliable pain relief; modest functional gains; lowest morbidity |
| **Subacromial balloon spacer (InSpace)** | Was proposed for lower-demand patients as a minimally invasive option | **START:REACTS RCT (Lancet 2022; 2-year follow-up 2024/2025)**: debridement alone outperformed debridement + balloon spacer at 12 and 24 months on OSS/WORC; authors explicitly do **not recommend** the balloon spacer — role now very limited, should not be offered as routine therapy outside of very selected cases/trials |
| **Superior capsular reconstruction (SCR)** | Younger, active patients with preserved teres minor/infraspinatus, positive external rotation lag less severe | Restores superior glenohumeral stability (fascia lata autograft or dermal allograft); technically demanding; graft failure rates reported up to 20–40% depending on graft type/series |
| **Lower trapezius tendon transfer (LTT)** (± latissimus dorsi transfer for combined posterosuperior loss) | Younger active patients with posterosuperior massive tears and preserved anterior cuff (subscapularis) | Recent comparative studies (2024–2025) suggest **LTT restores deltoid function and dynamic shoulder stability more effectively than SCR** in this population, with LTT providing a dynamic external rotation force couple rather than static superior restraint; growing preference for LTT in centers with expertise, especially when external rotation weakness (Hornblower sign) is prominent |
| **Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA)** | Cuff tear arthropathy, pseudoparalysis, older patients (typically >65–70 y), failed prior massive-tear surgery | Most reliable option for restoring active elevation when pseudoparalysis is present or glenohumeral arthritis coexists; trades some internal/external rotation and has finite implant longevity — use age/demand-appropriate counseling |

**Subacromial decompression (isolated, i.e., without cuff repair) for SAPS**: evidence base has substantially eroded its role. **CSAW trial** (Lancet 2017, with longer follow-up data since) found no clinically important difference between arthroscopic subacromial decompression, diagnostic arthroscopy alone, and no treatment for subacromial shoulder pain. **FIMPACT trial** (5- and 10-year follow-up) corroborates: no between-group difference exceeding the minimal important difference between decompression and diagnostic arthroscopy at long-term follow-up. Practical implication: isolated arthroscopic subacromial decompression should not be routinely offered as a stand-alone procedure for SAPS; reserve arthroscopy for cases with a clear structural lesion (cuff tear, refractory calcific deposit) rather than "impingement" alone.

**Calcific tendinopathy — arthroscopic excision**: reserved for deposits refractory to at least one round of barbotage/ESWT with persistent significant symptoms; intraoperative fluoroscopy or needle localization helps identify the deposit; complete excision is not always necessary/possible and is not clearly superior to partial removal — combine with cuff repair if a concomitant tear is debrided down to healthy tissue.

**SLAP repair vs biceps tenodesis vs debridement**:
- Type I: debridement only.
- Type II (most common): repair with suture anchors at the biceps anchor/superior labrum in patients <35–40 y, especially overhead/throwing athletes with a clear peel-back mechanism and failed structured PT. However, recent systematic reviews (2023–2025) show **biceps tenodesis achieves comparable or better return-to-play and reoperation rates than SLAP repair**, including in overhead athletes — tenodesis is increasingly favored, particularly in patients >35–40 y, revision settings, or when concomitant biceps pathology exists, given lower reoperation rates and avoidance of the stiffness/overtightening risk seen with repair. Baseball pitchers specifically have worse return-to-sport rates than other overhead athletes regardless of which procedure is chosen — counsel accordingly.
- Type III/IV: excise unstable bucket-handle fragment; repair or tenodese the biceps portion based on extent of tendon involvement.

**Biceps tenodesis vs tenotomy (isolated LHB pathology or concomitant with cuff repair)**:
- **Tenotomy**: simpler, faster, no fixation-related complications, but ~30–50% risk of a visible "Popeye" deformity and a small but real risk of cramping/fatigue discomfort; favored in older, lower-demand, sedentary patients where cosmesis and strength are lower priorities.
- **Tenodesis** (open subpectoral, or arthroscopic suprapectoral): preserves length-tension relationship and forearm supination/elbow flexion strength, avoids Popeye deformity; favored in younger, active, or cosmetically concerned patients. Subpectoral location theoretically reduces residual groove pain versus suprapectoral fixation. Fixation options include interference screw, suture anchor, or cortical button — no strong evidence one fixation method is clearly superior; construct choice driven by surgeon experience and bone quality.

**Pectoralis major rupture repair**:
- **Acute (<3–4 weeks)**: direct tendon-to-bone repair (suture anchors or transosseous tunnels, cortical button techniques increasingly popular for fixation strength) — outcomes are reliably good, with the majority of patients regaining near-normal strength and returning to sport/military duty.
- **Chronic/retracted (>4–6 weeks) or large tendon gap**: often requires allograft (e.g., Achilles tendon) reconstruction to bridge the defect since primary tendon-to-tendon apposition is not achievable; outcomes are still favorable but generally somewhat inferior to acute primary repair, and complication rates (particularly re-rupture) are higher.
- Time-to-repair is the single most consistent predictor of outcome — early referral for suspected pectoralis major rupture is a key practical takeaway to relay to referring physicians/trainers.

**Technical pearls and pitfalls**

| Procedure | Pearl | Pitfall to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Double-row/suture-bridge cuff repair | Tension the medial row first, lay lateral row to restore native footprint pressure without over-medializing the musculotendinous junction | Over-tensioning causes "gap-strangulation" of vascularity at repair site — worsens healing, not just a size problem |
| Large/massive tear mobilization | Release the rotator interval and perform an anterior or posterior interval slide before abandoning repair as "irreparable" | Excessive subscapularis or capsular release can destabilize the joint — release should be purposeful, not reflexive |
| SCR | Fascia lata autograft (harvested thicker, ~6–8 mm) has shown more durable outcomes than thin dermal allograft in several comparative series | Undersized or thin graft, or medial-only fixation without adequate lateral footprint fixation, is a common cause of early graft failure |
| Lower trapezius transfer | Use a tendon graft (Achilles allograft or similar) to bridge from the lower trapezius to the infraspinatus footprint, respecting the natural line of pull | Injuring the spinal accessory nerve or dorsal scapular neurovascular bundle during harvest — meticulous exposure required |
| rTSA for cuff tear arthropathy | Lateralize the glenosphere (bony-increased offset or lateralized implant design) in cases with a still-partially functional teres minor to preserve external rotation | Excessive baseplate superior tilt or under-inferior-overhang increases scapular notching risk |
| Biceps tenodesis | Set tension with the elbow at ~20–30° flexion to replicate native length-tension before final fixation | Over-tensioning causes loss of elbow flexion/supination strength and groove pain; under-tensioning risks residual Popeye-like bulge |
| SLAP repair | Confirm a true peel-back mechanism intraoperatively (arm in abduction-ER) before committing to anchor placement — many "SLAP" MRI findings are normal sublabral recesses | Over-tightening the posterosuperior labrum can restrict internal rotation and precipitate internal impingement symptoms postoperatively |
| Pectoralis major acute repair | Use cortical button or heavy transosseous suture fixation for maximal pull-out strength given the high failure load demanded in athletes | Failing to identify and repair both sternal and clavicular heads separately when both are torn — partial repair of only the more visible head under-restores strength |

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Rotator cuff repair (arthroscopic, small-medium tear, standard protection protocol)**
- **Weeks 0–6**: sling immobilization (with abduction pillow if tension high), passive ROM only (pendulum, passive forward elevation and external rotation within surgeon-specified limits), no active ROM or lifting. AAOS 2025 CPG notes outcomes are similar for early vs delayed (up to 8 weeks) mobilization for small-medium tears — protocol timing can be individualized to repair tension/quality rather than treated as fixed dogma.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling, progress to active-assisted then active ROM, initiate scapular stabilization and light isometrics; avoid resisted strengthening before ~8–12 weeks.
- **Weeks 12–16**: progressive resistive strengthening (theraband, light weights), restore full active ROM.
- **Months 4–6**: sport/heavy-labor-specific strengthening, plyometrics for overhead athletes.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often by 2–6 weeks (non-operative arm tasks); manual labor typically 4–6 months depending on repair size and job demand.
- **Return to sport**: non-contact/non-overhead sport ~4–6 months; overhead/throwing and contact sport 6–9 months, contingent on full painless ROM, ≥90% strength symmetry, and (where available) imaging confirmation of healing for high-risk repairs.

**Massive tear reconstructions (SCR, tendon transfer)**: more conservative — typically 6 weeks strict immobilization (abduction brace commonly used for tendon transfers to protect the repair in a position of reduced tension), passive ROM introduced gradually from week 4–6, active ROM from ~8–12 weeks, strengthening from 3–4 months; full recovery/plateau often 9–12 months given graft/transfer incorporation timelines.

**Calcific tendinopathy (post-barbotage or post-arthroscopic excision)**: sling for comfort only (days), early passive/active-assisted ROM within the first 1–2 weeks (no repair to protect), progress strengthening as tolerated by pain — much faster course than cuff repair since no tendon-to-bone healing is required unless concomitant cuff repair was performed.

**SLAP repair**: sling 3–4 weeks, avoid active elbow flexion/supination against resistance and biceps loading for ~6 weeks, passive ROM early, active ROM from ~6 weeks, strengthening from 8–12 weeks, throwing progression not started before 4–5 months, full return to competitive overhead sport typically 6–9 months (throwers on the longer end).

**Biceps tenodesis**: sling 2–4 weeks, avoid resisted elbow flexion/forearm supination for 6 weeks (protect fixation), progressive strengthening from 6–8 weeks, return to heavy lifting/sport 3–4 months.

**Pectoralis major repair**: sling/abduction brace 4–6 weeks with restricted external rotation and extension to protect the repair, passive ROM early, active ROM from ~6 weeks, resisted internal rotation/adduction strengthening deferred to ~10–12 weeks, return to heavy bench-press/contact sport typically 4–6 months, with some protocols extending to 6–9 months for allograft reconstructions.

**Physiotherapy content common to all protocols**: scapular stabilizer strengthening (serratus anterior, lower trapezius) throughout, posterior capsule stretching (sleeper stretch) for internal cuff pathology and SLAP, closed-chain proprioceptive work before progressing to open-chain resisted work, and a criterion-based (not purely time-based) progression through phases whenever repair integrity allows.

**Return-to-sport / return-to-work criteria checklist** (apply across diagnoses, adapted to the specific procedure's healing timeline):
- Full, painless active and passive ROM matching the contralateral side (or within an agreed functional deficit for massive-tear reconstructions).
- Strength ≥85–90% of the contralateral limb on isokinetic or handheld dynamometry where available.
- Negative provocative tests for the operated pathology (e.g., negative O'Brien's after SLAP repair, negative lag signs after cuff repair).
- Sport-specific functional testing passed (e.g., interval throwing program completed without symptom recurrence for overhead athletes; graded bench-press return for pectoralis major repairs).
- Imaging confirmation of healing (US or MRI) is reasonable for high-risk repairs (massive tears, revision surgery, elite athletes) before full-contact or maximal-load return, though not mandatory for routine small-medium repairs progressing on schedule.
- For manual-labor return to work: graded/light-duty return often possible earlier (6–10 weeks) with formal work-hardening before full unrestricted duty.

## Complications

- **Retear/failure of healing**: the dominant complication after cuff repair; rates range widely by tear size and technique — small tears <10–20%, large/massive tears up to 40–90% in some series depending on definition and imaging modality used for detection. Risk factors: tear size, chronicity, Goutallier ≥2, smoking, diabetes, poor tendon quality, revision setting.
- **Stiffness/adhesive capsulitis**: more common after aggressive early immobilization or in predisposed patients (diabetics); usually responds to PT, occasionally requires manipulation or arthroscopic capsular release.
- **Infection**: low but real risk (<1–2%) with arthroscopic repair; higher after open procedures/allograft reconstruction (pectoralis major, SCR).
- **Axillary or suprascapular nerve injury**: risk with portal placement, aggressive retraction, or during tendon transfer harvest (lower trapezius, latissimus) — meticulous anatomic technique required.
- **Anchor pullout/hardware failure**: particularly in osteoporotic bone; consider medializing anchors or augmenting fixation in elderly patients.
- **Popeye deformity**: expected cosmetic sequela of tenotomy (30–50%), avoided by tenodesis (though not zero risk with tenodesis failure).
- **Groove/residual biceps pain**: can occur after suprapectoral tenodesis; subpectoral fixation may reduce this.
- **Graft failure (SCR)**: reported rates up to 20–40% depending on graft material and series; higher with dermal allograft than fascia lata autograft in some comparative studies.
- **Re-rupture (pectoralis major)**: higher after chronic/allograft reconstruction than acute primary repair; steroid users and delayed presentations carry elevated risk.
- **Progression to cuff tear arthropathy**: the natural endpoint of chronic, untreated massive cuff insufficiency — underscores the rationale for early counseling/intervention in appropriate candidates before pseudoparalysis or arthritic change develops, since rTSA becomes the fallback once this occurs.
- **Scapular notching (post-rTSA)**: mechanical impingement of the humeral polyethylene against the inferior scapular neck; minimized by adequate glenosphere lateralization/inferior overhang and appropriate baseplate inferior tilt — a technique-dependent, largely preventable complication rather than an inherent implant limitation.
- **Deltoid dysfunction after transfer surgery**: temporary or, rarely, permanent deltoid weakness can follow aggressive deltoid splitting/retraction during open approaches (SCR, tendon transfer, rTSA) — favor muscle-sparing exposure where feasible.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2025 — AAOS updated Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Rotator Cuff Injuries** (replacing the 2019 CPG): broadened scope from impingement/bursitis through massive tears; explicitly does not support routine concomitant acromioplasty for small-medium full-thickness tears (no added benefit over repair alone), and states outcomes are similar with early versus delayed (up to 8 weeks) postoperative mobilization — meaningfully changes rehab protocol rigidity and intraoperative decision-making around decompression. [AAOS Updates Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Rotator Cuff Injuries](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-updates-clinical-practice-guideline-for-the-management-of-rotator-cuff-injuries/); [summary](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/9802/)
- **2025 — START:REACTS 2-year RCT follow-up** (subacromial balloon spacer for irreparable cuff tears): confirms the 12-month primary result — debridement alone outperformed debridement plus balloon spacer on WORC and patient global impression at 24 months, with no OSS benefit from the balloon. Practice implication: the InSpace balloon spacer should not be routinely offered for massive irreparable cuff tears; reserve for very selected cases outside standard practice. [Two-Year Follow-up of START:REACTS (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12044206/); [original Lancet RCT 2022](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(22)00652-3/fulltext)
- **2025 — Comparative study: lower trapezius tendon transfer vs SCR** for massive irreparable cuff tears shows LTT restores deltoid function and dynamic glenohumeral stability more effectively than SCR in cadaveric/biomechanical and early clinical comparisons — reinforces a shift toward LTT (particularly with prominent external rotation weakness/Hornblower sign) over static SCR in centers with transfer expertise. [Lower Trapezius Tendon Transfer vs SCR (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12034054/)
- **2025 — Systematic review, arthroscopic-assisted lower trapezius transfer vs SCR** for irreparable massive cuff tears (used to augment partial repairs): both improve outcomes but with variability by technique and graft choice — supports individualized selection rather than a one-size-fits-all salvage algorithm. [Improved yet varied outcomes, SCR vs aLTT (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749806323003766)
- **2025 — Biceps tenodesis vs SLAP repair systematic review in overhead throwing athletes** (16 studies, 547 patients, 2005–2023): similar clinical outcomes and return-to-play between the two procedures, but baseball pitchers specifically show worse return-to-sport regardless of technique chosen — reinforces counseling pitchers on realistic expectations independent of surgical choice. [Biceps Tenodesis and SLAP Repair — Systematic Review (Arthroscopy 2025)](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2025.01.061)
- **2023 — Meta-analysis: biceps tenodesis vs SLAP repair in younger patients**: no significant difference in return to play (78.5% vs 67.7% specific comparisons cited), ASES, or VAS pain scores; no significant difference in reoperation rate — supports tenodesis as an equally valid (and in some series, less morbid) alternative to repair even in younger patients, not just an older-patient fallback. [Similar outcomes between biceps tenodesis and SLAP repair (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37797939/)
- **2023 — Network meta-analysis of arthroscopic rotator cuff repair techniques** and **2023 systematic review comparing single-row vs double-row for different tear sizes**: confirms double-row/transosseous-equivalent repair yields significantly better functional scores and lower retear rates for large/massive tears (≥3 cm), with no significant benefit over single-row for small tears (<3 cm) — supports a tear-size-stratified rather than universal double-row policy. [Single-row vs double-row by tear size (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37655249/); [Network meta-analysis of arthroscopic RCR (BMC Surgery)](https://bmcsurg.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12893-023-02078-4)
- **2024 — Knotted vs knotless double-row retear meta-analysis**: no statistically significant difference in retear rates between knotted and knotless double-row constructs (pooled ~1888 subjects) — supports leaving construct choice to surgeon preference/cost rather than an evidence-driven mandate. [Knotted or knotless double-row retear rates (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10840561/)
- **2024/2025 — Network meta-analysis of calcific tendinopathy treatments** (33 RCTs, 2003–2023, 2102 participants): high-energy focused ESWT and ultrasound-guided barbotage/lavage rank as the most effective nonoperative interventions, both superior to sham and to low-energy ESWT — informs first-line nonoperative sequencing (favor high-energy ESWT or barbotage over low-energy modalities). [Treatments for rotator cuff calcific tendinitis — network meta-analysis (EFORT Open Reviews 2025)](https://eor.bioscientifica.com/view/journals/eor/10/7/EOR-2024-0078.xml)
- **2024 — Barbotage efficacy study**: confirms barbotage produces significant short-term (≈2-month) pain reduction for calcific tendinopathy but effect diminishes over longer follow-up, with a meaningful proportion of patients requiring further intervention (repeat injection, repeat barbotage, or surgery) — informs patient counseling that barbotage may need to be repeated rather than viewed as a single definitive treatment. [Determining the efficacy of barbotage for pain relief in calcific tendinitis (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39280166/)
- **2024/2025 — CSAW and FIMPACT longer-term follow-up data** continue to show no clinically important difference between arthroscopic subacromial decompression, diagnostic arthroscopy, and non-surgical/placebo control for subacromial pain syndrome at 5–10 years — the strongest and most durable evidence yet against routine isolated subacromial decompression, reinforcing that SAPS without a structural lesion should be managed nonoperatively/with arthroscopy reserved for concomitant pathology. [CSAW trial — Lancet](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-67361732457-1/fulltext); [FIMPACT 10-year follow-up (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12670246/); [FIMPACT 5-year return-to-work data (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8527687/)
- **2024/2025 — Pectoralis major repair outcome literature** (intramedullary suture anchor techniques in military populations, comprehensive return-to-activity review 2014–2024): reaffirms high patient-reported outcomes and return-to-duty/sport rates with modern fixation techniques (suture anchors, cortical buttons), while highlighting the continued absence of standardized return-to-activity criteria across centers — a gap orthopedic surgeons should account for when setting individualized return-to-sport timelines rather than relying on a single published protocol. [Pectoralis major repair with intramedullary suture anchors, military cohort (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38908466/); [Return to Functional Activity After Pectoralis Major Surgical Treatment — comprehensive review (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11927367/)
