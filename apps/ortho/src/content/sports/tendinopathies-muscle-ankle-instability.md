## Quick Reference

**Achilles tendon rupture**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1st line | Functional rehab, non-op (early WB brace, plantarflexion→neutral) | Re-rupture ~3.9% vs ~2.3% surgical (meta-analysis RR ~0.3 favoring surgery); gap acceptable <5 mm on US in plantarflexion |
| 2nd line | Percutaneous/mini-open repair | Lower wound complication than open (0.4% vs 6% infection) |
| Surgical | Open repair ± augmentation (FHL transfer, turndown) for chronic/neglected | Reserve for elite athletes, delayed presentation (>4-6 wks), re-rupture |

**Achilles tendinopathy**

| Step | Mid-portion | Insertional |
|---|---|---|
| 1st line | Heavy slow resistance (HSR) or eccentric (Alfredson) loading, 12 wks | HSR to flat ground/no dorsiflexion past neutral; avoid deep eccentric decline board (loads insertion adversely) |
| 2nd line | Shockwave (ESWT), topical/oral NSAID short course, activity modification | Radial ESWT — evidence "not certain" per recent RCT (2025) but reasonable adjunct |
| Injection | High-volume injection (HVI) — signal for benefit vs placebo; PRP inconclusive; **steroid peritendinous injection contraindicated** (rupture risk) | Same; avoid steroid near insertion/bursa |
| Surgical | Debridement ± FHL transfer if failed 6 mo conservative, >50% tendon involvement | Haglund resection + insertional debridement ± FHL augmentation if >50% detachment needed |

**Lateral / medial epicondylitis (tennis / golfer's elbow)**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1st line | Activity modification, eccentric wrist extensor (or flexor-pronator) loading, counterforce brace, wait-and-see (self-limited in ~80-90% by 1 yr) | Natural history favorable; avoid over-treating |
| 2nd line | PRP injection | Short-term (<2 mo) inferior to steroid; **superior to steroid at ≥6 mo** (VAS, DASH, MEPS) |
| Avoid | Corticosteroid injection as primary/repeated therapy | Faster short-term relief but worse long-term outcome and higher recurrence than PRP or physiotherapy alone |
| Surgical | Open/arthroscopic ECRB (or flexor-pronator) debridement/release | Reserve for >6-12 mo failed conservative care; success ~85-90% |

**Hamstring injury (BAMIC-graded)**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1st line | Progressive agility/trunk stabilization (PATS) or criterion-based loading protocol | Grade-dependent RTP; intramuscular tendon (c) involvement prolongs RTP substantially |
| Surgical | Rare for intramuscular strains; reserved for large intratendinous/chronic tears failing rehab | — |

**Proximal hamstring avulsion**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Non-op | Partial tears, <2 tendons, minimal retraction, low-demand patient | — |
| Surgical | Acute repair (within 4-6 wks) for complete 3-tendon avulsion with ≥2 cm retraction | Best outcomes with early repair; delayed repair (>6 mo) still beneficial but higher complication/lower strength recovery |

**Quadriceps / calf (gastrocnemius) strain**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1st line | BAMIC-graded rehab, identical framework to hamstring | "Tennis leg" (medial gastrocnemius myotendinous tear) — rule out plantaris rupture, DVT |
| Surgical | Rare — complete quadriceps tendon rupture (not strain) requires repair within 2 wks | See extensor mechanism monograph for quadriceps tendon rupture proper |

**Lateral ankle sprain / chronic instability**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Acute 1st line | POLICE/functional support (lace-up brace) + early mobilization, NOT prolonged immobilization | Functional rehab reduces recurrence vs cast; ~20-40% develop chronic instability |
| Chronic non-op | Structured proprioceptive/peroneal strengthening ≥3 mo | CAIT ≤24 defines functional instability (screening, not a surgical indication by itself) |
| Surgical | Modified Broström-Gould ± suture-tape internal brace | STA → faster return to sport, similar mid-term outcomes; consider primarily in revision, high-demand athletes, generalized laxity, poor tissue quality |

**Peroneal tendon pathology**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Non-op | Immobilization/bracing, PT, NSAIDs for tendinosis/subluxation without instability | — |
| Surgical | Tenosynovectomy/debridement, tubularization, tendon transfer (FHL/FDL) for >50% cross-sectional tear; SPR repair/reconstruction ± groove deepening for instability | AOFAS improves ~70→89 post-op; complication rate up to ~20-39% (mostly minor) across series |

**Distal biceps rupture**

| Step | Option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Non-op | Elderly, low-demand, sedentary patient | Accepts ~30-40% supination and ~15-40% flexion strength deficit |
| Surgical | Anatomic repair with cortical button ± interference screw/suture anchor, within 2-3 wks (ideally <2 wks before retraction/scarring) | Single-incision: lower complication overall in modern series but higher **PIN neuropraxia**; double-incision: lower nerve injury, higher heterotopic ossification/synostosis risk |

**Red flags across the cluster**
- **Achilles rupture:** palpable gap + positive Thompson test + inability to single-leg heel raise — do not miss on exam alone (up to 25% missed at first presentation).
- **Compartment syndrome** after severe calf/gastrocnemius strain or crush — pain out of proportion, tense compartment.
- **DVT** mimicking "tennis leg" — always assess calf swelling/Homans in medial gastrocnemius tear.
- **Vascular injury** with severe/complete hamstring avulsion (rare) — check distal pulses in high-energy mechanism.
- **PIN palsy** post distal biceps repair — document pre-op posterior interosseous nerve function (thumb/finger extension) before surgery, as pre-existing deficits are common (radial tunnel compression from mass effect).
- Sudden repeat "pop" after Achilles repair/rupture treatment at 6-12 wks = re-rupture until proven otherwise.

**Key numbers**
- Achilles gap <5 mm in 20° plantarflexion on dynamic US → non-op candidate.
- Achilles re-rupture: non-op ~3.9% vs op ~2.3% (pooled RCT data); with functional rehab non-op rates fall close to surgical rates.
- CAIT ≤24/30 = functional ankle instability; ATFL anterior drawer >3 mm side-to-side difference or talar tilt >9° = mechanical laxity (reference values, not absolute surgical thresholds).
- Proximal hamstring: >2 cm retraction / all 3 tendons avulsed = strong surgical indication.
- Distal biceps: repair ideally <3 wks (retraction/tendon quality deteriorates rapidly after 3-4 wks).
- Lateral epicondylitis: ~80-90% resolve within 12 months regardless of treatment ("wait it out" is a valid strategy).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Achilles tendon:** confluence of gastrocnemius and soleus aponeuroses, no true synovial sheath (paratenon instead), watershed hypovascular zone 2-6 cm proximal to insertion — the classic site of mid-portion rupture and tendinopathy. Insertion has a separate blood supply and biomechanical environment (compression + tension = fibrocartilaginous insertion), explaining why insertional disease behaves differently (compressive pathology against the calcaneus, worsened by ankle dorsiflexion) versus mid-portion tensile overload disease.

**Common extensor/flexor origin (epicondylitis):** lateral epicondylitis is primarily **angiofibroblastic tendinosis of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB)** origin — not an inflammatory process despite the "-itis" suffix; histology shows disorganized collagen, neovascularization, absence of inflammatory cells. Medial epicondylitis affects the common flexor-pronator origin (pronator teres/flexor carpi radialis), less common (10-20% of epicondylitis), higher association with ulnar neuritis (up to 50% coexistence) — always examine the ulnar nerve.

**Hamstring complex:** biceps femoris long head, semitendinosus, semimembranosus share a conjoint proximal origin at the ischial tuberosity (biceps femoris long head + semitendinosus) with semimembranosus having its own more lateral/anterior origin. Musculotendinous junction (distal, biceps femoris long head especially) is the classic site of sprint-type strains; the proximal free tendon/origin is the site of avulsion injuries. Sciatic nerve lies directly posterior/lateral to the conjoint tendon — at risk in proximal repair and can be tethered by scar in chronic avulsions (sciatic nerve entrapment syndrome).

**Lateral ankle ligament complex:** anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL, weakest, primary restraint to anterior translation and inversion in plantarflexion), calcaneofibular ligament (CFL, resists inversion in neutral/dorsiflexion, extra-articular, crosses both talocrural and subtalar joints), posterior talofibular ligament (PTFL, strongest, rarely injured in isolation). ~85% of sprains involve ATFL alone or ATFL+CFL. Peroneal tendons (longus/brevis) run in a common synovial sheath behind the fibula, stabilized by the superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR); brevis tears are typically longitudinal (attritional, against the fibular groove edge), longus tears more often at the os peroneum or cuboid tunnel.

**Distal biceps:** inserts on the radial tuberosity via a single footprint that externally rotates as the forearm pronates/supinates (bicipital tuberosity "cam" effect) — repair fixation angle/position affects supination strength recovery. The lacertus fibrosus, if intact, can mask a full rupture on exam (partial retraction only) and can itself be a source of persistent pain if not addressed.

**Gastrocnemius-soleus ("tennis leg") complex:** the medial gastrocnemius head crosses both the knee and ankle and is biomechanically disadvantaged relative to the uniarticular soleus during combined knee-extension/ankle-dorsiflexion loading — the classic mechanism for "tennis leg," a myotendinous strain/tear at the medial gastrocnemius-soleus junction, often misattributed clinically to a ruptured plantaris tendon (the plantaris itself, when present in ~90% of individuals, can rupture independently or contribute to the same clinical picture and is a genuine differential, not the primary lesion in most cases). Because the presentation (acute posterior calf pain/swelling, "shot" sensation) closely mimics deep vein thrombosis, and because a large intramuscular hematoma is itself thrombogenic, compression ultrasound to exclude DVT is a standard, non-optional part of work-up.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**Achilles rupture:** Thompson (Simmonds) squeeze test (absent plantarflexion = rupture), palpable gap, Matles test (resting knee-flexion foot droop), loss of single-leg heel raise. Up to 20-25% missed on initial ED exam — always test actively against resistance, not just passive tone. Dynamic ultrasound in resting equinus/plantarflexion quantifies tendon-end apposition gap — the key determinant for non-op eligibility.

**Achilles tendinopathy:** localized mid-portion (2-6 cm proximal) fusiform swelling/tenderness that moves with the tendon on ankle motion (distinguishes from paratenonitis, which is fixed) vs insertional tenderness directly at the calcaneal insertion ± retrocalcaneal bursitis ± visible Haglund prominence. Royal London Hospital test, arc sign. US shows hypoechoic thickening, neovascularization (Doppler); MRI for surgical planning/partial tear extent.

**Epicondylitis:** point tenderness 1 cm distal/anterior to lateral epicondyle (ECRB origin), pain with resisted wrist extension (Cozen's test) and passive wrist flexion with elbow extended (Mill's test) for lateral; resisted wrist flexion/pronation and passive extension for medial. Always test ulnar nerve (Tinel at cubital tunnel) with medial epicondylitis. MRI/US reserved for atypical presentation, failed conservative treatment, or pre-surgical planning.

**Hamstring/quadriceps/calf strain:** mechanism (sprint deceleration = hamstring MTJ; overstretch = hamstring; forceful eccentric quad contraction = rectus femoris), palpable defect, ecchymosis (may take 24-72h to appear), resisted strength testing, straight-leg-raise deficit. MRI is the imaging of choice for grading (BAMIC) and prognosis in athletes — obtain within first 24-72h for best signal, though findings evolve over first week.

**Proximal hamstring avulsion:** "sit down" mechanism (hip flexed, knee extended, forced eccentric load), immediate posterior thigh/buttock pain, "pop," sciatic-type radiating pain (nerve irritation from hematoma), palpable gap, ecchymosis tracking down posterior thigh/into popliteal fossa over days. MRI mandatory for surgical planning: number of tendons involved, retraction distance (measured from ischial tuberosity), and tendon quality.

**Ankle sprain (acute):** Ottawa Ankle Rules to select patients needing radiographs (malleolar zone tenderness or inability to bear weight 4 steps immediately and in ED). Anterior drawer (ATFL) and talar tilt (CFL) exams best performed at 4-5 days post-injury (acute guarding reduces sensitivity), compare to contralateral side. Squeeze test and external rotation (Kleiger) test to screen for syndesmotic injury — do not miss a high ankle sprain.

**Chronic ankle instability:** distinguish **mechanical instability** (pathologic laxity — positive anterior drawer/talar tilt, stress radiographs or stress US) from **functional instability** (subjective "giving way," proprioceptive/neuromuscular deficit, normal exam) — most patients have a mix of both. CAIT (Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool) questionnaire quantifies functional instability (≤24/30). Weight-bearing CT increasingly used to assess hindfoot alignment/varus and concomitant pathology (OCD, impingement, peroneal tears) before revision or complex reconstruction.

**Peroneal pathology:** tenderness/swelling posterior to lateral malleolus, peroneal subluxation testing (resisted eversion/dorsiflexion with palpation over the groove — subluxation over the fibular rim confirms SPR incompetence), differentiate from lateral ankle instability (may coexist — always examine peroneals in CAI work-up). MRI is the imaging standard; dynamic US can catch dynamic subluxation missed on static MRI.

**Distal biceps rupture:** palpable defect in the antecubital fossa, ecchymosis, weakness of supination (more affected than flexion, since brachialis/brachioradialis compensate flexion), **hook test** (inability to hook a finger under the intact tendon from lateral to medial — highly sensitive/specific), positive biceps squeeze test (analogous to Thompson test). MRI (FABS view) confirms and grades partial vs complete tear when exam is equivocal.

## Classification

### BAMIC — British Athletics Muscle Injury Classification (muscle strains, incl. hamstring/quadriceps/calf)

| Grade | Structural extent (MRI) | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Symptomatic, no MRI abnormality (or old fibrosis only) | Rehab as clinically indicated; often rapid RTP |
| 1 | Minimal — small area of increased T2 signal, <10% cross-sectional area (CSA) or focal | Short rehab course, RTP often 1-2 wks |
| 2 | Moderate — larger edema, up to ~50% CSA, no significant fiber discontinuity | RTP typically 2-4 wks |
| 3 | Extensive — >50% CSA edema/fiber disruption | RTP typically 4-8+ wks |
| 4 | Complete tear/full-thickness disruption with retraction | Surgical evaluation, prolonged rehab (often >2-3 mo) |
| Site suffix **a** | Myofascial (edema at fascia, no intramuscular tendon change) | Best prognosis at a given grade |
| Site suffix **b** | Musculotendinous junction (myotendinous) | Intermediate — most common site, standard RTP timelines |
| Site suffix **c** | Intramuscular tendon involvement (tendon disruption within muscle belly) | **Substantially prolongs RTP** (e.g., ~78 vs ~24 days in rugby cohorts) and raises re-injury risk — flag these explicitly in reports |

Grade + letter (e.g., "2b," "3c") is now the standard reporting format in elite sport MRI and should be used routinely in athlete-facing reports; the "c" (intramuscular tendon) designation is the single most important modifier for return-to-play counseling.

### Haglund deformity / insertional vs mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy

| Type | Definition | Key management difference |
|---|---|---|
| **Mid-portion tendinopathy** | Fusiform thickening 2-6 cm proximal to insertion, tensile-overload pathology | Loading protocol includes full dorsiflexion range (eccentric decline-board or HSR through full ROM); surgical debridement ± tendon transfer if >50% cross-section involved |
| **Insertional tendinopathy** | Pathology at the calcaneal insertion (fibrocartilaginous enthesis), often with retrocalcaneal bursitis | Loading modified to avoid deep dorsiflexion (compressive component) — HSR performed with heel flat on ground/floor-level, not off a step; surgical management may require detachment/reattachment |
| **Haglund deformity** | Prominent posterosuperior calcaneal tuberosity mechanically abutting/irritating the Achilles insertion and retrocalcaneal bursa ("pump bump") | Often coexists with insertional tendinopathy; surgical resection of the prominence ± insertional debridement is required when bony impingement is the driver, not just tendon debridement alone |

Practical distinction matters because eccentric decline-board loading (the classic Alfredson protocol), effective for mid-portion disease, is **relatively contraindicated/less effective and can aggravate insertional disease** — insertional cases should be loaded without exceeding neutral dorsiflexion.

### Puranen-Orava classification / MRI grading (hamstring strain, functional/clinical correlate)

| Grade | Clinical description | Correlate |
|---|---|---|
| I (mild) | Localized pain, no/minimal loss of strength, RTP within days | Approx. BAMIC 0-1 |
| II (moderate/partial) | Partial tear, restricted movement, palpable defect possible, RTP weeks | Approx. BAMIC 2-3 |
| III (severe/complete) | Complete rupture, marked functional loss, palpable gap, significant hematoma | Approx. BAMIC 4 / complete avulsion spectrum |

The Puranen-Orava scheme is a legacy clinical severity grade (mild/moderate/severe based on exam and functional loss); in current practice it is used alongside — and has largely been supplemented by — BAMIC MRI grading, which adds anatomic site (myofascial/MTJ/intratendinous) and quantitative CSA, both stronger predictors of RTP time than clinical grade alone.

### CFA — Chronic ankle instability classification framework (reference)

Used as an integrative reference framework (not a single validated numeric score) combining:

| Domain | Assessment | Management relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical instability** | Anterior drawer, talar tilt, stress imaging (arthrometry, stress radiographs/US) | Objective laxity supports surgical candidacy when combined with failed rehab |
| **Functional instability** | CAIT ≤24, subjective giving-way episodes, postural control/proprioceptive deficits | Primary target of non-operative neuromuscular rehab; may respond fully without surgery |
| **Associated intra-articular pathology** | Osteochondral lesions, anterolateral impingement/synovitis, peroneal tendon tears, syndesmotic injury | Found in up to ~40-60% of chronic cases at arthroscopy — must be addressed concurrently or outcome suffers regardless of ligament technique |
| **Tissue quality / risk factors for repair failure** | Generalized ligamentous laxity (Beighton), high BMI, revision setting, poor remnant tissue on exam/MRI | Favors adding suture-tape augmentation or considering free-graft (anatomic) reconstruction over isolated Broström-Gould |

Use this framework clinically to decide: (1) non-op vs operative candidacy (mechanical + functional instability both present, failed ≥3 mo structured rehab); (2) isolated modified Broström-Gould vs augmentation with suture-tape vs free tendon graft reconstruction (based on tissue quality/risk factor column); and (3) need for concomitant arthroscopic treatment of intra-articular pathology.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Achilles rupture:** functional rehabilitation protocol (early controlled motion in a hinged/removable boot, progressive dorsiflexion, early weight-bearing) is equivalent in outcome to plaster immobilization for non-operative patients and reduces stiffness/muscle atrophy (UKSTAR trial validated functional bracing vs plaster cast, n=527, no significant difference in patient-reported Achilles Tendon Rupture Score). Candidacy: gap <5 mm on dynamic US, ability to comply with progressive protocol, lower functional demand or shared decision-making with athlete after full risk discussion.
- **Achilles tendinopathy:** progressive tendon loading is first-line for both mid-portion and insertional disease — heavy slow resistance (HSR, e.g., 3x/week, 12 weeks) is now favored over the classic Alfredson eccentric-only protocol due to comparable outcomes with better tolerability/adherence and applicability to insertional disease (loaded without provocative dorsiflexion). Activity modification, load management, calf/soleus strength and capacity building, gradual return to plyometrics.
- **Epicondylitis:** wait-and-see is a legitimate strategy given ~80-90% resolve by 12 months; when treating, eccentric/isotonic wrist extensor (lateral) or flexor-pronator (medial) strengthening, counterforce bracing, activity/ergonomic modification. Avoid prolonged immobilization (deconditioning).
- **Muscle strains (hamstring/quad/calf):** criterion-based, phase progression (pain-free ROM → progressive strength → sport-specific/eccentric loading → plyometric/sprint mechanics) rather than fixed timelines; Nordic hamstring exercise integrated in later phases both for rehab and re-injury prevention (proven to reduce hamstring re-injury incidence in RCTs).
- **Proximal hamstring avulsion:** reserved for partial tears (<2 tendons involved), minimal retraction, and lower-demand/elderly patients — structured progressive loading similar to severe strain protocol, accepting a strength deficit trade-off.
- **Ankle sprain (acute):** functional treatment — early protected weight-bearing, lace-up brace or semi-rigid support (not below-knee cast) — outperforms prolonged immobilization; PT-supervised progressive proprioceptive and peroneal strengthening reduces recurrence and progression to chronic instability.
- **Chronic ankle instability:** minimum 3 months structured supervised neuromuscular/proprioceptive rehabilitation (balance board, peroneal strengthening, sport-specific agility) before considering surgery — a meaningful proportion of "functional" instability resolves with rehab alone even when some mechanical laxity persists.
- **Peroneal tendinosis (without instability):** activity modification, immobilization trial, orthotics for hindfoot varus, eccentric peroneal strengthening.
- **Distal biceps partial tear / low-demand complete rupture:** accept functional deficit (~30-40% supination strength, ~15-40% flexion strength) in elderly/sedentary/comorbid patients; strengthen compensators (brachialis, brachioradialis, pronator teres for flexion/supination substitution).

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Achilles tendinopathy:** Corticosteroid injection into or around the Achilles tendon is **contraindicated** as a standard treatment — associated with subsequent tendon rupture and should be avoided (peritendinous/bursal steroid for isolated retrocalcaneal bursitis is a separate, more defensible indication, still used cautiously). High-volume image-guided injection (HVI, saline/local anesthetic ± small corticosteroid dose, stripping neovascularization at the tendon-paratenon interface) shows benefit vs placebo at 12-24 weeks in a 2024/2025 systematic review and outperformed PRP at 12 weeks in comparative data — a reasonable second-line adjunct in mid-portion disease refractory to loading. PRP for Achilles tendinopathy remains inconclusive — meta-analyses do not support it as standard care; further RCT data needed before routine use. Radial extracorporeal shockwave therapy (rESWT) is a reasonable adjunct for insertional disease, though a 2025 sham-controlled RCT found evidence "not certain" at 12 weeks — counsel patients accordingly, it is not a guaranteed responder therapy.
- **Epicondylitis:** PRP injection is a reasonable second-line option, particularly when patients want an interventional option beyond exercise and want to avoid steroid — meta-analyses (2024-2025) consistently show PRP superior to corticosteroid at ≥6 months for pain (VAS) and function (DASH, MEPS), even though corticosteroid gives faster relief at <4-8 weeks. **Avoid repeated/serial corticosteroid injections** — short-term gain is offset by higher recurrence rates and potentially worse long-term outcome versus PRP or physiotherapy alone; use at most a single steroid injection if used at all, and counsel patients explicitly on this short-term-vs-long-term trade-off. Dry needling/percutaneous tenotomy and autologous blood injection are lower-evidence alternatives.
- **Chronic ankle instability:** intra-articular corticosteroid has a limited, non-curative role for concomitant synovitis/impingement symptom control while pursuing rehab — does not substitute for addressing ligament laxity.
- **Peroneal pathology:** image-guided diagnostic/therapeutic injection into the tendon sheath can help distinguish tenosynovitis from tear and provide short-term relief but not a substitute for structural repair when a tear is present.

### Operative

- **Achilles tendon rupture — repair techniques:**
  - **Percutaneous/mini-open repair** (e.g., Achillon, PARS, or similar jig-assisted systems): significantly lower wound infection risk (~0.4%) than open repair (~6%), shorter operative time; trade-off is a small but real risk of **sural nerve injury** with purely percutaneous techniques — mini-open/jig-assisted approaches under direct visualization mitigate this.
  - **Open repair** (Krackow or Bunnell core suture ± epitendinous augmentation): preferred for delayed presentation, re-rupture, or when direct tendon-end apposition/augmentation is needed; higher wound complication risk, particularly in smokers, diabetics, and open injuries.
  - **Chronic/neglected rupture (>4-6 weeks) or large gap:** V-Y advancement, FHL tendon transfer (adds active plantarflexion power, favored for gaps >5-6 cm or in athletes/high-demand patients), turndown flap (Bosworth/Silfverskiöld-type), or allograft reconstruction for massive defects.
  - Meta-analytic data (2025) confirm surgery lowers re-rupture risk versus non-operative treatment (~2.3% vs ~3.9%) and gives faster return to work (~19 days earlier), at the cost of surgical complication risk — decision should be individualized (athlete/high-demand vs sedentary/comorbid) and shared with the patient, not protocolized.
- **Achilles tendinopathy — surgery after ≥6 months failed conservative treatment:**
  - Mid-portion: open or endoscopic debridement of degenerative tissue/paratenon release ± tendon-splitting; if >50% of tendon cross-section requires excision, augment with **FHL tendon transfer** (single-incision technique preserves strength, transferred through a bone tunnel or interference screw into the calcaneus) to protect against post-debridement rupture and boost push-off power.
  - Insertional: resection of Haglund prominence + debridement of the diseased insertion; if detachment exceeds ~50% of the footprint, re-anchor with suture anchors; consider FHL augmentation for extensive insertional disease, especially in lower-demand/higher-risk-of-rerupture patients.
- **Lateral/medial epicondylitis:** open or arthroscopic ECRB (lateral) / flexor-pronator origin (medial) debridement and repair for symptoms refractory to ≥6-12 months conservative management (including a documented PRP or steroid trial). Arthroscopic approach allows concurrent evaluation/treatment of intra-articular pathology (plica, OCD, loose bodies) — favored when intra-articular pathology is suspected on imaging.
- **Hamstring/quad/calf strains:** surgery rarely indicated for intramuscular strains; consider for large chronic intratendinous tears/scar with persistent functional deficit, or symptomatic myotendinous junction disruption failing extended rehab in elite athletes (individualized).
- **Proximal hamstring avulsion:** anatomic repair via suture anchors (2-4 anchors) into the ischial tuberosity footprint through a prone or modified lithotomy approach; identify and protect the sciatic nerve throughout (it lies immediately deep/lateral to the conjoint tendon and is often adherent in delayed cases). Acute repair (within 4-6 weeks) gives the best strength recovery and RTP; **delayed repair (>6 months) still improves outcomes** versus non-op in complete avulsions but carries a higher complication rate (nerve irritation, wound issues) and generally inferior ultimate strength recovery due to fibrosis/retraction. Revision repair (for failed primary repair) has a documented complication profile and outcomes distinct from — generally inferior to — primary repair; counsel accordingly. Post-op bracing practice (hip extension/knee flexion limiting brace) is common but evidence for its necessity is mixed per recent systematic review.
- **Lateral ankle sprain / chronic instability — Broström-Gould and augmentation:**
  - **Modified Broström-Gould repair** (imbrication/direct repair of the ATFL ± CFL with reinforcement of the extensor retinaculum over the repair — the "Gould modification") remains the gold-standard anatomic procedure, open or arthroscopic; arthroscopic technique allows concurrent treatment of intra-articular pathology, reduces complication rate, and speeds recovery versus open in comparative series.
  - **Suture-tape internal brace augmentation** (synthetic tape secured from fibula to talus/calcaneus alongside the anatomic repair) is increasingly used, particularly for revision cases, high-demand athletes, generalized ligamentous laxity, or poor-quality remnant tissue — recent comparative/RCT data show **similar mid-term clinical outcomes to isolated repair but significantly faster return to sport**, without added complication burden in most series; it functions as a protective internal splint during ligament healing rather than a permanent structural replacement.
  - **Free tendon graft reconstruction** (e.g., using a semitendinosus, peroneus longus half-graft, or allograft, anatomically routed through fibular and talar/calcaneal tunnels) reserved for revision surgery, absent/attenuated native ligament tissue, or high-demand athletes with failed prior repair — higher morbidity, longer rehab, but more robust reconstruction when native tissue is inadequate.
  - Always address concomitant intra-articular pathology (osteochondral lesions, anterolateral impingement, syndesmotic instability) at the time of ligament surgery — a major driver of otherwise-unexplained post-operative dissatisfaction.
- **Peroneal tendon pathology:**
  - Tenosynovitis/partial longitudinal tears (<50% cross-section): debridement + tubularization (repair the tendon into a round/functional cross-section).
  - Extensive tears (>50% cross-section) or one tendon irreparable: **tenodesis of the torn tendon to the adjacent intact peroneal tendon**; complete non-functional tendon: FHL or FDL tendon transfer.
  - Peroneal subluxation/dislocation (SPR incompetence): SPR repair/reconstruction ± fibular groove deepening (for a shallow/flat retromalleolar groove) ± fibular osteotomy in select recurrent cases; can often be treated with SPR repair alone without bony procedures with good results per recent case series.
  - Address underlying hindfoot varus (calcaneal osteotomy) or CAI concurrently when present — peroneal disease and lateral ankle instability frequently coexist and untreated malalignment/instability predisposes to recurrent peroneal pathology.
- **Distal biceps rupture:**
  - **Timing:** repair ideally within 2-3 weeks of injury, before tendon retraction and scarring compromise primary anatomic repair; beyond 4-6 weeks, may require graft reconstruction (semitendinosus autograft, Achilles allograft, or dermal allograft) to bridge the gap.
  - **Single-incision anterior approach with cortical button ± interference screw:** favored in many contemporary series for better cosmesis, faster early recovery, and (in modern technique series) comparable or superior functional/satisfaction outcomes to double-incision; carries a higher risk of **posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) neuropraxia** (traction injury, usually transient) — meticulous retraction technique and supination during exposure reduce risk.
  - **Two-incision (Boyd-Anderson modified) approach:** lower nerve injury risk (avoids working directly in the PIN-adjacent zone) but higher risk of heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis if the posterior dissection violates the interosseous membrane broadly — meticulous subperiosteal technique and avoiding a single continuous posterior/anterior soft-tissue bridge disruption reduce this risk.
  - Fixation choice (cortical button vs interference screw vs suture anchor vs combined) driven by surgeon preference and bone quality; button constructs generally provide the highest biomechanical pull-out strength, supporting earlier active rehabilitation in reliable patients.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Achilles rupture (operative or non-op functional protocol) — representative accelerated/functional pathway:**
- **Weeks 0-2:** boot immobilization in equinus (~20-30° plantarflexion), non-weight-bearing or touch-down WB per surgeon protocol; wound/soft-tissue management if operative.
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive weight-bearing in boot as tolerated, wedges removed gradually (~1 wedge/1-2 wks) to bring foot toward neutral by ~6 weeks; begin gentle active ROM out of boot per protocol (~4 wks), avoid resisted dorsiflexion/stretching.
- **Weeks 6-8:** transition out of boot to normal footwear, begin gait retraining, gentle stationary bike, pool therapy; introduce closed-chain calf loading (double-leg heel raises) as strength allows.
- **Weeks 8-12:** progressive strengthening (double- then single-leg heel raises), proprioception, gentle jogging trial around 12 weeks if strength/symmetry criteria met (heel-raise endurance/height ≥~70-80% of contralateral).
- **3-6 months:** sport-specific and plyometric progression, calf circumference and heel-raise symmetry, hop testing.
- **Return to sport:** typically 6-9 months (elite/cutting sports may take up to 9-12 months); criteria-based on limb symmetry index (heel-raise height/repetitions ≥90%, hop tests ≥90%), not time alone. Return to sedentary work: 2-6 weeks; manual labor: 3-4 months depending on demands.

**Achilles tendinopathy (loading protocol):**
- Weeks 0-4: isometric loading for pain control (especially in-season athletes needing same-day analgesia) + begin HSR or eccentric program 3x/week.
- Weeks 4-12: progressive HSR (heavy, slow — e.g., 4-sec concentric/4-sec eccentric, 3-4 sets, progressing load) or eccentric decline-board program (mid-portion only), running/impact reintroduced gradually as pain allows (pain ≤3-4/10 during activity acceptable, resolving by next morning — the "24-hour rule").
- Weeks 12+: plyometric and sport-specific reloading; expect gradual response over 3 months, full response may take 6 months — set expectations accordingly.

**Epicondylitis:**
- Weeks 0-6: activity/ergonomic modification, counterforce brace, initiate eccentric wrist extensor/flexor-pronator program, ice/analgesia as needed.
- Weeks 6-12: progressive isotonic strengthening, grip work, gradual return to provocative activity/sport.
- Return to work/sport: variable, often 6-12 weeks for symptom control though full resolution may take up to 12 months; post-surgical (if performed) return to sport typically 4-6 months.

**Hamstring/quadriceps/calf strain (criterion-based, guided by BAMIC grade):**
- **Phase 1 (pain/protection):** relative rest, pain-free isometrics, gentle ROM; duration scales with grade (days for grade 0-1, up to 2-3 weeks for grade 3-4).
- **Phase 2 (loading/strength):** progressive isotonic strengthening, single-leg work, gradual eccentric loading (Nordic curls introduced once pain-free concentric strength restored) for hamstrings; trunk/core stabilization.
- **Phase 3 (running/power):** progressive running program, sprint mechanics, eccentric strength benchmarks (e.g., Nordic hamstring test symmetry) before sprint reintroduction.
- **Phase 4 (sport-specific/RTP):** agility, deceleration, sport-specific drills; RTP criteria are strength symmetry (≥90% contralateral), pain-free full-speed sprinting, and functional hop/agility test symmetry — not calendar time alone, though grade correlates strongly (grade 1: days-1wk; grade 2: 2-4 wks; grade 3: 4-8 wks; grade 4/intramuscular tendon "c": often >8 wks and re-injury risk is elevated).

**Proximal hamstring avulsion repair:**
- Weeks 0-2: brace limiting hip flexion/knee extension combination (protects repair), protected weight-bearing (usually partial to full with brace/crutches per surgeon protocol), no active hamstring contraction against resistance.
- Weeks 2-6: progressive hip/knee ROM, gentle isometrics introduced ~4-6 weeks.
- Weeks 6-12: progressive resistance training, closed-chain strengthening, gradual sitting tolerance normalization.
- Months 3-6: running progression, eccentric hamstring loading (Nordics), sport-specific drills.
- Return to sport: typically 6-9 months, criterion-based on strength symmetry (≥85-90%) and functional testing; earlier RTP correlates with earlier surgical timing (repair within 4-6 wks of injury).

**Ankle sprain (acute, functional protocol):**
- Days 0-3: POLICE (protection, optimal loading, ice, compression, elevation), early weight-bearing as tolerated in lace-up/semi-rigid support (not rigid cast) unless syndesmotic or fracture.
- Days 3-14: progressive ROM, isometric/isotonic peroneal strengthening, proprioceptive/balance training (single-leg stance progressions) started early — this phase is the single strongest lever against progression to chronic instability.
- Weeks 2-6: sport-specific agility, cutting/pivoting reintroduction, functional bracing/taping during return to sport for first season back.
- Return to sport: full pain-free ROM, strength symmetry, and successful sport-specific agility/hop testing — typically 2-6 weeks for grade I-II, up to 6-12 weeks for grade III (complete ATFL±CFL tear).

**Chronic ankle instability surgery (Broström-Gould ± suture tape):**
- Weeks 0-2: non-weight-bearing to protected weight-bearing in a boot/splint per surgeon protocol (suture-tape augmentation may allow slightly earlier protected WB given the internal splinting effect).
- Weeks 2-6: progressive weight-bearing in boot, gentle inversion/eversion-protected ROM initiated around 2-4 weeks.
- Weeks 6-8: transition to supportive footwear/brace, begin proprioceptive and peroneal strengthening.
- Weeks 8-12: progressive strengthening, agility introduction.
- Months 3-4 (isolated repair) to as early as months 2-3 (suture-tape augmented, per comparative RTS data): return to pivoting/cutting sport, criterion-based (strength/proprioceptive symmetry, sport-specific agility testing), with the suture-tape cohort in recent comparative studies achieving faster RTS at similar clinical outcome scores.

**Peroneal tendon repair:**
- Weeks 0-4: non-weight-bearing to protected WB in boot/cast (position often slight eversion/neutral to protect repair depending on procedure).
- Weeks 4-8: progressive WB, ROM, gentle strengthening.
- Weeks 8-12+: progressive peroneal strengthening, proprioception, gradual return to activity; sport-specific return typically 4-6 months.

**Distal biceps repair:**
- Weeks 0-2: splint/brace at ~90° flexion, protect repair; some protocols allow early protected motion within a hinged brace (extension-block) rather than full immobilization, depending on fixation strength (button constructs support earlier motion).
- Weeks 2-6: progressive active-assisted then active ROM, avoid resisted supination/flexion.
- Weeks 6-12: progressive resistance strengthening beginning with light isotonic work, advancing gradually.
- Months 3-4+: return to heavy lifting/manual labor and sport, guided by strength symmetry testing (~85-90% of contralateral); light desk work return often 2-4 weeks, heavy labor typically 4-6 months.

## Complications

- **Achilles rupture repair:** wound infection (open ~6% vs percutaneous/mini-open ~0.4%), sural nerve injury (higher with purely percutaneous technique), re-rupture (~2-3% operative, up to ~4% non-operative with modern functional rehab), deep vein thrombosis, adhesions/stiffness, sural neuroma.
- **Achilles tendinopathy surgery:** wound healing problems (especially insertional/Haglund resection), persistent pain (~10-25% partial responders even after surgery), sural nerve injury, re-rupture if FHL augmentation not used in extensive debridement.
- **Steroid injection into/around Achilles:** tendon rupture — the principal reason it is avoided; also subcutaneous atrophy, skin depigmentation, infection.
- **Epicondylitis surgery:** persistent/recurrent pain (~10-15%), lateral elbow instability if the lateral ulnar collateral ligament is inadvertently violated during aggressive ECRB debridement (a critical technical pitfall), infection, incomplete relief.
- **Proximal hamstring repair:** sciatic nerve irritation/neurapraxia (most common — often transient, occurs due to traction/retraction near the nerve), infection, heterotopic ossification, re-rupture, persistent posterior thigh/sitting discomfort, overall complication rate reported around 15% (major complications ~4-5%) in recent systematic review.
- **Broström-Gould / ankle stabilization:** superficial peroneal nerve injury/neuroma (most common), sural nerve injury (open lateral approaches), residual/recurrent instability (failure rate variably reported, generally low single digits to low teens depending on series and risk factors), stiffness, wound complications; suture-tape specific risks include hardware prominence/irritation and, rarely, tunnel-related fracture.
- **Peroneal tendon surgery:** persistent pain/stiffness, recurrent subluxation if SPR reconstruction insufficient, sural nerve injury, wound complications — pooled complication rates reported up to ~38% in older systematic review (predominantly minor, e.g., mild residual pain) versus near-zero complication series more recently with modern SPR-focused technique.
- **Distal biceps repair:** PIN neuropraxia (single-incision, usually transient, resolves over weeks-months in the large majority), lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury/numbness, heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis (higher with two-incision technique if posterior dissection is not meticulous), re-rupture (uncommon with modern fixation, low single-digit %), persistent strength deficit.
- **Chronic ankle instability overall:** untreated/undertreated concomitant intra-articular pathology (OCD, impingement) is a leading cause of "failed" ligament surgery — always assess and treat concurrently.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2025 (Arthroscopy)** — [Saggar et al., "Achilles Tendon Ruptures: Nonsurgical Versus Surgical Treatment"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40316367/): contemporary comparative analysis reaffirming that with modern functional rehabilitation, non-operative management approaches surgical outcomes for many patients, reinforcing individualized (not blanket) decision-making.
- **2025 (Annals of Medicine / meta-analysis)** — [Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs, operative vs non-operative acute Achilles rupture](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07853890.2025.2537349) (also PMC12624965): pooled RCT data confirm surgery lowers re-rupture risk (RR favoring surgery, minimally invasive RR ~0.28, open RR ~0.30) and shortens return-to-work (~19 days faster), while wound infection is markedly higher with open repair (6%) than minimally invasive (0.4%) — supports minimally invasive repair as the preferred surgical technique when operative treatment is chosen.
- **UKSTAR trial (Lancet)** — [Plaster cast versus functional brace for non-surgical treatment of Achilles tendon rupture](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(19)32942-3/fulltext): landmark multicentre RCT (n=527, 39 UK hospitals) establishing that functional bracing with early motion/weight-bearing is a valid, non-inferior alternative to plaster cast immobilization for non-operatively managed ruptures — the evidentiary basis for the "functional rehab" pathway referenced throughout this monograph.
- **2024 (JOSPT / AOPT Clinical Practice Guideline, 3rd revision)** — [Achilles Pain, Stiffness, and Muscle Power Deficits: Midportion Achilles Tendinopathy Revision – 2024](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2024.0302): updated CPG confirms high-load progressive tendon-loading exercise (encompassing eccentric, concentric, isometric, isotonic, and plyometric modalities) as first-line, superior to wait-and-see; reaffirms exercise as the cornerstone across the disease course including the return-to-plyometric phase.
- **2025 (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy — critical appraisal)** — [Appraisal of Clinical Practice Guideline: mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy revision – 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1836955325000530): independent appraisal of the 2024 AOPT CPG, useful for gauging guideline quality/certainty of individual recommendations when counseling patients on expected benefit.
- **2025 (Journal of Foot & Ankle Surgery / rESWT RCT)** — [Alsulaimani et al., shockwave therapy sham-controlled RCT for insertional Achilles tendinopathy](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02692155241295683): sham-controlled trial found radial shockwave did not clearly outperform sham at 12 weeks for insertional disease — tempers enthusiasm for rESWT as a reliably effective adjunct and should inform patient counseling on expected benefit magnitude.
- **2024/2025 (British Medical Bulletin)** — [High-volume injections in Achilles tendinopathy: a systematic review](https://academic.oup.com/bmb/article/152/1/35/7874883) (Hassan, Poku, Miah, Maffulli): HVI shown safe and beneficial versus placebo at 12-24 weeks and outperforming PRP at 12 weeks — supports HVI as a viable second-line adjunct for refractory mid-portion disease; PRP evidence remains insufficient to recommend as standard care.
- **2025 (PMC/meta-analysis)** — [Effect of Platelet-Rich Plasma Injection on the Treatment of Achilles Tendinopathy: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11603511/): confirms current insufficiency of evidence to support PRP as routine Achilles tendinopathy treatment — consistent with the "avoid over-promising PRP" counseling point above.
- **2025 (PMC/meta-analysis)** — [Platelet rich plasma versus corticosteroids for lateral epicondylitis: a meta-analysis of RCTs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11938920/): confirms the short-term (steroid favored, <2 months) vs long-term (PRP favored, ≥6 months) trade-off, directly supporting the "avoid repeated steroid, consider PRP for durable benefit" recommendation in this monograph.
- **2024 (AJSM)** — [Xu et al., "PRP Has Better Results for Long-term Functional Improvement and Pain Relief for Lateral Epicondylitis": Systematic Review and Meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03635465231213087): corroborating meta-analysis reinforcing durable PRP superiority over steroid at long-term follow-up.
- **2023 (British Journal of Sports Medicine, cited via PubMed)** — [Corticosteroid injections for lateral epicondylitis superior to PRP at 1 month but PRP more effective at 6 months](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37247780/): updated systematic review of level 1-2 studies quantifying the crossover point in relative efficacy — useful for setting patient expectations by injection choice and timeline.
- **2024 (KSSTA/ESSKA journal)** — [Liu et al., "Both arthroscopic one-step Broström-Gould and Lasso-loop stitch techniques achieved favourable clinical outcomes for chronic lateral ankle instability"](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.12167): confirms multiple all-arthroscopic anatomic repair techniques achieve comparable favorable outcomes, broadening acceptable technique options for arthroscopic stabilization.
- **2024 (Arthroscopy Techniques)** — [Guyonnet et al., "Arthroscopic Modified Broström Repair with Suture-Tape Augmentation of the CFL for Lateral Ankle Instability"](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.eats.2023.11.015): technique paper detailing arthroscopic augmentation of the CFL (not just ATFL) with suture-tape — relevant when CFL incompetence contributes to instability.
- **2026 (KSSTA/ESSKA journal)** — [Vermorel et al., "Suture tape augmentation in arthroscopic lateral ligament repair for chronic ankle instability yields similar clinical outcomes but faster return to sport compared to isolated repair"](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.70308): directly supports the monograph's recommendation that suture-tape augmentation's principal proven benefit is faster RTS at equivalent mid-term clinical scores, not necessarily lower failure rate — important for framing the augmentation decision with patients/athletes.
- **2024 (Journal of Foot and Ankle Surgery)** — [Modified Broström vs suture tape augmentation: a systematic review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1067251624002412): systematic-review-level synthesis further supporting comparable clinical outcomes between isolated repair and augmentation, with augmentation series trending toward faster sport return.
- **2025 (AJSM)** — [Lefèvre et al., "Outcomes of Revision Versus Primary Repair of Proximal Hamstring Avulsion Injury"](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465251359550): matched case-control data showing revision repair carries a distinct (generally inferior) outcome/complication profile versus primary repair — supports counseling patients toward optimized primary repair technique/timing to avoid revision scenarios.
- **2023 (systematic review, cited via PubMed)** — [Systematic Review of Complications Associated With Proximal Hamstring Tendon Repair](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37781641/): quantifies overall complication rate ~15.3% (major ~4.6%) after proximal hamstring repair — the source for the complication figures used in this monograph's Complications section.
- **2024 (PMC, review)** — [Management of Proximal Hamstring Injuries: Non-operative and Operative Treatment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11335994/): current narrative review consolidating indications for acute (within 4-6 weeks) versus delayed repair and non-operative candidacy criteria, consistent with recommendations above.
- **2025 (PMC/collegiate athlete cohort)** — [Application of the British Athletics Muscle Injury Classification in Collegiate Football Athletes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11951125/): validates BAMIC's applicability and prognostic value (RTP correlation) outside the elite European track/football population it was originally derived in, broadening its evidence base to US collegiate sport.
- **2022→2025 update trajectory (Science & Medicine in Football)** — [The BAMIC grading system as a predictor of return to play following hamstring injury in professional football players](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36148699/): establishes the grade-RTP correlation and the disproportionate impact of intramuscular tendon ("c") involvement referenced in the Classification and Quick Reference sections.
- **2025 (SAGE/BJSM affiliated)** — [Comparing single-incision and double-incision techniques in distal biceps tendon repair: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17585732251399838): current-generation comparative meta-analysis reassessing the single- vs double-incision complication/functional trade-off with modern technique series, informing the nuanced (no longer simply "double-incision is safer") counseling in this monograph.
