## Quick Reference

**Compartment syndrome red flags (any tibia fracture, any age, any energy):** pain out of proportion / with passive stretch of toes, a tense swollen leg, paresthesia in the first web space or sole — **do not wait for pulselessness or paralysis**, these are late/unreliable signs. Clinical exam sensitivity is only 13–54%; if in doubt, measure **intracompartmental pressure (ICP)** — Whitesides/Δp = diastolic BP − ICP **< 30 mmHg** is the accepted threshold for emergent fasciotomy (McQueen criterion), used especially in obtunded/intubated or equivocal patients. Reported incidence in tibial diaphyseal fractures is **2–9%**; high-energy mechanism, younger male patients, and polytrauma are the strongest risk factors. **Fasciotomy is a clinical diagnosis first, pressure-confirmed second — never delay for imaging.** UK practice is governed by the **BOAST** (British Orthopaedic Association Standards for Trauma) guidance on compartment syndrome and on open fractures, which underpins much of the timing language used across this monograph; the parallel UK reference for complex/open lower-limb fracture pathways is **NICE NG37/NG38**.

**Soft-tissue timing rule of thumb (plateau and pilon alike):** wait for the **wrinkle sign** (skin folds return over the fracture, fracture blisters re-epithelialize, edema subsides) — typically **7–21 days**, occasionally longer — before definitive open fixation through traumatized soft tissue. Span first, fix later.

**Open fracture antibiotics:** first dose **within 60–75 minutes** of injury (EAST/OTA); cefazolin (or equivalent gram-positive coverage) for all grades; add an aminoglycoside (gram-negative coverage) for Gustilo **III**; add penicillin for farmyard/fecal contamination. Continue **24 h** after each debridement for I–II, **72 h** after injury or ≤24 h after definitive soft-tissue coverage for III (not to exceed ~72 h total). Target soft-tissue coverage **within 5–10 days**.

### Schatzker classification (tibial plateau) — treatment implication

| Type | Description | Typical mechanism | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Lateral split (wedge), no depression | Low-energy, younger bone | Percutaneous screws if displacement <2 mm and knee stable; ORIF if displaced |
| II | Lateral split + depression | Varus/valgus + axial load | Elevate depressed fragment (bone graft/substitute void fill) + buttress plate |
| III | Pure lateral depression, no split | Axial load, often elderly osteoporotic bone | Elevation via cortical window + subchondral screws or plate; may tolerate more nonoperative range if minimally displaced and stable |
| IV | Medial plateau fracture (split ± depression) | High-energy varus, often associated with knee dislocation | High-energy injury — mandatory neurovascular exam, medial buttress plate, evaluate for concomitant ligament/vascular injury |
| V | Bicondylar without metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation | High-energy | Dual plating or single lateral locking plate depending on medial comminution pattern; consider staged approach |
| VI | Bicondylar with metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation | High-energy, often open/severe soft-tissue injury | Highest soft-tissue risk — spanning ex-fix first, staged ORIF, highest compartment syndrome risk of the group |

### Luo three-column classification — treatment implication

| Column | Definition (axial CT, apex of tibial spine as center) | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral column | Anterolateral quadrant fracture line | Lateral locking plate typically sufficient alone |
| Medial column | Posteromedial/anteromedial quadrant | Requires independent medial-side fixation (buttress) — a lateral plate alone will not control this column |
| Posterior column | Posterior quadrant, often posteromedial or posterolateral shear fragment | Frequently missed on AP/lateral radiographs and standard anterior approaches; needs posteromedial or posterolateral approach ± separate plate — the classic driver of Schatzker/AO under-recognition of posterior shear fragments |
| Three-column (any 2+ columns) | Combined injury | Dictates dual or triple plating strategy and choice of approach combination (e.g., anterolateral + posteromedial) — CT-based, not reliably assessed on plain films |

### AO/OTA 41/42/43 — bone segment overview

| Segment | Bone/location | A (extra-articular) | B (partial articular) | C (complete articular) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 41 | Proximal tibia/fibula (plateau) | Metaphyseal, extra-articular fracture | Split, depression, or split-depression of one condyle (≈ Schatzker I–III) | Bicondylar articular fracture (≈ Schatzker IV–VI); C3 = extensive articular comminution — worst prognosis |
| 42 | Tibial/fibular shaft | Simple fracture (spiral/oblique/transverse) — best union prognosis | Wedge fracture (fragment, intact contact after reduction possible) | Complex/comminuted fracture (segmental or irregular) — highest nonunion and malunion risk, most demanding for nailing technique |
| 43 | Distal tibia (pilon) | Extra-articular metaphyseal fracture | Partial articular — a portion of joint remains attached to shaft (fracture-dislocation pattern possible) | Complete articular — joint surface completely dissociated from shaft; C3 = articular + metaphyseal comminution — highest complication rate, most likely to need staged protocol |

Each letter subdivides further (e.g., A1–A3, B1–B3, C1–C3) by comminution/fragment number — used for research stratification and prognostication; in daily practice A/B/C plus a plain-language fracture description (comminution, displacement, articular step) drives the operative plan.

### Rüedi-Allgöwer classification (pilon fracture)

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Articular fracture, no significant displacement | Nonoperative or percutaneous screw fixation if stable and truly non-displaced (rare in high-energy pilons) |
| II | Articular fracture with displacement, minimal comminution | ORIF with anatomic joint reconstruction, generally single-stage if soft tissue allows |
| III | Articular fracture with significant comminution and displacement of metaphysis | Highest soft-tissue risk — staged protocol (spanning ex-fix → delayed definitive ORIF) essentially mandatory |

Rüedi-Allgöwer predates the AO/OTA 43-C system and correlates roughly with 43-B/C but does not separately encode metaphyseal comminution the way AO/OTA C3 does — most current literature reports both.

### Treatment ladder — quick table

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Definitive surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tibial plateau | Cast/brace + non-weight-bearing only for truly non-displaced/stable Schatzker I/III in low-demand patients | Hinged knee brace, protected weight-bearing, staged plan | ORIF (plating ± bone graft/void filler), rarely primary circular frame for severe soft-tissue injury or elderly comminuted patterns |
| Tibial shaft | Nonoperative (long-leg then functional/PTB brace) only for truly stable, minimally displaced, low-energy closed fractures meeting Sarmiento angulation/shortening criteria | — | Reamed intramedullary nail (suprapatellar semi-extended preferred) for most closed and Gustilo I–IIIA open shaft fractures; plating for peri-articular extension; circular/hybrid frame for severe segmental bone loss or contamination |
| Pilon | Nonoperative essentially never indicated for displaced pilon (reserved for true nondisplaced low-energy Rüedi I in medically unfit patients) | Spanning external fixation (temporizing, all high-energy patterns) | Staged ORIF once soft-tissue envelope permits, or definitive circular frame/minimally invasive plating for severe comminution/soft-tissue compromise |

**Key numbers to remember:** acceptable tibial shaft nonoperative alignment <5° varus/valgus, <10° anterior/posterior angulation, <1 cm shortening, <10° rotation; tibial plateau articular step-off/gap tolerance generally ≤2 mm for younger active patients (up to 3 mm sometimes accepted in low-demand elderly); pilon Rüedi III union takes months longer than II; time to fasciotomy from ACS onset should be **<6 h** to minimize irreversible muscle/nerve injury (function of ischemia time, not just diagnosis time).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Tibial plateau

The proximal articular surface of the tibia is subdivided into medial and lateral condyles separated by the intercondylar eminence bearing the cruciate ligament origins.

- The **medial condyle** is larger, concave, and has denser subchondral bone — more resistant to fracture.
- The **lateral condyle** is smaller, convex, and more cancellous — fractures more readily, which is why isolated lateral plateau fractures (Schatzker I–III) are far more common than isolated medial fractures.
- The medial condyle is roughly **2.5× as strong** as the lateral. A medial plateau fracture (Schatzker IV) therefore implies a much higher-energy varus mechanism and should raise suspicion for a **knee dislocation** with reduced/spontaneously relocated components, associated **popliteal artery** and **peroneal nerve** injury, and multiligamentous instability.
- The **popliteal neurovascular bundle** lies directly posterior, tethered proximally and distally (at the soleus arch) — vulnerable to traction injury with posterior fracture-dislocation patterns.
- The **anterior compartment musculature** and the **proximal tibiofibular joint/peroneal nerve** are at risk with lateral and posterolateral fracture patterns and during posterolateral surgical approaches.

### Tibial shaft

- The tibia is **subcutaneous along its anteromedial border** for its entire length — the primary reason for the historically high rate of open fractures and the enduring vigilance for soft-tissue compromise even in closed injuries.
- Comparatively **poor blood supply** versus other long bones: a single nutrient artery (branch of the posterior tibial artery, entering posterolaterally near the junction of the proximal and middle thirds) supplies the endosteal circulation, with periosteal supply from the surrounding muscular envelope (posterior tibialis, soleus, extensor/peroneal groups).
- **Reaming** disrupts endosteal blood supply transiently (restored by ~6 weeks via periosteal hypertrophy) but improves mechanical stability and healing rate for closed fractures — the biology-versus-biomechanics tradeoff underlying the reamed-vs-unreamed debate.
- The four **osteofascial compartments** (anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, deep posterior) are bounded by unyielding fascia — the anatomic basis for compartment syndrome.

### Distal tibial plafond (pilon)

- The horizontal weight-bearing articular surface of the distal tibia. Unlike the ankle fracture pattern (rotational, low-energy, malleolar), pilon fractures result from **axial load** — talus driven into the plafond by a fall from height, motor vehicle collision, or high-speed impact.
- Produces central articular impaction, comminution, and — critically — severe injury to the thin, poorly vascularized **anterior soft-tissue envelope** of the distal tibia (minimal muscle coverage, watershed skin perfusion).
- This soft-tissue fragility, not the bone injury itself, is the rate-limiting factor for surgical timing and the historic driver of wound complication rates as high as **25–40%** with immediate ORIF of high-energy patterns — the entire rationale for staged management.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History/mechanism:** distinguish low-energy (fall from standing, elderly, osteoporotic, often Schatzker I/III plateau, simple shaft spiral) from high-energy (MVC, fall from height, motorcycle — bicondylar plateau, comminuted shaft, pilon) — energy dictates soft-tissue risk and staging strategy far more than the fracture pattern alone.

**Examination:**
- Full skin survey for open wounds/fracture blisters — tense clear vs hemorrhagic; hemorrhagic blisters imply deeper dermal injury and predict worse wound complication risk.
- Compartment palpation and serial neurovascular checks, **every 1–2 h** in the at-risk patient (polytrauma, high-energy, prolonged limb ischemia, anticoagulated).
- Distal pulses with **ankle-brachial index (ABI)** if any asymmetry or knee dislocation is suspected — ABI <0.9 mandates CT angiography.
- Thorough knee ligamentous exam once the fracture is provisionally stabilized; a delayed exam under anesthesia at time of definitive surgery is often more reliable than the acute exam.

**Imaging:**
- Plain radiographs (AP/lateral/oblique) for initial assessment of all three injury types; **traction radiographs** for comminuted plateau/pilon fractures markedly improve fragment definition before CT.
- **CT with 2–3 mm cuts and coronal/sagittal reconstruction is mandatory** for surgical planning in displaced plateau and pilon fractures — this is what enables Luo column mapping and articular fragment counting; obtain after spanning ex-fix (not before) in high-energy staged cases so the ligamentotaxis reduction improves fragment visualization and reduces artifact from gross displacement.
- MRI selectively for plateau fractures with suspected meniscal/ligamentous injury influencing surgical plan (not routine, adds little to fixation decision-making in most bicondylar patterns).
- CT angiography for any pulse asymmetry, expanding hematoma, or knee dislocation-associated plateau pattern (Schatzker IV in particular).
- Serial exams and repeat imaging as the soft-tissue envelope evolves during the staged interval.

## Classification

*(See Quick Reference above for the fully spelled-out tables — Schatzker, Luo three-column, AO/OTA 41/42/43, and Rüedi-Allgöwer are enumerated there with per-type management implications, as required. This section adds classification context and caveats.)*

**Schatzker**
- Remains the most widely taught and communicated system in North American practice for tibial plateau fractures.
- Major limitation: derived from plain radiographs and under-represents posterior column/posteromedial shear fragments that are only reliably seen on CT — hence the rise of CT-based systems.

**Luo three-column classification** (Luo et al., 2010)
- Directly addresses the Schatzker gap by mapping fracture lines on an axial CT slice at the level of the tibial spines onto lateral, medial, and posterior columns.
- Now the dominant classification for **surgical approach planning** in complex bicondylar fractures because it directly predicts which combination of approaches (anterolateral, posteromedial, posterolateral) and how many plates are needed.
- A posterior column fragment identified only on CT and missed on the Schatzker read is the single most common cause of failed lateral-only fixation of a "Schatzker V/VI" fracture.

**AO/OTA**
- Provides the universal alphanumeric coding used in registries, billing, and international communication (41 = proximal tibia/fibula, 42 = tibial/fibular shaft, 43 = distal tibia).
- The A/B/C severity tiers and further 1–3 subgroups correlate broadly with increasing comminution and worsening prognosis across all three injury zones.
- The system to default to when writing for a European/AO-trained audience or entering registry data — the natural common language between Romanian/European and US-trained colleagues.

**Rüedi-Allgöwer** (1969)
- Pilon-specific and simple (three types by displacement/comminution) — useful for rapid communication and rough prognosis.
- Does not capture metaphyseal comminution independently of articular displacement the way AO/OTA 43-C3 does, so most contemporary pilon literature reports both systems together.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Tibial plateau**
- Reserved for truly non-displaced or minimally displaced (<2–3 mm step-off/gap), stable-through-full-range fractures — classically Schatzker I or III patterns in low-demand or medically unfit patients.
- Confirm stability by exam under fluoroscopy (varus/valgus stress at 0° and 20–30° flexion) showing no gross instability before committing to nonoperative treatment.
- Protocol: hinged knee brace, protected/touch-down weight-bearing 6–8 weeks, early gentle ROM to prevent arthrofibrosis, serial radiographs at 1–2 week intervals in the first month to detect secondary displacement (occurs in a meaningful minority and should trigger conversion to surgery).

**Tibial shaft**
- Sarmiento functional bracing remains a defensible option for low-energy, minimally displaced/comminuted closed fractures meeting alignment criteria (see Quick Reference numbers) — historic union rates >90% but with slower time-to-union and higher malunion/shortening risk than nailing.
- In current practice this is largely reserved for patients declining surgery, prohibitive anesthetic risk, or resource-limited settings, as IM nailing has become the default even for many closed, minimally displaced patterns given faster return to function.

**Pilon**
- Essentially never first-line for displaced high-energy pilon fractures.
- A true Rüedi I non-displaced fracture in a medically unfit or very low-demand patient is the only reasonable candidate, managed in a well-molded splint/cast with strict non-weight-bearing and close radiographic follow-up for displacement.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

Not a primary treatment modality for any of these three acute fracture diagnoses. Relevant adjuncts are procedural rather than pharmacologic.

- **Bone graft/bone-void-filler augmentation** (autograft, allograft, calcium-phosphate/calcium-sulfate synthetic substitutes) for the metaphyseal void left after elevation of a depressed plateau or pilon articular fragment. Synthetic substitutes have largely replaced iliac crest autograft for straightforward voids given equivalent subsidence outcomes and no donor-site morbidity, with autograft reserved for larger defects or compromised bone quality.
- **Local antibiotic delivery** (antibiotic-impregnated cement beads or absorbable calcium sulfate) is commonly used adjunctively in contaminated open fractures and at staged ex-fix pin sites/definitive fixation in patients with heavy contamination — this is infection-prophylaxis, not a fracture-healing injection.
- **Intra-articular corticosteroid or viscosupplementation injections** have no role in the acute setting and are only relevant later for post-traumatic osteoarthritis management, which is outside this monograph's acute-fracture scope.

### Operative

**Tibial plateau — staged vs. acute, and approach selection**

Indications for surgery: any displaced articular step-off/gap beyond the accepted threshold (≈2 mm active/young, more permissive in low-demand elderly), condylar widening, coronal instability on stress exam, or any Schatzker IV–VI/bicondylar pattern.

- High-energy bicondylar fractures with significant soft-tissue swelling, fracture blisters, or an open wound mandate **staged management**: spanning knee-bridging external fixator (pins in femur and tibial diaphysis, crossing the injured joint, restoring length/alignment via ligamentotaxis) applied urgently, followed by serial soft-tissue assessment and definitive CT.
- **Definitive ORIF is deferred until the wrinkle sign returns** (typically 7–21 days). A 2024 dual-center international comparative study found acute definitive fixation feasible in carefully selected bicondylar fractures with favorable soft tissue, reinforcing that staging should be soft-tissue-driven rather than reflexive for every bicondylar pattern — the decision is individualized, not fracture-pattern-mandated.
- **Approach/implant selection follows the Luo column analysis:** an isolated lateral column injury is addressed through an anterolateral approach with a lateral locking plate; a medial column component requires a separate posteromedial approach and buttress plate (a single lateral plate cannot resist varus collapse of an unaddressed medial column); a posterior column shear fragment requires a posteromedial or posterolateral approach (occasionally a direct posterior approach with the patient prone) — missing this fragment is the classic technical error leading to loss of reduction.
- **Posterolateral fragments:** exposure options include the anterolateral approach with capsular elevation, a posterolateral approach, or **fibular head osteotomy/supra-fibular-head approaches** — an area of continued technique refinement. 2024–2025 series describe modified partial fibular head osteotomy preserving the biceps femoris attachment, and supra-fibular-head anatomic plating, both aimed at improving posterolateral fragment access while limiting peroneal nerve and lateral collateral ligament morbidity.
- **Technical pearls:** use large pointed reduction clamps and provisional K-wires before definitive plating; restore the joint line first (subchondral raft screws just beneath the articular surface), then buttress the metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation; bone graft/void-filler the metaphyseal defect after elevation to prevent subsidence; protect the peroneal nerve with any lateral/posterolateral dissection.
- In bicondylar fractures with severe medial comminution or compromised soft tissue, a **circular (Ilizarov/hexapod) frame** is a reasonable alternative to open plating, particularly in elderly patients with fragile skin or when infection risk is prohibitive.

**Tibial shaft — nailing technique and approach**

Reamed intramedullary nailing is the standard of care for the great majority of closed and Gustilo I–IIIA open tibial shaft fractures.

- Union rates >90%; reaming (vs. unreamed) reduces nonunion risk in closed fractures (number needed to treat roughly 5–14 to prevent one nonunion across pooled trial data including SPRINT) without a clinically meaningful difference for open fractures.
- The **suprapatellar semi-extended approach** has become the preferred technique over the traditional infrapatellar approach for most proximal- and mid-shaft fractures: it allows nailing with the knee in 10–20° of flexion (versus the forced hyperflexion of infrapatellar technique), which corrects the deforming apex-anterior/valgus tendency seen in proximal-third fractures and improves fluoroscopic visualization and starting-point accuracy.
- Multiple 2023–2025 systematic reviews and meta-analyses show suprapatellar nailing yields shorter operative and fluoroscopy time, lower anterior knee pain scores, better Lysholm/Kujala functional scores, and — notably — a **lower rate of acute compartment syndrome and fasciotomy** compared with infrapatellar nailing, in addition to at least equivalent union rates.
- Infrapatellar nailing remains a reasonable choice for very distal or straightforward mid-shaft fractures in surgeons with greater comfort in that position, and avoids violating the suprapatellar pouch/patellofemoral joint (a theoretical concern for later patellofemoral chondral wear that has not translated into a clear clinical signal in current follow-up data).
- **Technical pearls:** obtain an accurate starting point (lateral to the tibial spine on AP, at the anterior articular margin on lateral, or use the semi-extended midline approach and confirm on both views before reaming); use blocking (Poller) screws for proximal- and distal-third fractures to control the reduction and prevent the nail from translating into a wide metaphyseal canal; restore length/rotation/alignment before final locking (clinical rotational check against the contralateral limb, fluoroscopic cortical diameter matching).
- Consider a **submuscular/bridge plate** instead of a nail for fractures with significant articular extension into the plateau or plafond, or for very distal/proximal metaphyseal fractures where nail fixation provides inadequate purchase.
- **Open fractures (Gustilo I–IIIC):** thorough irrigation and debridement (formal debridement can reasonably occur within a broader time window than the historic "6-hour rule," which current evidence no longer strictly supports as a hard cutoff, though earlier debridement remains preferred when logistically feasible), early appropriate antibiotics (see Quick Reference), and a staged plan for severe (IIIB/IIIC) injuries: initial debridement + temporary spanning external fixation or provisional nail, staged soft-tissue coverage (local or free flap) within roughly 5–10 days in coordination with plastic surgery, and definitive fixation timed to coverage.
- Recent 2025 retrospective data suggest that placing a definitive intramedullary nail prior to flap coverage in Gustilo IIIB/IIIC fractures may not meaningfully increase complication rates compared with staged nailing after coverage — an evolving area where single-center data is outpacing consensus guidance.
- Segmental bone loss or heavy contamination favors a **circular/hybrid external fixator** (with subsequent bone transport or Masquelet induced-membrane technique for defect reconstruction) over primary nailing.

**Pilon — staged protocol**

The staged protocol (Sirkin et al., 1999) remains the accepted standard for high-energy, comminuted (Rüedi III / AO 43-C) pilon fractures.

- **Stage 1 (urgent):** spanning external fixation (calcaneal and proximal tibial pins, crossing the ankle, restoring length/alignment/rough reduction via ligamentotaxis); fixation of the fibula only if it does not compromise later soft-tissue planning (some surgeons defer fibular fixation to the definitive stage to avoid a second incision through swollen tissue); elevation and ice; serial soft-tissue monitoring.
- **Stage 2 (definitive):** ORIF once the wrinkle sign returns (typically 10–21 days, occasionally 3+ weeks for the most severe injuries), through incisions planned to preserve a skin bridge of at least 7 cm from any fibular incision, with anatomic articular reduction (frequently requiring a direct anterior or anteromedial/anterolateral approach, sometimes combined posterolateral for posterior malleolar fragments), bone graft/void-filler for metaphyseal defects, and fixation with anatomically contoured locking plates positioned to minimize additional soft-tissue stripping (minimally invasive plate techniques favored where the pattern allows).
- For the most severe soft-tissue injuries or medically fragile patients, definitive **circular external fixation (Ilizarov/hexapod frame)** with limited internal fixation of the articular block is an alternative to plating that substantially reduces wound complication risk at the cost of pin-site care burden and frame time.
- Randomized and network meta-analysis data through 2025 continue to support staged management as reducing wound complications and deep infection versus early single-stage ORIF in high-energy patterns.
- A 2024–2025 RCT comparing two-stage ORIF against single-stage external fixation as a *definitive* strategy (not just fibular fixation) suggests frame-only definitive treatment is a viable alternative in select comminuted patterns.
- The national ENFORCE audit (UK, 2024) documents wide real-world variation in how strictly the classic staged protocol is followed, underscoring that this remains a judgment-driven pathway rather than a rigid algorithm.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Tibial plateau (post-ORIF, standard bicondylar pattern):**
- **0–2 weeks:** hinged knee brace locked or limited per fixation stability; strict non-weight-bearing; early passive/active-assisted ROM starting within the first 3–7 days if fixation is stable (protect against stiffness — arthrofibrosis is a major long-term morbidity); ankle pumps, quad sets, straight-leg raises; cryotherapy/elevation for swelling control.
- **2–6 weeks:** progress ROM toward full flexion as tolerated and tissue allows; continue non-weight-bearing to touch-down weight-bearing depending on fixation construct and fracture stability at surgeon discretion; wound check and suture/staple removal at 2 weeks.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive partial weight-bearing beginning around 6–8 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing, advancing to full weight-bearing by ~10–12 weeks for most stable constructs (bicondylar comminuted patterns or elderly osteoporotic bone may need to wait longer, closer to 12 weeks, before full weight-bearing); closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, gait training.
- **3–6 months:** progressive strengthening, proprioception/balance work, return to low-impact activity; **return to sedentary/light-duty work** typically feasible around 8–12 weeks, **return to manual labor** generally 4–6 months depending on physical demands.
- **6–12+ months:** return to impact/pivoting sport individualized to fracture severity, articular congruity achieved, and residual laxity; a 2025 RCT (International Orthopaedics) found immediate weight-bearing after stable internal fixation yielded better clinical outcomes with similar radiographic outcomes compared with delayed weight-bearing in selected patients — an evolving area suggesting some current protocols may be more conservative than necessary in well-fixed constructs, though this should be individualized rather than applied blanket-fashion to comminuted or elderly osteoporotic patterns.

**Tibial shaft (post-nailing):**
- **0–2 weeks:** weight-bearing as tolerated is standard for most statically locked nails in simple-to-moderately comminuted patterns (this is a major advantage of nailing over plating/bracing); ankle and knee ROM immediately; wound checks.
- **2–6 weeks:** progressive weight-bearing to full as tolerated by pain and radiographic callus; gait training, address any knee pain from suprapatellar/infrapatellar approach with targeted quadriceps/patellofemoral rehab.
- **6–12 weeks:** most patients achieve radiographic union in this window for simple fracture patterns (AO/OTA 42-A); complex/comminuted (42-C) patterns and open fractures take longer, often 4–6+ months.
- **Return to work:** desk work 2–4 weeks, manual labor typically 3–6 months pending union.
- **Return to sport:** running/pivoting sport generally deferred until solid radiographic union (bridging callus on 3+ cortices) and pain-free single-leg hop testing, commonly 4–6 months, longer for open/complex fractures.

**Pilon (post-definitive fixation):**
- **0–2 weeks:** strict non-weight-bearing, splint/boot immobilization, elevation, wound surveillance is paramount given historically high wound complication rates in this fracture group.
- **2–6 weeks:** early protected ROM of the ankle once wound healing is secure (typically starting 2–3 weeks), continued non-weight-bearing.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive partial weight-bearing beginning around 8–12 weeks depending on comminution and radiographic union — pilon fractures generally require a longer period of protected weight-bearing than plateau or shaft fractures given the articular comminution and metaphyseal healing demands.
- **3–6 months:** progression to full weight-bearing, ankle strengthening, gait normalization, edema management (chronic swelling is common and may persist a year or more).
- **Return to work:** sedentary 6–10 weeks, manual/standing labor 4–8 months.
- **Return to sport/impact activity:** highly individualized, often 6–12 months, and a meaningful proportion of high-energy Rüedi III/AO 43-C3 patients will not return to pre-injury impact activity due to post-traumatic osteoarthritis.

## Complications

- **Acute compartment syndrome** — the must-not-miss complication across all three diagnoses (see Quick Reference); most feared with tibial shaft fractures but well described after high-energy plateau (especially Schatzker VI) and pilon fractures, and after prolonged ligamentotaxis/traction. Delayed fasciotomy beyond approximately 6 hours of ischemia substantially worsens the likelihood of permanent muscle/nerve damage, contracture, and limb dysfunction.
- **Deep surgical site infection / osteomyelitis** — historically the dominant complication of early ORIF in high-energy plateau and pilon fractures through compromised soft tissue (reported wound complication rates up to 25–40% with immediate ORIF in the pre-staged-protocol era); the staged protocol substantially mitigates but does not eliminate this risk, and open fractures carry additional risk proportional to Gustilo grade.
- **Nonunion / delayed union** — more common with comminuted (AO/OTA C-type) shaft fractures, open fractures, unreamed nailing, and smoking; addressed with exchange reamed nailing, dynamization, plating, or bone grafting/Masquelet technique for segmental defects.
- **Malunion** — particularly a risk with proximal- and distal-third shaft fractures nailed via infrapatellar approach (apex-anterior/valgus deformity) — one reason suprapatellar semi-extended technique has gained favor; also a risk with inadequately buttressed medial or posterior columns in plateau fixation.
- **Post-traumatic osteoarthritis** — the dominant long-term morbidity of articular fractures (plateau and pilon); risk scales with initial articular step-off/gap, comminution grade, and quality of reduction; pilon fractures in particular carry substantial rates of radiographic OA by 5–10 years even with technically sound fixation, given the severity of initial cartilage injury (chondrocyte death from impact energy, not just malreduction).
- **Knee/ankle stiffness and arthrofibrosis** — particularly after prolonged immobilization or staged protocols with delayed ROM initiation; mitigated by early protected motion once fixation/soft tissue allow.
- **Neurovascular injury** — peroneal nerve (lateral/posterolateral plateau approaches, fibular head osteotomy, proximal fibula fractures), popliteal artery (Schatzker IV and knee dislocation-associated patterns), and traction/pin-related injury from external fixation.
- **Hardware-related complications** — symptomatic hardware requiring removal (common at the subcutaneous distal tibia/pilon and proximal tibia), peri-implant fracture, and infected nonunion requiring staged implant removal and reconstruction.
- **Anterior knee pain** — a recognized morbidity of both infrapatellar and suprapatellar tibial nailing approaches; generally lower with suprapatellar technique per pooled 2023–2025 meta-analytic VAS/Lysholm/Kujala data, though not eliminated.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2025 AAOS Updated Clinical Practice Guideline for Acute Compartment Syndrome** — updated AAOS guidance reinforcing that diagnosis is primarily clinical, with ICP measurement (Δp <30 mmHg) reserved for equivocal or unreliable exams (obtunded, regional block, pediatric); reaffirms fasciotomy without delay when clinical suspicion is high. [Guideline Central summary](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/9783/)
- **Acute compartment syndrome in tibial fractures: a meta-analysis (2025, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders)** — pools contemporary risk-factor data confirming young male sex, high-energy mechanism, and polytrauma as the strongest predictors, and quantifies clinical exam sensitivity (13–54%) versus ICP monitoring (sensitivity 94%, specificity 98%), supporting a low threshold for pressure monitoring in high-risk or unreliable patients. [BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12891-025-08586-z)
- **Acute versus staged fixation of bicondylar tibial plateau fractures: dual-centre international study (2024)** — challenges reflexive staging for every bicondylar pattern, showing acute definitive fixation is feasible and safe in carefully selected patients with favorable soft-tissue envelopes, reinforcing that staging decisions should be soft-tissue-driven rather than pattern-mandated. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38409547/)
- **Immediate weight-bearing after tibial plateau internal fixation: RCT (2025, International Orthopaedics)** — 106-patient RCT (Assiut University) found immediate post-operative weight-bearing produced better clinical outcomes with similar radiographic outcomes versus delayed weight-bearing in stable fixation constructs, suggesting some traditional delayed-weight-bearing protocols may be overly conservative for well-fixed patterns. [Springer/International Orthopaedics](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00264-025-06443-1)
- **Cochrane systematic review: surgical fixation methods for tibial plateau fractures (August 2024)** — updated Cochrane evidence synthesis on comparative surgical fixation strategies for tibial plateau fractures; underscores continued low-to-moderate certainty evidence across most head-to-head implant comparisons and the need for further high-quality RCTs. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39171616/)
- **Current concepts in tibial plateau fracture management — Spanish Orthopaedic Trauma Association review (2025)** — contemporary European (SETO) narrative review covering staged treatment strategy, CT-based (Luo-style) surgical planning, and weight-bearing progression, reflecting current European consensus alongside AO teaching. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12045298/)
- **Fibular head osteotomy for posterolateral tibial plateau exposure — refined technique (2025, Journal of Orthopaedics and Traumatology)** — describes a modified partial fibular head osteotomy preserving the biceps femoris attachment for improved posterolateral column exposure while limiting lateral collateral ligament and peroneal nerve morbidity, part of a broader 2024–2025 wave of posterolateral approach refinement (supra-fibular-head anatomic plating, articular-line approaches). [Springer/J Orthop Traumatol](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10195-025-00836-z)
- **Suprapatellar vs. infrapatellar intramedullary nailing — systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs (2024, European Journal of Trauma and Emergency Surgery; updated pooled analyses through 2025)** — confirms suprapatellar semi-extended nailing yields shorter operative/fluoroscopy time, lower VAS pain, better Lysholm/Kujala knee scores, and a lower rate of acute compartment syndrome/fasciotomy versus infrapatellar nailing, cementing suprapatellar as the preferred default approach for most tibial shaft fractures. [Springer/EJTES](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00068-023-02384-9); confirmatory 2025 RCT-level meta-analysis: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12487272/)
- **Intramedullary nailing for tibial shaft fractures in adults — updated evidence synthesis (2024, PMC/Cochrane-linked review)** — reaffirms reamed nailing lowers nonunion risk versus unreamed nailing in closed fractures (NNT ≈5–14) with no clinically meaningful difference in open fractures, consistent with SPRINT-era conclusions but updated with more recent trial data. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11663502/)
- **The acute management of Pilon fractures — ENFORCE national audit (2024, UK, 656 patients/27 centres)** — first large-scale contemporary audit of real-world adherence to staged soft-tissue damage-control protocols for pilon fractures, revealing substantial practice variation across UK trauma centres and highlighting gaps between published staged-protocol guidance and everyday practice. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39670543/)
- **Two-stage ORIF vs. single-stage definitive external fixation for complex pilon fractures — RCT (2024–2025)** — randomized comparison at a Level I trauma centre suggesting definitive circular/external fixation is a viable alternative to staged ORIF in select comminuted pilon patterns, broadening the definitive-treatment menu beyond plating once soft tissue is deemed unfavorable for open surgery. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12881023/)
- **Clinical outcomes and safety of surgical strategies for pilon fractures — network meta-analysis (2025)** — pooled comparison of staged ORIF, primary ORIF, and circular fixation strategies across operative time, blood loss, healing time, functional recovery, and complications, reinforcing staged management as the outcome-favoring default for high-energy comminuted patterns. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12622717/)
- **Intramedullary nail prior to flap coverage in Gustilo IIIB/IIIC open tibial fractures (2025, retrospective)** — challenges the traditional sequencing dogma of coverage-before-definitive-fixation, finding definitive nailing before flap coverage did not clearly increase complications in this small retrospective cohort — hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing, but relevant to staged open-fracture planning discussions with plastic surgery colleagues. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12616495/)
- **Current strategies in the prevention and management of infection in open fractures (2025, review)** — consolidated contemporary review of antibiotic timing/duration/spectrum and soft-tissue coverage windows for open long-bone (including tibial) fractures, broadly consistent with EAST practice management guideline positions (first dose within ~1 hour, gram-negative coverage added for type III, coverage targeted within 5–10 days). [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12165458/)
