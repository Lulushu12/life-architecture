## Quick Reference

**Red flags — do not miss:**
- Anatomic snuffbox/scaphoid tubercle tenderness after fall on outstretched hand with **normal initial radiographs** → treat as occult scaphoid fracture until proven otherwise (immobilize + re-image/MRI); missed proximal-pole fractures progress to AVN and nonunion silently.
- **Wrist "sprain" with persistent pain and swelling after high-energy trauma** → rule out perilunate dislocation; up to 25% are missed on the initial ED film (look for disrupted Gilula's arcs, "spilled teacup" lunate, capitate overlap).
- **DRUJ/ulnar-sided pain with ulnar-positive variance** in an athlete/laborer → chronic ulnar impaction; do not dismiss as "TFCC sprain" without variance measurement.
- Any carpal injury with a **SL gap on clenched-fist AP** or **DISI on lateral (scapholunate angle >70°)** → dissociative instability, refer for stress views/arthroscopy even if plain films are otherwise "normal."

**Key numbers:**

| Parameter | Normal / Threshold | Significance |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Scaphoid vascularity.** The scaphoid receives its dominant supply from the **dorsal carpal branch of the radial artery**, entering dorsally at the distal pole/waist ridge and running **retrograde** to supply 70–80% of the bone including the entire proximal pole. A separate volar branch (superficial palmar arch) supplies only the distal 20–30% (tubercle). This retrograde, watershed pattern explains why **proximal-pole fractures carry the highest nonunion and avascular necrosis (AVN) risk** — a fracture there can devascularize the entire proximal fragment. There is essentially no periosteal blood supply over 80% of the scaphoid surface (covered in cartilage), making revascularization from surrounding tissue impossible without a dedicated vascular pedicle — the rationale for vascularized grafting in AVN nonunion.

**Carpal ligaments and the "intercalated segment."** The scaphoid links the proximal and distal carpal rows; the **scapholunate interosseous ligament (SLIL)** — dorsal (thickest, primary restraint to SL diastasis), proximal (membranous), and volar components — and the **lunotriquetral interosseous ligament (LTIL)** (opposite pattern: volar component strongest) are the primary intrinsic stabilizers. Extrinsic (capsular) ligaments — dorsal intercarpal ligament (DIC), radioscaphocapitate (RSC), long/short radiolunate (LRL/SRL) — are secondary stabilizers whose failure converts a "dynamic" (stress-only) instability into a "static" (fixed, radiographically visible) one. Loss of scaphoid ligamentous restraint lets the scaphoid flex/pronate (rotary subluxation) while the lunotriquetral unit, tethered to the triquetrum, extends — producing the **dorsal intercalated segment instability (DISI)** pattern (SL angle >70°). LT disruption produces the opposite: **volar intercalated segment instability (VISI)**.

**Kienböck vascular anatomy.** The lunate has intraosseous vascular patterns classified (Gelberman) as **Type I** (single dorsal or single volar vessel, no intraosseous anastomosis — highest risk) and **Type II** (dual dorsal+volar supply with anastomoses — protective). Extraosseous supply comes from the dorsal and volar radiocarpal arches. Ulnar-negative variance increases radiolunate shear/load concentration and is an established (though not universal) risk factor; a **Type I lunate morphology** (flat proximal hamate facet, single-facet articulation with the hamate) reduces load-sharing with the capitate and is also implicated.

**TFCC and ulnar-sided load.** The triangular fibrocartilage complex (articular disc, meniscus homologue, dorsal/volar radioulnar ligaments, ulnolunate/ulnotriquetral ligaments, ECU subsheath) transmits ~18–20% of axial load across the wrist at neutral ulnar variance; this rises sharply with ulnar-positive variance, predisposing to central TFCC degeneration and **ulnocarpal (ulnar) impaction** against the lunate/triquetrum, distinct from acute traumatic peripheral tears (which occur regardless of variance and are the reparable, vascularized outer 10–20% — Palmer 1B).

### Diagnosis-specific pathophysiology notes

**Scaphoid fracture/nonunion.** ~70–80% occur at the waist; energy is transmitted through wrist hyperextension + radial deviation + intercarpal supination, placing the volar cortex in tension and dorsal cortex in compression — untreated waist fractures collapse into the classic **humpback deformity** (flexion at the fracture site, dorsal cortical gapping) as the volar cortex resorbs, producing secondary DISI even when the fracture eventually "unites."

**Scapholunate/lunotriquetral injury.** Isolated SLIL injury without extrinsic ligament failure is often asymptomatic on static imaging (predynamic/dynamic stages) — the extrinsic RSC/DIC ligaments compensate until they too fail or attenuate, at which point static DISI appears; this staged failure is the biomechanical basis for the Garcia-Elias model below. LT injury produces the mirror-image VISI pattern because the triquetrum, no longer tethered to the lunate, follows the hamate into flexion while the SL unit (intact) extends relatively.

**Perilunate dislocation.** Represents the most severe expression of the Mayfield progressive instability sequence — a single high-energy hyperextension event propagating circumferentially around the lunate (or through bone in greater-arc variants) rather than a stepwise chronic process; the lunate itself, tethered by intact volar radiolunate ligaments, tends to stay reduced relative to the radius while the rest of the carpus (and eventually the lunate itself in stage IV) displaces around/away from it.

**SNAC/SLAC wrist.** Both represent the same predictable radial-to-ulnar arthritic progression (radial styloid → radioscaphoid → capitolunate) driven by loss of normal scaphoid kinematics — either through SL ligament incompetence (SLAC) or nonunion-related malalignment (SNAC) — with the radiolunate joint characteristically spared until very late because the lunate's articulation with the radius is not directly dependent on scaphoid position.

**Kienböck disease.** Multifactorial — vascular (Type I single-supply lunate), mechanical (ulnar-negative variance, Type I lunate-hamate morphology concentrating load through a smaller radiolunate articulation), and possibly repetitive microtrauma (occupational vibration exposure) converge to produce lunate ischemia, collapse, and secondary carpal malalignment.

**TFCC/ulnar impaction.** Distinguish **acute traumatic** injury (typically peripheral, vascularized zone, reparable, mechanism = fall/axial load with forearm rotation) from **chronic degenerative ulnocarpal impaction** (central, avascular zone, associated with ulnar-positive variance, a wear phenomenon rather than a single injury) — the two have different natural histories and treatment algorithms despite overlapping symptoms.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**Provocative test summary:**

| Test | Technique | Positive finding suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Snuffbox/tubercle tenderness | Palpate anatomic snuffbox and volar scaphoid tubercle | Acute scaphoid fracture (sensitive, low specificity) |
| Watson scaphoid shift | Thumb pressure on scaphoid tubercle, wrist moved ulnar→radial deviation | Dynamic SL instability (subluxation "clunk") |
| Reagan LT ballottement / Kleinman shear | AP-directed shear stress across isolated LT joint | LT ligament injury |
| Midcarpal shift test | Axial load with forearm pronated, wrist moved radial→ulnar | Midcarpal instability (CIND) |
| Ulnocarpal stress test | Axial load + ulnar deviation + passive forearm rotation | TFCC tear / ulnar impaction |
| DRUJ ballottement / piano-key sign | AP translation of distal ulna relative to radius, forearm neutral/pronated/supinated | DRUJ instability (TFCC foveal/radioulnar ligament disruption) |
| Axial load/grind at STT joint | Compression + rotation at scaphotrapezial joint | Concomitant STT arthrosis (affects salvage choice) |

**History/mechanism.** FOOSH with wrist extension >90–95° and radial/ulnar deviation predicts scaphoid fracture vs perilunate injury patterns; higher-energy hyperextension with intercarpal supination classically produces the Mayfield progressive perilunate instability sequence.

**Exam maneuvers:**
- **Anatomic snuffbox and scaphoid tubercle (volar) tenderness** — sensitive but nonspecific for acute scaphoid fracture; tubercle tenderness has better specificity.
- **Watson scaphoid shift test** — thumb pressure over the scaphoid tubercle while moving the wrist from ulnar to radial deviation; a painful "clunk" as the scaphoid subluxates dorsally over the radial rim suggests SL instability (dynamic).
- **Lunotriquetral ballottement (Reagan) / LT shear test (Kleinman)** — pain/laxity with AP-directed shear across the LT joint suggests LT injury.
- **Midcarpal shift test** — for carpal instability nondissociative (CIND)/midcarpal instability.
- **Ulnocarpal stress test** (axial load + ulnar deviation + passive pro/supination) and **DRUJ ballottement/piano-key sign** — TFCC/ulnar impaction and DRUJ instability.

**Imaging.**
- **Scaphoid series**: PA, lateral, PA in ulnar deviation (extends and profiles the scaphoid), and pronated/semi-supinated oblique views. Initial films miss 20–30% of true fractures.
- **Occult fracture pathway**: with clinical suspicion and normal initial films, options are (a) **immobilize and repeat radiographs at 10–14 days** (cortical resorption improves fracture visibility) or (b) **early MRI** (within days). MRI has reported sensitivity ~97–100% and specificity ~95–99% for occult scaphoid fracture and is now favored where accessible, as it shortens time to diagnosis/return to work/sport and avoids unnecessary immobilization of the 60–80% of suspected cases who do not have a fracture (bone bruise or ligament injury instead). CT is reserved for surgical planning (displacement/comminution/humpback deformity assessment), not first-line occult diagnosis, due to lower sensitivity for non-displaced trabecular injury.
- **Nonunion/AVN workup**: CT (sagittal reconstructions along the true scaphoid axis) for humpback deformity, dorsal cortical angle, and lunate-scaphoid angulation; MRI with gadolinium (or intraoperative punctate bleeding) to assess proximal fragment vascularity — sensitivity/specificity for AVN prediction is imperfect, and many surgeons still use intraoperative bleeding as the practical determinant of viability.
- **SL/LT injury**: static radiographs (PA, lateral, clenched-fist AP, radial/ulnar deviation views) are often normal in early/dynamic instability. **Wrist arthroscopy remains the diagnostic gold standard**, graded by the **Geissler classification** (I–IV, based on ligament laxity and probe/scope pass-through between SL/LT joints) and correlated with the Garcia-Elias staging below.
- **Kienböck**: standard radiographs stage I–IIIA disease; MRI is required for stage I (radiographically occult) and to assess lunate viability/marrow signal before revascularization procedures.
- **Ulnar variance**: measured on a true neutral-rotation PA radiograph (shoulder abducted 90°, elbow flexed 90°, forearm neutral) — pronated/supinated views falsely change apparent variance by several millimeters and must not be used for surgical planning.

## Classification

### Herbert-Fisher classification (acute scaphoid fracture)

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Tubercle fracture | Stable; cast, high union rate |
| **A2** | Incomplete waist fracture | Stable; cast vs screw both reasonable (SWIFFT population) |
| **B1** | Distal oblique fracture | Unstable pattern; fixation generally favored |
| **B2** | Complete waist fracture (displaced or unstable) | Fixation recommended — displacement, comminution, or vertical-oblique orientation increases nonunion risk with cast alone |
| **B3** | Proximal pole fracture | Fixation strongly favored — highest AVN/nonunion risk from retrograde vascularity; fragment-specific/headless screw |
| **B4** | Trans-scaphoid perilunate fracture-dislocation | Emergent reduction + open fixation of scaphoid and repair of associated ligament injuries |
| **B5** | Comminuted fracture | Fixation ± bone graft; humpback correction if comminution is volar |
| **C** | Delayed union (radiographic union delayed beyond ~6–12 wks without established nonunion) | Continued immobilization vs surgical fixation depending on trajectory; low threshold to fix if plateauing |
| **D1** | Fibrous (established) nonunion | Surgical grafting required |
| **D2** | Pseudarthrosis (sclerotic nonunion, cyst formation) | Surgical grafting required, often vascularized if AVN present |

### Mayo (location) / Russe (fracture-line orientation) — scaphoid fracture description

| System | Category | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mayo (location)** | Distal tubercle | Extra-articular, distal to STT joint | Very stable, casts reliably |
| | Distal third | Distal to the waist | High union with cast; short immobilization |
| | Waist | ~70–80% of fractures | Cast vs screw per SWIFFT if undisplaced; screw if displaced |
| | Proximal third | Proximal to waist, still >1/3 pole remaining | Elevated nonunion/AVN risk; fixation favored |
| | Proximal pole | Small proximal fragment | Highest AVN/nonunion risk; fragment-specific fixation, consider primary vascularized graft in delayed presentation |
| **Russe (orientation)** | Type I — Horizontal oblique | Fracture plane parallel to long axis; compressive forces predominate | Most stable pattern, best cast healing rates |
| | Type II — Transverse | Perpendicular to long axis | Moderate stability |
| | Type III — Vertical oblique | Fracture plane runs more vertically, shear forces predominate | Least stable, highest displacement/nonunion risk with nonoperative treatment; favor fixation |

### Mayfield classification (progressive perilunate instability, "greater/lesser arc" injury)

| Stage | Structures disrupted | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Scapholunate ligament | SL dissociation only | If isolated and acute: SL repair ± capsulodesis; radiographically may show only SL widening |
| **II** | + Capitolunate joint (lunocapitate) | Perilunate dislocation — capitate dislocates dorsal to lunate | Emergent reduction; open repair of SL and capitolunate capsule |
| **III** | + Lunotriquetral ligament | Progression around ulnar side to LT joint | Add LT repair/pinning to the above |
| **IV** | + Dorsal radiocarpal ligament (lunate dislocates volarly) | True lunate dislocation ("spilled teacup" sign); lunate rotates and displaces into carpal tunnel | Surgical emergency (median nerve compression risk); open reduction and repair of all disrupted ligaments, carpal tunnel release if median nerve symptoms |

*Lesser-arc injuries* = pure ligamentous perilunate dissociation through the above stages. *Greater-arc injuries* = the force passes through bone (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial styloid perilunate fracture-dislocation) rather than purely through the SL/LT ligaments — these require fracture fixation in addition to the ligamentous repair implied by the corresponding stage.

### Watson classification (SLAC stages) — also applies to SNAC by analogous progression

| Stage | Arthritic pattern | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Degeneration limited to radial styloid / distal scaphoid articulation | Radial styloidectomy ± SL reconstruction if the SL joint itself is reconstructable |
| **II** | Progression to the entire radioscaphoid joint (scaphoid fossa) | Scaphoidectomy + four-corner fusion, or PRC if capitate head/lunate fossa cartilage preserved |
| **III** | Capitolunate (midcarpal) joint involved; radiolunate joint typically spared | PRC contraindicated (capitate arthritis) — favor scaphoidectomy + 4-corner fusion |
| **(IV — extended)** | Pancarpal involvement including radiolunate joint (uncommon; seen with inflammatory arthritis or neglected disease) | Total wrist fusion or total wrist arthroplasty; salvage denervation as adjunct |

**SNAC (scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse)** follows the same three-stage radial-to-ulnar progression but is driven by chronic scaphoid nonunion with humpback deformity rather than SL ligament failure; staging and salvage ladder are managed identically.

### Lichtman classification (Kienböck disease)

| Stage | Radiographic/MRI findings | Carpal height | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Normal or near-normal radiographs; MRI shows marrow edema/signal change, possible linear fracture | Normal | Immobilization/NSAIDs; consider revascularization if symptoms persist; joint leveling if ulnar-negative |
| **II** | Lunate sclerosis on radiographs, no collapse | Normal | Joint leveling (radial shortening if ulnar-negative) or capitate shortening/STT fusion if neutral/positive; ± revascularization |
| **IIIA** | Lunate collapse (loss of height/fragmentation) | Maintained | Joint leveling/limited fusion ± vascularized graft; scaphoid not yet fixed in flexion |
| **IIIB** | Lunate collapse with fixed scaphoid flexion/rotation, proximal capitate migration | Decreased (ratio <0.54) | Requires correction of carpal collapse: STT/scaphocapitate fusion, PRC, radial shortening + capitate shortening, or vascularized graft with structural (corticocancellous) support |
| **IV** | Stage IIIB changes plus radiocarpal and/or midcarpal osteoarthritis | Decreased | Salvage: PRC, limited/total wrist fusion, denervation, or wrist arthroplasty |

### Garcia-Elias staging of scapholunate instability progression

| Stage | Ligamentous status | Radiographic findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predynamic** | SLIL partial tear/attenuation (dorsal component intact) | Normal static and stress films; positive arthroscopy only (Geissler I–II) | Activity modification; arthroscopic debridement ± thermal/electrothermal shrinkage (limited modern role); observe |
| **Dynamic** | Complete SLIL (intrinsic) tear, secondary stabilizers (DIC, extrinsic) intact | Normal static films; SL gap/DISI appears only on stress views (clenched fist) | Arthroscopic-assisted reduction and pinning, or open repair + dorsal capsulodesis if acute (<6–8 wks from injury) |
| **Static reducible** | SLIL + extrinsic ligament (DIC/STT) injury | SL widening/DISI present on static films but passively correctable | Open repair/reconstruction (Brunelli-type tenodesis, SL Internal Brace); consider Blatt capsulodesis as adjunct |
| **Static fixed (irreducible)** | Chronic complete disruption, secondary contracture | Fixed DISI, not passively correctable, no arthritic changes yet | Ligament reconstruction (three-ligament tenodesis, bone-retinaculum-bone/Garcia-Elias graft) if cartilage preserved; consider limited fusion if reconstruction unreliable |
| **SLAC** | End-stage — arthritic degeneration per Watson staging | Joint space loss, osteophytes per SLAC stage I–III | Salvage ladder (styloidectomy → PRC/4-corner fusion → wrist fusion) |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Undisplaced/minimally displaced (<1 mm) scaphoid waist fracture**: thumb-spica cast (short-arm vs long-arm has not shown a consistent benefit for waist fractures; long-arm sometimes used for proximal-pole or in children) for **6–10 weeks**, with radiographic (and clinical) reassessment; union rates ~88–95%. The UK **SWIFFT** multicentre RCT (Dias et al.) found no clinically important difference in Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) at 52 weeks between cast immobilization and early screw fixation for bicortical waist fractures, with faster radiographic union and earlier return to work/sport in the surgical group but a higher complication rate (implant-related, CRPS, scar sensitivity) — this remains the primary evidence basis for offering cast treatment as a legitimate first-line option, reserving surgery for patient preference, occupational/athletic demands requiring earlier return, or fracture instability.
- **Predynamic/partial SL or LT ligament injury**: splinting 4–6 weeks, activity modification, structured hand therapy for proprioceptive/dart-thrower's-motion training; reserve surgery for persistent instability symptoms.
- **Kienböck stage I** (and select stage II with low symptom burden): immobilization, NSAIDs, activity modification; monitor for progression with serial imaging.
- **Stable peripheral or degenerative central TFCC tears without instability**: immobilization/bracing 4–6 weeks, NSAIDs, activity/ergonomic modification; central (Palmer 1A) and degenerative (Class 2) tears in low-demand patients often remain asymptomatic enough to avoid surgery indefinitely.
- **SLAC/SNAC — early or low-demand**: activity modification, splinting, NSAIDs before committing to salvage surgery.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Corticosteroid injection** (radiocarpal, DRUJ, or ulnocarpal) — diagnostic (confirms pain generator) and short-term therapeutic for degenerative TFCC/ulnocarpal impaction and early SLAC/SNAC arthrosis; effect typically wanes over weeks to a few months and does not alter disease progression.
- **Diagnostic wrist arthroscopy** doubles as first-line minimally invasive treatment: debridement of central (Palmer 1A) or degenerative TFCC tears, debridement/thermal treatment of predynamic SL/LT tears (Geissler I–II), and arthroscopic-assisted reduction and pinning for dynamic instability — avoids an open capsulotomy and allows simultaneous accurate Geissler grading to guide further treatment.
- **PRP and biologic augmentation** for chronic ligament injury — limited, low-quality evidence in the wrist specifically; not currently supported as a standalone treatment by any major guideline body; occasionally used as an adjunct to arthroscopic debridement or open repair without strong outcome data.

### Operative

**Scaphoid fracture fixation**
- **Percutaneous screw fixation** (volar-percutaneous for waist/distal fractures; dorsal-percutaneous for proximal-pole fractures, which allows more central, longer screw placement along the true central axis) using a headless compression screw (Herbert screw historically; modern variable/differential-pitch designs — Acutrak, OmniFix-type). Indications: displaced (>1 mm) or unstable (vertical-oblique/Russe III, comminuted) waist fractures, proximal-pole fractures, athletes/manual laborers wanting earlier return, patient preference after informed discussion of the SWIFFT trade-offs.
- **Pearls**: screw should be centrally placed along the true longitudinal axis on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy — eccentric placement is the most common technical error and increases nonunion risk; for proximal-pole fractures a dorsal approach with the wrist flexed gives better central purchase than a volar approach.
- **Open reduction** reserved for displaced/comminuted fractures needing direct visualization, humpback correction, or associated perilunate injury.

**Scaphoid nonunion**
- **Non-vascularized bone grafting** (Russe inlay/Matti-Russe technique, or a corticocancellous wedge from the iliac crest/distal radius for humpback correction) + screw fixation — appropriate for nonunion with a **viable proximal pole** (no AVN on MRI/intraoperative bleeding) and minimal deformity; reported union rates ~85–95% in this favorable subgroup.
- **Vascularized bone grafting** — favored for **AVN of the proximal pole, failed prior grafting, or long-standing (>1 year) nonunion**:
  - **Pedicled 1,2-intercompartmental supraretinacular artery (1,2-ICSRA) graft** from the distal radius — workhorse for nonunion with preserved but compromised vascularity; reported union rates ~80–90%.
  - **Free vascularized medial femoral condyle (MFC) graft** — preferred for established AVN/failed pedicled graft, providing a larger corticocancellous block with a robust independent pedicle (descending genicular artery) anastomosed to the radial artery or a branch; union rates in modern series commonly cited in the 85–100% range even in AVN nonunion, though this requires microsurgical expertise and added operative time.
  - Recent comparative literature (2024–2025 meta-analyses/systematic reviews, see Evidence section) continues to support **reserving vascularized grafting for AVN/proximal-pole/revision cases** rather than routine use in all nonunions, given added morbidity (donor site, operative time) without proven benefit when the proximal pole is viable.
- **Humpback deformity correction**: intraoperative restoration of scaphoid length/angulation using a corticocancellous wedge graft before fixation is essential — failure to correct humpback perpetuates DISI and midcarpal malalignment even after union.
- **Screw vs plate**: headless screw remains standard; a volar locking plate is occasionally used for unstable comminuted nonunion needing structural support, though evidence is limited to small series.

**Scapholunate ligament injury**
- **Acute (<3–6 weeks), primary repairable tissue**: direct SLIL repair (suture anchor into the lunate/scaphoid dorsal ridge) with dorsal capsulodesis (Blatt) augmentation, or arthroscopic-assisted reduction and percutaneous SL/scaphocapitate pinning for dynamic instability without adequate tissue for open repair.
- **Chronic, static reducible/fixed without arthritis**: ligament reconstruction — options include:
  - **Modified Brunelli / three-ligament tenodesis (Garcia-Elias/Wolfe)** using a strip of FCR tendon rerouted through the scaphoid and lunate to recreate the SL and dorsal intercarpal ligament vectors.
  - **Bone-retinaculum-bone (dorsal capsuloligamentous) autograft**.
  - **SL Internal Brace / suture-tape augmentation** of primary repair or reconstruction — increasingly used to protect the reconstruction during healing and reduce loss of reduction; early-to-midterm series (2024–2026, see Evidence) report reasonable maintenance of SL gap/angle, though comparative long-term data versus classic tenodesis are still maturing.
  - **Scapholunate axis method (SLAM) / arthroscopic reduction and association of the scapholunate (ARSL)** with suture-tape — less invasive arthroscopic-assisted reconstruction gaining adoption.
- **Static fixed with early arthrosis confined to the radial styloid**: radial styloidectomy combined with reconstruction may be considered; once midcarpal arthritis develops, proceed to the SLAC salvage ladder rather than reconstruction.

**Lunotriquetral injury**
- Acute, reparable: **arthroscopic debridement** (partial tears) or **open/arthroscopic-assisted LT repair with pinning** (complete tears, Geissler III–IV).
- Chronic symptomatic instability with ulnar-positive variance: address the **ulnar impaction component with ulnar shortening osteotomy**, which often resolves symptoms by unloading the LT joint even without direct ligament repair.
- Refractory chronic instability: **LT ligament reconstruction** (tendon weave, e.g., ECU slip) or **LT arthrodesis** as a salvage for painful chronic VISI, accepting a modest nonunion rate (~10–20% in reported series) and some loss of ulnar deviation.

**Perilunate dislocation**
- **Emergent closed reduction** (traction-countertraction with thumb pressure on the lunate, following the reverse Mayfield sequence) under sedation to decompress the median nerve and skin, followed by **splinting**, is the immediate step regardless of eventual definitive treatment.
- **Definitive treatment is surgical** in essentially all cases: combined **dorsal (primary) ± volar approach** — dorsal approach allows SL and LT ligament repair/pinning and assessment/repair of the dorsal capsule; volar approach (carpal tunnel exposure) is added for median nerve decompression, volar ligament (RSC) repair, or when closed reduction fails.
- Fixation typically with **K-wires across SL and LT joints** (± suture anchors for direct ligament repair) maintained **8–10 weeks**; trans-scaphoid (greater-arc) variants additionally require scaphoid screw fixation.
- **Timing**: reduce emergently; definitive open repair is commonly performed within the first **24–96 hours to ~2 weeks** — earlier repair is generally associated with better outcomes, though outcomes literature (see Evidence) shows persistent moderate residual disability (grip strength deficits, reduced ROM, later SLAC progression) even after technically adequate repair, so patient counseling about long-term prognosis is essential regardless of timing.
- **Missed/chronic (>6 weeks) perilunate injury**: open reduction becomes progressively harder; consider proceeding directly toward a salvage strategy (PRC/4-corner fusion) if reduction is not achievable or cartilage damage is already present.

**SNAC/SLAC wrist — salvage ladder**
- **Stage I**: radial styloidectomy (isolated, or combined with SL reconstruction/scaphoid excision as appropriate to the underlying diagnosis).
- **Stage II**: choice between **scaphoidectomy + four-corner fusion (4CF)** (capitate-lunate-triquetrum-hamate fused, typically with a dorsal circular plate or headless screws) and **proximal row carpectomy (PRC)** — decision driven by capitate head/lunate fossa cartilage status (PRC requires healthy cartilage there), patient age/demand (PRC favored in lower-demand/older patients, less reliably durable under high load), and surgeon preference. Recent meta-analyses (2024, see Evidence) trend toward **PRC showing comparable or superior motion and equivalent-to-superior functional outcomes** versus 4CF in appropriately selected stage II wrists, while 4CF retains an advantage where midcarpal cartilage is already compromised.
- **Stage III**: capitolunate arthritis present — **PRC is contraindicated**; scaphoidectomy + 4CF is the standard salvage.
- **Stage IV / pancarpal**: **total wrist fusion** (reliable pain relief, sacrifices motion) or **total wrist arthroplasty** (motion-preserving, lower-demand patients, higher revision/complication rates than fusion) depending on patient demand and bone stock.
- **Wrist denervation** (partial, e.g., posterior interosseous + anterior interosseous nerve neurectomy) can be used as a standalone procedure in lower-demand patients or as an adjunct to any salvage stage for residual pain.
- **Capitate resurfacing/pyrocarbon implants** are an emerging motion-preserving adjunct/alternative at the capitolunate articulation, with early-to-midterm series reported (see Evidence) but not yet established first-line.

**Kienböck disease**
- **Stage I–IIIA, ulnar-negative variance**: **radial shortening osteotomy** (joint leveling) — unloads the lunate by transferring load to the ulna; typically 2–4 mm shortening via a transverse or step-cut osteotomy with plate fixation.
- **Stage I–IIIA, ulnar-neutral/positive variance** (radial shortening not biomechanically effective): **capitate shortening/capitate-hamate fusion** or **STT (scaphotrapeziotrapezoid) fusion**, which unload the lunate by an alternative mechanism (shortening the capitate column or redistributing load through the distal row).
- **Revascularization procedures** — pedicled vascularized bone graft from the distal radius (4+5 extensor compartmental artery, ECA) or metacarpal, sometimes combined with **core decompression** and/or temporary external fixation for lunate unloading/distraction — used across stages I–IIIA, particularly when variance is neutral or the surgeon wishes to directly address bone viability rather than only mechanics.
- **Stage IIIB**: requires correcting the fixed carpal collapse in addition to unloading — **STT or scaphocapitate fusion** (corrects scaphoid flexion and shortens the capitate column), combined with radial shortening if ulnar-negative, or a **vascularized graft with structural (corticocancellous) support** to restore lunate height.
- **Stage IV / advanced collapse with arthrosis**: salvage — **PRC**, **limited intercarpal fusion**, **wrist denervation**, or **total wrist fusion/arthroplasty**; **lunate excision with tendon/prosthetic interposition** is largely historical given poor long-term results, though **lunate prosthetic arthroplasty** (pyrocarbon) has renewed interest with reasonable early-to-midterm outcomes reported in a 2024 systematic review (see Evidence) for unreconstructable lunate collapse without established pancarpal arthritis — still not first-line and evidence quality remains limited.

**TFCC (chronic)/ulnar impaction**
- **Arthroscopic central debridement** for degenerative/central (Palmer Class 1A or Class 2 degenerative) tears without instability — good short/medium-term pain relief, does not address underlying ulnar-positive mechanics.
- **Peripheral tear repair** (Palmer 1B, vascularized outer zone) — outside-in, inside-out, or all-inside arthroscopic suture repair depending on tear location relative to the radioulnar ligaments and foveal insertion; **foveal reattachment** (open or arthroscopic) specifically for radioulnar ligament avulsion causing DRUJ instability.
- **Arthroscopic or open wafer procedure** (resection of 2–4 mm of distal ulnar head/articular surface through a central TFCC perforation) for **mild ulnar-positive variance (typically ≤2–3 mm)** with central impaction — avoids osteotomy healing time but does not address DRUJ or ECU mechanics.
- **Ulnar shortening osteotomy (USO)** — the definitive procedure for **moderate-to-large ulnar-positive variance** causing impaction, whether or not a TFCC tear is present, and simultaneously tightens the TFCC/radioulnar ligaments (improving DRUJ stability) and tensions the interosseous membrane. Technique: transverse or **step-cut/oblique osteotomy** at the ulnar diaphysis with a dedicated compression plate/jig system, shortening 2–4 mm to bring variance to neutral or slightly negative.
  - **Pearls**: use a dedicated cutting/compression guide to ensure parallel cuts and avoid malrotation; step-cut/oblique designs increase bone contact area and may reduce nonunion risk versus a simple transverse cut, though comparative outcome data (2025, see Evidence) suggest both oblique-locking-plate and transverse-nonlocking constructs can achieve reliable union with attention to technique — construct choice is less critical than surgical execution.
  - **Pitfall**: overshortening or malrotation causes DRUJ incongruity/impingement pain; symptomatic plates requiring removal are reported in up to 20–30% of cases in some series and should be discussed with patients preoperatively.
- **DRUJ stabilization** (ligament reconstruction, e.g., using a tendon graft, or arthroplasty for arthritic DRUJ) reserved for chronic instability not addressed by TFCC repair/USO alone.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Phase overview by procedure:**

| Procedure | Protected/immobilization phase | AROM begins | Strengthening begins | Return to sport/heavy labor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Scaphoid fracture, cast | 6–10 wks (location-dependent) | On union confirmation | ~10–12 wks | 12–16 wks, CT-confirmed union for contact sport |
| Scaphoid fracture, screw | 1–2 wks splint | 1–2 wks | 6 wks | 8–12 wks |
| Scaphoid nonunion graft | 6–12 wks (longer for vascularized/structural graft) | After CT union (10–12 wks+) | 12 wks+ | 4–6 months |
| SL repair/reconstruction | K-wires 8–10 wks | 8–10 wks (wire removal) | 12 wks | 4–6 months |
| Perilunate repair | K-wires 8–10 wks | 8–10 wks (wire removal) | 3 months | 4–6 months |
| Kienböck surgery | 6–12 wks (longer for fusion) | 8–12 wks | 8–12 wks | 3–4 months |
| Ulnar shortening osteotomy | 2 wks splint, then brace | 1–2 wks (forearm rotation prioritized) | After bridging callus (8–12 wks) | 3–4 months |

**Scaphoid fracture — cast treatment**: thumb-spica immobilization 6–10 weeks depending on fracture location (distal third ~6 wks, waist ~8–10 wks, proximal pole ~10–12+ wks), with clinical/radiographic reassessment every 2–3 weeks; CT if union is equivocal at 10–12 weeks before committing to prolonged casting. Following radiographic union: progressive AROM over 1–2 weeks, then strengthening from ~week 10–12, return to contact sport/heavy labor typically 12–16 weeks and only after CT-confirmed union in high-demand athletes.

**Scaphoid fracture — screw fixation**: short-term splint/removable brace for comfort 1–2 weeks (screw provides enough stability for early motion), AROM starting within the first 1–2 weeks, progressive strengthening from 6 weeks, return to sport/heavy manual work at 8–12 weeks pending radiographic union (union is typically evident by 6–8 weeks with rigid fixation).

**Scaphoid nonunion grafting (vascularized or non-vascularized)**: thumb-spica immobilization 6–8 weeks (longer, 10–12 weeks, for vascularized MFC grafts with structural block), serial CT to confirm union before advancing loading (union commonly assessed 10–12 weeks postoperatively, may take up to 4–6 months), then AROM and progressive strengthening; return to unrestricted heavy use only after confirmed union, often 4–6 months total.

**SL repair/reconstruction**: K-wire protection typically maintained **8–10 weeks**; wrist immobilized in a cast/removable brace during this period with the wires in place. After wire removal: begin gentle AROM (weeks 8–10), dart-thrower's-motion pattern training and scapholunate proprioceptive/neuromuscular retraining (a hallmark of modern SL rehab, exploiting the plane of motion that minimizes SL joint strain), progressive strengthening from ~12 weeks, return to sport/heavy labor 4–6 months. Avoid resisted wrist extension/radial deviation loading (e.g., push-ups, heavy gripping in extension) until at least 3 months.

**Perilunate dislocation, post-repair**: cast/brace with K-wires in place 8–10 weeks, wire removal followed by gradual AROM, hand therapy focused on regaining flexion/extension arc (often permanently reduced 20–30% versus contralateral side), strengthening from 3 months; return to heavy labor/sport 4–6 months with counseling that residual stiffness and long-term SLAC-pattern arthritis risk remain even with optimal treatment.

**Kienböck surgery (joint leveling/limited fusion/revascularization)**: cast/splint immobilization 6–8 weeks (longer, up to 10–12 weeks, for limited intercarpal fusions until trabecular bridging is seen on CT), progressive AROM and strengthening from 8–12 weeks, return to heavy labor 3–4 months; vascularized graft procedures often follow a similar 8–12 week protected phase before loading.

**Ulnar shortening osteotomy**: splint/brace 2 weeks then removable brace, AROM of the wrist/forearm (pronosupination) started early (within 1–2 weeks) to avoid stiffness, avoid axial loading/heavy gripping until osteotomy shows bridging callus (typically 8–12 weeks), full unrestricted use/sport by 3–4 months; forearm rotation is the ROM domain most at risk and should be tracked specifically.

**General principles across this cluster**: certified hand therapy input from the earliest safe point (edema control, scar management, desensitization) improves outcomes; a graded return-to-sport/return-to-work protocol (light duty at union onset → progressive loading → unrestricted at confirmed radiographic union + functional strength ≥90% of contralateral side) is preferred over a fixed calendar date, particularly for proximal pole fractures, vascularized grafts, and any ligament reconstruction where premature loading risks attenuation/re-gapping.

**Return-to-work criteria (practical, applicable across the cluster):**
- Sedentary/desk work: as soon as comfortable in immobilization, typically within days.
- Light manual work (occasional lifting <5 kg): once out of rigid immobilization and pain-controlled, often 4–6 weeks.
- Heavy manual labor/repetitive loading: radiographic (or CT) union/bony bridging confirmed, near-symmetric grip strength, and full functional ROM — typically 3–4 months for fracture/osteotomy healing, 4–6 months after ligament reconstruction or vascularized grafting.

**Return-to-sport criteria:**
- Non-contact, low-load sport (cycling, running): early, once splint/brace allows and pain-free.
- Contact/collision sport or sport requiring loaded wrist extension (gymnastics, football, climbing, racquet sports): require confirmed union (CT if any doubt) or, for ligament procedures, completion of the protected phase plus demonstrated dynamic stability on exam, generally not before 3–4 months (fracture/osteotomy) or 4–6 months (ligament reconstruction, vascularized graft, perilunate repair).
- A removable protective orthosis/taping for an additional 4–6 weeks after return to high-risk sport is a reasonable transitional measure, particularly after SL/perilunate reconstruction.

## Complications

**Frequency snapshot:**

| Complication | Approximate incidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Scaphoid nonunion, untreated unstable fracture | 45–90% | Depends on displacement/instability |
| Scaphoid nonunion, treated waist fracture (cast) | ~5–12% | Higher with delayed presentation |
| Proximal pole AVN/nonunion | Disproportionately higher than waist/distal | Retrograde vascular supply |
| Ulnar shortening osteotomy — symptomatic hardware requiring removal | Up to 20–30% (series-dependent) | Counsel preoperatively |
| Ulnar shortening osteotomy — nonunion | Low single digits to ~5% | Construct/technique dependent |
| LT arthrodesis nonunion | ~10–20% | Accepted trade-off for chronic VISI salvage |
| CRPS/stiffness after scaphoid surgery vs cast | More frequent with surgery (SWIFFT) | Key counseling point favoring cast in eligible patients |

- **Nonunion** — scaphoid fracture (untreated/undertreated unstable fractures 45–90%; treated waist fractures with cast ~5–12%; proximal pole disproportionately higher); ulnar shortening osteotomy (reported low single digits to ~5% depending on construct); LT arthrodesis (~10–20%).
- **Avascular necrosis** — proximal pole scaphoid fractures (retrograde vascular supply); progression to nonunion and secondary SNAC arthritis if untreated.
- **Malunion / humpback deformity** — scaphoid nonunion healed in flexed/shortened position, driving secondary DISI and midcarpal overload → accelerates SNAC progression even after "union."
- **Progressive carpal collapse / SNAC-SLAC arthritis** — the natural end-stage of untreated scaphoid nonunion or SL ligament disruption; underscores the rationale for early diagnosis and stabilization.
- **Hardware complications** — screw prominence/malposition (especially in proximal-pole fixation where central purchase is technically demanding), symptomatic plate requiring removal after USO (up to 20–30% in some series), K-wire migration/pin-tract infection in perilunate/SL fixation.
- **Nerve injury** — dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve and superficial radial nerve branches at risk with dorsal wrist approaches (SL repair, 4-corner fusion, dorsal ICSRA graft harvest); median nerve injury/persistent neuropathy after perilunate dislocation (acute compression) if decompression delayed.
- **DRUJ incongruity/impingement** after ulnar shortening osteotomy from overshortening or malrotation; conversely, undercorrection leaves residual impaction symptoms.
- **CRPS/stiffness** — reported more frequently after surgical fixation than cast treatment in the SWIFFT cohort; a genuine trade-off to discuss when counseling patients toward operative treatment of an otherwise cast-amenable fracture.
- **Recurrent SL gapping/loss of reduction** after ligament reconstruction — a persistent limitation of tenodesis techniques over long-term follow-up, motivating ongoing interest in augmentation techniques (Internal Brace, suture-tape) though comparative long-term re-gapping rates against classic reconstruction are not yet mature.
- **Failed PRC/4CF** — persistent radiocapitate/radiolunate arthritis (PRC) or nonunion/adjacent-joint arthritis (4CF) requiring conversion to total wrist fusion.

## Recent Evidence

- **[Cast versus surgical fixation, SWIFFT 5-year radiological follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41475366/)** (Dias JJ, Brealey SD, Coleman E, et al., *Bone Joint J*, 2026) — long-term radiographic outcomes from the original SWIFFT cohort, extending the trial's 52-week PRWE-equivalence finding with union/deformity data out to 5 years; reinforces that cast treatment remains a defensible first-line option for undisplaced bicortical waist fractures rather than an inferior compromise.
- **[Clinical effectiveness of early surgical fixation versus cast immobilization, SWIFFT 5-year follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41475372/)** (Dias JJ, Brealey SD, Coleman E, et al., *Bone Joint J*, 2026) — companion 5-year clinical/PROM outcomes paper; together with the radiological report, this is the longest-term prospective RCT data available for the cast-vs-screw decision and should be the primary citation when counseling patients beyond the 1-year horizon.
- **[Cost-effectiveness of early surgical fixation versus cast immobilization, SWIFFT 5-year follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41475368/)** (Hinde S, Richardson G, Coleman E, et al., *Bone Joint J*, 2026) — extends the trial's health-economic analysis; consistent with the original 2021 cost-effectiveness report, informs shared decision-making where healthcare-system cost is a factor.
- **[Scaphoid fractures and associated ligament injury: five-year follow-up of the SWIFFT trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41916572/)** (Johnson NA, Francis R, Choudhary S, et al., *Bone Joint J*, 2026) — examines concomitant SL/LT ligament injury in the SWIFFT cohort and its influence on long-term outcome, adding nuance to "isolated" scaphoid fracture management by quantifying how often concurrent ligament injury is present and its prognostic effect.
- **[Vascularized Versus Non-vascularized Bone Grafting for Scaphoid Non-union: A Meta-Analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39974267/)** (Guria et al., *Cureus*, 2025) — pools comparative series of vascularized vs non-vascularized grafting; consistent with prior practice, supports reserving vascularized grafts for AVN/proximal-pole/revision scenarios rather than routine first-line use in nonunions with a viable proximal pole.
- **[Surgical treatment of carpal scaphoid non-union: a systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40603779/)** (Bocchino et al., *Eur J Orthop Surg Traumatol*, 2025) — broad contemporary synthesis of nonunion surgical options (screw alone, non-vascularized graft, pedicled and free vascularized graft), useful as an up-to-date reference for technique selection counseling.
- **[MRI for Scaphoid Nonunion: Utilization Rates, Factors Associated With Utilization, and Subsequent Vascularized Bone Graft Use](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39614839/)** (Shapiro et al., *J Hand Surg Am*, 2025) — real-world practice-pattern data on how often preoperative MRI is obtained before nonunion surgery and how it correlates with the vascularized-graft decision, relevant to standardizing the preoperative workup described above.
- **[Retinaculum Grafts in Scapholunate Ligament Reconstruction: A Systematic Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40688641/)** (Estermann L, Yong M, York BS, et al., *J Wrist Surg*, 2024) — synthesizes outcomes of extensor retinaculum-based SL reconstruction techniques, supporting their continued role alongside tendon-weave (Brunelli-type) methods in the chronic static-instability tier of the treatment ladder.
- **[Arthroscopic Suture Anchor Scapholunate Capsulodesis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40688638/)** (Chung SR, Carratalá V, Delgado PJ, et al., *J Wrist Surg*, 2024) — describes and reports outcomes of an all-arthroscopic suture-anchor capsulodesis technique, reflecting the ongoing shift toward less invasive, arthroscopically-guided management of dynamic/early static SL instability.
- **[Mid-term outcomes of scapholunate ligament reconstruction with internal brace augmentation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41355139/)** (Mylonas T, Stefanou N, Koskiniotis A, et al., *J Hand Surg Eur Vol*, 2026) — mid-term (not just short-term) data on Internal Brace-augmented SL reconstruction, an important addition since most prior Internal Brace literature was early-follow-up only; supports durability of the augmentation strategy discussed in the Operative section.
- **[Four-Corner Fusion Versus Proximal Row Carpectomy for SLAC and SNAC Wrist: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38416092/)** (Hones et al., *J Hand Surg Am*, 2024) and its companion **[Proximal Row Carpectomy with and without Capitate Resurfacing: A Preliminary Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38648294/)** (Hones et al., *JBJS Rev*, 2024) — pooled comparative outcomes; the trend toward favorable PRC outcomes reflected in the salvage-ladder guidance above is drawn from these two 2024 analyses, including early data on capitate-resurfacing implants as a PRC adjunct.
- **[Lunate prosthetic arthroplasty for the treatment of Kienböck's disease: A systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39866368/)** (Vaghela KR, Selby A, Heras-Palou C, Johnson N, *J Hand Microsurg*, 2024) — reassesses prosthetic lunate replacement (largely abandoned after poor historical results) using modern pyrocarbon implants; supports its cautious re-emergence as a salvage option in unreconstructable stage IIIB/IV lunate collapse, as reflected above.
- **[A Comparison of Outcomes in Acute Perilunate Injuries: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Treatment Approaches](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38415721/)** (Abola MV, Gerber BA, Rocks MC, et al., *Hand (N Y)*, 2024/2025) — pools outcomes across surgical approaches (dorsal, volar, combined) for perilunate dislocation/fracture-dislocation; informs the approach-selection discussion and reinforces the counseling point that residual functional deficits are common regardless of technique.
- **[Optimized Step-Cut Ulnar Shortening Osteotomy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41421859/)** (Kodama A, Munemori M, Sumida Y, et al., *J Orthop Sci*, 2026) — describes a dedicated-guide/plate step-cut technique for USO aimed at reducing invasiveness; relevant to the technical-pearls discussion of osteotomy geometry and hardware selection.
- **[Comparison of Dynamic Compression Plate Systems for Ulnar Shortening Osteotomy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39658455/)** (Miyashima Y, Uemura T, Okada M, et al., *J Orthop Sci*, 2025) — compares oblique/locking-plate versus transverse/non-locking-plate USO constructs for union and complication rates, the primary source behind the "construct choice is less critical than execution" guidance above.
