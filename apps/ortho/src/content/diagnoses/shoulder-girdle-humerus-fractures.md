## Quick Reference

### Treatment ladder by diagnosis

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical indications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clavicle midshaft** | Sling 2–4 wk, early ROM, analgesia | Figure-of-8 (no proven benefit over sling) | Shortening >2 cm, displacement >100% (bone-on-bone loss), comminution (Z-fragment), open, tenting skin, neurovascular injury, floating shoulder, symptomatic nonunion |
| **Proximal humerus** | Sling 2–3 wk + early pendulum/passive ROM | Formal supervised physio at 2–3 wk | Displacement >1 cm or angulation >45° at any of 4 parts, head-split, articular displacement >25–40%, glenohumeral dislocation, vascular injury, open fracture; age/bone quality drive fixation vs arthroplasty choice |
| **Humeral shaft** | Functional bracing (Sarmiento), 90%+ union | — | Segmental/comminuted fracture with unacceptable alignment (>20° varus/valgus, >20° AP angulation, >3 cm shortening), bilateral humeral fractures, floating elbow, pathologic fracture, vascular injury, open fracture, radial nerve palsy after closed reduction/manipulation (secondary palsy), polytrauma |
| **Scapula body/spine** | Sling, early ROM at 1–2 wk | Supervised physio | Glenopolar angle <22°, lateral border offset >20 mm, angular deformity >45°, displaced scapular neck with concomitant clavicle fracture ("floating shoulder"), open fracture |
| **Glenoid (intra-articular)** | Nonoperative if congruent, <25% articular involvement, stable | — | Articular step-off/gap >4–5 mm, >25% involvement of anterior or >33% posterior glenoid with instability, associated instability/subluxation |
| **AC joint** | Sling + early ROM (types I–II, most III) | — | Type III in high-demand/overhead athletes (selective), types IV–VI (all) |
| **SC joint** | Sling, observation (anterior) | Closed reduction under GA | Posterior dislocation = surgical/reduction emergency (mediastinal structures) |

### Key numbers to remember

- **Clavicle shortening >2 cm or displacement ≥100%**: threshold historically used to trigger ORIF discussion in displaced midshaft fractures (derived from Canadian Orthopaedic Trauma Society data).
- **Proximal humerus "4-part rule"**: >1 cm displacement OR >45° angulation between any two of the four segments = displaced, per Neer.
- **Glenopolar angle** normal 30–45°; <22° predicts poor outcome with nonoperative scapula neck management.
- **Humeral shaft functional bracing**: union rates 90–98% in isolated closed fractures (Sarmiento series); acceptable angulation up to 20° varus/valgus, 20° antero-posterior, 3 cm shortening, 30° rotation.
- **Primary (fracture-associated) radial nerve palsy**: ~70–85% recover spontaneously within 3–4 months; observe. **Secondary (post-manipulation/iatrogenic) palsy**: explore.
- **Rockwood classification**: types I–III do NOT reliably need surgery (except select high-demand type III); IV–VI need surgery.
- **AC joint coracoclavicular distance**: normal 1.1–1.3 cm; >25–100% increase vs contralateral side used in Rockwood grading.
- **PROFHER trial (2015, 5-year follow-up 2024)**: no significant difference in Oxford Shoulder Score between surgical and nonoperative treatment of displaced proximal humerus fractures at 2 and 5 years — landmark evidence against routine surgery in the elderly.

### Surgical approach cheat-sheet

| Diagnosis / implant | Approach | Key structure at risk |
|---|---|---|
| Clavicle plating | Direct anterosuperior or anteroinferior longitudinal incision | Supraclavicular sensory nerves (expect a patch of numbness distal/inferior to the scar — warn patients), subclavian vessels/brachial plexus posteriorly |
| Proximal humerus ORIF/hemiarthroplasty/rTSA | Deltopectoral (standard) or anterolateral (deltoid-splitting, limited to distal axillary nerve excursion) | Axillary nerve (deltoid-splitting approach — do not extend >5 cm below acromion), cephalic vein (deltopectoral interval) |
| Proximal humerus antegrade nail | Superior, split (not detach) supraspinatus | Rotator cuff (supraspinatus), long head of biceps |
| Humeral shaft, proximal/mid-shaft plating | Anterolateral (Henry) | Radial nerve as it crosses from posterior to anterior compartment distally; musculocutaneous nerve |
| Humeral shaft, distal-third plating | Posterior (triceps-splitting) or extended lateral | Radial nerve in spiral groove — direct identification recommended |
| Humeral shaft retrograde nail | Posterior, olecranon fossa entry | Ulnar nerve, risk of supracondylar fracture propagation |
| Scapular neck/body, posterior glenoid | Posterior (Judet or minimally-invasive modified Judet) | Suprascapular and axillary nerves (infraspinatus/teres minor interval) |
| Anterior glenoid rim | Deltopectoral or arthroscopic-assisted | Axillary nerve, musculocutaneous nerve, subscapularis |
| AC joint reconstruction | Superior "sabre" incision centered on the coracoid/distal clavicle | Suprascapular nerve (deep to coracoid during tunnel drilling) |
| SC joint open reduction | Transverse incision over the joint | Innominate vein/artery, trachea, esophagus (posterior dissection) |

### Red flags (any diagnosis)

- Open fracture, skin tenting/threatened skin, vascular compromise (absent/asymmetric pulses, expanding hematoma) — vascular injury reported in up to 5–10% of displaced proximal humerus fractures and higher in glenohumeral fracture-dislocations; axillary artery most at risk.
- Brachial plexus injury (especially with high-energy scapulothoracic dissociation — check for a "flail, cool, pulseless" limb with scapular fracture = surgical emergency, mortality-associated pattern).
- Posterior SC dislocation — mediastinal compression (dyspnea, dysphagia, venous congestion) — CT angiogram, cardiothoracic surgery on standby for closed/open reduction.
- Floating shoulder (ipsilateral clavicle + scapular neck fracture) — assess the "second strut"; true double disruption of the superior shoulder suspensory complex is unstable and usually needs fixation of at least one component.
- Secondary radial nerve palsy after fracture manipulation/casting — mandates exploration (nerve likely entrapped).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The **shoulder girdle** is a highly mobile, minimally constrained system suspended from the axial skeleton by a single true articulation — the **sternoclavicular (SC) joint** — with the scapulothoracic "joint" and glenohumeral (GH) joint providing the remainder of motion. The clavicle acts as a strut that maintains the width and projection of the shoulder; loss of its integrity (fracture, or combined injury) collapses the shoulder medially, inferiorly, and anteriorly.

**Superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC)** — Goss/Williams concept: a bony-soft-tissue ring comprising the glenoid, coracoid, coracoclavicular (CC) and acromioclavicular (AC) ligaments, distal clavicle, acromion, and the acromial-clavicular strut. Disruption at **two points** of this ring ("double disruption") destabilizes the entire complex (e.g., floating shoulder = clavicle fracture + scapular neck fracture); a single-point disruption is usually stable because the remaining ring elements splint it.

**Clavicle**: S-shaped tubular bone, subcutaneous, with no medullary canal continuity of uniform diameter — makes intramedullary fixation technically demanding. Medial 2/3 convex anteriorly, lateral 1/3 concave anteriorly; thinnest and weakest at the junction of the middle and outer thirds, explaining the ~80% prevalence of midshaft fractures. Deforming forces: sternocleidomastoid pulls medial fragment superiorly; weight of arm plus pectoralis major/deltoid pulls lateral fragment inferomedially — producing the classic "z" or overriding deformity with shortening. Subclavian vessels and brachial plexus lie directly posterior/inferior — at risk with comminuted, posteriorly-displaced fragments or penetrating mechanisms.

**Proximal humerus**: four osseous segments per Codman/Neer — humeral head (articular segment), greater tuberosity (GT, supraspinatus/infraspinatus/teres minor insertion), lesser tuberosity (LT, subscapularis insertion), and shaft. Blood supply classically credited to the **anterolateral ascending branch of the anterior circumflex humeral artery ("arcuate artery")**, but contemporary work (Hettrich et al.) shows the **posterior circumflex humeral artery** supplies the majority of the head via posteromedial and posterolateral vessels through the rotator cuff insertion and medial calcar — this is why a preserved medial metaphyseal (calcar) extension of >8 mm and intact medial hinge (Hertel's criteria) predict lower avascular necrosis (AVN) risk even in displaced 3-/4-part fractures. Axillary nerve runs 5–7 cm below the lateral acromial edge around the surgical neck — most commonly injured nerve in proximal humerus fracture/dislocation (up to 45% subclinical EMG involvement, ~5–30% clinical).

**Humeral shaft**: radial nerve spirals from posteromedial to lateral in the **spiral (radial) groove** at the junction of the proximal 2/3 and distal 1/3 of the shaft, tethered by the lateral intermuscular septum ~10 cm proximal to the lateral epicondyle (Hollstein-Lorenz point) — the anatomic reason distal-third/spiral (Holstein-Lewis) fractures carry the highest primary radial nerve palsy rate (~15–22%).

**Scapula**: broad, well-muscled flat bone (subscapularis anteriorly; supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor posteriorly) — high-energy soft-tissue envelope explains why body/spine fractures usually heal well nonoperatively even with substantial displacement, while the glenoid articular surface behaves like any other intra-articular fracture (step-off intolerant). Association with **ipsilateral pulmonary contusion, rib fractures, and closed head injury** in >80–90% of cases (high-energy "marker injury" — mandate full trauma survey).

**AC joint**: a diarthrodial joint stabilized by (1) AC ligaments (capsular, horizontal plane stability — primary restraint to posterior translation) and (2) coracoclavicular ligaments — conoid (medial, restrains superior translation, primary vertical stabilizer) and trapezoid (lateral, restrains AC compression/horizontal). Deltotrapezial fascial sleeve is the key soft-tissue restraint whose disruption in high-grade injuries (IV–VI) explains persistent deformity.

**SC joint**: least congruent joint in the body, stabilized by strong capsular ligaments (anterior/posterior), interclavicular ligament, and costoclavicular ligament; has its own intra-articular disc. Posterior displacement is dangerous because of proximity to trachea, esophagus, great vessels, and vagus/phrenic nerves in the retrosternal space.

**Pediatric/adolescent caveat**: the medial clavicular physis is the last physis in the body to close (fuses ~22–25 years) and does not appear on plain radiographs until the late teens. Many apparent "SC joint dislocations" in patients under 25 are actually **medial physeal (Salter-Harris type I/II) fractures**, which remodel well and are usually managed nonoperatively with observation even when displaced — always keep this differential in mind before consenting a young patient for open SC joint surgery, and use CT to distinguish true dislocation from physeal injury when the distinction changes management.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (direct blow to shoulder = clavicle/AC; FOOSH = proximal humerus in elderly osteoporotic falls; direct high-energy trauma = scapula, humeral shaft, floating shoulder), hand dominance, occupation/sport (overhead athlete changes AC joint threshold for surgery), comorbidities (diabetes, smoking, osteoporosis, prior shoulder pathology — rotator cuff status changes proximal humerus surgical decision-making).

**Exam**: full neurovascular exam is mandatory and must be documented before any manipulation — axillary nerve (deltoid contraction + regimental badge sensation — NB: sensation can be intact with a motor axillary palsy, so test resisted abduction/deltoid contraction, not just sensation), radial nerve (wrist/finger/thumb extension, first dorsal webspace sensation), pulses, capillary refill. Inspect for skin tenting, open wounds, deformity. Palpate the entire clavicle, AC and SC joints (step-off, instability with cross-body adduction/piano-key sign), scapula, and full humeral length. Assess for a "floating shoulder" pattern and for concomitant chest wall injury (auscultate, palpate ribs).

**Imaging**:
- **Clavicle**: AP + 20° cephalic tilt (Zanca-like) view; consider AP chest to assess for pneumothorax/rib fractures in high-energy mechanism.
- **Proximal humerus**: true AP (Grashey), scapular-Y, axillary lateral (mandatory — do not accept a trauma series without an adequate axillary view to exclude GH dislocation and assess head-splitting/impaction). CT with 2D/3D reconstruction routinely for surgical planning in displaced 3-/4-part patterns — dramatically improves inter-observer reliability of Neer classification versus plain film alone.
- **Humeral shaft**: AP/lateral of full humerus including shoulder and elbow joints (rule out floating elbow).
- **Scapula/glenoid**: AP shoulder, scapular-Y, axillary; CT (with 3D reconstruction) essentially mandatory for glenoid/intra-articular fractures and to measure glenopolar angle, medialization, and angular deformity for body fractures.
- **AC/SC joint**: bilateral AP (Zanca view: 10–15° cephalic tilt, reduced kV) for AC comparison; **CT is the study of choice for SC joint** — plain films (serendipity view) are unreliable for anterior vs posterior direction; CT angiography if posterior SC dislocation suspected (assess innominate vessels, trachea).
- MRI selectively for occult AC/rotator cuff pathology, or glenoid labral/capsular assessment in equivocal instability.

**Special assessment — floating shoulder / SSSC double disruption**: when both a clavicle fracture and a scapular neck (or glenoid) fracture are identified on the same side, formally assess combined displacement (not each fracture in isolation) — glenopolar angle, clavicle shortening, and glenohumeral alignment on a true AP/scapular-Y/axillary series or CT. True double disruption with combined displacement (glenohumeral malalignment, scapular medialization) is unstable and generally warrants fixation of at least the clavicle (often sufficient to restore the strut and indirectly stabilize the scapular component); an intact posterior periosteal/muscular sleeve on CT converts many apparent "floating shoulder" injuries into effectively stable single-point disruptions manageable nonoperatively.

**Special assessment — scapulothoracic dissociation**: suspect in high-energy trauma with a "flail, pulseless (or vascularly compromised), and neurologically devastated" upper limb plus lateral displacement of the scapula on chest radiograph ("scapular displacement" or increased distance from spinous processes to medial scapular border compared with the contralateral side). This is a limb- and life-threatening closed traction avulsion injury of the shoulder girdle from the thoracic wall, not a simple fracture pattern — obtain emergent CT angiography, involve vascular and brachial plexus surgery, and counsel on the high rate of associated permanent brachial plexus injury and need for limb salvage versus amputation discussion.

## Classification

### Neer classification (proximal humerus)

Based on the four anatomic segments (articular head, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, shaft) and whether displacement criteria are met: **>1 cm displacement or >45° angulation** between fragments defines a "part."

| Category | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **1-part (minimally displaced)** | Any fracture pattern (even 4 lines) not meeting displacement criteria — ~80–85% of all proximal humerus fractures | Nonoperative: sling immobilization, early pendulum/passive ROM at 1–2 wk |
| **2-part** | One segment displaced relative to the other three (surgical neck, anatomic neck, GT, or LT variants) | Surgical neck 2-part: often ORIF if unstable/displaced, especially in younger patients; isolated displaced GT >5 mm (overhead athletes/younger) or >1 cm (general) — ORIF, risk of subacromial impingement if left displaced |
| **3-part** | Two segments displaced relative to the remaining two (e.g., surgical neck + GT, head remains attached to one tuberosity) | ORIF (locking plate) in physiologically younger/good bone; consider rTSA in elderly with poor bone quality/rotator cuff compromise |
| **4-part** | All four segments displaced from one another | Highest AVN risk (up to 25–40% with classic valgus-impacted variant lower); rTSA favored in patients >65–70y, especially valgus-impacted or head-split variants with comminution; ORIF viable if medial hinge/calcar intact (Hertel positive predictors) in younger patients |
| **Head-splitting** | Articular surface fractured into ≥2 major fragments | Usually rTSA or hemiarthroplasty (young, reconstructible) — poor candidate for ORIF |
| **Fracture-dislocation** (anterior/posterior) | Any of the above with GH joint dislocation | Urgent reduction; treat as displaced fracture pattern above; higher vascular injury risk (check axillary artery) |

**Practical caveat**: inter- and intra-observer reliability of Neer on plain radiographs is only moderate (kappa ~0.4–0.6); CT improves but does not fully resolve this — treat Neer as a communication/decision-framework, not a precise anatomic truth.

### AO/OTA Classification — Chapter 11 (Proximal Humerus) and Chapter 12 (Humeral Shaft)

**AO/OTA 11 (proximal humerus)** — organized by fracture complexity and vascularity risk:

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **11-A** (extra-articular, unifocal) | 2-part fracture patterns (surgical neck, GT, or LT), one fracture line, extracapsular | Lowest AVN risk; often amenable to minimally invasive plating or nailing |
| **11-B** (extra-articular, bifocal) | Two extra-articular fracture lines (e.g., surgical neck + tuberosity), head still has one soft-tissue attachment preserved | Intermediate AVN risk; ORIF favored if hinge preserved |
| **11-C** (articular) | Fracture line extends into the articular surface / anatomic neck involvement | Highest AVN risk; anatomic-neck component is the classic AVN predictor; arthroplasty considered more strongly here in older patients |

Each group subdivides further (.1/.2/.3 and impacted/displaced qualifiers) mirroring Neer parts but the A/B/C tier is what drives the AVN-risk conversation and is widely used in registries/trials (it is the classification used in PROFHER-style trial reporting).

**AO/OTA 12 (humeral shaft)**:

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **12-A** (simple) | Spiral, oblique, or transverse fracture, single fracture plane | Excellent functional bracing candidate; if surgery chosen, both nailing and plating give reliable fixation |
| **12-B** (wedge) | A butterfly/wedge fragment present, but after reduction the main fragments still have cortical contact | Bracing still viable if alignment acceptable; if unstable, plating usually preferred to preserve length/rotation via direct visualization |
| **12-C** (complex/comminuted) | Segmental or comminuted, no reconstructable cortical contact between main fragments | Higher failure rate with bracing (shortening/malunion); operative fixation (plate typically preferred for length/rotation control, nail with adequate proximal/distal locking as alternative) |

### Allman / Robinson Classification (clavicle)

**Allman (anatomic, 3 groups)**:

| Group | Location | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Middle third | ~80% of all clavicle fractures |
| **II** | Distal (lateral) third | ~15%; further subdivided (see below) — CC ligament status is key |
| **III** | Medial third | ~5%; rare, consider mediastinal injury with high-energy mechanism |

**Robinson classification (more granular, prognostic — predicts nonunion)**:

| Type | Description | Nonunion relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Medial fifth | Rare |
| **2A** | Middle two-thirds, undisplaced (<100% width) | Low nonunion risk (~1–5%) nonoperative |
| **2B1** | Middle two-thirds, displaced, simple or single-butterfly fragment, no comminution | Nonunion risk nonoperative ~15% |
| **2B2** | Middle two-thirds, displaced, comminuted or segmental | Highest nonunion risk nonoperative, reported up to 20–30% in some series — key group where operative fixation is most strongly considered |
| **3** | Lateral fifth | Analogous to Neer distal clavicle classification |

**Neer classification of distal (lateral) clavicle fractures** (commonly paired with Robinson type 3, worth knowing alongside Allman/Robinson):

| Neer type | CC ligaments | Stability | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Intact | Stable | Nonoperative |
| II (IIA/IIB) | Torn medial to fracture (conoid disrupted) | Unstable, high nonunion risk (~30%) | Operative fixation favored (hook plate, CC suture-button, or superior plate with CC augmentation) |
| III | Intact, fracture involves AC articular surface | Stable (may develop late AC arthrosis) | Usually nonoperative; late excision if symptomatic |
| IV (pediatric variant) | Periosteal sleeve avulsion, ligaments intact to periosteum | Usually stable | Nonoperative in children/adolescents |
| V | Comminuted, ligaments attached to neither fragment (both to a free inferior fragment) | Highly unstable | Operative fixation |

### Ideberg Classification (glenoid fractures) — as modified by Goss/Ideberg

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Avulsion fracture of the glenoid rim (Ia anterior, Ib posterior) | Nonoperative unless large fragment (>25% anterior / >33% posterior arc) causing instability → ORIF |
| **II** | Fracture line exits through the lateral scapular border/glenoid fossa inferior to the glenoid, creating an inferior fragment that may displace with the humeral head | Nonoperative if congruent and stable; ORIF if displaced fragment causes subluxation |
| **III** | Fracture line exits superiorly through the scapular body/spine or through the coracoid base, often associated with AC joint injury or clavicle fracture (ring disruption) | Often needs fixation given ring-disruption association; assess SSSC |
| **IV** | Fracture line runs transversely through the glenoid fossa and exits through the medial scapular border | Depends on displacement/step-off; ORIF if articular step-off >4–5 mm |
| **V** | Combination of type IV with either inferior (Va), superior (Vb), or both (Vc) glenoid neck fracture components, creating a floating glenoid fragment | Usually operative given instability and displacement magnitude |
| **VI** (some modifications) | Comminuted glenoid fossa fracture | Complex reconstruction, may need buttress plating; salvage arthroplasty in older patients with articular destruction |

### Rockwood Classification (AC joint injury)

| Type | AC ligament | CC ligament | Deltotrapezial fascia | Clinical/radiographic finding | Management |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Sprained, intact | Intact | Intact | Tender AC joint, normal X-ray | Nonoperative: sling, ice, early ROM as tolerated |
| **II** | Torn | Sprained, intact | Intact | AC joint widened, CC distance normal or minimally increased (<25%), clavicle slightly elevated relative to acromion | Nonoperative: sling 1–2 wk then progressive ROM |
| **III** | Torn | Torn | Intact (usually) | CC distance increased 25–100% vs contralateral; clavicle displaced superiorly but deltotrapezial fascia intact limits displacement | Nonoperative first-line for most (RCTs/meta-analyses show comparable mid-term outcomes); selective early ORIF/reconstruction in high-demand laborers/overhead athletes or heavy manual workers wanting fastest predictable return |
| **IV** | Torn | Torn | Torn | Clavicle displaced posteriorly, often buttonholed through/into trapezius (best seen on axillary view) | Operative: CC ligament reconstruction ± AC fixation |
| **V** | Torn | Torn | Torn (extensive) | CC distance increased >100–300% vs contralateral, gross superior displacement, marked deltotrapezial stripping | Operative: anatomic CC reconstruction (allograft/autograft ± suspensory device), acute repair preferred over delayed |
| **VI** | Torn | Torn | Torn | Clavicle displaced inferiorly, subacromial or subcoracoid (rare, high-energy) | Operative: reduction and CC reconstruction, often urgent given neurovascular proximity (subcoracoid variant) |

**Practical note**: Types I–III are collectively the most controversial group in the literature; current evidence (2024–2025 systematic reviews, see Evidence section) shows no clear superiority of surgery for standard type III but a possible edge in selected physically active young patients undergoing anatomic CC reconstruction.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Clavicle (midshaft, non-displaced or acceptably displaced)**: simple sling for comfort 2–4 weeks (not figure-of-8 — multiple RCTs show no functional or cosmetic advantage and worse comfort/compliance than a simple sling); encourage elbow/wrist/hand motion immediately, pendulum shoulder exercises as pain allows within the first 1–2 weeks, progressive active ROM once early callus is visible (~3–4 wk), avoid loaded overhead activity/contact sport until radiographic union (typically 6–10 wk in adults, faster in children/adolescents). Union rates nonoperative for displaced midshaft fractures reported ~95–97% in the classic COTS 2007 RCT population but functional/nonunion outcomes are patient-selection dependent (see Robinson 2B2 caveat above).

**Proximal humerus (1-part / minimally displaced, and select stable 2-/3-part in low-demand elderly)**: sling immobilization 1–3 weeks (comfort-driven, not strict immobilization dogma), early passive/pendulum ROM started within the first 1–2 weeks per BOAST-aligned pathways, progressing to active-assisted ROM around week 3–4 once early union/pain allows, then active and resisted strengthening from 6–8 weeks guided by radiographic progress. The **PROFHER trial** (JAMA 2015, long-term data through 2024) found no clinically or statistically significant difference in Oxford Shoulder Score between surgical (locking plate or hemiarthroplasty, surgeon's choice) and nonoperative treatment of displaced 2-/3-/4-part fractures in patients (mean age ~66) at 2 years and durable at 5 years — this remains the central evidence base justifying a nonoperative-first posture in older, lower-demand patients even with displaced fracture patterns, and underlies NICE/BOAST-aligned UK pathways favoring nonoperative management unless specific red flags (see Quick Reference) are present.

**Humeral shaft**: **Sarmiento functional bracing** is the nonoperative gold standard — apply after initial swelling subsides (days 1–2, sometimes after a brief period in a coaptation splint or hanging arm cast), circumferential brace compresses soft tissue to maintain alignment via hydraulic effect, worn until union (typically 8–12 weeks). Requires an intact soft-tissue envelope, patient compliance, and gravity-dependent positioning (poor candidate if unable to sit/stand upright regularly, obese/pendulous breast tissue, or transverse fracture pattern prone to distraction). Union rates 90–98% for isolated closed shaft fractures in Sarmiento's original series; lower for very proximal (near-metaphyseal) or very distal fractures where soft-tissue compression is less effective, and for segmental/comminuted (AO 12-C) patterns.

**Scapula body/spine (extra-articular, acceptable alignment per glenopolar angle/medialization thresholds)**: sling for comfort 1–2 weeks, early pendulum and passive ROM starting within the first week to prevent stiffness (scapulothoracic and glenohumeral stiffness is the dominant complication of prolonged immobilization here, not nonunion — scapula has excellent healing potential from its muscular envelope), progressive active ROM from 3–4 weeks, strengthening from 6 weeks.

**AC joint types I–III**: sling for comfort (typically discontinued by 1–2 weeks), early ROM, activity-based progression; return to contact sport typically 4–8 weeks for type I/II, 6–12 weeks for type III depending on symptom resolution and sport-specific demands.

**SC joint (anterior dislocation)**: closed reduction attempt is often unsuccessful/unstable to hold reduction — most anterior SC dislocations are managed symptomatically (sling, activity modification) even if some residual subluxation persists, since function is generally preserved and cosmetic deformity is the main residual issue.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

Corticosteroid or local anesthetic injection has a limited but real role in this cluster: **diagnostic/therapeutic AC joint injection** (steroid + local anesthetic under ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance) is useful both to confirm the AC joint as a pain generator in equivocal chronic AC arthrosis/post-traumatic cases and to provide symptomatic relief while deciding on delayed distal clavicle excision after a Neer III or low-grade AC injury with persistent symptoms. **Suprascapular nerve block** or interscalene regional block is a useful perioperative adjunct for postoperative analgesia after proximal humerus ORIF/arthroplasty or scapula fixation, reducing early opioid requirement. There is no evidence-based role for routine intra-articular steroid injection in acute fracture management of any diagnosis in this cluster (as opposed to chronic post-traumatic AC or GH arthrosis, which is outside this monograph's scope). Platelet-rich plasma and orthobiologic augmentation at the fracture site remain investigational for these fracture types and are not part of standard care pathways.

### Operative

**Clavicle (midshaft) — plating**: precontoured superior or anteroinferior locking plate remains the workhorse; anteroinferior position lower profile/less prominent hardware and avoids subclavian neurovascular structures during drilling but is technically less forgiving; superior plating gives better biomechanical purchase (greater bone stock) but higher symptomatic-hardware/removal rates (up to 20–30%) given subcutaneous location. Intramedullary devices (e.g., titanium elastic nails, cannulated screw systems) offer a minimally invasive alternative with smaller scars and easier removal, best suited to simple transverse/short-oblique patterns without significant comminution; not ideal for comminuted (Robinson 2B2) or very short medial/lateral fragments. Landmark RCT evidence (Canadian Orthopaedic Trauma Society 2007) showed operative plating of displaced midshaft fractures reduced nonunion (2.2% vs 15.1%) and improved early functional scores versus nonoperative treatment, driving a shift toward more liberal fixation of displaced/shortened/comminuted fractures over the last 15 years — but subsequent literature (see Evidence section) shows this functional advantage narrows by 1 year, reframing the decision as one of trading a small absolute nonunion-risk reduction and faster early recovery against a real reoperation/hardware-removal burden, to be individualized by patient factors (occupation, activity level, cosmetic priorities, comminution pattern).

**Distal clavicle (Neer II)**: options include hook plate (reliable reduction but requires a second surgery for removal at ~3–4 months due to subacromial impingement/rotator cuff irritation risk), coracoclavicular suture-button/suspensory fixation (avoids second surgery, biomechanically sound, technically demanding coracoid tunnel placement), or a combination (superior locking plate + CC augmentation) for comminuted/small lateral fragments.

**Proximal humerus — ORIF (locking plate)**: indicated in physiologically younger patients with reconstructable patterns (2-/3-part, and 4-part with intact medial hinge/calcar per Hertel criteria) via deltopectoral approach; technical pearls — restore the calcar screw trajectory (inferomedial screws supporting the head, "calcar screws," reduce varus collapse — the single most consistent predictor of fixation failure in the literature), avoid over-tightening/impingement of the plate under the greater tuberosity (place plate 5–8 mm below the top of the GT and posterior to the bicipital groove), use heavy non-absorbable suture cerclage through the rotator cuff tendon-bone junction (not just tuberosity bone, which is often osteoporotic and comminuted) for tuberosity fixation and augmentation, restore head height/version, obtain intraoperative fluoroscopy to confirm no screw penetration (dynamic rotation views essential — static AP/axillary alone under-detects penetration). Pitfalls: varus malreduction, screw cut-out/penetration in osteoporotic bone, AVN (higher with anatomic-neck involvement, disrupted medial hinge, head-splitting).

**Proximal humerus — intramedullary nailing**: favored for surgical-neck-predominant 2-part fractures, particularly in osteoporotic bone where a straight antegrade nail with multiplanar proximal locking (including calcar/inferomedial locking options in modern nail designs) provides load-sharing fixation through a smaller incision than plating; less soft-tissue stripping than an extensile deltopectoral plate approach. Entry point precision at the apex of the humeral head (avoid rotator cuff violation — split rather than detach supraspinatus) is critical to avoid iatrogenic cuff injury.

**Proximal humerus — hemiarthroplasty vs reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA)**: hemiarthroplasty was historically favored for head-splitting/unreconstructable fractures in younger patients with intact rotator cuff and glenoid, but has been progressively supplanted by rTSA in the elderly (>65–70y) because outcomes depend heavily on tuberosity healing in hemiarthroplasty (unpredictable, especially in osteoporotic comminuted tuberosities), whereas rTSA's semi-constrained mechanics render it far less dependent on tuberosity healing for a functional outcome. A 2024–2025 multicenter RCT (see Evidence section) demonstrated rTSA superiority over locked plate fixation at 2 years for displaced fractures in the elderly, reinforcing the trend toward rTSA as primary treatment (rather than salvage after failed ORIF) in appropriately selected older patients with unreconstructable 3-/4-part and head-splitting patterns. Technical pearls: restore the greater tuberosity around the prosthesis when possible (improves external rotation/abduction strength even with a constrained implant), lateralized/inferior glenosphere positioning to reduce scapular notching, avoid excessive humeral lengthening (risk of axillary nerve traction).

**Humeral shaft — plating (ORIF, compression or bridge plating, anterolateral or posterior approach)**: preferred when precise anatomic reduction and rotational control are paramount (segmental/comminuted 12-C patterns, associated radial nerve exploration needed, revision of failed nailing/nonunion), allows direct visualization/protection of the radial nerve during exposure. Anterolateral (Henry-type) approach for proximal/middle-third fractures; posterior (triceps-splitting) approach favored for distal-third fractures and when direct radial nerve exploration is planned. HUMMER multicenter cohort (2023) found plating gave faster shoulder function recovery and fewer implant-related reoperations than nailing but a higher rate of postoperative (iatrogenic) temporary radial nerve palsy (10.5% vs 0.6% in that cohort) — nerve identification and protection technique is the modifiable variable, not an inherent property of plating itself.

**Humeral shaft — intramedullary nailing (antegrade or retrograde)**: preferred for pathologic fractures (adds prophylactic fixation length), segmental fractures where plating both fragments is morbid, polytrauma (faster, less blood loss, earlier mobilization), and in some proximal-third or very distal patterns where plate fixation is technically difficult. Antegrade nailing risks rotator cuff injury at the entry portal (shoulder pain is the most common patient-reported complaint post-nailing, reported in up to 10–20%) and technique-dependent risk of iatrogenic fracture propagation/comminution at insertion; retrograde nailing avoids the shoulder but carries risk of supracondylar fracture at the entry site and elbow stiffness. Recent comparative literature (2023–2025 retrospective/registry cohorts) is mixed — some series show higher nonunion with plating versus nailing after propensity matching, contradicting older teaching that plating has a lower nonunion rate; radial nerve palsy risk appears lower with nailing in several recent series (nerve is not directly visualized/exposed) but nail insertion/reaming carries its own (lower but nonzero) iatrogenic palsy risk. Overall: **no single implant is unequivocally superior** — selection should be individualized to fracture morphology (segmental/comminuted favors plate for length/rotation control unless polytrauma logistics favor nail), surgeon experience, and whether radial nerve exploration is independently indicated.

**Radial nerve palsy — management algorithm**: 
- **Primary palsy** (present at initial injury, closed fracture, no penetrating mechanism): observe. Spontaneous recovery in ~70–90% within 3–4 months (higher for neurapraxia/axonotmesis from closed fractures, especially non-Holstein-Lewis patterns). Serial clinical exam ± baseline EMG/NCS at 6 weeks if no clinical recovery signs, to establish a baseline and help decide on exploration timing if no improvement by 3–4 months.
- **Open fracture with radial nerve palsy**: explore at the time of irrigation/debridement — direct visualization is safe and avoids missing a laceration.
- **Secondary palsy** (new palsy after closed reduction or cast/splint manipulation): explore promptly — high likelihood of nerve entrapment in the fracture site or between fragments.
- **No recovery by 3–4 months (primary palsy)**: obtain EMG/NCS and consider exploration; if surgical fixation of the fracture itself is otherwise indicated (nonunion, malalignment), combine nerve exploration with the fixation procedure.
- **At the time of any operative fixation for a fracture with radial nerve palsy**, always identify and protect (or explore if intraoperative findings suggest entrapment/laceration) the nerve regardless of palsy timing.

**Scapula/glenoid — ORIF**: posterior (Judet or modified minimally-invasive) approach for glenoid neck, scapular body, and posterior glenoid rim fractures; anterior (deltopectoral) approach for anterior glenoid rim fractures; arthroscopic-assisted fixation increasingly reported for anterior glenoid rim (bony Bankart-equivalent) fragments in the setting of instability, offering less soft-tissue morbidity. Buttress or spanning plate constructs for comminuted glenoid fossa (Ideberg VI) with articular reconstruction as the primary goal; consider primary rTSA in older patients with unreconstructable comminuted glenoid fossa fractures and poor bone quality.

**AC joint — surgical reconstruction (types IV–VI, selected III)**: acute repair (within 3 weeks) allows primary CC ligament repair augmented with suture-button/suspensory device (e.g., cortical button systems) or allograft/autograft tendon reconstruction (e.g., semitendinosus free graft reconstructing conoid and trapezoid limbs — the "Weaver-Dunn modification" or anatomic double-tunnel techniques); chronic/delayed presentations generally require ligament reconstruction (native ligament tissue is no longer usable) rather than primary repair, with somewhat inferior outcomes and higher loss-of-reduction rates than acute reconstruction — reinforcing that delayed high-grade AC injuries requiring surgery should not be deferred indefinitely.

**SC joint — posterior dislocation**: attempt closed reduction under general anesthesia (traction/abduction with a bolster between the scapulae, direct posteriorly-directed pressure or towel-clip reduction) with thoracic surgery on standby given risk of great vessel injury during manipulation; if closed reduction fails or the joint is unstable after reduction, open reduction with ligament/ meniscal disc repair or reconstruction (e.g., figure-of-8 free tendon graft reconstruction) is performed — avoid internal fixation with smooth pins across the SC joint (historically associated with catastrophic pin migration into the mediastinum/great vessels and is now considered obsolete/contraindicated).

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Clavicle (nonoperative and postoperative ORIF)**:
- **0–2 wk**: sling for comfort; elbow, wrist, hand active ROM immediately; pendulum shoulder exercises as tolerated.
- **2–6 wk**: discontinue sling as comfort allows; progressive active-assisted then active ROM, avoid resisted/loaded activity; radiographic check for early callus.
- **6–12 wk**: progressive strengthening once radiographic union present (~6–10 wk nonoperative, similar or slightly faster with stable ORIF); return to light manual work typically 6–8 wk, contact/overhead sport typically 10–12 wk and only after radiographic union confirmed.

**Proximal humerus (nonoperative)**:
- **0–2 wk**: sling, pendulum and passive-assisted forward flexion/external rotation (therapist- or table-supported) started early per BOAST-aligned pathways — early motion (within 1–2 weeks) improves short-term pain/function versus delayed immobilization (≥3–4 wk) though differences converge by 6–12 months per systematic review evidence.
- **3–6 wk**: active-assisted ROM progressing to active ROM as callus matures; discontinue sling for most activities.
- **6–12 wk**: resisted strengthening (rotator cuff and periscapular) once union is radiographically evident; avoid heavy loading before ~8–10 wk.
- **3–6 mo**: return to full unrestricted activity/sport once strength and ROM near-symmetric, and no residual instability.

**Proximal humerus (post-ORIF)**: similar timeline to nonoperative but often slightly accelerated passive ROM from day 1–2 (surgeon-dependent, informed by fixation stability and tuberosity repair integrity) with active-assisted ROM from 2–4 wk and resisted strengthening deferred to 8–12 wk to protect tuberosity healing.

**Proximal humerus (post-rTSA)**: passive/active-assisted ROM from day 1–2 (implant is inherently more stable than anatomic reconstruction), avoid active internal rotation/extension behind the back for 6 wk if subscapularis was repaired, active strengthening from 6 wk, full activity by 3–4 months; deltoid and rotator cuff (teres minor) strength are the main determinants of final functional outcome, not ROM alone.

**Humeral shaft (functional bracing or post-fixation)**: elbow, wrist, hand motion from day 0; shoulder pendulum exercises early; active shoulder/elbow ROM progressed as fracture stability allows (brace typically worn until radiographic union at 8–12 wk); resisted strengthening deferred until union confirmed; return to heavy lifting/contact sport typically 4–6 months.

**Scapula/glenoid**: sling for comfort 1–2 wk, early pendulum and passive ROM within the first week (stiffness is the dominant risk, not displacement, for extra-articular patterns), progressive active ROM from 2–4 wk, strengthening from 6 wk; intra-articular glenoid fixation follows a similar but slightly more conservative timeline respecting articular healing, with resisted loading deferred to ~8–10 wk.

**AC joint (nonoperative)**: sling for comfort (typically ≤1–2 wk), early ROM, progressive strengthening from 2–4 wk; return to contact sport 4–8 wk (type I–II) or 6–12 wk (type III) guided by symptom resolution and sport-specific demand (overhead/collision athletes at the longer end).

**AC joint (post-reconstruction)**: sling/abduction brace 4–6 wk protecting the CC reconstruction, passive/active-assisted ROM within brace limits from 2 wk, full active ROM from 6 wk, resisted strengthening from 8–12 wk, return to contact/overhead sport at 4–6 months once graft/construct maturation and full strength are confirmed.

**Return-to-work / return-to-sport general criteria across this cluster**: radiographic evidence of healing appropriate to the structure (cortical bridging for shaft fractures, trabecular consolidation for metaphyseal/proximal humerus fractures), painless active ROM within 10–20% of the contralateral side, strength within ~85–90% of contralateral on dynamometry for high-demand return-to-sport decisions, and no residual instability on clinical exam.

## Complications

- **Clavicle nonunion**: nonoperative displaced midshaft fractures historically ~15% (COTS RCT control arm), higher (reported up to 20–30%) in comminuted (Robinson 2B2) patterns; risk factors — displacement, comminution, smoking, older age, female sex, refracture. Operative fixation reduces nonunion to low single digits but does not eliminate it (hardware failure, infection-associated nonunion).
- **Clavicle malunion**: shortening/angulation can produce measurable scapular dyskinesis and reduced strength/endurance even without frank nonunion — a key rationale historically cited for fixing significantly shortened/displaced fractures, though its real-world functional impact is debated in recent literature.
- **Symptomatic hardware / need for removal (clavicle plating)**: reported in a substantial minority (up to 20–30% in some series) given the subcutaneous location — an important counseling point when discussing operative fixation for "borderline" displaced fractures.
- **Proximal humerus AVN**: strongly associated with anatomic-neck fracture line, disrupted medial hinge/calcar (<8 mm), and head-splitting patterns; may present late (months to years) — surveil at follow-up intervals beyond the initial healing window in high-risk patterns.
- **Proximal humerus screw cut-out/penetration, varus collapse**: the dominant ORIF-specific failure mode, especially in osteoporotic bone without calcar screw support — the single most preventable technical failure.
- **Axillary nerve injury**: from the initial fracture-dislocation itself or from surgical approach/retraction; usually neurapraxic, most recover over weeks to months but warrants documented baseline and follow-up exam.
- **Rotator cuff dysfunction / tuberosity malunion or nonunion**: after both nonoperative and operative treatment of tuberosity-involving fractures — a major driver of poor functional outcome independent of union status of the "fracture" per se.
- **Humeral shaft nonunion**: ~2–10% overall (varies by fixation/bracing and fracture pattern), higher in comminuted/segmental patterns, smokers, and NSAID users (theoretical/some clinical evidence for impaired healing); atrophic vs hypertrophic pattern guides revision strategy (compression plating ± bone graft for atrophic, often exchange nailing or improved mechanical stability alone suffices for hypertrophic).
- **Iatrogenic radial nerve injury (humeral shaft surgery)**: reported rates vary widely by approach/technique/implant in recent literature (roughly 0–10%+), generally higher with plating via approaches requiring extensive nerve mobilization; nerve identification and gentle handling are the modifiable factors.
- **Scapulothoracic dissociation** (not a simple fracture but a severe SSSC-pattern injury): high association with brachial plexus avulsion and vascular injury — limb salvage vs amputation decision-making required in severe cases; distinct from routine scapula fracture management.
- **AC joint post-traumatic arthrosis**: can develop after any grade of injury, including seemingly minor type I–II sprains, and after operative reconstruction; distal clavicle excision is the salvage procedure for refractory symptomatic cases.
- **Loss of reduction after AC reconstruction**: a recognized failure mode of suture-button/suspensory constructs, more common in chronic reconstructions than acute repairs.
- **SC joint chronic instability/pain**: more common after posterior dislocation with associated ligament disruption; mediastinal complications (rare but catastrophic) are the reason posterior SC dislocation is treated as a surgical/reduction emergency.
- **Infection**: overall low (<2–5%) across closed injuries treated with standard ORIF in this cluster, but higher with open fractures, prolonged operative time, and revision surgery; deep infection after proximal humerus arthroplasty is a feared complication given the difficulty of staged revision in this anatomic region.
- **Post-traumatic glenohumeral or AC osteoarthritis**: a long-term (years) complication of intra-articular glenoid step-off, tuberosity malunion altering joint mechanics, or persistent AC joint incongruity — patients should be counseled at the index visit that a "good" fracture outcome does not exclude late arthritic change.
- **Stiffness (adhesive capsulitis-like presentation)**: the most common complication overall across nonoperative proximal humerus, scapula, and AC joint injuries when immobilization is prolonged beyond the minimum required for comfort — reinforces the early-motion philosophy reflected throughout the Rehabilitation Protocol section.
- **Refracture**: reported after both nonoperative clavicle management and after premature hardware removal (clavicle plates); counsel patients to avoid contact/high-risk activity until full radiographic union, and to expect a period of relative bone weakness for several weeks after elective plate removal.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **PROFHER trial 5-year data (Handoll et al., long-term follow-up; widely cited through 2024–2025)** — confirmed the original 2-year finding of no clinically important difference in Oxford Shoulder Score between surgical (locked plating/hemiarthroplasty) and nonoperative management of displaced proximal humerus fractures in older adults, durable out to 5 years — continues to anchor nonoperative-first pathways in guideline documents. [ProFHER trial NIHR monograph](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK280157/)
- **"Young Shoulder CARE" trial protocol (Trials, 2025)** — a new pragmatic multicenter RCT specifically targeting the 50–65 year age group (a population underrepresented in PROFHER and other elderly-focused trials), randomizing osteosynthesis vs nonsurgical treatment for displaced proximal humerus fractures — signals an evidence gap the field is actively closing for higher-demand, younger-than-65 patients; registered NCT06416618 (May 2024), still enrolling/reporting as of 2025. [Young Shoulder CARE trial protocol, Trials (2025)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13063-025-09283-x)
- **RCT: reverse shoulder arthroplasty superior to locked plate fixation at 2 years for displaced proximal humeral fractures in the elderly** — a multicenter randomized trial reporting rTSA outcomes superior to ORIF at the 2-year mark, adding higher-quality (Level I) evidence supporting the ongoing shift toward primary rTSA over ORIF in appropriately selected older patients with unreconstructable patterns. [Long-term outcomes of reverse shoulder arthroplasty vs. locked plate fixation, J Shoulder Elbow Surg (2024)](https://www.jshoulderelbow.org/article/S1058-2746(24)00849-8/fulltext)
- **Long-term (extended) outcomes of reverse shoulder arthroplasty vs nonoperative treatment for 3-/4-part proximal humerus fractures in elderly patients (2024)** — follow-up of a prior RCT extending comparison of rTSA against nonoperative care, adding to the still-evolving picture of which elderly subgroups actually benefit from surgery versus benign neglect. [Long-term outcomes: rTSA vs nonoperative treatment, J Shoulder Elbow Surg (2024)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1058274624008498)
- **Systematic review/meta-analysis: rTSA vs locked plate fixation for proximal humeral fractures in the elderly (PLOS One, with 2025 correction)** — pooled analysis of ~10 comparative studies (244 plate, 287 rTSA patients) found that functional superiority of either treatment remains statistically unresolved even with high-powered trials, tempering the "rTSA is simply better" narrative and underscoring patient-specific selection rather than a blanket protocol shift. [rTSA vs locked plate fixation systematic review, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0317005)
- **HUMMER multicenter prospective cohort study (J Bone Joint Surg, 2023)** — direct comparison of plate osteosynthesis vs intramedullary nailing for humeral shaft fractures; plating gave faster shoulder function recovery and fewer implant-related reoperations, but significantly higher rates of postoperative (iatrogenic) temporary radial nerve palsy (10.5% vs 0.6%) — the most-cited contemporary data point clinicians now use when consenting patients for either fixation method. [HUMMER study, J Bone Joint Surg (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37220192/)
- **Propensity-matched comparison: nonunion and complications, IM nailing vs plate fixation of humeral shaft fractures (2025)** — found significantly higher nonunion risk with plating (ORIF) versus nailing after matching, and higher radial nerve palsy risk with ORIF (RR = 2.37) — challenges older teaching that plating is uniformly more reliable for union, adding nuance to the nail-vs-plate debate. [Nonunion and complications: IM nailing vs plate fixation, humeral shaft fractures (2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12614682/)
- **Systematic review: influence of posterior vs anterolateral approach on radial nerve palsy incidence after humeral shaft plating (2025)** — reports a high rate of iatrogenic radial nerve palsy after plating regardless of surgical approach chosen, arguing that meticulous intraoperative nerve handling — not approach selection alone — is the key modifiable factor. [Posterior vs anterolateral approach and radial nerve palsy, humeral shaft plating (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877056825001410)
- **Systematic review of humeral shaft fracture (OTA/AO 12) complicated by iatrogenic radial nerve injury (Eur J Med Res, 2024)** — consolidated contemporary incidence and recovery data for iatrogenic (surgery-related) radial nerve injury across fixation methods, useful benchmark for informed consent discussions. [Systematic review: humeral shaft fracture with iatrogenic radial nerve injury, Eur J Med Res (2024)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40001-024-01981-7)
- **Systematic review and meta-analysis: surgical vs nonsurgical management of displaced midshaft clavicle fractures (2025, ~10 studies including 8 RCTs)** — operative treatment showed superior early functional outcomes (higher Constant scores, lower DASH) and more rapid radiographic union with lower nonunion/malunion rates, but higher reoperation rate (largely symptomatic hardware); long-term functional scores and satisfaction converged between groups — reinforces that the operative-vs-nonoperative decision for midshaft clavicle fractures should weight early-recovery priorities and reoperation tolerance rather than assume a clear long-term winner. [Surgical vs non-surgical management of displaced midshaft clavicle fractures, systematic review and meta-analysis (2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12586730/)
- **"Temporal trends in the management of midshaft clavicle fractures" (systematic review, 2025)** — documents an ongoing real-world trend toward increased operative fixation rates for displaced midshaft clavicle fractures over the past decade, contextualizing practice patterns against the equipoise reflected in RCT/meta-analytic data above. [Temporal trends in management of midshaft clavicle fractures (2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12696431/)
- **Systematic review and meta-analysis: surgical vs conservative treatment of acute Rockwood type III AC joint dislocation (EFORT Open Reviews, 2025)** — concluded no clear literature consensus exists on optimal management of type III injuries; nonsurgical treatment reduces complications and speeds return to work, while surgery may better restore anatomy, with physically active young adults showing a possible slight outcome advantage with operative treatment — the most current synthesis clinicians can cite when counseling type III patients on shared decision-making. [Surgical vs conservative treatment of acute Rockwood III AC dislocation, EFORT Open Reviews (2025)](https://eor.bioscientifica.com/view/journals/eor/10/3/EOR-2024-0077.xml)
- **NHS Lothian proximal humeral fracture pathway guideline (updated September 2024)** — a regional BOAST-aligned guideline reflecting current UK practice: early physiotherapy referral, imaging pathway (trauma series with mandatory axillary view, CT for surgical planning), and criteria for orthopaedic surgical referral versus fracture-clinic-led nonoperative management — a practical, current template for institutional pathway design. [Proximal Humeral Fracture pathway guideline, NHS Lothian (Sept 2024)](https://apps.nhslothian.scot/files/sites/2/Proximal-guidelines-Sept-2024.pdf)
- **Rehabilitation protocols in proximal humerus fracture management: a systematic review (2024)** — synthesized the evidence on early versus delayed mobilization, concluding early physiotherapy improves pain and function in the short term with outcomes converging with delayed mobilization by 6–12 months — supports the early-motion-when-stable approach reflected in this monograph's rehabilitation section. [Rehabilitation protocols in proximal humerus fracture management, systematic review (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11437559/)
