## Quick Reference

**Red flags requiring immediate action**
- **Irritable hip + fever + refusal to weight-bear in a child** → treat as septic arthritis until excluded; do not defer aspiration while "trialing" NSAIDs if Kocher criteria are met.
- **Unstable SCFE (Loder)** — cannot walk even with crutches — is an orthopedic urgency; risk of osteonecrosis is high and rises further with delay or with attempted closed reduction/manipulation before fixation.
- **DDH missed beyond walking age** (>18 months, especially >8 years) shifts management from bracing/closed methods toward combined open reduction + pelvic/femoral osteotomy, with worse long-term outcome.
- **Bilateral, painless, symmetric limitation of hip abduction in an infant** — do not dismiss as "tight hips"; screen for DDH.
- **Any limp with hip or knee pain in a child** — always examine the hip; SCFE frequently presents as isolated knee pain via obturator nerve referral, and a missed stable slip can progress to unstable.

**Compact treatment ladder**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| DDH (0–6 mo) | Pavlik harness (Graf IIc–D, dislocated/dislocatable) | Rigid abduction orthosis (Tübingen, Frejka) for residual immaturity or mild dysplasia | Closed reduction + spica (harness failure or presentation 6–18 mo); open reduction ± pelvic/femoral osteotomy if closed fails or presentation late |
| Perthes | Symptomatic (NSAIDs, activity modification, physiotherapy, brief protected weight-bearing during synovitis/fragmentation) | Petrie casting / abduction bracing (historic, limited current use) | Containment surgery (femoral varus osteotomy, Salter/Pemberton acetabular osteotomy, shelf, or combined) in at-risk hips age >6–8y with lateral pillar B/B-C |
| SCFE (stable) | — (no non-operative option once diagnosed) | — | In-situ single-screw fixation (standard of care); prophylactic contralateral pinning individualized |
| SCFE (unstable) | — | — | Urgent in-situ pinning (gentle positional reduction only) vs modified Dunn (open reduction of head-neck deformity) at experienced centers — debate ongoing |
| Transient synovitis | Rest, NSAIDs, weight-bearing as tolerated, safety-net follow-up in 24–72h | Repeat exam/US if not improving by 48–72h | Hip aspiration if septic arthritis cannot be excluded |
| Femoral anteversion (idiopathic in-toeing) | Observation — natural history is spontaneous improvement to age ~8–10y in the vast majority | Gait training/physiotherapy (limited evidence, low harm) | Femoral derotational osteotomy reserved for severe (>50° anteversion), functionally limiting, persistent beyond age 8–10, usually adolescent |
| Coxa vara (developmental) | Observation if mild (neck-shaft angle >100–110°, Hilgenreiner-epiphyseal angle <45–60°) | Serial radiographic follow-up for progression | Valgus-producing subtrochanteric osteotomy if HEA >60°, progressive deformity, or neck-shaft angle <100–110° with limp/abductor lurch |

**Key numbers**
- DDH birth prevalence: dislocatable/dislocated hip on newborn exam ~1–2/1000 live births; instability on screening exam up to ~1–2%; risk factors (breech, family history, female sex, first-born, oligohydramnios) raise risk 5–10-fold.
- Graf α angle: normal (Type I) ≥60°; dysplastic threshold α <50° (Type IIc and worse) generally treated.
- Pavlik harness success: ~85–95% for Graf IIc/dislocatable hips treated <6 months of age; success falls substantially (<50%) in true teratologic/irreducible dislocations and with treatment initiated after 6–7 months.
- Avascular necrosis (AVN) risk with Pavlik use in a truly dislocated (non-reducible) hip held in forced abduction ("Human position" abuse) — hence the rule: if not reduced/reducing within 3–4 weeks, abandon the harness ("Pavlik disease" avoidance).
- Perthes prognosis by age at onset: onset <6 years generally has good remodeling potential regardless of treatment; onset ≥8 years and lateral pillar B/C carries the worst prognosis and is the group most likely to benefit from containment surgery.
- Herring lateral pillar C or age ≥8y at onset predicts Stulberg III–V (fair-to-poor) outcome in a majority of hips in classic natural-history cohorts.
- SCFE Southwick (head-shaft) angle severity: mild <30°, moderate 30–50°, severe >50° — informs in-situ vs corrective osteotomy decision-making.
- SCFE stability (Loder): stable = weight-bear with/without crutches (AVN risk ~0–5%); unstable = cannot weight-bear at all (AVN risk historically 20–50%+, main driver of long-term disability).
- Kocher criteria for septic hip (each present adds risk): non-weight-bearing, ESR >40 mm/h, fever >38.5°C, WBC >12,000/µL — 4/4 predicted ~93–99% probability of septic arthritis in the original derivation cohort (later validation cohorts show substantially lower predictive values; CRP >20 mg/L added in later Caird modification improves discrimination).
- Femoral anteversion: normal newborn ~30–40°, adult ~10–15°; idiopathic in-toeing from excess anteversion resolves spontaneously in the large majority by age 8–10 years — surgery is uncommon.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Hip joint development**: the acetabulum and proximal femur develop in intimate mechanical interdependence beginning in utero; normal development requires a concentrically reduced femoral head providing a spherical stimulus to the triradiate cartilage and acetabular growth plate. Any mechanical factor that allows femoral head subluxation/dislocation in utero or the neonatal period (breech positioning, oligohydramnios, swaddling with hips extended and adducted, female hormonal ligamentous laxity) removes this stimulus and produces progressive acetabular dysplasia — shallow, sloped acetabular roof, redundant/hypertrophied ligamentum teres, inverted labrum, and a thickened, constricting iliopsoas tendon that can convert a reducible hip into an irreducible one (hourglass constriction of the capsule).

**DDH spectrum**: ranges from mild acetabular dysplasia (concentrically located but shallow socket) → subluxation (partial loss of femoral head coverage) → frank dislocation (complete loss of contact, femoral head riding on ilium). "Teratologic" dislocation (present and fixed in utero, associated with neuromuscular conditions such as arthrogryposis or myelomeningocele) is pathophysiologically distinct from typical/positional DDH and responds poorly to harness treatment.

**Perthes disease**: idiopathic avascular necrosis of the proximal femoral epiphysis in the growing child, attributed to disruption of the tenuous blood supply (predominantly the lateral epiphyseal vessels, branches of the medial femoral circumflex artery, which are vulnerable during the age window before a mature retinacular and ligamentum teres supply develops). The disease is self-limited but passes through four Waldenström radiographic stages (initial/necrosis, fragmentation, reossification, healed/remodeling) over roughly 2–4 years; the femoral head is biomechanically vulnerable to deformation during fragmentation when it is soft and the child continues to load it — this is the physiological basis for "containment" (keeping the softened head well-covered by the acetabulum, which acts as a mold, during the vulnerable window).

**SCFE**: a Salter-Harris type I fracture through the proximal femoral physis, with the epiphysis remaining in the acetabulum (tethered by the ligamentum teres and periosteum) while the metaphysis displaces anteriorly and externally rotates relative to the epiphysis — so radiographically the "head slips posteriorly/inferiorly" relative to the neck. Occurs during the adolescent growth spurt when the physis is mechanically weakest (relative physeal widening, increased shear stress from a more vertically oriented physis, and endocrine influences that reduce physeal shear strength — hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency/treatment, renal osteodystrophy, and obesity-associated relative growth hormone/estrogen effects). Peak age 11–14 (girls) / 12–16 (boys); obesity is the single strongest modifiable/associated risk factor. Bilaterality at presentation ~20–25%, with a further ~20–40% of unilateral presenters going on to develop a contralateral slip (highest risk in the youngest patients and those with open triradiate cartilage), which underlies the ongoing prophylactic-pinning debate.

**Transient synovitis**: a self-limited, presumed post-viral or reactive synovitis of the hip, most common ages 3–8, causing an effusion and irritable hip; must be distinguished from septic arthritis, which is a surgical emergency because residual cartilage/physeal damage from untreated pyogenic infection can be permanent within days.

**Femoral anteversion and coxa vara**: femoral anteversion is the normal (if exaggerated) angle between the femoral neck axis and the transcondylar axis of the distal femur, physiologically decreasing from ~30–40° at birth to ~10–15° in adulthood as gait-related torsional forces remodel the femur; persistent excess anteversion produces in-toeing gait, compensatory increased hip internal rotation/decreased external rotation, and is largely a self-correcting variant of normal rather than a disease. Developmental coxa vara is a distinct entity — a defect of ossification/growth at the inferomedial femoral neck physis producing progressive varus angulation (decreased neck-shaft angle) and a vertically oriented, unstable physis (increased Hilgenreiner-epiphyseal angle) that, if untreated, can progress to a Trendelenburg gait, limb-length discrepancy, and eventual femoral neck stress fracture/non-union of the physis.

**Associated conditions worth actively screening for at diagnosis**

| Diagnosis | Association | Action |
|---|---|---|
| DDH | Congenital muscular torticollis, metatarsus adductus, positional plagiocephaly ("intrauterine packaging" cluster) | Examine hips carefully in any infant with one of these findings even without classic DDH risk factors |
| Perthes | Lower birth weight, second-hand smoke exposure, and (in some cohorts, not consistently replicated) mild coagulopathy/thrombophilia associations | Not diagnostic triggers but relevant to counseling; no established indication for routine thrombophilia work-up outside atypical/bilateral synchronous presentations |
| SCFE | Obesity (strongest association), hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency/treatment, renal osteodystrophy, panhypopituitarism | Endocrine work-up as above for atypical presentations |
| Coxa vara | Skeletal dysplasias (e.g. cleidocranial dysplasia), fibrous dysplasia, prior proximal femoral fracture/physeal injury | Consider syndromic/metabolic work-up if bilateral or associated with other skeletal findings |

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**DDH risk factors and relative screening priority**

| Risk factor | Approximate relative risk | Screening implication |
|---|---|---|
| Breech presentation (especially frank breech, third trimester) | ~5–10x baseline | Ultrasound regardless of clinical exam findings — most consistently endorsed universal-selective-screening indication |
| Positive family history (parent or sibling with DDH) | ~2–5x baseline | Ultrasound at 4–6 weeks even with normal exam |
| Female sex | ~4–5x baseline (girls comprise ~80% of cases) | Contributes to risk stratification, not an isolated screening trigger on its own |
| First-born | ~elevated, additive with other factors | Considered alongside other risk factors, not isolated trigger |
| Oligohydramnios / intrauterine packaging factors (e.g. torticollis, metatarsus adductus co-occurrence) | Elevated, variable | Prompts careful clinical exam; ultrasound if any exam equivocality |
| Positive clinical exam (Barlow/Ortolani-positive or limited abduction) | Diagnostic, not just risk factor | Ultrasound or radiograph (age-dependent) confirmatory imaging and treatment pathway |

**DDH**
- Newborn exam: Barlow test (adducts and posteriorly loads a located hip to elicit dislocation) and Ortolani test (abducts and anteriorly lifts a dislocated hip to reduce it with a palpable "clunk"); both lose sensitivity after 2–3 months as capsular/soft-tissue contracture sets in.
- After ~3 months: look for limited hip abduction (most reliable sign in this age group), asymmetric thigh/gluteal skin folds (low specificity alone), apparent limb-length discrepancy, positive Galeazzi sign (knee height asymmetry with hips/knees flexed — implies unilateral dislocation or true limb-length difference), and, once walking, a Trendelenburg gait or lumbar hyperlordosis (bilateral disease).
- Imaging: ultrasound is the modality of choice <4–6 months (cartilaginous femoral head not yet visible on plain film); anteroposterior pelvis radiograph becomes reliable once the femoral capital ossific nucleus appears (~4–6 months), assessed by acetabular index, Shenton's line continuity, and IHDI grade.
- Screening strategy: universal clinical newborn exam is standard of care essentially everywhere; universal ultrasound screening (as practiced in parts of Central Europe, e.g. the Graf-originated Austrian/German model) versus selective ultrasound screening of risk-factor-positive infants (breech presentation, positive family history, clinical instability) remains debated — most English-speaking guideline bodies (AAP, current UK NIPE-linked pathways) favor selective ultrasound targeted at risk factors and equivocal exams over universal ultrasound, citing higher over-treatment/cost with universal screening without clearly demonstrated reduction in late-presenting dislocation; several continental European centers and payers continue universal or near-universal ultrasound protocols. A pragmatic approach used widely: clinical exam on all newborns + selective ultrasound for breech presentation (regardless of exam), positive family history, and any exam abnormality, at 4–6 weeks of age.

**Screening strategy comparison by region/guideline body**

| Approach | Typical adopters | Rationale | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal clinical exam (all newborns, repeated at well-child visits) | Essentially universal standard of care | Low-cost, high-yield for frank dislocation | Misses subtle dysplasia not yet clinically detectable |
| Selective ultrasound (risk-factor or exam-triggered) | AAP-aligned US practice, UK NIPE-linked pathways | Targets highest-yield population, limits over-treatment/cost | Can miss dysplasia in infants without recognized risk factors |
| Universal ultrasound screening | Austria, parts of Germany/Central Europe (Graf-originated model) | Maximizes early detection sensitivity | Higher rate of over-diagnosis/over-treatment of physiologically immature hips that would mature spontaneously; resource-intensive |
| Delayed selective imaging (radiograph at walking age for at-risk-but-normal-exam infants) | Adjunct in several systems as a safety net | Catches late-declaring dysplasia missed by early screening | Later diagnosis carries worse prognosis than early detection |

For a Romanian/broader Central-European practice, awareness of both the AAP-aligned selective-screening literature and the regional universal-ultrasound tradition is valuable, since national and institutional protocols vary and both approaches appear in referral patterns.

**Perthes**
- Presentation: insidious-onset limp (often painless or mildly painful), age typically 4–10 years (peak 5–7), male predominance (~4–5:1), bilateral in ~10–20% (usually asynchronous — synchronous bilateral disease should prompt work-up for an underlying skeletal dysplasia or hematologic condition, e.g. sickle cell, Gaucher, hypothyroidism).
- Exam: restricted internal rotation and abduction, positive Drehmann sign (obligate external rotation with passive hip flexion — shared with SCFE), abductor lurch/Trendelenburg.
- Imaging: AP and frog-leg lateral pelvis radiographs are the mainstay for staging (Waldenström) and prognostic classification (lateral pillar); MRI (including perfusion-weighted sequences) is increasingly used early for extent-of-involvement and revascularization assessment, and arthrography/MR arthrography intraoperatively or preoperatively to assess containment and femoral head sphericity/hinge abduction.

**SCFE**
- Presentation: adolescent, often obese, with a limp and hip, groin, thigh, or referred knee pain (always examine the hip in a child presenting with isolated knee pain); acute-on-chronic presentations (background chronic symptoms with an acute worsening, often after minor trauma) are the most common pattern and behave more like unstable/acute slips regarding AVN risk.
- Exam: obligate external rotation of the hip with passive flexion (Drehmann sign), loss of internal rotation, antalgic or externally rotated foot-progression gait; assess weight-bearing ability with and without assistance to determine Loder stability, which is the single most important prognostic and management-defining variable.
- Imaging: AP pelvis and frog-leg or true lateral (cross-table lateral preferred in a painful/unstable hip — do NOT force a frog-leg lateral in a suspected unstable slip, as positioning itself can displace the epiphysis further) — measure Southwick head-shaft angle bilaterally, assess Klein's line (line along superior femoral neck should intersect a portion of the epiphysis; failure to intersect suggests slip). Screen the contralateral hip at every visit — early/subtle contralateral slips are easily missed.
- Atypical SCFE presentations warrant endocrine/metabolic work-up: age <10 years, age >16 years, weight below the 50th percentile (i.e. a non-obese SCFE), short stature, or bilateral synchronous presentation should prompt screening for hypothyroidism (TSH, free T4), growth hormone deficiency or its treatment, renal osteodystrophy (renal function panel), and, less commonly, panhypopituitarism — these children behave differently (more often bilateral, more often atypical age) and require coordinated pediatric endocrinology follow-up alongside orthopedic management.

**Transient synovitis vs septic arthritis (Kocher criteria)** — the central differential in the acutely irritable pediatric hip:

| Kocher predictor | Points toward septic arthritis |
|---|---|
| Non-weight-bearing | Present |
| ESR >40 mm/h | Present |
| Fever (temperature >38.5°C) | Present |
| WBC >12,000 cells/mm³ | Present |
| (Caird modification) CRP >20 mg/L | Present — improves discrimination, especially with 1 other criterion positive |

Predicted probability of septic arthritis in the original Kocher derivation cohort: 0 criteria ~<0.2%, 1 criterion ~3%, 2 criteria ~40%, 3 criteria ~93%, 4 criteria ~99%. Subsequent external validation cohorts (including the Caird CRP-modified rule) have consistently shown lower predictive values than the original derivation, particularly at the lower criterion counts, so the rule should inform — not replace — clinical judgment; ultrasound-confirmed effusion with a low pretest probability and improving trend can be managed with close observation, while any diagnostic uncertainty with a tense effusion warrants aspiration (cell count, Gram stain, culture — synovial WBC >50,000–100,000/µL with PMN predominance strongly favors septic arthritis, though partially treated or Kingella kingae infections may present with lower counts). Kingella kingae is an increasingly recognized cause of pediatric septic arthritis, particularly under age 4, often with a blunted inflammatory response (lower fever, normal/mildly elevated ESR/CRP) that can produce false reassurance on Kocher scoring — PCR-based synovial fluid testing improves yield over culture alone.

**Femoral anteversion / coxa vara**
- Anteversion assessed clinically by internal/external rotation arc with the child prone, knees flexed 90° (Craig's/trochanteric prominence angle test); increased anteversion produces increased internal rotation (often >70°) with reduced external rotation. CT or MRI rotational profile reserved for surgical planning in severe, persistent cases being considered for osteotomy.
- Coxa vara assessed radiographically: neck-shaft angle and Hilgenreiner-epiphyseal angle (HEA, angle between Hilgenreiner's line and a line along the proximal femoral physis) — HEA is the more reliable predictor of progression; HEA >60° predicts progressive deformity requiring surgery in the large majority of reported series, while HEA <45° often stabilizes or improves with growth.

**Differential diagnosis of the acutely painful/limping pediatric hip** (framework for triage before committing to a single diagnosis):

| Diagnosis | Typical age | Key discriminators |
|---|---|---|
| Transient synovitis | 3–8y | Afebrile or low-grade fever, well-appearing, Kocher criteria low, improves within days |
| Septic arthritis | Any, esp. <4y | Toxic/unwell, high fever, refusal to weight-bear, elevated inflammatory markers, tense effusion |
| SCFE | 10–16y (peak adolescent growth spurt) | Obligate external rotation with flexion, obese habitus common, may present as isolated knee pain |
| Perthes | 4–10y (peak 5–7) | Insidious, often painless or mild limp, restricted internal rotation/abduction |
| Osteomyelitis (proximal femur/pelvis) | Any | May coexist with septic arthritis; point tenderness over metaphysis, MRI most sensitive |
| Toxic/reactive arthritis post-viral | Variable | Recent viral illness, polyarticular pattern possible, self-limited |
| Juvenile idiopathic arthritis | Variable, often <16y | Morning stiffness, other joints involved, chronicity >6 weeks |
| Leukemia/malignancy | Any | Night pain, systemic symptoms, cytopenias, disproportionate pain to exam findings |
| Discitis/pelvic osteomyelitis | <5y typically | Refusal to sit/stand, back pain, low-grade fever, elevated ESR/CRP more than WBC |
| Occult fracture (toddler's fracture, apophyseal avulsion) | Any | Clear mechanism/trauma history, point tenderness, radiographs first-line |

## Classification

**Graf classification (hip ultrasound, static/morphological method)**

| Type | α angle | β angle | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | ≥60° | <55° (Ia) or >55° (Ib) | Mature, normal hip | No treatment |
| IIa (physiologically immature, <12 wk) | 50–59° | variable | Immature bony rim, still developing | Re-scan at 4–6 weeks; treat if not matured by ~12 weeks |
| IIa(-)/IIb | 50–59° | variable | Immature/deficient at an age where maturation should have occurred (IIb = same angles but ≥12 weeks old) | Treat (harness) if IIb or IIa(-) with risk factors |
| IIc | 43–49° | <77° | Critical zone — significantly deficient bony roof, still (usually) located | Treat — high risk of progression to subluxation/dislocation |
| D | 43–49° | ≥77° | Decentered/subluxated | Treat — harness or reduction |
| III | <43° | — | Dislocated, cartilage roof displaced, echogenic (compressed) | Treat — reduction (harness if young/reducible, otherwise closed/open reduction) |
| IV | <43° | — | Dislocated, cartilage roof interposed/displaced further, everted labrum | Treat — reduction, often requires closed/open reduction rather than harness |

**IHDI (International Hip Dysplasia Institute) classification** — standardizes the historical Tönnis grading of femoral head position on AP pelvis radiograph relative to Perkin's and Hilgenreiner's lines, designed for better inter-observer reliability:

| Grade | Femoral head ossific nucleus position | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Medial to Perkin's line, below/at level expected for a located hip | Normal/located — observation or continue harness monitoring |
| II | Medial to Perkin's line but above the acetabular roof line (mild proximal migration) | Mild subluxation — usually still amenable to harness/closed methods |
| III | Lateral to Perkin's line, below the level of the triradiate cartilage | Significant dislocation — closed reduction typically required |
| IV | Lateral to Perkin's line, at or above the level of the iliac crest (highest dislocation) | Severe/high dislocation — often requires open reduction ± femoral shortening |

**Tönnis classification (dislocation grading, radiographic, historically widely used alongside/underlying IHDI)**

| Grade | Description | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ossific nucleus medial to Perthes-Perkin line, mild lateral displacement | Least severe — highest closed-reduction success |
| 2 | Ossific nucleus at level of acetabular roof | Moderate — closed reduction usually feasible |
| 3 | Ossific nucleus at level of the ilium | Marked — closed reduction success decreases |
| 4 | Ossific nucleus above the level of the ilium (highest dislocation) | Most severe — highest rate requiring open reduction |

**Herring lateral pillar classification (Perthes, radiographic, based on height of the lateral 1/3 of the femoral epiphysis on AP radiograph during early fragmentation stage)** — the primary prognostic and treatment-selection tool in current practice:

| Group | Lateral pillar height | Prognosis / management implication |
|---|---|---|
| A | No loss of height, full lateral pillar integrity | Excellent prognosis regardless of age or treatment — observation, no containment surgery needed |
| B | >50% of original lateral pillar height maintained | Good prognosis if age <8y at onset; containment surgery favored if age ≥8y or if features of "head-at-risk" present (Catterall head-at-risk signs: Gage sign, lateral calcification, lateral subluxation, horizontal physis, metaphyseal cysts) |
| B/C border (<20% height loss but poorly ossified/narrow central pillar) | Intermediate | Treated as B or C depending on additional risk features and surgeon judgment — described by Herring as a distinct borderline group with intermediate outcomes |
| C | <50% of original lateral pillar height maintained | Worst prognosis — containment surgery generally offered, especially age ≥8y, though benefit is debated even with surgery in the most severe C hips |

**Waldenström stages (Perthes, radiographic natural history of the disease process itself, not a treatment-selection tool but essential for staging and timing of intervention)**

| Stage | Findings | Duration (approx.) | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial (necrosis) | Widened joint space, subchondral fracture line may be seen, smaller/denser epiphysis | Weeks to ~6 months | Diagnosis often made here; head not yet at maximal deformation risk |
| Fragmentation | Epiphysis fragments radiographically (resorption of necrotic bone), most vulnerable to deformation under load | ~6 months to 1 year | Highest-risk window for femoral head deformation — containment decisions/surgery timed to precede or occur during this stage |
| Reossification (healing) | New bone forms progressively from periphery inward | ~1–3 years | Head shape becomes progressively fixed; late containment surgery has diminishing benefit |
| Healed (remodeling) | Reossification complete; further remodeling of head shape possible with growth remaining | Until skeletal maturity | Residual deformity assessed by Stulberg classification |

**Stulberg classification (Perthes, final outcome at skeletal maturity, congruency and sphericity of femoral head vs acetabulum)**

| Class | Description | Long-term implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Spherical head, normal joint (essentially normal hip) | Minimal to no increased risk of premature OA |
| II | Spherical head but with coxa magna, short femoral neck, or acetabular abnormality — still spherically congruent | Good long-term outcome, low OA risk |
| III | Non-spherical (ovoid) head, spherically incongruent, but congruent-shaped joint overall | Moderate risk of OA typically in the 5th decade or later |
| IV | Flat femoral head, congruent with a correspondingly flattened acetabulum | Higher OA risk, earlier onset |
| V | Flat femoral head, incongruent with a normally round acetabulum (worst mechanical mismatch) | Highest OA risk, earliest and most severe degeneration |

**Loder classification (SCFE stability, clinical, the single most important classification for management and prognosis)**

| Type | Definition | AVN risk | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stable | Patient can walk, with or without crutches, weight-bearing on the affected limb | ~0–5% | In-situ pinning, generally can be scheduled semi-urgently (within 24–48h) rather than as a middle-of-the-night emergency |
| Unstable | Patient cannot walk or bear weight on the affected limb at all, even with crutches | Historically reported 20–50%+ | Surgical urgency (same-day/urgent); gentle positional reduction with in-situ pinning is the most widely practiced approach; modified Dunn procedure is an alternative at high-volume centers — see Treatment/Operative for the debate |

**Southwick head-shaft angle (SCFE severity grading, radiographic, measured on frog-leg lateral, difference between affected and normal contralateral head-shaft angle)**

| Severity | Angle (difference from normal side) | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | <30° | In-situ fixation adequate; minimal risk of femoroacetabular impingement from deformity |
| Moderate | 30–50° | In-situ fixation still generally favored; some surgeons consider concurrent or staged osteochondroplasty for anticipated impingement |
| Severe | >50° | Highest risk of residual cam-type femoroacetabular impingement after simple in-situ fixation; candidate group for corrective osteotomy (subcapital/Dunn-type or subtrochanteric/Imhäuser-type) at experienced centers, weighed against the higher AVN risk of head-neck realignment procedures |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**DDH — Pavlik harness protocol**: indicated for Graf IIc and worse (dislocatable, subluxable, or dislocated but reducible) hips, typically initiated before 6 months of age (efficacy drops substantially with older presentation). The harness holds the hip in flexion (100–110°, avoiding hyperflexion which risks femoral nerve palsy) and abduction (no more than ~60°, avoiding forced/extreme abduction which risks avascular necrosis) via the "human position" — it works by dynamic muscle-driven reduction, not passive splinting, so the child must be allowed to kick. Weekly clinical and ultrasound follow-up initially to confirm reduction; the harness is generally continued full-time until Graf I maturity is documented, then weaned to part-time/nighttime use. Documented failure to reduce (persistent dislocation) by 3–4 weeks of harness wear is an indication to abandon the harness rather than persist — continued forced-abduction positioning of an irreducible hip is the classic mechanism for iatrogenic AVN. Contraindicated/likely to fail in teratologic dislocations (arthrogrypotic, myelomeningocele-associated), which should proceed directly to alternative closed or open methods. Adjunct rigid abduction orthoses (Tübingen splint, Frejka pillow) are used in some centers for milder immaturity/dysplasia (Graf IIa-/IIb) as an alternative to or step-down from the Pavlik harness, and a 2024 systematic review found broadly comparable success rates between Tübingen splint and Pavlik harness across Graf grades, supporting either as reasonable first-line options depending on local expertise (Nair et al., J Orthop 2024).

**Pavlik failure management**: defined as persistent instability/dislocation despite adequate harness wear, or a hip that reduces but re-dislocates on attempted weaning. Options include: reapplication of the Pavlik harness for a further trial in carefully selected cases (a 2024 series found meaningful additional success with a second harness trial rather than automatically escalating to closed/open reduction) (Tomaru et al., J Pediatr Orthop 2024); switching to a rigid abduction orthosis; or proceeding to closed reduction under arthrography with spica casting (typically age 6 weeks–18 months), reserving open reduction (medial or anterior/Smith-Petersen approach, with concomitant psoas tenotomy for the constricting iliopsoas and capsulorrhaphy) for hips that fail closed reduction, present after walking age, or have interposed soft tissue (labrum, pulvinar, ligamentum teres, transverse acetabular ligament) blocking concentric reduction on arthrogram. Manual/osteopathic therapy as an adjunct after Pavlik failure has been described in small series but is not an evidence-based substitute for definitive reduction (Giorgi et al., J Man Manip Ther 2024).

**Perthes — non-operative/symptomatic management**: for lateral pillar A hips (any age) and lateral pillar B hips in children <8 years, symptomatic management is the mainstay — activity modification and protected/limited weight-bearing during the acute synovitic and early fragmentation phases, NSAIDs, and physiotherapy focused on maintaining hip abduction and internal rotation range of motion (the range most threatened by capsular contracture and most important for containment). Petrie casting (long-leg abduction casts) and formal abduction bracing were historically used to maintain containment non-operatively but have fallen out of favor at most centers due to poor compliance, skin/growth morbidity, and a 2026 longitudinal quality-of-life study finding no clear functional advantage over less restrictive management (De Hoyos et al., J Pediatr Orthop 2026) — most contemporary North American and European pediatric orthopedic units reserve bracing/casting for a small subset of patients and favor either simple symptomatic care for good-prognosis groups or surgical containment for at-risk hips rather than an intermediate non-operative "containment" device.

**Transient synovitis — observation and safety-netting protocol**: for a well-appearing, afebrile or low-grade febrile child with a low pretest probability by Kocher criteria and a reassuring ultrasound (effusion present but no other red flags), a structured trial of outpatient observation is appropriate and avoids unnecessary aspiration — scheduled (not PRN) NSAIDs for 3–5 days, weight-bearing as tolerated, explicit safety-netting instructions to return immediately for worsening pain, spiking fever, or inability to weight-bear, and a mandatory clinical re-check at 48–72 hours if not substantially improved. Symptoms typically peak within the first 24–48 hours and resolve within 1–2 weeks; a child who is not clearly improving by the 48–72 hour mark should be re-investigated (repeat inflammatory markers, repeat ultrasound, consider aspiration) rather than continuing to observe, since a genuinely improving trend is the strongest reassurance that septic arthritis has been correctly excluded.

**SCFE**: there is no accepted non-operative pathway once the diagnosis is confirmed — even a mildly symptomatic, stable, minimally displaced slip is treated as a surgical diagnosis because of the risk of progression to a more severe or unstable slip. The only "non-operative" element is protected non-weight-bearing/urgent immobilization while arranging surgery.

**Femoral anteversion**: observation is first-line and appropriate for the overwhelming majority — natural remodeling with growth (femoral anteversion decreases progressively through childhood) resolves the in-toeing gait in most children by age 8–10. Gait training and physiotherapy are sometimes offered but have limited high-quality evidence of accelerating resolution; they carry minimal harm and may be reasonable for family reassurance/functional coaching. Shoe inserts, twister cables, and night bracing/splints have not been shown to alter the natural history and are not recommended by most contemporary pediatric orthopedic guidance.

**Coxa vara**: mild, non-progressive deformity (HEA <45–60°, minimal limp) can be observed with serial radiographs at 6–12-month intervals through the period of most rapid growth, since a subset stabilizes or improves.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

**Hip aspiration** (diagnostic and therapeutic) is the central adjunctive procedure across this cluster — indicated whenever septic arthritis cannot be confidently excluded in an irritable pediatric hip (equivocal Kocher score, tense effusion on ultrasound, clinical deterioration on observation). Performed under ultrasound guidance or fluoroscopy, typically anterior or lateral approach; synovial fluid sent for cell count/differential, Gram stain, culture, and (where available) PCR panel including Kingella kingae, particularly in children under 4 given its atypical, blunted inflammatory presentation.

**Arthrography** is used intraoperatively (and sometimes preoperatively) in DDH to confirm concentric reduction quality, identify soft-tissue blocks to reduction (inverted labrum, hypertrophied ligamentum teres, constricted capsule), and in Perthes to assess femoral head sphericity and containment/hinge-abduction dynamically under fluoroscopy — this directly informs the choice and adequacy of containment surgery.

**Intra-articular corticosteroid injection** has no established, guideline-endorsed role in the routine management of any diagnosis in this cluster (unlike adult hip OA) — Perthes synovitis is managed with rest/NSAIDs rather than injection, and SCFE/DDH are mechanical-structural problems not addressed by injection therapy.

### Operative

**DDH — closed reduction and spica casting**: performed under general anesthesia with arthrography to confirm concentric, stable reduction; the "safe zone" of abduction (the arc between the angle of redislocation on adduction and the angle of subluxation/impingement on excessive abduction) should be reasonably wide (generally regarded as safe if a stable arc of at least ~20° exists) before accepting the reduction — a narrow safe zone predicts recurrent instability and should prompt adjunctive psoas tenotomy or reconsideration of open reduction. Spica cast in a "human position" (avoiding extremes of abduction to protect against AVN) for approximately 12 weeks, generally with a cast change under anesthesia at 6 weeks, followed by transition to an abduction orthosis. A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of late-presentation DDH found comparable long-term acetabular remodeling outcomes between well-executed closed and open reduction when concentric reduction is achieved, reinforcing that reduction quality — not method alone — is the dominant driver of outcome (Benaroch et al., Ann Med Surg 2025).

*Pearls and pitfalls*: obtain a post-reduction CT or MRI (not just a static radiograph) to confirm the reduction is truly concentric before committing to a 6–12 week cast — a subtly subluxated "reduction" left uncorrected is a common source of late residual dysplasia. Percutaneous adductor longus tenotomy is a low-morbidity adjunct that widens the safe zone in tight hips and should be used liberally rather than accepting a narrow arc. Avoid the "frog-leg"/forced-abduction extreme when molding the cast — this is the single most preventable cause of iatrogenic AVN in this pathway.

**DDH — open reduction ± osteotomy**: indicated when closed reduction fails, in children presenting after walking age (roughly >18 months, though thresholds vary by center), or when a narrow safe zone/interposed soft tissue precludes stable closed reduction. Anterior (Smith-Petersen) approach is most common for isolated open reduction in infants/toddlers; medial (Ludloff) approach is used by some centers in very young infants to avoid violating the abductor musculature, at the cost of a higher reported AVN risk in some series and a blind view of the acetabulum. Concomitant procedures selected by age and residual deformity: femoral shortening derotation osteotomy (reduces pressure on the reduced head, corrects excess femoral anteversion, and allows reduction without excessive tension in older/higher dislocations — generally added for Tönnis III–IV or IHDI III–IV dislocations and for children walking >2 years); pelvic osteotomy for persistent acetabular dysplasia — Salter innominate osteotomy (redirectional, requires a congruent, spherical femoral head and open triradiate cartilage, single-plane correction) versus Pemberton acetabuloplasty (incomplete osteotomy hinging on the triradiate cartilage, reshapes rather than redirects the acetabulum, useful for more severe dysplasia) versus Dega/San Diego osteotomy (more versatile multi-planar reshaping, popular for neuromuscular and syndromic dysplasia as well as residual DDH dysplasia); triple innominate or Ganz periacetabular osteotomy reserved for the older child/adolescent with closed/closing triradiate cartilage and residual dysplasia.

*Pearls and pitfalls*: through an anterior approach, release the iliopsoas at the pelvic brim (not through the joint) to avoid injuring the medial femoral circumflex vessels that supply the femoral head; excise a redundant/hypertrophied ligamentum teres and inverted labrum only as needed to achieve concentric reduction — over-aggressive capsulorrhaphy can itself restrict remodeling. When combining femoral shortening with pelvic osteotomy in a high (Tönnis IV/IHDI IV) dislocation, resist the temptation to fully correct limb length in one stage — modest intentional shortening reduces reduction tension and AVN risk, and can be revisited later if a residual discrepancy proves clinically significant.

**Perthes — containment surgery**: the guiding principle is to reposition either the femur (varus derotation osteotomy, redirecting the anterolateral epiphysis into the acetabular socket) or the acetabulum (Salter or Pemberton osteotomy, redirecting/reshaping the socket over the femoral head) — or both in combined procedures for more severe deformity/subluxation — so that the deforming/fragmenting femoral head is contained within a spherical mold during the vulnerable fragmentation stage, with the goal of preserving sphericity and achieving a Stulberg I–II outcome. Candidacy is driven by the Herring lateral pillar classification combined with age at onset: the strongest, most consistent indication is lateral pillar B (or the B/C border) in a child ≥8 years at onset, a group in which multiple natural-history cohorts show containment surgery improves the odds of a spherical/congruent outcome compared with non-operative management; lateral pillar A hips do well regardless of treatment and are not offered surgery; lateral pillar C hips have the worst prognosis and the benefit of surgical containment is genuinely debated in the literature, though many surgeons still offer it in younger lateral pillar C patients. Timing matters — containment surgery is most effective when performed before or early in the fragmentation stage, before the head has become fixed in a deformed, incongruent shape; late surgery (reossification stage) shows diminishing structural benefit, a point reinforced by a 2026 cohort specifically examining containment surgery performed during reossification, which found inferior radiographic remodeling compared to earlier intervention (Zhang et al., Front Pediatr 2026). Salvage procedures (shelf acetabuloplasty, Chiari osteotomy, or a combination) are reserved for older children/adolescents with established lateral head extrusion/hinge abduction beyond the window for true containment — a 2026 comparative series specifically evaluated lateral shelf acetabuloplasty in older children with Perthes and found it a useful salvage option when standard containment is no longer feasible (Du et al., J Pediatr Orthop 2026). For late-onset Perthes (onset in later childhood/early adolescence, historically the worst-prognosis subgroup), femoral varus derotation osteotomy remains a mainstay salvage/containment option, and recent skeletal-maturity outcome data continue to support meaningful radiographic benefit in appropriately selected late-onset cases (Ibrahim et al., Malays Orthop J 2025).

*Pearls and pitfalls*: when performing a femoral varus derotation osteotomy, avoid over-correcting into excessive varus (target neck-shaft angle generally no lower than ~110–120°) — over-correction creates an iatrogenic abductor lever-arm deficiency and Trendelenburg gait that can be as functionally limiting as the disease itself, and greater trochanteric overgrowth/apophysiodesis may later be needed to rebalance the abductor mechanism. When choosing femoral versus pelvic (Salter/Pemberton) versus combined containment, femoral osteotomy is generally preferred when the deformity is primarily one of coverage correctable by redirecting the femoral head, while acetabular osteotomy or a combined procedure is favored when the acetabulum itself is dysplastic/shows lateral subluxation on arthrogram — assess this dynamically under fluoroscopic arthrography intraoperatively rather than by radiograph alone.

**SCFE — in-situ fixation (standard of care for the great majority of slips)**: single cannulated screw placed under fluoroscopic guidance, centrally located in the epiphysis on both AP and lateral views, perpendicular to the physis, with adequate purchase (generally 5 or more threads across the physis, ideally reaching the center of the epiphysis without penetrating the joint — subchondral bone should be approached but not violated). A single screw is favored over multiple screws in most contemporary practice, as it achieves adequate stabilization with lower reported rates of penetration-related chondrolysis and technically simpler revision if needed. For unstable slips, the widely practiced approach is gentle positional reduction on the fracture table (achieved simply by positioning the leg, without forceful manipulation) followed by in-situ screw fixation — aggressive closed reduction maneuvers are avoided given evidence they increase AVN risk by further compromising the tenuous remaining blood supply.

*Pearls and pitfalls*: confirm central screw position on both AP and true lateral fluoroscopic views before final seating — an epiphysis-centered screw on AP that is actually eccentric/peripheral on the lateral view risks inadequate purchase and slip progression. Stop 2–5 mm short of the subchondral bone (confirmed on both views) to avoid intra-articular penetration, historically implicated in chondrolysis. Do not attempt forceful closed reduction of an unstable slip on the fracture table beyond simple, gentle positioning — pressure-driven reduction maneuvers have been specifically associated with higher AVN rates in several series and are now widely discouraged in favor of "reduction by positioning only."

**SCFE — modified Dunn procedure (open, via surgical hip dislocation)**: an open technique using a Ganz surgical hip dislocation approach to directly visualize and correct the head-neck deformity, allowing controlled reduction of the slipped epiphysis onto the metaphysis with the retinacular blood supply protected and visually confirmed intact throughout (a key theoretical advantage over closed methods, where blood supply status is inferred rather than seen), typically combined with an epiphyseal fixation and often a concurrent femoral neck osteochondroplasty to address the cam deformity. Proposed primarily for unstable/severe slips at high-volume centers with surgeons experienced in the technique, on the rationale that anatomic realignment reduces long-term femoroacetabular impingement and may reduce chondrolysis risk compared with fixation of a residually deformed hip.

**The in-situ pinning vs modified Dunn debate**: this remains one of the most actively contested questions in pediatric orthopedics and should be framed to the reader as unresolved rather than settled. Proponents of routine in-situ pinning (even for unstable slips) point to its technical simplicity, wide reproducibility outside specialist referral centers, and historical AVN rates that, while elevated in unstable slips, are not clearly improved by the modified Dunn approach in several comparative series — plus a real risk that the more technically demanding modified Dunn dissection itself compromises the retinacular vessels in less experienced hands, potentially causing the very AVN it aims to prevent. Proponents of the modified Dunn approach point to case series from expert centers reporting good functional outcomes and low AVN rates when performed by high-volume surgeons, arguing it better addresses the anatomic deformity and reduces late impingement surgery. Comparative literature remains dominated by case series and case-control studies rather than randomized trials: a 2024 case-control study restricted to stable/moderate SCFE found no significant difference in outcomes between in-situ pinning and modified Dunn in that lower-risk population, questioning routine use of the more invasive procedure outside unstable/severe slips (Sisman et al., J Child Orthop 2024), while long-term (7-year) prospective case-series data on the modified Dunn in moderate-to-severe SCFE continue to report favorable functional and radiographic outcomes in expert hands, supporting its continued use as a center-dependent option for that more severe subgroup (Fahmy et al., J Orthop Traumatol 2026). Practical synthesis for a general/community pediatric orthopedic practice: in-situ pinning remains the default for the large majority of slips (stable, and most unstable slips managed promptly); referral to a high-volume center for consideration of modified Dunn is reasonable for severe, unstable slips, particularly when there will be unavoidable delay to fixation, but is not mandatory given the absence of definitive comparative superiority data.

**SCFE — prophylactic contralateral fixation**: individualized rather than routine in most contemporary practice; favored in patients with open triradiate cartilage, young age at presentation (<10–12y), modifiable endocrinopathy, obesity, and in populations where reliable clinical/radiographic follow-up cannot be assured, given the substantial contralateral slip rate and the comparatively low morbidity of prophylactic single-screw fixation versus the consequences of a missed/late contralateral slip.

**SCFE — late presentation / residual deformity**: severe residual head-shaft deformity after in-situ fixation of a severe slip, or chondrolysis/AVN sequelae, may require secondary corrective osteotomy (subtrochanteric Imhäuser-type osteotomy, historically more widely used, versus subcapital realignment via a Dunn-type approach) or, in end-stage AVN/degenerative sequelae, ultimately total hip arthroplasty — a 2026 15-year outcome series of contemporary THA specifically for SCFE sequelae confirmed durable results in this young population when arthroplasty is eventually required, supporting THA as a reliable salvage option rather than a feared last resort (Guarin Perez et al., J Arthroplasty 2026).

**Femoral derotational osteotomy**: reserved for severe (generally >50°), functionally significant, persistent excess femoral anteversion beyond the age when spontaneous correction would be expected (typically not considered before age 8–10, and often deferred to adolescence to allow maximal natural remodeling first). Performed at the subtrochanteric or distal femoral level depending on associated pathology (subtrochanteric for isolated torsional correction; distal femoral when combined with patellofemoral instability correction, e.g. concurrent MPFL reconstruction). Fixation options include intramedullary nail versus plate — a 2026 randomized controlled trial comparing intramedullary nailing versus plate fixation for adolescent femoral derotational osteotomy found comparable union and complication rates, giving surgeons evidence-based flexibility to select fixation based on local expertise and cost rather than a demonstrated superiority of one implant (Grønseth et al., J Pediatr Orthop 2026); a 2026 comparative series of surgical techniques for idiopathic excessive anteversion similarly found several viable technique variants without one clearly superior in outcome, reinforcing that technique selection remains surgeon- and case-dependent (Beatty et al., J Pediatr Orthop 2026).

**Coxa vara — valgus osteotomy**: subtrochanteric valgus-producing osteotomy (with or without derotation) restores the neck-shaft angle and normalizes the Hilgenreiner-epiphyseal angle, indicated for HEA >60°, progressive deformity on serial imaging, neck-shaft angle <100–110° with functional abductor weakness/limp, or a widening physeal defect ("Fish sign") suggesting risk of frank femoral neck non-union/stress fracture if untreated.

*Pearls and pitfalls*: aim to overcorrect the HEA slightly below 38–40° at the time of surgery, since some loss of correction with growth is expected and undercorrection is a well-documented cause of recurrence requiring repeat osteotomy; a blade-plate or locking pediatric hip plate construct provides more reliable angular stability during healing than simple screw fixation given the high shear forces across this region. Counsel families explicitly that recurrence requiring revision osteotomy is not rare (reported in a meaningful minority of series, especially when surgery is performed very young with substantial remaining growth), and that a period of continued radiographic surveillance through skeletal maturity is required regardless of initial correction quality.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**DDH — post-harness/post-reduction**
- Weeks 0–6 (post-closed reduction spica): immobilization, no formal rehabilitation; monitor for cast complications (skin breakdown, compartment concerns with limb swelling), interval radiograph/CT or MRI in cast to confirm maintained reduction.
- Weeks 6–12: cast change under anesthesia if planned, continued spica immobilization; total spica duration ~10–14 weeks depending on age and reduction stability.
- Post-cast (~week 12 onward): transition to removable abduction orthosis, typically weaned over 2–3 months; gentle active range-of-motion play (no formal physiotherapy usually required at this age); interval ultrasound/radiograph surveillance for acetabular remodeling through at least age 2, and periodic follow-up radiographs through skeletal maturity given the risk of late residual dysplasia (a meaningful minority require secondary pelvic osteotomy years later even after successful initial reduction).
- Return to normal activity: unrestricted once orthosis weaned and reduction/containment confirmed stable — most toddlers resume normal gait development on their own developmental timeline.

**Perthes — post-containment surgery**
- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing (touch-down or partial, per surgeon protocol and fixation construct) in a hip spica (younger children, especially after combined osteotomy) or brace/crutches (older children after isolated osteotomy); passive/active-assisted ROM emphasizing abduction and internal rotation once soft-tissue healing allows.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing as radiographic union of the osteotomy is confirmed; physiotherapy focused on hip abductor strengthening (critical given the abductor lever-arm changes after varus osteotomy) and restoring symmetric ROM.
- Months 3–6: full weight-bearing, return to low-impact activity; contact/pivoting sports typically deferred until osteotomy union is solid and abductor strength has normalized (often 4–6 months), with continued radiographic surveillance through the remaining fragmentation/reossification stages regardless of surgery, since containment surgery changes head position but does not shorten the underlying disease course.
- Long-term: surveillance radiographs through skeletal maturity to determine final Stulberg class; patients with Stulberg III–V benefit from patient education regarding earlier-onset hip osteoarthritis risk and activity counseling, with periodic adult hip surveillance thereafter.

**SCFE — post in-situ pinning**
- Weeks 0–2: protected weight-bearing (touch-down/toe-touch to partial per surgeon protocol) on crutches for stable slips fixed promptly; unstable slips typically kept more strictly protected (touch-down only) for a longer initial period given the higher AVN risk and theoretical benefit of unloading the compromised epiphysis.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, guided by pain and radiographic screw position; gentle active ROM, avoiding forced internal rotation/flexion that stresses the fixation.
- Weeks 6–12: full weight-bearing typically achieved; physiotherapy for hip and core strengthening, gait normalization, and gradual return to low-impact recreational activity.
- Months 3–6: return to sport/full activity individualized — earlier for uncomplicated stable slips with good screw position and no AVN signs on interval imaging (often by 3–4 months), later and more cautiously for unstable slips or any radiographic AVN concern, where activity is typically restricted pending serial MRI/radiographic reassurance.
- AVN surveillance: any unstable slip or one with delayed presentation warrants interval clinical and radiographic (± MRI) follow-up through at least the first postoperative year, as radiographic AVN changes (subchondral collapse, sclerosis) may not be apparent for several months.
- Contralateral surveillance: continued clinical/radiographic monitoring of the unaffected hip through skeletal maturity in patients not treated with prophylactic fixation.

**Femoral derotational osteotomy / coxa vara osteotomy**
- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing (partial, per fixation construct — plate constructs are often more restrictive initially than locked intramedullary nail constructs), active-assisted ROM, avoidance of torsional/twisting loads across the osteotomy site.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing to full as radiographic union is confirmed; physiotherapy for hip and thigh strengthening, gait retraining to normalize foot progression angle now that the underlying torsional deformity is corrected.
- Months 3–4: return to full unrestricted activity/sport once solid radiographic union confirmed and strength/ROM symmetric with the contralateral limb; return-to-sport timing is broadly similar regardless of nail versus plate fixation per comparative trial data.

**Return to school / return to sport — summary by diagnosis**

| Diagnosis | Return to school/daily activity | Return to sport (pivoting/contact) |
|---|---|---|
| DDH (post-harness, no surgery) | Immediate — no restriction once treatment complete | Not applicable at this age; normal developmental activity unrestricted |
| DDH (post-reduction/osteotomy) | After cast/brace removal and confirmed stable reduction (~3–4 months) | Low-impact activity once orthosis weaned; higher-impact play per surgeon judgment, typically by 6 months |
| Perthes (non-operative) | Throughout — activity modification during acute/fragmentation phases rather than full school absence | Deferred until fragmentation stage substantially resolved on serial imaging; individualized, often 12–18+ months from diagnosis |
| Perthes (post-containment surgery) | After initial immobilization period, ~6–8 weeks | Low-impact by 3–4 months; contact/pivoting deferred to solid osteotomy union and normalized abductor strength, typically 4–6 months |
| SCFE (stable, in-situ pinning) | 1–2 weeks (crutch-assisted return acceptable) | Individualized, often 3–4 months with good screw position and no AVN signs |
| SCFE (unstable) | 2–4 weeks, protected | Deferred pending AVN surveillance imaging, often 6+ months |
| Femoral derotational osteotomy | 2–3 weeks (partial weight-bearing, crutch-assisted) | Full sport at 3–4 months with confirmed radiographic union |
| Transient synovitis | Immediate once pain-free and weight-bearing normalized (usually days) | Immediate once asymptomatic — self-limited condition with no structural sequela |

## Complications

- **DDH**: avascular necrosis of the femoral head — reported rates vary widely by series and treatment method, roughly 0–5% with well-monitored Pavlik treatment of a genuinely reducible hip, rising substantially (some series >20–60%) with forced/excessive abduction of an irreducible hip held in the harness ("Pavlik disease") or with aggressive closed/open reduction under tension in higher-grade dislocations — this is the single most feared and most preventable complication of treatment itself. Residual acetabular dysplasia requiring secondary pelvic osteotomy later in childhood/adolescence occurs in a meaningful minority even after apparently successful initial reduction, underscoring the need for surveillance radiographs through skeletal maturity. Redislocation after reduction; femoral nerve palsy from excessive hip flexion in the Pavlik harness (typically resolves with brief harness loosening, generally within days to weeks); premature physeal closure after femoral osteotomy.
- **Perthes**: femoral head deformity/incongruity (the core natural-history risk, graded by Stulberg outcome — a majority of Herring C hips and hips with onset ≥8 years progress to Stulberg III–V despite treatment); premature physeal arrest with resultant coxa breva/greater trochanteric overgrowth (relative trochanteric overgrowth producing abductor lever-arm dysfunction and a Trendelenburg gait — may itself require later trochanteric advancement/epiphysiodesis, generally timed around ages 8–10 once overgrowth is established); leg-length discrepancy (typically mild, 1–2 cm, from relative growth arrest of the affected proximal femoral physis); and early-onset hip osteoarthritis in Stulberg III–V hips, classically appearing in the 4th–6th decade of life — a key long-term counseling point for these patients as adults.
- **SCFE**: avascular necrosis (the most devastating complication, strongly associated with slip instability and reduction maneuvers — historically the leading cause of poor outcome and early arthroplasty in this population; rates approximate 0–5% in stable slips versus historically 20–50%+ in unstable slips, though contemporary gentle-positioning protocols aim to reduce this); chondrolysis (acute cartilage necrosis, historically linked to intra-articular screw penetration — incidence has fallen substantially, from double-digit percentages in early multi-pin series to well under 5% with modern single-screw, subchondral-stop technique); residual femoroacetabular impingement (cam-type, from the unremodeled head-neck deformity, particularly after in-situ fixation of moderate-severe slips) predisposing to labral tears and premature OA — a major part of the rationale offered for the modified Dunn approach in severe slips; contralateral slip in roughly 20–40% of unilateral presenters if not prophylactically fixed, highest in the youngest patients; implant-related irritation requiring later screw removal (common, generally elective once physeal closure is confirmed).
- **Transient synovitis / septic arthritis**: the dominant complication risk is a missed septic arthritis diagnosis — delayed treatment beyond even 24–48 hours risks irreversible articular cartilage destruction, physeal damage/growth arrest, and (particularly in the neonate/infant, where transphyseal vessels may still supply the femoral head) AVN; over-aspiration/over-treatment of true transient synovitis carries a small but real procedural risk (iatrogenic joint infection, though rare) and should be avoided when pretest probability is genuinely low based on a validated clinical prediction rule and clinical gestalt together.
- **Femoral anteversion/coxa vara surgery**: nonunion or delayed union at the osteotomy site (uncommon with modern rigid fixation, but a recognized risk at the subtrochanteric level given relatively lower vascularity), over- or under-correction of rotation (assessed intraoperatively and postoperatively by clinical rotational profile — under-correction is the more common technical pitfall), infection, implant-related symptoms requiring removal (more common with plate fixation, less so with buried intramedullary constructs, informing the ongoing nail-vs-plate discussion), and inadvertent valgus/varus malalignment during simultaneous rotational-angular correction of coxa vara.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- 2023 — [Comparison of Clinical Prediction Rules in Pre-school Aged Children With Septic Hip Arthritis Due to Different Pathogens](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37253715/) (Hagedoorn et al., J Pediatr Orthop): re-examines Kocher-type prediction rules across different causative pathogens, reinforcing that clinical prediction rules perform inconsistently across organisms (including Kingella) and should not be applied as a rigid binary cutoff.
- 2024 — [Reapplication of the Pavlik Harness for Treatment of Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip After Initial Pavlik Harness Failure](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37981894/) (Tomaru et al., J Pediatr Orthop): supports a second, carefully monitored harness trial in selected Pavlik-failure cases before escalating to closed/open reduction, refining the "failure management" pathway.
- 2024 — [Standardized In-harness Ultrasound Protocol Improves Success Rate of Brace Treatment for Dislocated Hips](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38647138/) (Pargas-Colina et al., J Pediatr Orthop): a structured in-harness ultrasound monitoring protocol improved reduction success rates for dislocated hips undergoing Pavlik treatment, supporting protocolized ultrasound surveillance rather than ad hoc imaging during harness treatment.
- 2024 — [Comparison of outcomes of different Graf grades of DDH in infants treated with Tübingen splint versus Pavlik harness — a systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38075458/) (Nair et al., J Orthop): finds broadly comparable success between the Tübingen rigid abduction splint and the Pavlik harness across Graf grades, supporting either as first-line depending on center preference/expertise.
- 2024 — [In situ pinning or modified Dunn procedure? A case-control study including only stable/moderate SCFE cases](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39539477/) (Sisman et al., J Child Orthop): found no significant outcome difference between the two techniques when restricted to stable/moderate slips, arguing against routine use of the more invasive modified Dunn outside severe/unstable slips.
- 2025 — [Late closed or open reduction of DDH: a systematic review and meta-analysis of outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41180725/) (Benaroch et al., Ann Med Surg): finds comparable long-term acetabular remodeling between closed and open reduction in late-presenting DDH when concentric reduction is achieved, reinforcing reduction quality over method as the key driver of outcome.
- 2025 — [Pinning and Arthroscopic Trimming for Mild SCFE: Comparative Study With Traditional In Situ Pinning and Modified Dunn Procedure](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40134264/) (Aprato et al., J Pediatr Orthop): describes combined in-situ pinning with arthroscopic head-neck junction trimming as an intermediate option for mild slips at risk of impingement, adding a third pathway to the pinning-vs-Dunn debate for milder deformity.
- 2025 — [Outcome at Skeletal Maturity of Femoral Varus Osteotomy on Late Onset Legg-Calve-Perthes Disease](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41537040/) (Ibrahim et al., Malays Orthop J): confirms durable radiographic benefit from femoral varus derotation osteotomy at skeletal maturity in the historically worst-prognosis late-onset Perthes subgroup.
- 2026 — [Contemporary Total Hip Arthroplasty for Sequelae of Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis: 15-Year Outcomes of 165 Cases](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41966419/) (Guarin Perez et al., J Arthroplasty): confirms durable long-term THA outcomes as a reliable salvage option for SCFE sequelae (AVN, severe residual deformity), useful for counseling on the eventual end-stage pathway.
- 2026 — [Long-term outcomes of the modified Dunn procedure in moderate and severe SCFE: a prospective case series with 7-year follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41665709/) (Fahmy et al., J Orthop Traumatol): favorable long-term functional/radiographic outcomes with the modified Dunn in moderate-severe slips at an expert center, supporting continued center-dependent use for this subgroup.
- 2026 — [Intramedullary Nailing Versus Plate Fixation for Femoral Derotational Osteotomy in Adolescents: A Randomized Controlled Trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42114046/) (Grønseth et al., J Pediatr Orthop): RCT-level evidence of comparable union/complication rates between nail and plate fixation for adolescent derotational osteotomy, supporting implant choice by surgeon/context rather than a demonstrated superiority.
- 2026 — [Femoral Derotational Osteotomy for Idiopathic Excessive Femoral Anteversion: A Comparison of Surgical Techniques](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42224477/) (Beatty et al., J Pediatr Orthop): comparative technique series finding multiple viable derotational osteotomy approaches without clear superiority of one, reinforcing individualized technique selection.
- 2026 — [Longitudinal Quality of Life Outcomes in Children Treated With Petrie Casting and Wide-abduction Bracing for Legg-Calve-Perthes Disease](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42186862/) (De Hoyos et al., J Pediatr Orthop): longitudinal QoL data finding no clear functional advantage of non-operative bracing/casting containment devices, informing the ongoing shift away from these historic non-operative containment methods toward either symptomatic care or surgical containment.
- 2026 — [Long-term radiographic outcomes following containment surgery for Legg-Calve-Perthes disease in the reossification stage](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42146927/) (Zhang et al., Front Pediatr): finds inferior remodeling with containment surgery delayed into the reossification stage compared with earlier (initial/fragmentation-stage) surgery, reinforcing the importance of timing containment before head shape becomes fixed.
