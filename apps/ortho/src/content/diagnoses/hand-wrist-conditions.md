## Quick Reference

**Compressive neuropathies — treatment ladder**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carpal tunnel syndrome | Night splint 6 wk, activity mod. | Corticosteroid injection (NNT ~2-3 for short-term relief) | Open or endoscopic CTR (WALANT-friendly) | EDX confirms/grades; steroid response predicts surgical benefit |
| Cubital tunnel syndrome | Elbow extension splint (esp. night), activity/positional mod. | — (injection not favored; risk to ulnar nerve) | In situ decompression ± anterior transposition (subQ/submuscular) or medial epicondylectomy | McGowan grade drives operative threshold and technique |

**Tendon/soft-tissue conditions — treatment ladder**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger finger | NSAIDs, splint (MCP-blocking) | Corticosteroid injection (success 50-90% depending on Green grade/diabetes) | Open or percutaneous A1 pulley release | Diabetics: lower injection success, higher recurrence |
| De Quervain tenosynovitis | Thumb spica splint, NSAIDs | Corticosteroid injection into 1st compartment (success ~60-90%, lower if subcompartment septum present) | 1st extensor compartment release | Watch for superficial radial nerve; check for septated EPB subcompartment |
| Dupuytren disease | Observation if no MCP/PIP contracture | Collagenase injection or needle (percutaneous) fasciotomy | Limited (selective) fasciectomy ± dermofasciectomy | Tubiana stage and joint(s) involved drive modality choice |
| Ganglion cyst | Observation (spontaneous resolution common) | Aspiration ± steroid (recurrence 30-50%) | Open or arthroscopic excision (recurrence 1-5% wrist dorsal) | Rule out occult dorsal wrist ganglion in unexplained wrist pain |

**Thumb CMC arthritis (Eaton-Littler) — stage-based**

| Stage | First-line | Surgical (if refractory) |
|---|---|---|
| I | Splint, NSAIDs, activity mod, injection | Volar ligament reconstruction (rarely, young high-demand) |
| II-III | Splint/injection trial | Trapeziectomy ± LRTI / suspensionplasty; CMC arthrodesis (young laborers) |
| IV (pantrapezial) | Splint/injection trial | Trapeziectomy-based procedures (implant arthroplasty largely abandoned) |

**Key differentials by presentation**

| Presentation | Consider | Discriminator |
|---|---|---|
| Radial-sided wrist pain, positive grind test | Thumb CMC arthritis vs De Quervain vs scaphoid pathology | Grind test localizes to CMC joint line; Finkelstein localizes to 1st compartment; snuffbox tenderness suggests scaphoid |
| Nocturnal hand paresthesias | CTS vs cervical radiculopathy (C6/C7) vs thoracic outlet syndrome | CTS spares thumb-side proximal forearm/neck symptoms; Spurling's/neck ROM provocation suggests cervical source |
| 4th/5th finger paresthesias | Cubital tunnel vs Guyon's canal (ulnar tunnel) vs cervical (C8/T1) | Guyon's canal spares dorsal ulnar hand sensation (dorsal cutaneous branch arises proximal to Guyon's canal); cubital tunnel involves dorsal ulnar hand |
| Dorsal wrist mass | Ganglion cyst vs extensor tenosynovitis vs giant cell tumor of tendon sheath vs carpal boss | Transillumination positive in ganglion; carpal boss is bony-hard and fixed over the 2nd/3rd CMC joints |
| Finger unable to fully extend | Trigger finger (flexor-side block) vs Boutonnière deformity vs extensor tendon subluxation (sagittal band) | Trigger finger catches/locks in flexion with a palpable A1 nodule; sagittal band injury shows tendon subluxation to the ulnar/radial gutter on active MCP flexion |
| Painful, swollen, flexed digit after minor puncture | Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis vs cellulitis vs septic MCP/PIP arthritis | Kanavel signs localize to the sheath; joint aspiration distinguishes septic arthritis; cellulitis lacks pain on passive tendon excursion |

**Red flags**
- **Kanavel's 4 signs** (see Classification) → OR within hours, not days.
- Rapidly progressing hand infection with crepitus, hemorrhagic bullae, pain out of proportion → necrotizing soft-tissue infection until proven otherwise.
- Compartment syndrome of the hand (tense, painful intrinsic-minus posture, pain with passive stretch) after crush injury.
- High-pressure injection injury (grease/paint gun) — looks benign, is a surgical emergency (extensive debridement within hours).
- Bite wounds (human/animal) over MCP joint ("fight bite") — presume joint violation until proven otherwise; do not close primarily.
- Acute median nerve deficit after distal radius fracture/reduction — urgent reassessment for acute CTS.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Median nerve / carpal tunnel.** The carpal tunnel is bounded dorsally/radially/ulnarly by the carpal bones and volarly by the transverse carpal ligament (flexor retinaculum), transmitting the median nerve and 9 flexor tendons. CTS results from elevated intracanal pressure (idiopathic synovial hypertrophy, fluid retention states, tenosynovitis) causing chronic nerve ischemia, demyelination, and eventually axonal loss. Risk factors: female sex, pregnancy, hypothyroidism, diabetes, obesity, RA, dialysis-associated amyloid, repetitive/vibratory hand use (association debated).

**Ulnar nerve / cubital tunnel.** The ulnar nerve passes posterior to the medial epicondyle through the cubital tunnel, roofed by Osborne's ligament (arcuate ligament between the two heads of FCU) and floored by the elbow's medial collateral ligament. Compression is dynamic (nerve subluxation, elbow flexion narrowing the tunnel volume ~55%) and/or static (heterotopic bone, ganglion, hypertrophic synovium, cubitus valgus post-fracture). Second most common compressive neuropathy after CTS.

**Dupuytren disease.** A fibroproliferative disorder of the palmar fascia with myofibroblast-driven collagen type III deposition replacing normal type I fascial bands, forming pathologic cords (pretendinous cord → MCP contracture; spiral/central/lateral cords → PIP contracture, displacing the neurovascular bundle superficially and centrally — a key intraoperative hazard). Northern European ancestry, male sex, family history, diabetes, alcohol use, epilepsy medication, manual labor/vibration (weaker association) are risk factors.

**Trigger finger (stenosing flexor tenosynovitis).** Mismatch between the flexor tendon (often nodular thickening of FDS/FDP) and the A1 pulley causes mechanical catching as the tendon passes beneath the pulley at the MCP level. Associated with diabetes (higher prevalence, multi-digit, worse response to injection), RA, and repetitive gripping.

**De Quervain tenosynovitis.** Stenosing tenosynovitis of the first dorsal extensor compartment (APL and EPB) at the radial styloid. Up to 30-60% of patients have a septated subcompartment separating APL and EPB tendons — a common cause of injection/surgical failure if unrecognized.

**Thumb CMC (trapeziometacarpal) joint.** A biconcave-biconvex saddle joint stabilized primarily by the volar (anterior oblique/"beak") ligament. Repetitive shear/pinch loading leads to attritional cartilage wear beginning volar-ulnar, progressing to dorsal subluxation of the metacarpal base and pantrapezial arthritis. Far more common in post-menopausal women.

**Flexor tendon anatomy.** FDP inserts on the distal phalanx base (flexes DIP), FDS bifurcates and inserts on the middle phalanx (flexes PIP); both traverse a fibro-osseous flexor sheath with annular (A1-A5) and cruciate (C1-C3) pulleys — A2 and A4 are biomechanically critical and must be preserved/reconstructed to avoid bowstringing. Tendon nutrition is dual: vincular blood supply + synovial diffusion, most tenuous in Zone II ("no man's land").

**Extensor tendon anatomy.** Thinner, flatter, more superficial than flexors; the extensor mechanism includes the sagittal bands (MCP extension, central slip stabilization), central slip (PIP extension — injury → Boutonnière), lateral bands/triangular ligament, and terminal tendon (DIP extension — injury → mallet). Lower tensile strength but a broader repair surface than flexors.

**Ganglion cysts.** Mucin-filled synovial outpouchings from a joint capsule or tendon sheath via a one-way valve mechanism; dorsal wrist ganglia (~60-70%) arise from the scapholunate interval, volar wrist ganglia from the radiocarpal/STT joint (near the radial artery — vascular risk), and volar retinacular (flexor sheath) ganglia at the A1 pulley.

**Fingertip anatomy.** The pulp is compartmentalized by fibrous septa anchoring skin to bone (felon abscess loculation); the germinal matrix (nail production) and sterile matrix (nail adherence) determine nail regrowth after injury; the nail bed lies directly on the distal phalanx periosteum.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**CTS.** History: nocturnal paresthesias, thumb-index-middle-radial ring finger distribution, symptom relief with shaking the hand (flick sign). Exam: Phalen's (sens ~68-73%, spec ~73-83%), Tinel's at wrist (sens ~50-60%), Durkan carpal compression test (sens/spec highest among provocative tests, ~64-87%/83-95% pooled), thenar atrophy/weak thumb opposition in advanced disease. **Electrodiagnostics (NCS/EMG)** remain the reference standard for confirming diagnosis, grading severity (mild/moderate/severe based on distal motor/sensory latency, amplitude, and denervation on EMG), and are recommended by AAOS before surgery in atypical presentations or when the diagnosis is uncertain; not mandatory in classic presentations per some guidance but strongly favored by US practice patterns and payers. High-resolution ultrasound (median nerve CSA >9-10 mm² at the pisiform level) is a validated adjunct/alternative, cheaper and increasingly used in Europe.

**Cubital tunnel.** History: 4th/5th finger paresthesias, worse with elbow flexion (phone use, sleeping). Exam: elbow flexion-compression test (sens ~75%, higher than Tinel's alone), Tinel's at the cubital tunnel, Wartenberg's sign (abducted small finger), Froment's sign (ulnar-innervated adductor pollicis weakness), intrinsic wasting (first dorsal interosseous, hypothenar) in advanced disease, clawing (ring/small) in severe/chronic cases. NCS/EMG grade severity and localize (across-elbow conduction velocity <50 m/s or >10 m/s drop vs forearm segment is diagnostic); MRI/US reserved for atypical cases (mass, prior trauma, subluxation assessment).

**Dupuytren.** Palpable pretendinous cords, MCP/PIP flexion contracture measured by goniometer per joint, tabletop test (positive = unable to place palm flat = surgical/procedural threshold classically), Garrod's pads (knuckle pads), plantar (Ledderhose) or penile (Peyronie) fibromatosis association (diathesis markers: bilateral disease, family history, early onset <50y, ectopic disease — predict recurrence).

**Trigger finger.** Clinical diagnosis: palpable nodule at A1 pulley, painful catching/locking on active flexion-extension, tenderness at the MCP flexion crease. Grade per Green classification (below).

**De Quervain.** Finkelstein test (thumb tucked into fist, ulnar deviation — classic, high sensitivity but poor specificity) vs Eichhoff test (examiner-applied ulnar deviation without active thumb flexion) — distinguish from intersection syndrome (pain/crepitus ~4-6 cm proximal to wrist) and first CMC arthritis (grind test).

**Thumb CMC arthritis.** Grind test (axial load + rotation reproduces pain — high sensitivity), CMC joint line tenderness, metacarpal base prominence/adduction posture with MCP hyperextension in advanced disease. Imaging: Robert view (true AP of CMC1, hyperpronated) is the most sensitive plain radiograph for early joint space narrowing/osteophytes; lateral and Eaton stress views assess subluxation.

**Flexor/extensor tendon injury.** Assess resting cascade, active DIP flexion with PIP blocked (FDP), active PIP flexion with adjacent fingers blocked in extension (FDS), extensor lag at MCP/PIP/DIP, wound exploration through full active ROM to catch partial lacerations that retract with position change. Always document neurovascular exam (2-point discrimination, capillary refill, Allen's test if vascular concern) before any anesthesia/block.

**Ganglion cyst.** Transillumination (positive = cystic), ultrasound or MRI if diagnosis uncertain, occult mass suspected, or pre-op planning; MRI also helps exclude solid tumors (giant cell tumor of tendon sheath) in atypical presentations.

**Metacarpal/phalangeal fractures.** Assess rotational alignment clinically (fingernail plane symmetry, composite fist for scissoring) — rotation is invisible on plain radiographs and must be checked clinically. Standard AP/lateral/oblique hand films; CT for intra-articular comminution (base fractures, Bennett/Rolando thumb fractures) pre-operatively.

**Hand infections.** Kanavel's 4 cardinal signs for flexor sheath infection (see Classification). Felon: tense, throbbing pulp swelling. Paronychia: periungual erythema/fluctuance. Labs (CRP/WBC) and blood cultures for systemic signs; radiographs to exclude retained foreign body/osteomyelitis; early surgical consultation is diagnostic and therapeutic — imaging should never delay debridement of a clinically clear flexor sheath infection.

**Imaging modality selection**

| Modality | Primary use in hand/wrist conditions |
|---|---|
| Plain radiographs (AP/lateral/oblique) | First-line for all fractures, CMC/thumb arthritis staging (Robert view), calcific tendinopathy, retained foreign body screening |
| Ultrasound | CTS (median nerve CSA), De Quervain subcompartment mapping, ganglion cyst confirmation, dynamic tendon subluxation assessment, guided injections, foreign body localization |
| MRI | Occult ganglion/soft-tissue mass characterization, atypical nerve compression (mass lesion), osteomyelitis workup in chronic hand infection, TFCC/ligament injury when concurrently evaluating the wrist |
| CT | Intra-articular fracture comminution (Bennett/Rolando, complex metacarpal base), pre-operative fracture mapping, malunion assessment |
| Nerve conduction studies / EMG | Reference standard for CTS/cubital tunnel severity grading, distinguishing peripheral compression from cervical radiculopathy/polyneuropathy, pre-operative baseline in atypical or medicolegal cases |

## Classification

**Verdan flexor tendon zones (hand/wrist)**

| Zone | Anatomic boundaries | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Distal to FDS insertion (distal to A4, over middle phalanx to fingertip) | FDP only; often direct repair or advancement (avoid >1 cm advancement — risk of quadriga); consider suture anchor if very distal/avulsion (jersey finger) |
| II | A1 pulley to FDS insertion ("no man's land") — over proximal/middle phalanx | Both FDP+FDS in tight fibro-osseous sheath; highest adhesion/re-rupture risk; core+epitendinous repair with A2/A4 pulley preservation is critical; early active motion protocol standard |
| III | Distal edge of carpal tunnel (transverse carpal ligament) to A1 pulley (palm) | Lumbrical origin region; generally good prognosis, less sheath constraint |
| IV | Within the carpal tunnel | Multiple tendons + median nerve in confined space; often combined nerve injury; may need carpal tunnel release as part of exposure/repair |
| V | Proximal to carpal tunnel, forearm | Multiple structures often injured together (spaghetti wrist); best prognosis zone generally, but multi-structure repair complexity |

**Extensor tendon zones**

| Zone | Location | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | DIP joint | Mallet injury pattern; usually splint (see mallet criteria) unless large/subluxed fragment |
| II | Middle phalanx | Direct suture repair; splint DIP extension |
| III | PIP joint (central slip) | Boutonnière risk if missed; splint PIP extension (DIP free) 4-6 wk even for closed/partial injuries |
| IV | Proximal phalanx | Tendon broader/flatter here; repair + short-arc/relative motion protocol |
| V | MCP joint | Sagittal band injury (boxing/fight-bite risk — presume joint violation with any wound here) |
| VI | Dorsum of hand, metacarpals | Multiple tendon lacerations common (junctura tendinum can mask complete laceration — test each finger independently) |
| VII | Wrist (extensor retinaculum) | Repair retinaculum partially/with pulley reconstruction to prevent bowstringing; nearby sensory branches at risk |
| VIII | Distal forearm, musculotendinous junction | Often combined with muscle injury; more forgiving of imperfect repair strength given surrounding muscle bulk |

**Eaton-Littler classification (thumb CMC / trapeziometacarpal arthritis)**

| Stage | Radiographic findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Normal or slightly widened joint space (synovitis/effusion), no subluxation | Splinting, NSAIDs, injection; surgery rarely needed (volar ligament reconstruction only in young high-demand with instability) |
| II | Joint space narrowing, subluxation <1/3, small osteophytes/loose bodies <2 mm | Nonop trial first; if refractory: trapeziectomy ± LRTI/suspensionplasty or arthrodesis |
| III | Further narrowing, subluxation >1/3, osteophytes/loose bodies >2 mm | Same surgical menu as Stage II; arthrodesis attractive in younger manual laborers |
| IV | Pantrapezial arthritis — scaphotrapezial (STT) joint also involved | Trapeziectomy-based procedures preferred (STT involvement is a relative contraindication to isolated arthrodesis and to STT-sparing implants) |

**Tubiana classification (Dupuytren disease — digital involvement severity by total flexion deformity)**

| Stage | Total flexion contracture (MCP+PIP+DIP) | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| N | Nodule only, no contracture | Observation |
| N/I | Nodule + <45° contracture | Observation vs early procedural intervention if progressive |
| I | 0-45° | Collagenase or needle fasciotomy reasonable first-line, especially for isolated MCP cord |
| II | 45-90° | Collagenase, needle fasciotomy, or limited fasciectomy depending on cord anatomy; PIP involvement favors fasciectomy for durability |
| III | 90-135° | Limited fasciectomy generally preferred (better correction, more durable for PIP disease); collagenase/fasciotomy less reliable |
| IV | >135° | Fasciectomy ± dermofasciectomy (with skin graft) for recurrent/severe disease; salvage PIP arthrodesis or amputation for fixed, longstanding PIP contracture with NV compromise |

**Green classification (trigger finger severity)**

| Grade | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I (pretriggering) | Pain, tenderness at A1 pulley, history of catching without demonstrable catching on exam | Splint/NSAID; injection reasonable |
| II | Demonstrable catching, actively correctable | Injection first-line (best injection response) |
| III A | Demonstrable catching requiring passive correction | Injection reasonable but lower success; surgery if fails/recurs |
| III B | Active incomplete flexion (locked in extension) | Surgical release favored, especially if diabetic |
| IV | Fixed flexion contracture, passively uncorrectable | Surgical A1 release required, may need concomitant PIP contracture management |

**McGowan classification (cubital tunnel syndrome)**

| Grade | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Subjective sensory symptoms only, no weakness | Non-operative: splinting, activity modification, nerve gliding |
| IIa | Objective sensory loss, subjective weakness without measurable motor deficit | Trial of non-operative management, reassess with EDX; surgery if no improvement |
| IIb | Measurable intrinsic weakness | Operative decompression favored (in situ vs transposition per surgeon/anatomy) |
| III | Severe intrinsic wasting/paralysis, clawing, established sensory loss | Operative decompression indicated but counsel guarded prognosis for full recovery of intrinsic function |

**Kanavel's 4 cardinal signs (pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis)**

| Sign | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fusiform ("sausage") swelling | Uniform swelling of the entire digit | Any combination of 2+ signs (classically all 4, though sensitivity of the full tetrad is imperfect early) with clinical suspicion → urgent surgical washout |
| 2. Flexed posture at rest | Digit held in slight flexion to minimize sheath pressure/pain | Same |
| 3. Tenderness along flexor tendon sheath | Pain to palpation from palm to fingertip along the sheath | Same |
| 4. Pain with passive extension | Disproportionate pain on passive digit extension (most sensitive early sign) | Same — this sign often precedes the full tetrad and should trigger urgent evaluation alone |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**CTS:** Night-only wrist splint in neutral (0°) for 6-8 weeks is first-line, particularly in mild-moderate disease and pregnancy-associated CTS (often resolves postpartum). NSAIDs have limited independent efficacy. Activity/ergonomic modification for occupational contributors. Diuretics for fluid-related CTS (pregnancy, dialysis) have weak evidence.

**Cubital tunnel:** Elbow extension (or 45-60° flexion) night splinting to avoid prolonged flexion, activity modification (avoid leaning on elbow, minimize sustained flexion e.g., phone use), nerve gliding exercises. Effective mainly in McGowan I-IIa; corticosteroid injection is not standard practice given proximity of the nerve and lack of robust benefit data.

**Dupuytren:** Observation appropriate for nodules without joint contracture (Tubiana N). No proven disease-modifying non-operative therapy (radiotherapy has limited/uncertain evidence, used more in some European centers for early progressive disease pre-contracture).

**Trigger finger:** MCP-blocking splint (worn primarily at night, 6-10 weeks) and activity modification; most effective in Green I-II and short symptom duration.

**De Quervain:** Thumb spica splint (wrist neutral-slight extension, thumb in radial abduction) 4-6 weeks, NSAIDs; modest standalone efficacy, usually combined with injection.

**Thumb CMC arthritis:** Hand-based thumb spica splint, activity modification (avoid sustained pinch/grip), NSAIDs, joint protection/OT education. OARSI and general OA principles (weight management, activity pacing) apply by extrapolation from broader OA guidance.

**Ganglion cyst:** Reassurance/observation is appropriate first-line given high spontaneous resolution rate, especially in asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic cysts.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

**CTS corticosteroid injection:** Provides significant short-term (up to ~3 months) symptom relief with NNT commonly cited around 2-3 for meaningful improvement at short-term follow-up; benefit attenuates by 6-12 months in a large proportion of patients, and injection response (magnitude/duration) is a useful prognostic indicator for likely benefit from later surgery. Ultrasound guidance improves accuracy of needle placement (reduces intraneural/intratendinous injection risk) and is increasingly favored where available; reasonable alternative to blind landmark-based injection. A 2025 meta-analysis of RCTs (Daou et al., JBJS Open Access) reaffirms that surgical release provides superior and more durable symptom relief and function compared with corticosteroid injection at intermediate/long-term follow-up, supporting injection as a bridge/diagnostic-therapeutic step rather than a long-term strategy in moderate-severe disease.

**Trigger finger corticosteroid injection:** Single injection into the flexor sheath at the A1 pulley level; success rates roughly 50-90% depending on Green grade (best in I-II), diabetes status (lower success, especially insulin-dependent and multi-digit disease), and duration of symptoms. A second injection is reasonable if partial response; persistent/recurrent triggering after 2 injections favors surgical release.

**De Quervain corticosteroid injection:** Into the first dorsal compartment; success ~60-90% for a single injection, lower when a septated EPB subcompartment is present and not specifically targeted (a common cause of "failed" injection) — ultrasound guidance helps identify septation and improves accuracy.

**Dupuytren — collagenase Clostridium histolyticum (CCH):** Enzymatic disruption of the pathologic cord via office injection followed by manual manipulation/extension 24-72h later. Best suited to MCP joint cords with contracture in the moderate range (Tubiana I-II); less predictable for PIP cords. Contracture recurrence at 5 years is substantial (many series report >50% recurrence of ≥20° by 3-5 years, similar order of magnitude to needle fasciotomy). Risks: skin tears (~9-25% in trials), tendon rupture (rare but reported, more common with off-label PIP use), complex regional pain syndrome (rare).

**Dupuytren — percutaneous needle fasciotomy (aponeurotomy):** Office-based, minimally invasive division of the cord with a needle under local anesthesia; fastest recovery, lowest up-front cost, and can be repeated, but highest recurrence rate of the three modalities (5-year recurrence commonly cited >50-85% in some series, though functional retreatment thresholds are more relevant than radiographic/clinical recurrence alone). Best for older/lower-demand patients, isolated MCP cords, or those wanting rapid return to function and accepting higher retreatment likelihood. Lower complication rate than surgery (nerve injury risk exists but is generally low in experienced hands given the subcutaneous, non-dissecting technique).

**Comparative evidence (2024-2025):** Multiple recent RCTs and network meta-analyses (Räisänen et al. 2024, Ann Intern Med; Hauksson et al. 2024, JBJS Open Access; Dias et al. 2024 DISC trial, Health Technology Assessment; Nann et al. 2024 network meta-analysis) converge on: limited fasciectomy achieves the greatest and most durable correction (especially for PIP contracture) but has the longest recovery and highest complication burden (nerve injury risk, wound complications, longer time off work); collagenase and needle fasciotomy have comparable early correction to each other and to surgery for isolated MCP disease with much faster recovery, but both have substantially higher recurrence rates than fasciectomy by 3-5 years. This supports a **shared decision-making framework**: fasciectomy for PIP-predominant or recurrent disease and patients prioritizing durability; collagenase/fasciotomy for MCP-predominant disease, multiple-ray involvement wanting to avoid multiple open procedures, or patients prioritizing rapid recovery over durability.

**Ganglion cyst aspiration:** Large-bore needle aspiration ± corticosteroid instillation; recurrence 30-50% (some series higher for volar wrist ganglia); reasonable first invasive step given low morbidity, but counsel patients on recurrence likelihood versus excision.

### Operative

**CTS release:** Open carpal tunnel release (OCTR) via longitudinal incision releasing the transverse carpal ligament under direct vision remains the workhorse, with cure/major-improvement rates >90%. Endoscopic release (single- or dual-portal) offers faster return to work/less pin-tenderness (grip strength and 2-point discrimination recover a few weeks faster on average) with equivalent long-term outcomes and symptom relief to open release in most meta-analyses; slightly higher rate of incomplete release/reoperation and a learning curve with transient nerve injury risk (recurrent motor branch, ulnar neurovascular bundle) reported in early-experience series. WALANT (lidocaine + epinephrine, no tourniquet) is now widely adopted for both open and endoscopic CTR — comparable safety/efficacy to tourniquet-based technique, avoids tourniquet pain/sedation, and allows intra-operative active motion confirmation. Ultrasound-guided/minimally invasive percutaneous release techniques are increasingly reported (mini-open, thread/knife-based) with good early results but less long-term comparative data than open/endoscopic.

**Cubital tunnel decompression:** In situ decompression (simple release of Osborne's ligament ± intermuscular septum) is favored as first-line surgery for most McGowan I-IIb cases without gross instability, given lower morbidity and equivalent outcomes to transposition in multiple RCTs/meta-analyses for mild-moderate disease. Anterior transposition (subcutaneous or submuscular) reserved for: nerve subluxation/snapping over the medial epicondyle, recurrent/revision cases, significant cubitus valgus or bony deformity, or when in situ release leaves inadequate glide. Medial epicondylectomy is a less commonly used alternative. Submuscular transposition has higher perceived reliability for severe/revision cases but longer immobilization and higher wound morbidity than subcutaneous transposition.

**Trigger finger release:** Open A1 pulley release via a small transverse/longitudinal palmar incision under WALANT is standard, near 100% resolution of triggering with low complication rates; digital nerve injury is the main risk (particularly thumb — radial digital nerve crosses obliquely near the A1 pulley). Percutaneous release (needle-based, under US guidance in some series) is a reasonable alternative for radial 3 digits with good safety profiles reported, but carries a higher theoretical nerve/tendon injury risk than open release, especially in the thumb and small finger where anatomy is less forgiving — most surgeons still prefer open release for thumb and small finger.

**De Quervain release:** First dorsal compartment release through a transverse incision, with mandatory intraoperative inspection for a separate EPB subcompartment (release it specifically to avoid incomplete decompression — the leading cause of persistent symptoms) and protection of the superficial branch of the radial nerve (leading cause of painful/symptomatic neuroma if injured).

**Thumb CMC arthritis surgery:** **Trapeziectomy** (simple excision) is the technical foundation of most reconstructive options and has excellent long-term pain relief with fewer complications than more complex reconstructions; **LRTI (ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition, e.g., FCR- or APL-based)** adds a tendon anchor/spacer historically believed to prevent proximal metacarpal migration and improve pinch strength, though multiple RCTs (including long-term follow-up data) show LRTI does not reliably outperform simple trapeziectomy or trapeziectomy with simple hematoma-distraction arthroplasty on validated outcome scores, while adding donor-site morbidity and operative time. **Suture-suspensionplasty** (e.g., suture-button, internal brace, "mini TightRope"-type constructs) has emerged as a faster, less morbid alternative to tendon-based LRTI, aiming to maintain the trapeziectomy space and pinch strength with a shorter recovery; 2022-2024 RCTs (Morais et al. 2022; Graesser et al. 2024) show suspensionplasty achieves comparable pain relief, grip/pinch strength, and patient-reported outcomes to LRTI at 1-year follow-up with generally faster early recovery, and a 2025 network meta-analysis (Challoumas et al.) of 21 RCTs found no clinically important differences among the major trapeziectomy-based techniques (simple trapeziectomy, LRTI, suspensionplasty) in mid-term outcomes — reinforcing that technique choice should weigh surgeon experience, operative time, and recovery profile over an assumed superiority of any single reconstruction. **CMC arthrodesis** is reserved for younger, high-demand manual laborers (Eaton II-III without STT involvement) trading motion for stability/strength, with nonunion rates historically 10-25% depending on technique/fixation. **Implant (pyrocarbon/metal-on-polyethylene) arthroplasty** has largely fallen out of favor in most centers due to higher revision rates compared with trapeziectomy-based procedures, though newer implant designs are still used selectively in some European practices.

**Flexor tendon repair:** Core suture with ≥4-strand technique (e.g., cruciate, Adelaide, Strickland-type) plus a circumferential epitendinous suture is the modern standard to permit early active motion (EAM) protocols — 2-strand repairs are biomechanically insufficient for EAM and predispose to gap/rupture. A2 and A4 pulleys must be preserved or vented judiciously (venting rather than complete sacrifice when repair bulk causes triggering) to prevent bowstringing. WALANT allows intraoperative active motion testing to confirm gliding and identify triggering/gapping before skin closure — increasingly the preferred anesthetic approach for elective and many acute flexor repairs. FDP avulsion (jersey finger) repaired with suture anchor or pull-out button depending on retraction level and bone stock; delayed presentation (>3-4 weeks) may preclude primary repair, requiring staged reconstruction (silicone rod then tendon graft) or DIP fusion as salvage. The 2026 IFSSH consensus (Tang et al.) codifies core-plus-epitendinous repair with early active motion as the international standard of care where resources/therapy access allow.

**Extensor tendon repair:** Zone I/II (mallet-region) injuries: closed injuries managed by splinting per criteria above; open/large-fragment/subluxed injuries fixed with extension block pinning, hook plate, or screw fixation depending on fragment size. Zone III (central slip): acute open injury repaired primarily with PIP extension splinting; closed central slip injury (missed Boutonnière) managed with dynamic/static PIP extension splinting for 4-6 weeks even without surgery in many cases, reserving reconstruction for chronic fixed deformity. Zones IV-VII: direct end-to-end or core-suture repair depending on tendon thickness, protected with relative motion or static/dynamic splinting.

**Ganglion cyst excision:** Open excision with identification and ligation/excision of the pedicle back to its joint/tendon sheath origin (dorsal wrist ganglia to the scapholunate interval capsule) minimizes recurrence (open dorsal wrist excision recurrence ~1-5%); arthroscopic excision offers comparable recurrence rates with smaller scars and faster recovery in appropriately selected dorsal wrist ganglia, and is increasingly preferred where arthroscopic expertise is available. Volar wrist ganglia require care around the radial artery.

**Metacarpal/phalangeal fracture fixation:** Indications for surgery: unacceptable angulation/shortening (see Quick Reference), malrotation, multiple/open fractures, intra-articular fractures with step-off, unstable fracture patterns. Options: closed reduction and splinting/casting (stable, acceptable alignment), percutaneous K-wire fixation (intramedullary or crossed, minimally invasive, avoids extensive soft-tissue stripping), plate/screw fixation (rigid fixation for comminuted/unstable/articular fractures, allows earlier motion but higher risk of tendon adhesion/irritation from hardware), and intramedullary headless screw fixation (increasingly popular for metacarpal neck/shaft fractures — allows earlier active motion with less soft tissue disruption than plating). Boxer's fracture: closed reduction (Jahss maneuver) and buddy taping/short-arm ulnar gutter cast for acceptable alignment; percutaneous pinning (antegrade intramedullary or transverse) for unacceptable angulation, malrotation, or multiple metacarpal fractures. Thumb Bennett/Rolando fractures: closed reduction and percutaneous pinning for simple Bennett; ORIF or external fixation/CT-guided fixation for comminuted Rolando fractures given the deforming pull of APL.

**Fingertip injury management:** Distal tip amputations without exposed bone: healing by secondary intention (semi-occlusive dressing) with excellent results, especially in children; composite grafting has poor success in adults. Exposed bone/larger defects: local flap coverage selected by defect location/size (V-Y advancement volar/lateral, cross-finger flap, thenar flap, homodigital island flap) versus shortening/primary closure/revision amputation depending on patient priorities (length vs simplicity/recovery). Nail bed injuries: repair germinal/sterile matrix lacerations with fine absorbable suture under loupe magnification; replace or stent the nail fold to prevent adhesions; associated distal phalanx fractures are treated as open fractures (repair nail bed, may not require formal fixation if fracture is stable after nail bed repair splints it). Subungual hematoma: trephination (nail preserved) for large/painful hematomas even with underlying fracture, provided the nail margin is intact — nail removal reserved for lacerated nail margin/unstable nail plate.

**Hand infections — operative principles:** Flexor sheath (pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis): urgent surgical drainage/irrigation of the sheath (limited incisions with catheter irrigation, or open exposure for advanced/necrotic cases) plus IV antibiotics empirically covering Staph aureus (including MRSA coverage in high-prevalence settings) and Streptococcus, narrowed by culture; delay beyond 24-48h from clear Kanavel signs worsens outcomes (tendon necrosis, stiffness, amputation risk). Recent literature (Sawyer et al. 2024; Hennessy et al. 2026) notes emerging interest in selective non-operative (IV antibiotics + close monitoring) management for early/mild presentations without frank sheath distension, but surgical drainage remains standard of care once Kanavel signs are established, and evidence for routine non-operative management is still preliminary/lacks consensus guidelines. Felon: incision (high lateral, avoiding the pulp's central septa and the digital nerve; or through-and-through with drain for large abscesses), avoiding the volar midline/flexion creases and never a full "fish-mouth" (risks unstable/painful scar and pulp necrosis). Paronychia: eponychial marsupialization ± partial nail plate removal for simple cases; deeper/chronic cases may need nail bed exploration. "Fight bite" (MCP joint laceration from a tooth): presume joint capsule/extensor tendon violation, formal wound exploration and irrigation in the OR (not simple bedside closure), broad-spectrum antibiotics covering oral flora (including Eikenella corrodens — augmentin or equivalent), and the wound is left open/loosely approximated, never primarily closed tightly.

**Hand infection empiric coverage by mechanism**

| Mechanism/setting | Typical organisms | Empiric coverage consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Spontaneous felon/paronychia, general population | Staph aureus (incl. MRSA in endemic areas), Streptococcus spp. | Anti-staphylococcal agent with MRSA coverage where local prevalence is significant |
| Human bite / fight bite | Eikenella corrodens, Staph aureus, Streptococcus spp., oral anaerobes | Amoxicillin-clavulanate or equivalent beta-lactamase-covering regimen; formal OR exploration for MCP joint bites |
| Animal bite (cat/dog) | Pasteurella multocida, Staph, Streptococcus, anaerobes | Amoxicillin-clavulanate; cat bites especially high infection risk given puncture depth |
| Immunocompromised/diabetic, chronic wound | Polymicrobial, gram-negatives, atypical organisms (fungal in chronic/resistant cases) | Broader empiric coverage pending culture; lower threshold for surgical debridement and infectious disease involvement |
| Water/aquatic exposure | Aeromonas hydrophila (fresh water), Vibrio spp./Mycobacterium marinum (salt water/aquarium) | History-directed coverage; M. marinum causes indolent, culture-negative-on-routine-media infection — consider with chronic atypical presentations |

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**CTS post-release.** Soft dressing/removable splint for comfort 3-7 days (splinting not mandatory beyond this for open or endoscopic release), early active finger/wrist motion encouraged from day 1-3 to limit stiffness/adhesion, scar massage from ~2 weeks once incision sealed. Light activities of daily living immediately; return to light desk work often within days (WALANT particularly facilitates this), heavier manual labor/lifting typically restricted 4-6 weeks; grip strength recovery continues over 3 months, full recovery of strength/pillar pain resolution up to 3-6 months.

**Cubital tunnel post-decompression.** In situ decompression: early motion, brief splint immobilization (days) mainly for comfort, progressive activity as tolerated; light duty in 1-2 weeks, heavier labor 4-6 weeks. Transposition (especially submuscular): longer immobilization (1-3 weeks in a posterior splint/sling protecting the transposed segment), then progressive elbow ROM, with heavier lifting/resistance restricted 6-8 weeks. Sensory recovery precedes motor; intrinsic recovery in McGowan III cases may be incomplete regardless of technique/timing.

**Dupuytren.** Post-fasciectomy: hand therapy starting within days, extension splinting (night, sometimes daytime initially) for 3-6 months to counter scar contracture, edema control, scar management (silicone, massage), progressive AROM/PROM. Return to light activity 2-3 weeks, heavy manual work 6-8 weeks. Post-collagenase/needle fasciotomy: nighttime extension splinting for weeks to months is commonly recommended (evidence for optimal duration is limited but widely practiced) to help maintain correction; return to activity within days given minimal tissue disruption.

**Trigger finger post-release.** Immediate active motion encouraged (no formal immobilization needed); light activity within days, unrestricted use typically by 2-4 weeks once the incision is healed; residual triggering sensation/tenderness at the pulley site can persist for weeks-months even after successful release and should be counseled pre-operatively.

**De Quervain post-release.** Early active thumb/wrist motion within the first week to prevent adhesion/tendon subluxation, avoid resisted thumb extension/abduction for 2-3 weeks, progressive strengthening from 3-4 weeks, return to unrestricted manual activity ~4-6 weeks.

**Thumb CMC surgery.** Trapeziectomy ± suspensionplasty/LRTI: thumb spica splint/cast immobilization 3-4 weeks (shorter for suture-suspensionplasty in many protocols given internal stabilization, longer for tendon-graft-based LRTI), then transition to removable splint with progressive AROM 4-6 weeks, strengthening from 6-8 weeks, light activity ~6 weeks, full unrestricted grip/heavy lifting 3 months. CMC arthrodesis: immobilization 6-8 weeks until radiographic union, then progressive mobilization of adjacent joints (IP, wrist) that were spared, heavy loading deferred to ~3 months.

**Flexor tendon repair — early active motion (EAM) protocol** (contingent on ≥4-strand core repair): Days 0-3: dorsal blocking splint (wrist ~20-30° flexion, MCP ~40-60° flexion, IP free/near full extension), begin place-and-hold or true active flexion (per surgeon protocol, e.g., modified Duran, Indiana/Strickland-based active protocols, or relative motion flexion for Zone I-III) within the first 3-5 days under hand therapy supervision. Weeks 3-4: progress from protected active motion to increased active ROM within the splint, initiate gentle blocking exercises as healing allows. Weeks 4-6: discontinue splint for controlled activities, progress AROM, begin light functional use. Weeks 6-8: begin passive stretching for residual stiffness, light resistance/putty exercises. Weeks 8-12: progressive strengthening, return to full unrestricted activity/heavy labor and contact sport generally not before 10-12 weeks, guided by tendon gliding and strength milestones rather than calendar alone. Re-rupture rates with modern EAM protocols in Zone II are commonly reported in the 4-10% range in specialized hand therapy settings (lower than older passive-motion-only protocols' adhesion/stiffness burden, but requiring close therapy supervision and patient compliance — EAM should not be used without a structured, monitored therapy program).

**Extensor tendon repair.** Zone I (mallet): continuous DIP extension splinting 6-8 weeks full-time, then 2-4 weeks night-only; PIP/hand motion unrestricted throughout. Zone III (central slip): PIP extension splinting (DIP free, encourage DIP flexion to prevent lateral band migration) 4-6 weeks, then progressive PIP flexion. Zones IV-VII: relative motion splinting or short-arc motion protocols increasingly preferred over rigid full immobilization, allowing earlier controlled motion to reduce adhesion while protecting the repair (typically 3-4 weeks of protection then progressive mobilization); return to heavy use/sport ~8-12 weeks.

**Ganglion cyst excision.** Soft dressing, early digit motion; wrist splint/immobilization 1-2 weeks for dorsal/volar wrist excisions to protect capsulotomy, progressive ROM from 2 weeks, strengthening from 4-6 weeks, full activity by 6-8 weeks.

**Metacarpal/phalangeal fractures.** Non-operative stable fractures: protected motion within a splint/buddy tape from the outset when possible (early motion reduces stiffness — a major complication of hand fractures), progressing to unrestricted motion by 4-6 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing. Operative fixation (K-wire/plate/IM screw): early protected active motion is a major advantage of rigid fixation (screw/plate) allowing therapy to begin within days; K-wire fixation typically requires protection until pin removal (~3-4 weeks) before more aggressive mobilization. Return to contact sport/heavy manual labor generally 6-8 weeks with radiographic union.

**Fingertip injuries/nail bed repair.** Protective splint/dressing 1-2 weeks, early adjacent joint motion, desensitization/scar massage program for hypersensitivity (common and expected for weeks-months), return to work depends on occupation (desk work days, manual labor/contact activities 4-6 weeks or per bony healing if fracture present).

**Hand infections post-washout.** Early motion as soon as the wound/soft tissue allows (within days once acute inflammation controlled) to prevent tendon adhesion and joint stiffness — arguably the most important long-term determinant of outcome after flexor sheath washout; formal hand therapy referral standard once infection is controlled; return to work variable (days for desk work once wounds allow, weeks for manual labor), full functional recovery can take months, and some stiffness/reduced motion is a realistic expectation after severe or delayed-presentation infections.

## Complications

- **CTS release:** incomplete release (persistent symptoms, more common with limited/endoscopic exposure or missed accessory ligaments), pillar pain (common, usually self-limited over months), recurrent CTS (scar tethering, incomplete release), nerve injury (recurrent motor branch, palmar cutaneous branch — cause painful neuroma/numbness), infection (uncommon), complex regional pain syndrome (rare).
- **Cubital tunnel decompression:** incomplete decompression, new/worsened ulnar nerve symptoms, nerve subluxation after in situ release (favors transposition in predisposed anatomy), wound complications (more with submuscular transposition), medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury (painful neuroma — common if not identified/protected), persistent intrinsic weakness in advanced (McGowan III) cases regardless of technique.
- **Dupuytren treatment:** digital nerve/artery injury (highest risk with needle fasciotomy in inexperienced hands and with collagenase manipulation), skin tears/lacerations (collagenase, especially with aggressive manipulation), tendon rupture (rare, collagenase), flare reaction/CRPS, hematoma, recurrence (all modalities, rate hierarchy: fasciectomy lowest, collagenase/fasciotomy higher), incomplete correction of longstanding PIP contracture (joint capsule/collateral ligament contracture may not fully correct regardless of technique).
- **Trigger finger release:** digital nerve injury (especially thumb/small finger), incomplete release/recurrent triggering, bowstringing (rare with isolated A1 release), stiffness, infection.
- **De Quervain release:** superficial radial nerve injury/neuroma (most common complication), incomplete release from missed EPB subcompartment (leading cause of persistent symptoms), tendon subluxation if retinaculum released too widely without volar leaflet preservation.
- **Thumb CMC surgery:** persistent pain/incomplete relief, loss of pinch/grip strength (variable by technique), scapho-metacarpal impingement/subsidence of the trapeziectomy space over time, sensory branch (radial sensory/palmar cutaneous median) neuroma, nonunion of arthrodesis (10-25% depending on series/fixation), hardware-related symptoms with arthrodesis fixation, implant loosening/dislocation with prosthetic arthroplasty.
- **Flexor tendon repair:** tendon rupture (highest risk in the first 3-6 weeks, especially days 7-21 as suture strength is lowest relative to healing tissue), adhesion formation/stiffness (major cause of poor outcome, especially Zone II), pulley bowstringing (if A2/A4 over-vented), quadriga (over-advancement of FDP causing incomplete flexion of adjacent fingers), lumbrical-plus deformity, infection.
- **Extensor tendon repair:** extension lag (common, variable severity), Boutonnière deformity (missed/undertreated central slip injury), swan-neck deformity (chronic mallet with compensatory PIP hyperextension), adhesions/stiffness, skin breakdown from prolonged splinting.
- **Ganglion excision:** recurrence (1-5% dorsal wrist open excision; higher for aspiration alone), wrist stiffness, scar tenderness, injury to adjacent structures (radial artery in volar wrist ganglion excision, posterior interosseous nerve branches dorsally).
- **Metacarpal/phalangeal fracture fixation:** malunion (rotational malunion is functionally most significant), nonunion (uncommon in hand fractures given rich vascularity, more common with open/comminuted injuries or infection), hardware irritation/tendon adhesion (particularly dorsal plating under extensor tendons), stiffness (the most common overall complication of hand fractures, related to both injury and prolonged immobilization), pin tract infection (K-wire fixation), extensor lag.
- **Fingertip/nail bed injury:** nail deformity (ridging, splitting) from inadequately repaired germinal matrix, hook nail deformity (inadequate bony/soft tissue support distally), cold intolerance (common after fingertip amputation/replant, can persist years), hypersensitivity/neuroma, growth arrest in pediatric distal phalanx physeal injuries.
- **Hand infections:** tendon necrosis/rupture (delayed treatment of flexor sheath infection), joint stiffness/contracture, osteomyelitis, digit loss/amputation in severe or delayed presentations, recurrent infection, systemic sepsis in immunocompromised or delayed-presentation patients.

## Recent Evidence

- **2025 — AAOS/ASSH Clinical Practice Guideline, "Management of Carpal Tunnel Syndrome" (Shapiro et al., *J Am Acad Orthop Surg*).** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39637428/) Updated joint AAOS/American Society for Surgery of the Hand evidence-based guideline on diagnosis and treatment pathways for adult CTS; consolidates recommendation strength for splinting, injection, electrodiagnostics, and surgical release (open vs endoscopic), reaffirming surgery as most durable for moderate-severe disease while supporting injection/splinting as reasonable non-operative steps for milder disease.
- **2025 — Daou R, Daher M, et al., "Carpal Tunnel Release Versus Local Corticosteroid Injection for Carpal Tunnel Syndrome: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials," *JB JS Open Access*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41431704/) Pooled RCT data confirm surgical release produces superior and more durable symptom and function scores than corticosteroid injection at intermediate/longer-term follow-up, reinforcing injection's role as a short-term/diagnostic bridge rather than definitive treatment in moderate-severe CTS.
- **2024 — Räisänen MP, et al., "Surgery, Needle Fasciotomy, or Collagenase Injection for Dupuytren Contracture: A Randomized Controlled Trial," *Annals of Internal Medicine*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38346307/) Head-to-head RCT of all three major Dupuytren treatment modalities; informs the shared decision-making framework of durability (fasciectomy) versus speed of recovery (collagenase/needle fasciotomy).
- **2024 — Hauksson I, et al., "Collagenase Clostridium histolyticum Versus Needle Fasciotomy for Primary Metacarpophalangeal Dupuytren Contracture: Five-Year Results," *JBJS Open Access*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39569009/) Long-term (5-year) follow-up showing recurrence trajectories for CCH versus percutaneous needle fasciotomy in isolated MCP-cord disease, useful for counseling patients on expected durability of office-based procedures.
- **2024 — Dias J, et al., "Collagenase Injection Versus Limited Fasciectomy Surgery to Treat Dupuytren's Contracture in Adult Patients in the UK: DISC," *Health Technology Assessment* (NIHR-funded multicentre RCT).** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39644138/) Pragmatic UK-wide RCT directly informing NHS-relevant cost-effectiveness and outcome comparisons between collagenase and limited fasciectomy — a key resource-allocation reference for European practice.
- **2024 — Nann A, et al., "Surgical Management of Dupuytren Disease: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-analyses," *Hand*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37246411/) Network meta-analysis ranking treatment modalities by correction and recurrence, supporting fasciectomy's durability advantage particularly for PIP joint disease.
- **2025 — Shaheen A, et al., "Effectiveness and Safety of Dupuytren Contracture Treatments: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Using the GRADE Approach," *Plastic and Reconstructive Surgery*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39451139/) GRADE-rated synthesis strengthening the evidence base for modality selection by joint involved and patient priorities (durability vs recovery speed).
- **2024 — Graesser EA, et al., "A Prospective Randomized Pilot Study: One-Year Outcomes of Ligament Reconstruction Tendon Interposition Versus Suture Tape Suspensionplasty for Thumb Carpometacarpal Joint Arthritis," *Journal of Hand Surgery America*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38934993/) RCT-level evidence that suture-tape suspensionplasty achieves comparable 1-year outcomes to LRTI with a less invasive technique, supporting the shift toward suspensionplasty as a faster-recovery alternative to tendon-based reconstruction.
- **2025 — Challoumas D, et al., "Surgery for Trapeziometacarpal Joint Arthritis: A Network Meta-Analysis of Randomized Studies," *Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40497265/) Network meta-analysis of 21 RCTs finding no clinically important difference in mid-term outcomes among simple trapeziectomy, LRTI, and suspensionplasty — evidence against routinely adding tendon interposition/suspension over simple trapeziectomy alone.
- **2026 — Baljer B, et al., "Surgery for Thumb (Trapeziometacarpal Joint) Osteoarthritis," *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42206643/) Updated Cochrane review of surgical options for thumb CMC OA, the highest tier of evidence synthesis currently available for this decision and consistent with the network meta-analysis findings above.
- **2026 — Tang JB, Lalonde D, Fernandes CH, Sadek AF, Besmens IS, "The IFSSH Consensus and Current Guidelines on Flexor Tendon Repairs and Reconstruction," *Journal of Hand Surgery European Volume*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41637469/) International Federation of Societies for Surgery of the Hand consensus statement codifying ≥4-strand core repair with epitendinous suture and early active motion as the international standard where therapy resources allow — the most current cross-society guideline reference for flexor tendon technique and rehabilitation.
- **2023 — Newington L, Bamford E, Henry SL, "Relative Motion Flexion Following Zone I-III Flexor Tendon Repair: Concepts, Evidence and Practice," *Journal of Hand Therapy*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37029053/) Reviews the growing evidence base for relative motion flexion splinting as an alternative/adjunct to traditional dorsal blocking splints in early active motion protocols for proximal flexor zone repairs.
- **2023 — Shaw AV, Verma Y, et al., "Relative Motion Orthoses for Early Active Motion After Finger Extensor and Flexor Tendon Repairs: A Systematic Review," *Journal of Hand Therapy*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37037728/) Systematic review supporting relative motion splinting/orthoses as an effective, less bulky alternative to traditional immobilization-based protocols for both extensor and flexor repairs.
- **2024 — Degreef I, Lalonde DH, "WALANT Surgery of the Hand: State of the Art," *EFORT Open Reviews*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38726975/) European perspective synthesizing the maturing WALANT evidence base — cost, patient satisfaction, and expansion beyond carpal tunnel/trigger finger into flexor tendon repair and distal radius fixation, relevant to European practice adoption patterns.
- **2026 — Koehler SM, Ayhan E, "What's New in Wide-Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet Hand Surgery?," *Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42181019/) Current-year update summarizing consistent noninferiority of WALANT functional outcomes across an expanding range of hand procedures, supporting its continued spread as default anesthesia for most elective hand surgery.
- **2024 — Sawyer JD, et al., "Operative Versus Nonoperative Management of Pyogenic Flexor Tenosynovitis: An Analysis of the National Readmissions Database," *Journal of Hand Surgery Global Online*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39991610/) Large database analysis examining outcomes of operative versus non-operative management of flexor sheath infection — part of an emerging (still preliminary, non-consensus) body of evidence questioning whether all cases require immediate surgical washout, though surgical drainage remains the standard of care for established Kanavel signs.
- **2026 — Hennessy M, Forder BH, Tucker S, Wormald JCR, "Optimal Management of Pyogenic Flexor Tenosynovitis of the Hand: A Cohort Study," *Journal of Hand Microsurgery*.** [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42239939/) Notes the continued absence of formal evidence-based guidelines for flexor sheath infection management and documents real-world variation in surgical technique and antibiotic duration, an area still lacking a unifying society guideline as of 2026.
