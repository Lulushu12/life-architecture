## Quick Reference

**Red flags requiring immediate action**
- Any knee dislocation (even if spontaneously reduced) → mandatory serial vascular exam ± ankle-brachial index (ABI); ABI < 0.9 → CT angiography; hard signs (absent pulse, expanding hematoma, active bleeding, pulsatile mass) → immediate vascular surgery consult, no imaging delay.
- **Popliteal artery injury** occurs in ~5–15% of true knee dislocations (higher in anterior/posterior high-energy patterns); missed injury with delayed revascularization (>8h ischemia) carries amputation rates historically cited up to 86% vs <15% when treated within 8h.
- **Peroneal nerve injury** in 15–40% of posterolateral/PLC-involved dislocations — document foot drop/sensory exam before any reduction maneuver and again after.
- Segond fracture on plain film = ACL rupture until proven otherwise (association >75–90%) — obtain MRI, do not treat as isolated avulsion.
- Compartment syndrome — reperfusion after prolonged ischemia or high-energy MLKI is a recognized trigger; check compartments serially.

**Compact treatment ladder**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACL rupture | Structured quadriceps/neuromuscular rehab (young low-demand, sedentary, willing to modify sport) | Delayed ACLR if instability persists after rehab trial (KANON-style "rehab-then-decide") | Early ACLR (within 3 months per AAOS 2022) for young pivoting-sport athletes, combined injury, objective instability |
| PCL injury (isolated) | Grade I–II: brace + quad-focused rehab, near-full return expected | Grade III isolated: trial of bracing/rehab in low-demand patients; reassess laxity at 3 months | Grade III with functional instability, combined injuries, bony avulsion → repair/reconstruction |
| MCL injury (isolated) | Grade I–II: hinged brace, early motion, near-universal healing | Grade III isolated (non-combined): 6-week brace trial first — MCL has excellent intrinsic healing | Grade III with valgus instability at 6 weeks, combined ACL/MCL with gap/Stener-like displacement, chronic laxity |
| LCL/PLC injury | Grade I–II isolated: brace, avoid varus loading | — (PLC has poor healing potential; low threshold to move to surgery vs MCL) | Grade III / any combined PLC injury: repair (acute, <3wk) or reconstruction (chronic or delayed presentation) |
| Multiligament knee injury / dislocation | Vascular clearance, reduce & splint in slight flexion, staged imaging | Bridge external fixation if vascular repair, open injury, or gross instability precluding brace | Definitive staged or single-stage ligament reconstruction, typically 2–6 weeks post-injury after swelling/motion optimized |

**Key numbers**
- ACL: KANON trial — no significant difference in 2-, 5-, and 11-year PROs between early ACLR and rehab-with-optional-delayed-ACLR; ~50% of rehab-first patients ultimately crossed over to surgery by 5 years; 44% radiographic OA at 11 years regardless of arm.
- STABILITY 1 trial: adding LET to hamstring autograft ACLR reduced clinical graft failure from 40% (ACLR alone) to 25% (ACLR+LET) at 2 years in patients <25y with risk factors (RRR 0.38).
- Cross-bracing protocol (CBP): recent 2-year controlled cohort data show ~70% recurrent instability and ~62% concurrent medial meniscal tear rate in CBP vs 2.5%/2.5% in surgical ACLR comparison group in pivoting athletes — tempers earlier enthusiasm for routine non-operative "ligament healing" pathways in this population.
- RTS timing: return before 9 months post-ACLR associated with up to 7-fold higher second ACL injury risk even with 90% limb symmetry index (LSI) achieved.
- Graft trend: quadriceps tendon autograft use rose from <5% (1998–2014) to ~20% by 2023 in the ACL Study Group survey; hamstring use declining from a 2014 peak (~62%).
- Physeal-sparing ACLR generally indicated: boys <12y bone age, girls <10y bone age; transphyseal ACLR favored once bone age ≥15y (male) / ≥13y (female).
- Knee dislocation spontaneous reduction rate at scene: up to 50% — a normal-appearing knee on ED arrival does not exclude dislocation; treat any grossly unstable knee with a documented mechanism as a reduced dislocation until proven otherwise.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**ACL**: originates posteromedial aspect of lateral femoral condyle, inserts anterior to the tibial intercondylar eminence; functionally two bundles — anteromedial (AM, taut in flexion) and posterolateral (PL, taut in extension) — controlling anterior tibial translation and rotational (pivot-shift) stability. Mean tensile failure load ~2160N (younger cadaveric data); most clinical ruptures are non-contact, deceleration/pivoting mechanisms with valgus-flexion-internal rotation moment. Concomitant meniscal tears in 50–70% of acute presentations (lateral more common acutely, medial more common in chronic ACL-deficient knees).

**PCL**: largest, strongest knee ligament; anterolateral bundle (taut in flexion, primary restraint) and posteromedial bundle (taut in extension); originates from lateral aspect of medial femoral condyle, inserts on posterior tibia ~1 cm below the joint line. Primary restraint to posterior tibial translation and secondary restraint to external rotation at high flexion angles. Isolated PCL injury is uncommon (~3–20% of knee ligament injuries reported, varies by series) — most occur in combination with PLC or as part of MLKI/dislocation. Classic mechanism: dashboard injury (posteriorly directed force on proximal tibia with knee flexed) or hyperflexion with plantarflexed foot in sport.

**MCL**: superficial layer (primary valgus restraint, femoral origin proximal/posterior to medial epicondyle, tibial insertion ~6 cm distal to joint line beneath pes anserinus) and deep layer (meniscofemoral/meniscotibial ligaments, capsular thickening). Excellent intrinsic healing capacity due to good vascularity and extra-articular location — basis for near-uniform non-operative management of isolated injuries.

**LCL/Posterolateral corner (PLC)**: "arcuate complex" — LCL (fibular collateral ligament, primary varus restraint), popliteus tendon/popliteofibular ligament (rotational and varus restraint, resists external rotation and posterior translation), and posterolateral capsule; biceps femoris and iliotibial band contribute secondary stability. Unlike MCL, PLC structures are extra-articular but have relatively poor intrinsic healing due to a more limited vascular bed and higher inherent strain at injury — chronic PLC insufficiency is a well-recognized cause of ACL/PCL graft failure if unaddressed ("if you fix the cruciate and ignore the corner, the graft will stretch out or fail").

**Multiligament knee injury (MLKI) / knee dislocation**: defined functionally as disruption of ≥2 of the 4 major ligament complexes (ACL, PCL, MCL, PLC), not requiring radiographic dislocation to be present at time of exam (many reduce spontaneously). The popliteal artery is tethered proximally (adductor hiatus) and distally (soleus arch), making it vulnerable to traction injury with tibiofemoral displacement — intimal tears can present with initially normal pulses and delayed thrombosis, which is why serial exams (not a single normal pulse check) are mandatory.

**Epidemiology context**: ACL rupture incidence is estimated at roughly 68–120 per 100,000 person-years in population-based registries, with a marked female predominance in cutting/pivoting sports (soccer, handball, basketball) attributed to a combination of neuromuscular, anatomic (notch width, tibial slope), and hormonal factors. Isolated PCL injury is comparatively uncommon in isolation but is frequently under-recognized because patients often present with a relatively well-functioning knee and subtle exam findings — a large proportion of "isolated" PCL tears are in fact missed low-grade combined injuries. Knee dislocation is rare (estimated <0.02% of orthopedic trauma admissions in most series, likely an underestimate given spontaneous reduction) but disproportionately high-stakes given the vascular and neurologic risk.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (contact vs non-contact, direction of force, sport), audible "pop," immediate effusion (hemarthrosis within 1–2h suggests ACL tear ~70% of the time), ability to bear weight, prior knee injury/surgery, occupation and sport/level for shared decision-making.

**Differential diagnosis to actively exclude**: patellar dislocation/instability (also causes hemarthrosis and a "pop," but with medial patellofemoral ligament tenderness and apprehension rather than Lachman laxity), meniscal tear without ligament injury (joint-line tenderness, McMurray/Thessaly, no instability on stress testing), tibial plateau or distal femoral fracture (obtain radiographs before stress-testing an acutely painful knee), quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture (palpable defect, extensor lag, inability to perform straight-leg raise), and osteochondral fracture (loose-body sensation, effusion out of proportion, requires CT/MRI characterization).

**Associated-injury screening checklist** (perform routinely regardless of which ligament is primarily suspected, since combined injury is the rule rather than the exception in higher-energy presentations): meniscal exam (joint-line tenderness, McMurray), patellofemoral apprehension, full four-ligament stress testing (not just the suspected structure), vascular and neurologic exam as above, and a compartment/soft-tissue-envelope assessment in any high-energy presentation.

**Exam by structure**
- **ACL**: Lachman test (most sensitive, 87–98%), anterior drawer (less sensitive, hamstring guarding), pivot-shift (most specific for functional instability, best under anesthesia).
- **PCL**: posterior drawer (most sensitive), posterior sag sign (Godfrey test), quadriceps active test, reverse pivot-shift (indicates combined PLC injury).
- **MCL**: valgus stress at 0° (tests PCL/posteromedial capsule/ACL as secondary restraints too) and at 30° (isolates MCL); grade by gapping.
- **PLC/LCL**: varus stress at 0° and 30°, dial test (external rotation asymmetry) at 30° and 90° — asymmetry at 30° only = isolated PLC; asymmetry at both 30° and 90° = combined PLC + PCL injury. Posterolateral drawer test.
- **Vascular** (mandatory for any suspected dislocation): pulses, capillary refill, ABI both legs — ABI <0.9 has high sensitivity for surgically significant arterial injury and mandates CTA.
- **Neurologic**: peroneal nerve (dorsiflexion, first dorsal web space sensation) — document before and after any reduction.

**Imaging**
- Plain radiographs first: assess for avulsion fractures (Segond fracture = anterolateral capsular avulsion, arcuate sign = PLC/fibular styloid avulsion, tibial eminence avulsion in pediatric ACL, reverse Segond = posteromedial tibial avulsion associated with PCL/MCL injury), and confirm reduction post-dislocation.
- MRI: gold standard for soft-tissue characterization of all four ligaments, menisci, articular cartilage, and bone bruise pattern (classic ACL bone bruise: lateral femoral condyle + posterolateral tibial plateau "kissing" contusions).
- CT angiography: indicated for any hard vascular signs, ABI <0.9, or high clinical suspicion after high-energy dislocation, even with normal pulses, given the risk of occult intimal injury.
- Stress radiographs (Telos device or manual stress views): useful for quantifying PCL laxity (side-to-side posterior translation) and objectively grading combined instability, increasingly used to guide operative vs non-operative decisions in PCL injury.

**Special-test diagnostic performance and pattern recognition**

| Test | Structure | Approx. sensitivity/specificity | Clinical note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lachman | ACL | Sens ~87–98%, Spec ~90–99% | Most reliable single acute-setting test; perform at 20–30° flexion |
| Anterior drawer | ACL | Sens ~40–70% (lower acutely) | Reduced sensitivity from hamstring guarding/effusion in acute injury |
| Pivot-shift | ACL (rotatory/functional) | Spec >95%, sens variable, best under anesthesia | Correlates best with functional instability and patient-reported outcome; graded 0–III |
| Posterior drawer | PCL | Sens ~90% | Most sensitive PCL test; grade by tibial step-off relative to femoral condyles |
| Posterior sag (Godfrey) | PCL | High specificity | Passive test, avoids quadriceps-guarding confound |
| Quadriceps active test | PCL | Confirmatory | Reduction of posterior sag with active quad contraction at 90° flexion |
| Dial test at 30° only | Isolated PLC | — | >10–15° side-to-side difference = PLC injury |
| Dial test at 30° AND 90° | Combined PCL+PLC | — | Asymmetry persisting at 90° implicates PCL involvement, not PLC alone |
| Valgus stress at 0° | MCL + secondary restraints (ACL/posteromedial capsule) | — | Gapping at full extension implies more severe/combined injury, not isolated MCL |
| Valgus stress at 30° | Isolated MCL | — | Isolates superficial MCL from cruciate/capsular contribution |

**Bone bruise / associated-injury imaging pattern recognition**

| Pattern on MRI/radiograph | Implicated ligament injury |
|---|---|
| Lateral femoral condyle + posterolateral tibial plateau "kissing" contusion | ACL rupture (classic pivot-shift bone bruise pattern) |
| Segond fracture (lateral tibial condyle avulsion) | ACL rupture + anterolateral structure injury (>75–90% association) |
| Reverse Segond fracture (posteromedial tibial avulsion) | PCL and/or MCL injury |
| Arcuate sign (fibular styloid avulsion) | PLC injury (popliteofibular ligament/biceps femoris complex) |
| Deep sulcus sign (lateral femoral notch >2 mm) | ACL rupture, chronic ACL insufficiency |
| Anterior tibial bone bruise + posterior femoral condyle contusion | PCL injury (dashboard-type mechanism) |
| Tibial eminence avulsion (pediatric) | Pediatric-equivalent of ACL rupture — treat as ACL injury, consider fragment fixation |

## Classification

**Schenck classification of knee dislocation (KD)** — based on the pattern of ligaments disrupted, not mechanism:

| Type | Ligaments involved | Notes / management implication |
|---|---|---|
| KD I | One cruciate (ACL or PCL) + one collateral (MCL or PLC); cruciate can be partial | Lowest-energy pattern; still requires full vascular workup; often amenable to single-stage reconstruction |
| KD II | Both cruciates (ACL + PCL) intact collaterals | Rare pure pattern; "bicruciate" injury |
| KD III-M | Both cruciates + MCL (PLC intact) | Higher-energy; associated with medial-sided soft tissue compromise |
| KD III-L | Both cruciates + PLC/LCL (MCL intact) | Highest peroneal nerve injury and vascular injury rates of the "III" subtypes; PLC repair/reconstruction is technically demanding and time-sensitive |
| KD IV | Both cruciates + both collaterals (global instability) | Highest-energy, highest vascular injury rate; often requires staged reconstruction and external fixation bridging |
| KD V | Multiligament injury with periarticular fracture (fracture-dislocation) | Add "C" for arterial injury and "N" for nerve injury as modifiers (e.g., KD IIIL-C-N); treated per combined ortho-trauma/vascular protocol |

Modifiers: **C** = vascular injury present, **N** = neurologic injury present — always appended when applicable (e.g., KD-IIIL-C denotes a KD IIIL pattern with arterial injury).

*Historical note*: the older Kennedy classification described knee dislocation by tibiofemoral displacement direction (anterior, posterior, lateral, medial, rotatory) rather than ligament pattern; anterior dislocations (hyperextension mechanism) were classically associated with the highest reported popliteal artery injury rates. Schenck's ligament-pattern-based system has superseded this for surgical planning because vascular risk correlates more with energy and ligament pattern than with displacement direction alone, but the historical association between hyperextension/anterior displacement and vascular injury remains clinically relevant when triaging urgency of vascular workup.

**Fanelli classification of posterolateral corner (PLC) injury** (functional/anatomic, based on varus and external rotation instability):

| Type | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Type A | Isolated popliteofibular ligament/popliteus laxity — increased external rotation (dial test) only, no varus opening | Usually amenable to non-operative trial if isolated; if surgery needed, popliteofibular ligament reconstruction/repair alone |
| Type B | Popliteofibular + LCL laxity — increased external rotation + mild-to-moderate (5–10 mm) varus opening at 30° | Combined popliteus/popliteofibular + LCL reconstruction typically needed |
| Type C | Full PLC disruption — popliteofibular, LCL, and lateral capsule/arcuate ligament complex all incompetent — increased external rotation + gross (>10 mm) varus opening | Comprehensive PLC reconstruction (anatomic, multi-strand) required; highest failure risk if under-recognized and left unaddressed alongside cruciate reconstruction |

**MCL grading (I–III)**:

| Grade | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Tender, <5 mm valgus gapping at 30° vs contralateral, firm endpoint | Symptomatic treatment, brief brace/ice, early sport-specific return, typically 1–3 weeks |
| II | 5–10 mm gapping at 30°, firm endpoint, partial tear | Hinged brace 2–4 weeks, protected weight-bearing, progressive ROM; return 4–6 weeks |
| III | >10 mm gapping at 30°, no firm endpoint (or gapping at 0° indicating combined posteromedial/cruciate injury), complete tear | Isolated: brace trial 6 weeks first-line given excellent healing potential, most heal without surgery; combined with ACL or with valgus instability persisting at 6 weeks → surgical repair/reconstruction, often staged with the cruciate |

**Segond fracture** — avulsion fracture of the lateral tibial condyle at the attachment of the anterolateral ligament/lateral capsular ligament, seen as a small bony fleck on AP knee radiograph just distal to the lateral tibial plateau. **Association**: pathognomonic marker of ACL rupture (reported association >75–90% in most series) and frequently co-exists with lateral meniscal tears. Management implication: a Segond fracture on plain film should trigger MRI and be treated as a marker of combined anterolateral/ACL instability, not as an isolated avulsion needing fixation — it also supports consideration of concomitant lateral extra-articular augmentation (ALL reconstruction/LET) at the time of ACLR given the anterolateral structure injury it signals.

**PCL grading by posterior tibial translation** (supplementary grading system used alongside Schenck/Fanelli to stage isolated PCL injury and guide the non-operative vs operative threshold):

| Grade | Posterior tibial translation (posterior drawer, 90° flexion) | Tibial plateau position vs femoral condyles | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 0–5 mm | Plateau remains anterior to condyles | Non-operative first-line in essentially all cases |
| II | 5–10 mm | Plateau flush with condyles | Non-operative first-line; brace + quad rehab, reassess at 3 months |
| III | >10 mm | Plateau posterior to condyles | Non-operative trial reasonable if isolated and low-demand; strongly consider reconstruction if combined with PLC/other ligament injury, bony avulsion, or persistent functional instability |

## Treatment

**Shared decision-making framing**: for ACL rupture in particular, the operative-vs-non-operative decision should be presented to the patient as a genuine choice with quantified trade-offs rather than a default pathway — KANON-level evidence supports that carefully selected, well-counseled patients treated with structured rehabilitation and optional delayed reconstruction achieve comparable long-term patient-reported outcomes to early surgery, while early surgery more reliably restores objective stability and reduces secondary meniscal injury risk in high-demand pivoting athletes. Document the discussion of sport/occupation demands, instability episodes, and patient preference explicitly, and revisit the decision if a rehabilitation-first patient develops recurrent giving-way.

### Non-operative

- **ACL** — appropriate for: low-demand patients, older/sedentary patients, those willing to modify or avoid pivoting sports, and as a first-line "rehabilitation-first" pathway in appropriately counseled young active patients per the KANON model (structured quadriceps-focused exercise therapy with criteria-based reassessment; ~50% avoid surgery at 5 years, but roughly half eventually cross over). Copers vs non-copers should be identified with a structured return-to-activity screening (quadriceps strength symmetry, single-leg hop tests, absence of giving-way episodes) before committing to a purely non-operative course. **Cross-bracing protocol (CBP)**: a specific non-operative approach involving knee immobilization at ~90° flexion for 4 weeks to appose the ACL stumps for possible healing, then progressive ROM. Enthusiasm has been substantially tempered by recent 2-year controlled cohort data (Porter & Shadbolt) showing ~70% recurrent instability and ~62% concurrent medial meniscal tear versus 2.5%/2.5% with surgical ACLR in pivoting-sport athletes — CBP should currently be considered investigational for competitive pivoting athletes pending confirmatory trials, and is not a substitute for individualized shared decision-making.
- **PCL** — first-line for essentially all Grade I–II isolated injuries and for many isolated Grade III injuries in low-to-moderate demand patients: PCL brace (dynamic, anterior tibial support) for 2–4 weeks, immediate quadriceps activation (avoid hamstring-dominant exercise which increases posterior tibial sag), progressive weight-bearing and ROM. Long-term physiotherapy-led brace/exercise cohorts show durable improvement in patient-reported outcomes at 5 years, with need for delayed reconstruction, when it occurs, concentrated in the first 2 years.
- **MCL** — first-line for essentially all isolated Grade I–III injuries: hinged knee brace, early protected ROM (avoid prolonged immobilization — stiffness is the main complication), progressive strengthening; even Grade III isolated tears are given a structured 6-week trial before surgery is considered, given the ligament's strong intrinsic healing.
- **LCL/PLC** — reasonable only for low-grade (Fanelli Type A, isolated, partial) injuries without instability; brace in extension/slight flexion, avoid varus stress and pivoting activity. Given the PLC's comparatively poor healing biology, the threshold to move to surgery is much lower than for the MCL, and non-operative management of higher-grade or combined PLC injury risks chronic instability and secondary cruciate graft failure.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- Intra-articular corticosteroid injections are generally avoided in the acute post-ligamentous-injury period given theoretical concerns about soft-tissue/graft healing and infection risk around planned surgery.
- Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) has been studied as an augmentation for ACL graft healing (bone tunnel and graft maturation) and for isolated MCL/PCL healing acceleration; evidence remains heterogeneous and it is not currently part of any major guideline's core recommendations — consider only as an adjunct within shared decision-making, not a substitute for correct mechanical management.
- Aspiration of a tense traumatic hemarthrosis can improve comfort and facilitate early exam/rehab but does not alter the underlying ligament management pathway.
- Vascular intervention (not a "procedure" in the elective sense but essential adjunct): therapeutic anticoagulation, endovascular stenting, or open repair/bypass for confirmed popliteal artery injury — coordinated with vascular surgery as a time-critical parallel pathway to ligament management, not sequential to it.

### Operative

**ACL reconstruction**
- **Indications**: young/active patients in pivoting/cutting sports, objective instability (positive pivot-shift) that fails or is unlikely to respond to rehabilitation, combined ligament injury, and per AAOS 2022 CPG, surgery within 3 months of injury is now favored over the prior 5-month window because the risk of secondary meniscal/chondral injury rises with delay and time-to-instability episodes.
- **Graft choice by age/sport/demand**:
  - **Bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft**: historically the "gold standard" for high-level pivoting athletes given bone-to-bone healing and lower graft-rupture rates in young high-demand populations in several registry analyses; trade-offs include anterior knee pain, kneeling pain, and small risk of patellar fracture/patellar tendon rupture.
  - **Hamstring tendon (semitendinosus ± gracilis) autograft**: lower donor-site morbidity, preserves extensor mechanism; historically associated with somewhat higher graft failure rates in young high-risk patients when used alone — this is the population in which the STABILITY trial demonstrated the benefit of adding LET.
  - **Quadriceps tendon autograft**: usage has risen sharply (from <5% pre-2014 to ~20% of primary ACLR by 2023 in ACL Study Group survey data), favored for larger cross-sectional area/strength, lower anterior knee pain than BPTB, and increasingly used in both primary and revision settings; particularly attractive in athletes wanting to avoid patellar tendon donor morbidity (e.g., volleyball/basketball jump-sport athletes) and in revision ACLR where it has become a leading choice.
  - **Allograft**: reasonable for low-demand/older patients or as an adjunct in multiligament reconstruction to limit donor-site morbidity/operative time; avoid as isolated graft choice in young high-demand pivoting athletes given consistently higher re-rupture rates in registry data.
  - **Pediatric/skeletally immature**: physeal-sparing (all-epiphyseal or extra-physeal/over-the-top with iliotibial band) techniques recommended for boys with bone age <12y and girls <10y; transphyseal techniques (with small-diameter, vertically-oriented tunnels avoiding central physeal violation, soft-tissue graft, and avoidance of hardware crossing the physis) are favored as skeletal maturity approaches (bone age ≥15y male / ≥13y female); age-specific "over-the-top" strategies tailored to prepubescent, young-adolescent, and older-adolescent growth stages have been described to balance graft stability against growth-disturbance risk. Growth disturbance (limb-length discrepancy, angular deformity) remains a real but low-frequency risk even with physeal-sparing technique (reported ~2–3% in recent systematic reviews), and graft rupture rates in pediatric ACLR (~8% in recent meta-analysis) generally exceed adult rates, reinforcing the need for close family counseling and adherence-focused rehab.
- **Lateral extra-articular augmentation (LET or anterolateral ligament reconstruction)**: added to ACLR in patients with high-risk profiles — age <25, high-grade pivot-shift (grade 2–3), participation in pivoting/contact sport, generalized ligamentous laxity, revision setting, or a Segond fracture signaling anterolateral structure injury. STABILITY 1 (ISAKOS-sponsored RCT, 618 patients age 14–25) showed ACLR+LET reduced clinical graft failure from 40% to 25% at 2 years (RRR 0.38, p<0.0001) with no significant increase in adverse events; a modest, transient reduction in quadriceps strength at 6–12 months normalizes by 1 year. This has shifted many high-volume centers toward routine LET/ALL augmentation in the described high-risk subgroup rather than reserving it for revision cases only.
- **Fixation options**: femoral-side suspensory fixation (cortical button) vs interference screw (metal or bioabsorbable) — suspensory fixation is favored with soft-tissue grafts and smaller tunnel diameters, while BPTB bone plugs typically use interference screw fixation for bone-to-bone healing; tibial-side fixation similarly ranges from interference screw to staple/washer backup, chosen based on bone quality and graft type.
- **Technical pearls**: anatomic tunnel placement (avoid the historically "too-vertical" femoral tunnel that under-controls rotation); confirm graft tensioning at appropriate flexion angle per graft type; address meniscal and chondral pathology concurrently, including recognition of ramp lesions (posteromedial meniscocapsular tears, best seen on MRI or via posteromedial portal viewing, associated with residual rotational laxity if unrepaired) and lateral meniscal root/posterior horn tears; in combined injuries, sequence tunnels/fixation to avoid tunnel convergence.
- **Pitfalls**: missing a concomitant PLC or PCL injury preoperatively (leads to early ACL graft attritional failure — "cruciate graft stretches out if the corner or PCL is not addressed"); missing a ramp lesion or lateral meniscal root tear (residual instability despite a technically sound ACLR); over-aggressive early rehab in combined-ligament reconstructions risking secondary-restraint overload; in revision ACLR, failing to address tunnel widening/malposition (may require staged bone-grafting before re-reconstruction).

**PCL reconstruction**
- Indications: Grade III isolated PCL with persistent symptomatic instability after an adequate non-operative trial, combined ligament injury (virtually all MLKI patterns with PCL involvement), bony avulsion with displacement (often amenable to primary fragment fixation instead of ligament reconstruction), and chronic PCL insufficiency with functional limitation or early degenerative change.
- Technique options: single-bundle vs double-bundle (anterolateral + posteromedial) reconstruction — double-bundle offers theoretically better restoration of both flexion and extension stability but is technically demanding with limited proven clinical superiority; transtibial tunnel technique carries a recognized "killer turn" graft-bending risk that can predispose to attritional graft failure, favoring tibial inlay technique in some surgeons' hands, particularly for revision or combined posterior instability cases.
- Pearls/pitfalls: address associated PLC/posteromedial corner injury in the same setting or PCL graft will be overloaded and stretch out; avoid excessive posterior tibial tunnel angle to minimize the killer-turn effect; graft tensioning performed near full extension for most techniques.

**MCL / posteromedial corner repair-reconstruction**
- Indications: Grade III instability persisting after a structured 6-week brace trial (isolated cases), combined ACL/MCL injury with gross valgus instability or Stener-like proximal MCL displacement precluding healing, chronic medial instability.
- Acute repair (primary suture ± augmentation) favored within the first 2–3 weeks for tissue-quality-dependent avulsion-type tears; reconstruction (hamstring or allograft) favored for chronic/attenuated tissue or mid-substance tears with poor repair tissue quality.

**PLC/LCL repair-reconstruction**
- Acute repair (within ~2–3 weeks of injury) is preferred when tissue quality allows, since PLC structures retract and scar down poorly if repair is delayed.
- Chronic or delayed presentations (the majority in practice, given frequent missed diagnosis) require anatomic reconstruction (e.g., fibular-based sling techniques, Larson or Arciero-type reconstructions) using autograft/allograft to recreate the LCL, popliteofibular ligament, and popliteus tendon function.
- Pitfall: under-diagnosis is common because varus laxity can be masked by an intact ACL/PCL tensioning the knee in the exam room — always perform dial test comparison and document at both 30° and 90° of flexion.

**Multiligament knee injury / knee dislocation reconstruction**
- Sequencing: vascular repair (if present) takes absolute priority; external fixation is used as a temporizing bridge when vascular repair, open injury, gross instability, or soft-tissue compromise precludes bracing or early definitive fixation.
- Timing of ligament reconstruction: typically staged or single-stage at 2–6 weeks post-injury once swelling resolves and a reasonable ROM arc is regained — earlier surgery (<3 weeks) in the acute window is favored particularly for PLC repair given its poor healing tissue quality if delayed, while very early combined cruciate reconstruction in a stiff, swollen knee risks arthrofibrosis.
- Current international consensus (Murray et al. 2024) favors surgical management for most multiligament knee injuries, particularly in younger patients, over non-operative bracing, given the poor natural history of combined instability.
- Pitfall: failing to recognize and treat all injured structures in one coordinated plan (staged or combined) — leaving an unaddressed PLC or MCL injury is the single most common cause of early failure of cruciate reconstructions performed in the MLKI setting.

**Representative staging framework for MLKI surgical management**

| Stage | Timing | Typical content |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 0 (acute/ED) | Hours | Vascular clearance, reduction, splint in slight flexion, neurovascular re-check post-reduction |
| Stage 1 (damage control, if needed) | Day 0–3 | Vascular repair if present, external fixation if construct unstable/open injury/soft-tissue compromise, meniscal/cartilage repair opportunistically if arthroscopically feasible without stressing the healing envelope |
| Stage 2 (definitive reconstruction) | Week 2–6 | PLC/PMC repair or reconstruction (time-sensitive given healing biology) combined or staged with cruciate reconstruction(s); MCL addressed operatively only if unstable at reassessment |
| Stage 3 (rehabilitation) | Week 6 onward | Individualized, weakest-structure-driven progressive rehab as detailed below |

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Master weight-bearing and ROM milestone comparison** (post-operative pathways; non-operative pathways generally progress faster within each structure's own protocol above)

| Diagnosis | Weight-bearing at 2 wk | Weight-bearing at 6 wk | ROM goal by 6 wk | Brace-free ambulation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ACL (isolated) | WBAT with crutches, brace unlocked or absent per protocol | Full WB, no brace for ambulation | Full flexion | ~2–4 weeks |
| PCL (isolated, operative) | Touch-down/protected WB, brace in extension | Progressing to full WB | 0–90° (avoid unprotected posterior sag) | ~6 weeks |
| MCL (isolated, operative) | Protected WB in hinged brace | Full WB | 0–90° | ~4–6 weeks |
| PLC/LCL (operative) | Non-/touch-down WB in extension brace | Progressing to full WB | 0–90°, avoid terminal extension stress | ~6–8 weeks |
| MLKI (combined, operative) | Non-/touch-down WB per fixation construct | Protected, partial WB | Individualized to weakest structure, often <90° | ~8–12 weeks |

**Return-to-work guidance by occupational demand**

| Occupation category | Typical return (ACL/isolated ligament, post-op) | Typical return (MLKI, post-op) |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary/desk-based | 1–2 weeks (with crutches/brace as needed for commute/mobility) | 4–8 weeks |
| Light manual (standing, light lifting) | 4–8 weeks | 3–6 months |
| Heavy manual/labor, ladders, uneven terrain | 4–6 months | 6–12 months, individualized |

**Phase 0 (0–1 week, all diagnoses)**: control effusion/pain (RICE, NSAIDs per surgeon preference), early quadriceps activation (quad sets, straight-leg raise once extension lag resolves), protect healing structures per brace/weight-bearing protocol specific to injury pattern, restore passive extension early (loss of extension is a strong predictor of later arthrofibrosis).

**ACL post-reconstruction**
- Weeks 0–2: weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches (brace locked in extension for ambulation per surgeon protocol), ROM 0–90° goal, quad activation, patellar mobilization, cryotherapy.
- Weeks 2–6: progress to full weight-bearing without brace lock, ROM to full flexion, closed-chain strengthening (mini-squats, leg press), stationary bike once ROM allows, gait normalization.
- Weeks 6–12: open- and closed-chain progressive resistance training, proprioceptive/balance training, begin light jogging around 10–12 weeks if quadriceps strength and effusion criteria met.
- Months 3–6: sport-specific agility drills, plyometrics introduced with strict quality criteria (avoid dynamic valgus), non-contact/change-of-direction training progression.
- Months 6–9+: return-to-sport testing battery (quadriceps and hamstring limb symmetry index ≥90%, single-leg hop tests ≥90% LSI, ACL-RSI psychological readiness score, movement-quality screening for dynamic valgus). Earliest RTS clearance at 9 months given evidence that return before 9 months carries up to a 7-fold higher second-ACL-injury risk even at 90% LSI. Note that passing an RTS battery reduces graft-rupture risk in meta-analysis but has not been shown to reliably predict contralateral or overall subsequent knee injury — reinforcing the case for continued neuromuscular training after clearance, not just a one-time pass/fail test. Return-to-work timing varies by occupation: desk work 1–2 weeks with crutches as needed, manual/labor occupations typically 4–6 months depending on physical demand.

**PCL non-operative**
- Weeks 0–4: dynamic PCL brace (anterior tibial support), avoid isolated open-chain hamstring exercise (posteriorly directed tibial force), quad-dominant strengthening, progressive ROM avoiding prolonged flexion beyond 90° while brace-free.
- Weeks 4–12: progressive weight-bearing without brace for daily activity, closed-chain quad strengthening, stationary bike, gradual return to straight-line running around 8–12 weeks if stable.
- Months 3–6: sport-specific and cutting/pivoting progression with periodic laxity reassessment (stress radiographs if concern for progression); most Grade I–II and many isolated Grade III patients return to sport by 4–6 months.

**MCL**
- Grade I–II: hinged brace as needed for comfort, early full weight-bearing, ROM typically unrestricted from the outset; return to sport 1–3 (Grade I) to 4–6 (Grade II) weeks based on pain-free full ROM and functional testing.
- Grade III (non-operative trial): hinged brace 6 weeks with progressive ROM, protected weight-bearing early transitioning to full, reassess valgus stability at 6 weeks — if stable, continue rehab to return by 8–12 weeks; if unstable, proceed to surgery.
- Post-operative MCL repair/reconstruction (isolated or combined): brace-protected ROM 0–90° for first 2–4 weeks, progressive weight-bearing, strengthening from 6 weeks, return to sport typically 4–6 months (longer if combined with cruciate reconstruction).

**PLC/LCL post-operative**
- Weeks 0–6: strict non-weight-bearing or touch-down weight-bearing (protocol-dependent) in an extension brace to protect the repair/reconstruction from varus and rotational stress, gentle passive ROM avoiding terminal extension stress on repair.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing, ROM advancement, gentle closed-chain strengthening avoiding varus loading.
- Months 3–9: progressive strengthening, agility, and sport-specific training; return to cutting/pivoting sport typically not before 9–12 months given the biomechanical demands on the reconstructed corner.

**Multiligament knee injury / dislocation**
- Rehab is individualized around the "weakest link" structure (usually PLC or PCL, given the poorest healing/lowest mechanical tolerance) — typically a protected, delayed-ROM approach for the first 4–6 weeks (partial/non-weight-bearing per fixation construct), followed by progressive ROM and strengthening from 6–12 weeks, with sport-specific return generally not before 9–12 months and often 12+ months given the cumulative healing demands of multiple reconstructed structures.
- Arthrofibrosis prevention is a central rehab priority in MLKI — early, closely supervised ROM work balanced against ligament protection is a recurring technical tension requiring close surgeon-physiotherapist communication.

**Consolidated return-to-sport criteria (ACLR — representative composite battery)**

| Domain | Criterion | Typical threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Time from surgery | Minimum elapsed time | ≥9 months (not merely a strength/hop-test pass) |
| Quadriceps strength | Isokinetic limb symmetry index (LSI) | ≥90% (some protocols require ≥90% for both quad and hamstring) |
| Hop testing | Single-leg hop, triple hop, crossover hop, 6-m timed hop | ≥90% LSI on all four |
| Patient-reported outcome | IKDC subjective score | Comparable to normative/pre-injury values |
| Psychological readiness | ACL-RSI score | Higher scores associated with successful, sustained RTS; low scores flag need for continued psych-focused input |
| Movement quality | Video/2D drop-vertical-jump or clinician screen | Absence of dynamic valgus collapse |
| Effusion/ROM | Clinical exam | Full ROM, no effusion, no giving-way episodes during progression |

Passing this composite battery meaningfully reduces graft-rupture risk but has **not** been shown in recent meta-analyses to reliably predict contralateral or overall subsequent knee injury — RTS clearance should be framed to patients as risk-reduction, not risk-elimination, and neuromuscular/injury-prevention training (e.g., FIFA 11+, cutting-mechanics retraining) should continue after clearance, particularly in cutting/pivoting sport.

**Emerging rehabilitation adjuncts worth discussing with the physiotherapy team**: blood-flow-restriction (BFR) training in the early post-operative phase to mitigate quadriceps atrophy when full-load resistance exercise is contraindicated; neurocognitive/dual-task training (reactive agility, externally-paced decision-making drills) addressing the persistent altered visual reliance and threat-related movement behavior documented in athletes who otherwise meet standard physical RTS criteria; and criterion-based (rather than purely time-based) progression through every rehab phase, since fixed calendar-based advancement has consistently underperformed individualized milestone-based advancement in the contemporary literature.

## Complications

- **ACL**: graft failure/re-rupture (registry data: roughly 5–15% in primary ACLR depending on age/graft/activity level, higher in young high-risk patients without LET — hence the STABILITY rationale), contralateral ACL injury (comparable or higher risk than ipsilateral re-rupture in some cohorts), arthrofibrosis/cyclops lesion, anterior knee pain (more with BPTB), post-traumatic osteoarthritis (44% radiographic OA at 11 years in KANON regardless of treatment arm — surgery has not been shown to prevent long-term OA), tunnel malposition, infection (rare, <1%).
- **PCL**: residual posterior laxity (common even after reconstruction), graft "killer turn" attritional stretch with transtibial technique, patellofemoral and medial compartment OA over time related to persistent posterior tibial translation altering joint loading.
- **MCL**: stiffness/arthrofibrosis if over-immobilized, chronic valgus laxity if under-treated Grade III, saphenous nerve injury (infrapatellar branch) with surgical exposure.
- **PLC/LCL**: peroneal nerve injury/palsy (pre-existing from initial trauma or iatrogenic during reconstruction), chronic varus instability if unrecognized/undertreated, recurrent laxity/graft stretch given the corner's demanding biomechanical environment, and secondary failure of an associated cruciate graft if the PLC is not addressed concurrently.
- **MLKI/knee dislocation**: popliteal artery injury with limb-threatening ischemia (time-critical — amputation risk rises steeply beyond 8h warm ischemia), peroneal nerve palsy (often permanent, especially with KD-IIIL pattern), compartment syndrome (including post-revascularization reperfusion), arthrofibrosis (the most common complication overall in MLKI surgical series), deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism (prolonged immobilization + trauma = elevated VTE risk, consider chemoprophylaxis per institutional protocol), and post-traumatic osteoarthritis, which is essentially universal at long-term follow-up given the severity of initial cartilage/meniscal injury.

**Approximate complication frequency summary** (rates vary widely by series, energy of injury, and technique — use as order-of-magnitude reference, not precise benchmarks)

| Complication | Approximate rate | Structure(s) most implicated |
|---|---|---|
| Graft re-rupture (primary ACLR, general population) | ~5–15% | ACL |
| Graft failure, hamstring ACLR without LET, high-risk young patient | ~40% at 2 years | ACL (STABILITY control arm) |
| Graft failure, hamstring ACLR with LET, high-risk young patient | ~25% at 2 years | ACL (STABILITY intervention arm) |
| Contralateral ACL injury | Comparable to or exceeding ipsilateral re-rupture risk in some cohorts | ACL |
| Radiographic OA at 11 years post-ACL injury (either treatment arm) | ~44% | ACL (KANON) |
| Arthrofibrosis after MLKI reconstruction | Most common single complication in surgical series (double-digit %) | MLKI/all structures |
| Popliteal artery injury in true knee dislocation | ~5–15% (higher in high-energy patterns) | MLKI |
| Peroneal nerve injury with PLC-involved dislocation | ~15–40% | PLC/MLKI |
| Amputation risk with >8h warm ischemia (historical data) | Up to ~86% | MLKI vascular injury |
| Growth disturbance after physeal-sparing pediatric ACLR | ~2–3% | Pediatric ACL |
| Graft rupture, pediatric ACLR (general) | ~8% (recent meta-analysis) | Pediatric ACL |

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- [AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline update for Management of ACL Injuries (2022, still current guideline in force through this window)](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-updates-clinical-practice-guideline-for-management-of-anterior-cruciate-ligament-injuries/) — shortened the recommended window for surgical reconstruction of acute isolated ACL tears from 5 months to within 3 months of injury, reflecting rising meniscal/chondral injury risk with delay; also newly endorses hop-test-based functional evaluation as one RTS factor and injury-prevention neuromuscular training programs for at-risk athletes.
- [Association Between ACL Continuity on MRI at 5 Years and 11-Year Outcomes: Secondary Analysis of the KANON Trial (Filbay et al., AJSM 2025)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/03635465251339061) — extends KANON follow-up to 11 years, confirming no clinically important difference in patient-reported outcomes between early-ACLR and rehabilitation-with-optional-delayed-ACLR arms, while documenting 44% radiographic OA at 11 years in both groups — reinforces that surgical timing strategy does not appear to alter long-term OA trajectory.
- [Cross Bracing Protocol for ACL Rupture Has Unacceptably High Failure Rate Relative to Surgical Stabilization: A 2-Year Controlled Cohort Study (Porter & Shadbolt)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41622530/) — direct comparative cohort data showing ~70% recurrent instability and ~62% concurrent medial meniscal tear with the cross-bracing protocol versus 2.5%/2.5% with surgical ACLR in pivoting-sport athletes at 2 years; substantially tempers prior enthusiasm for CBP as a routine alternative to surgery in this population and should be discussed explicitly with patients considering the protocol.
- [Lateral Extra-Articular Tenodesis Reduces Failure of Hamstring Tendon Autograft ACLR: 2-Year Outcomes From the STABILITY Study RCT](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31940222/) with continued follow-up analyses through 2023–2025 including [Lateral Extra-Articular Tenodesis as an Adjunct to ACL Reconstruction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40308341/) — confirms durable reduction in clinical graft failure (40%→25%) with LET added to hamstring ACLR in patients under 25 at high risk of failure, without a significant increase in adverse events; has driven wider adoption of routine LET/ALL augmentation in this risk profile.
- [Lateral Extra-articular Procedures Combined With ACL Reconstructions Lead to a Higher Return to Pre-Injury Level of Sport: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (2024–2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11894468/) — corroborates STABILITY findings across a broader literature base, supporting LET/ALL as an evidence-based adjunct rather than an outlier single-trial result.
- [25-Year Trends in ACL Reconstruction: Results from the Biennial ACL Study Group Survey 1998–2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2059775425006571) — documents the graft-choice shift: quadriceps tendon autograft rising to ~20% of primary ACLR by 2023 (from <5% pre-2014) while hamstring autograft use declines from its 2014 peak (~62%) — a practical signal for surgeons benchmarking their own graft-selection patterns against contemporary norms.
- [Age-Specific Over-the-Top Techniques for Physeal-Sparing ACL Reconstruction in Skeletally Immature Patients (Grassi et al., KSSTA 2025)](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.12607) — refines pediatric ACLR technique selection by growth stage (prepubescent, young adolescent, older adolescent), reinforcing extra-physeal/over-the-top strategies to minimize growth-plate violation while maintaining rotational and translational stability.
- [Physeal-Sparing ACL Reconstruction Has Equivalent Survivorship and Functional Outcomes as Transphyseal Reconstruction but Does Not Prevent Growth Disturbance: Systematic Review (2025)](https://www.thekneejournal.com/article/S0968-0160(25)00159-0/abstract) — important nuance for counseling families: physeal-sparing technique reduces but does not eliminate growth-disturbance risk, and graft survivorship is comparable between physeal-sparing and transphyseal approaches when technique is properly matched to skeletal maturity.
- [Long-term Follow-up of Patients with Acute PCL Injury Treated Non-operatively with a Physiotherapy-led Exercise and Support Brace Intervention](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12048365/) — 5-year data supporting durable patient-reported outcome improvement with structured non-operative PCL management, with need for delayed reconstruction (when it occurs) concentrated within the first 2 years — useful for setting patient expectations on the non-operative PCL pathway's time horizon.
- [Operative and Non-operative Management Can Result in Acceptable Long-Term Outcomes for Isolated PCL Injuries in Paediatric Patients (Batley et al., J Exp Orthop 2025)](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/jeo2.70421) — extends the reassuring non-operative PCL data specifically to the pediatric population, supporting a similarly conservative-first approach in skeletally immature patients with isolated PCL injury.
- [New Algorithm for Management of Multiligament Knee Injuries: A Tertiary Level Trauma Center Experience (2024–2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12296687/) — proposes a structured, reproducible pathway for vascular screening, staged fixation, and reconstruction timing in MLKI, aligning with international consensus recommendations (Murray et al. 2024) that favor surgical management for most multiligament injuries, particularly in younger patients, over non-operative bracing.
- [Delaying ACL Reconstruction and Treating with Exercise Therapy Alone May Alter Prognostic Factors for 5-Year Outcome: Exploratory Analysis of the KANON Trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5754848/) — identifies that surgery, cartilage injury, and patient-reported measures within 4 weeks of ACL rupture are prognostic for 5-year outcome regardless of treatment arm, informing which rehab-first patients are more likely to need eventual crossover to surgery.
- [Return-to-Sport (RTS) Tests and Criteria Following ACLR: A Scoping Review (2025)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0968016025002145) together with [Association Between Passing RTS Testing and Re-injury Risk After ACLR: Systematic Review and Meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11064852/) — 2024–2025 syntheses showing passing RTS batteries reduces graft-rupture risk but is not a reliable predictor of subsequent contralateral or overall knee injury, supporting continued post-clearance neuromuscular training rather than treating RTS testing as a definitive "safe to stop" checkpoint.
- [Integrating Psychological and Physical Assessments to Obtain Optimal Return-to-Sport Outcomes After ACLR: Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis (2025)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2025.2534224) — reinforces that combining ACL-RSI psychological-readiness scoring with physical/hop-test batteries outperforms physical testing alone in predicting successful, sustained return to sport.
