## Quick Reference

**Definition anchor:** PJI is a biofilm disease on an implant surface; no single test is 100% sensitive/specific — diagnosis is a synthesis of clinical, serum, synovial, microbiological and histological data (EBJIS 2021, ICM 2018/2025).

### Classification-to-management snapshot

| System | Category | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EBJIS (2021)** | Infection unlikely | No major criteria, and minor criteria below threshold | Reassess alternative diagnosis; do not treat empirically |
| | Infection likely | 1 minor clinical/serum criterion + ≥1 minor synovial/microbiology criterion, but not meeting "confirmed" | Proceed to aspiration/further work-up before committing to surgical infection protocol |
| | Infected (confirmed) | ≥1 major criterion (sinus tract, visible pus, 2 positive cultures of same organism) OR combination of minor criteria above threshold | Full infection surgical pathway (DAIR/one-stage/two-stage) |
| **2018 ICM/MSIS** | Infected | ≥1 major criterion OR preoperative score ≥6 (or intra-op score ≥6 combining pre + intra-op points) | Treat as PJI |
| | Inconclusive | Score 4–5 (or 3–5 preop) | Use intraoperative findings (purulence, frozen section, single positive culture, next-generation sequencing) to reclassify |
| | Not infected | Score <3–4 | Treat as aseptic failure |
| **Tsukayama/Zimmerli timing** | Type I – Positive intra-op culture (incidental) | ≥2 positive cultures at index revision presumed aseptic | Prolonged antibiotics only, no re-operation if patient well |
| | Type II – Early postoperative | Onset <1 month (Tsukayama) / <3 months (Zimmerli-style acute) | DAIR with modular exchange, high salvage rate if <3–4 weeks symptoms |
| | Type III – Acute hematogenous | Sudden onset in previously well prosthesis, any time post-op, from a remote source | DAIR feasible if <3 weeks symptom duration and stable implant |
| | Type IV – Chronic | Onset >1 month post-op (Tsukayama) with indolent symptoms ≥3–4 weeks | Implant removal — one-stage or two-stage exchange; DAIR contraindicated |
| **McPherson host grading** | Host type A | Uncompromised, no comorbidity | Best prognosis for retention/single-stage strategies |
| | Host type B | 1–2 compromising comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, RA, immunosuppression, smoking, hepatic disease) | Moderate risk; optimize before surgery |
| | Host type C | Severely compromised (≥3 comorbidities, or single "severe" one: malnutrition, chronic renal failure/dialysis, IV drug use, active malignancy, liver failure) | High failure risk; consider suppression/salvage over aggressive eradication |
| | Local extremity grade 1–3 | 1 = none; 2 = compromised soft tissue (scarring, sinus, prior surgery); 3 = severely compromised (multiple prior operations, vascular disease, chronic lymphedema) | Guides soft-tissue reconstruction planning, need for flap coverage |

### Treatment ladder per diagnosis

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical salvage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acute PJI** (postoperative <4–6 wk, or hematogenous <3 wk symptoms, stable well-fixed implant, no sinus) | DAIR with full modular component exchange + 12-week targeted antibiotics | Repeat DAIR only if clear technical failure and favorable host; otherwise convert to staged exchange | One- or two-stage exchange if DAIR fails or criteria not met |
| **Chronic PJI** (>4–6 wk symptoms, loose implant, sinus tract, or biofilm-mature organism) | Two-stage exchange (spacer + 6–12 wk antibiotics, then reimplantation) | One-stage exchange in selected patients (identified organism, adequate soft tissue, no gross purulence, host optimized) | Resection arthroplasty / arthrodesis / amputation for unsalvageable cases |
| **Culture-negative PJI** | Empiric broad-spectrum regimen (vancomycin + anti-Gram-negative agent) pending NGS/extended culture; treat per staging as above | Sonication of explant, extended (14-day) culture, 16S PCR, mNGS/tNGS to redirect therapy | Same surgical algorithm as culture-positive; do not withhold surgery awaiting a pathogen |

### Red flags
- **Sinus tract or visible communication with joint** → PJI confirmed regardless of any other test (EBJIS/ICM major criterion) — do not aspirate through it for "diagnosis," go to theatre.
- **Symptom duration >3–4 weeks** in an otherwise "acute" presentation → biofilm likely mature; DAIR success drops sharply — favor staged exchange.
- **CRP >115 mg/L, cemented implant, liver cirrhosis, chronic renal failure, revision (not primary) index surgery** — the 5 KLIC components; ≥2 points raises DAIR failure risk substantially.
- **Unstable/loose implant or exposed cement mantle** → DAIR is contraindicated irrespective of timing.
- **Difficult-to-treat organisms** (rifampin-resistant staphylococci, fungi, multidrug-resistant Gram-negatives, quinolone-resistant Gram-negatives when biofilm-active regimen relies on quinolone) → lower DAIR/one-stage success; consider two-stage or suppression-only strategy in frail hosts.

### Key numbers to memorize
- ICM 2018 score: **≥6 = infected, 4–5 = inconclusive, ≤3 = not infected** (preoperative decision points; intra-op score also ≥6 confirms).
- ICM 2018 minor threshold (chronic, no antecedent inflammatory disease): **ESR ≥30 mm/h, CRP ≥10 mg/L (aspiration threshold used more commonly is CRP >10 mg/L), synovial WBC ≥3,000 cells/µL, synovial PMN% ≥80%, positive leukocyte esterase, single positive culture, ≥5–10 PMN/HPF on 5 high-power fields.**
- EBJIS acute-setting synovial WBC threshold is higher (~10,000/µL) because postoperative inflammation elevates baseline counts.
- Alpha-defensin: sensitivity ~90–97% (ELISA better than lateral-flow), specificity ~95–97% — most useful when serum markers/synovial WBC are equivocal or in inflammatory arthropathy/metal-on-metal cases.
- **12-week minimum total antibiotic duration** after debridement is now the ICM 2025-endorsed floor for most PJI regimens (longer for chronic suppression candidates).
- DAIR success overall: roughly **60–90%** depending on timing/host/organism; drops below 50% beyond 3–4 weeks of symptoms or with Staph aureus + poor host.
- Two-stage reported infection-free survival: **~85–90%** at mid-term; one-stage in well-selected chronic PJI cohorts now approaches similar figures (**~85–98%** in recent RCT/registry data) — selection criteria matter more than "stage number."

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

PJI is fundamentally a **biofilm disease**. Within hours to days of bacterial adhesion to an implant or necrotic bone/cement surface, organisms embed in a self-produced extracellular polymeric matrix that:
- Reduces antibiotic penetration and metabolic activity of embedded bacteria (persister cells), rendering standard MIC-based dosing ineffective.
- Shields organisms from host phagocytosis and complement-mediated killing.
- Matures over roughly **3–4 weeks** in most models — the biological basis for the "acute vs chronic" timing cutoffs used clinically (Tsukayama, Zimmerli, and modern DAIR-eligibility criteria all converge near this window).

**Routes of contamination:**
1. **Direct/perioperative inoculation** — the dominant route for early postoperative infection; skin flora (coagulase-negative staphylococci, S. aureus) seeded at the time of index surgery or shortly after via wound breakdown.
2. **Hematogenous seeding** — from a remote focus (dental, urinary, skin/soft tissue, bacteremia of any source) onto a previously well-functioning prosthesis at any time post-implantation; classic acute hematogenous presentation.
3. **Contiguous spread** — from an adjacent focus of infection (e.g., untreated osteomyelitis, infected hardware nearby).

**Microbiology:** Staphylococci (S. aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci, notably S. epidermidis) account for **60–70%** of culture-positive cases. Streptococci and enterococci follow, often in hematogenous seeding. Gram-negative organisms and polymicrobial infections are more frequent in wound-healing complications, sinus tracts and diabetic/immunocompromised hosts. Fungal and mycobacterial PJI are rare but important in immunosuppressed or previously multiply-treated joints. **Rifampin-susceptible staphylococcal biofilm** is the key determinant of DAIR/retention success because rifampin is the only widely available agent with reliable anti-biofilm activity against staphylococci when combined with a partner drug (to avoid resistance emergence).

**Host and local factors modulating risk** (formalized in McPherson grading, see Classification): diabetes (especially poorly controlled, HbA1c >7–8%), obesity, rheumatoid arthritis/immunosuppression, smoking, malnutrition (albumin <3.5 g/dL, transferrin <200 mg/dL, total lymphocyte count <1500/mm³), chronic kidney/liver disease, prior surgery on the joint, and local soft-tissue compromise (radiation, multiple incisions, chronic lymphedema).

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

### History and examination
- Onset timing relative to index arthroplasty (critical for Tsukayama/Zimmerli staging and DAIR eligibility) — establish the exact date of symptom onset, not just presentation date.
- Pain character: infection classically causes pain at rest and night pain, in contrast to aseptic loosening (activity-related, start-up pain).
- Wound history: persistent drainage beyond 5–7 days postoperatively, dehiscence, hematoma requiring evacuation, prolonged erythema.
- Systemic signs: fever, rigors, malaise — more common in acute/hematogenous presentations, often absent in chronic low-grade infection.
- Sinus tract — pathognomonic (major criterion in both EBJIS and ICM); document location and probe only in the operating theatre, not in clinic (risk of introducing secondary contamination and unreliable culture).
- Screen for a hematogenous source: dental disease, skin/soft-tissue infection, UTI, recent bacteremia, IV drug use.

### Serum markers
- **CRP and ESR** — first-line screening, cheap, widely available. ICM 2018 chronic thresholds: CRP ≥10 mg/L, ESR ≥30 mm/h (hip and knee combined threshold; knee-specific CRP threshold sometimes cited slightly higher). Both markers are nonspecific (elevated by any inflammatory process) but a combination with both normal has a high negative predictive value for chronic PJI.
- In the **acute postoperative window** (<6 weeks), both CRP and ESR are physiologically elevated from surgery itself; use trend (failure to fall, or a secondary rise after an initial decline) rather than an absolute threshold.
- **D-dimer** has been proposed as an additional serum marker with reasonable sensitivity but variable specificity; used adjunctively, not as a standalone criterion in most algorithms.
- **IL-6** — more rapid normalization kinetics than CRP; useful research/adjunct marker where available.

### Synovial fluid analysis
- **Aspiration is the pivotal diagnostic step** whenever PJI is suspected and the diagnosis is not already confirmed by a sinus tract. Off antibiotics for a minimum of 2 weeks whenever feasible before aspiration to avoid false negatives.
- Send for: total WBC count with differential (% PMN), culture (aerobic + anaerobic, extended incubation), and where available alpha-defensin and/or leukocyte esterase strip testing.
- ICM 2018 chronic thresholds: synovial WBC ≥3,000 cells/µL, PMN% ≥80%. EBJIS uses a similar chronic threshold but a substantially higher acute-phase WBC cutoff (~10,000 cells/µL) to account for normal postoperative synovial inflammation.
- **Leukocyte esterase** strip test (colorimetric, ++ reading) is a rapid, low-cost point-of-care minor criterion with good specificity.

### Alpha-defensin
- An antimicrobial peptide released by activated neutrophils, specific to a true infective/neutrophil-driven process rather than mechanical/inflammatory synovitis (useful in inflammatory arthropathy, metal-on-metal/adverse local tissue reaction, and equivocal serum/synovial marker cases).
- **ELISA format**: sensitivity ~90–97%, specificity ~95–97% — the more accurate of the two formats per recent (2024–2025) systematic reviews/meta-analyses.
- **Lateral-flow (point-of-care) format**: faster but modestly lower sensitivity than ELISA; still specific.
- Not a replacement for culture — a positive alpha-defensin does not identify the organism or guide antibiotic selection, and it can be falsely positive with certain metal debris/crystalline synovitis.

### Microbiology technique
- **Multiple intraoperative tissue samples (≥3, ideally 5–6)** from distinct sites, separate instruments per sample, sent for extended culture (≥14 days) to capture low-virulence/slow-growing organisms (e.g., Cutibacterium acnes in shoulder PJI).
- **Sonication of the removed implant**: dislodges biofilm-embedded organisms from the implant surface into fluid for culture; increases sensitivity over standard tissue culture, particularly valuable when the patient has received recent antibiotics or in low-grade chronic infection.
- Hold antibiotics for at least 2 weeks pre-op when clinically safe to maximize culture yield.

### Next-generation sequencing (NGS) and molecular diagnostics
- **Metagenomic NGS (mNGS)** sequences all nucleic acid in a sample (synovial fluid, sonicate, or tissue) and identifies pathogens without a priori targeting; **targeted NGS (tNGS)** amplifies a curated panel of orthopedic pathogens first, improving sensitivity for low-biomass samples.
- In culture-negative PJI cohorts, mNGS identifies a causative organism in roughly **85–90%** of cases where standard culture is negative, and turnaround is markedly faster (~1.3 days vs ~5.2 days for culture in comparative series).
- Clinical role: reserved for culture-negative PJI meeting clinical/ICM criteria, cases with prior antibiotic exposure, and situations where targeted (rather than broad empiric) antimicrobial therapy would change management. Interpret with caution — detects DNA from dead/non-viable organisms and can pick up low-level contaminants, so results must be correlated with the clinical picture and ideally combined with culture and histology.

### Histology
- Frozen section with **≥5 PMN per high-power field averaged across 5 fields** (classic threshold, various modifications exist) is a validated intraoperative minor criterion, useful when preoperative work-up is inconclusive and a same-anesthetic decision (retain vs remove) is needed.

### Imaging
- **Plain radiographs**: baseline for implant position, loosening (progressive radiolucent lines, migration), periosteal reaction — nonspecific for infection but essential for surgical planning.
- **CT**: better delineation of bone loss, osteolysis, and periprosthetic fracture pattern for revision planning; metal artifact reduction protocols help.
- **MRI** with metal artifact reduction sequences: increasingly used to assess soft-tissue/abscess extent and rule out adverse local tissue reaction as a mimic.
- **Nuclear medicine** (labeled-leukocyte scan with bone marrow scan, FDG-PET): reserved for equivocal cases after standard work-up, particularly useful when aspiration is non-diagnostic or contraindicated; not first-line given cost/availability and interobserver variability.
- Imaging is adjunctive — it never overrides a positive major criterion (sinus tract, purulence, concordant positive cultures) or a diagnostic ICM/EBJIS score.

## Classification

### EBJIS 2021 definition (European Bone and Joint Infection Society)

Developed jointly with MSIS and the ESCMID Study Group for Implant-Associated Infections; designed as a **rule-in / rule-out algorithm** usable at every stage of work-up (clinic, aspiration, theatre) rather than a single-timepoint score.

| Category | Criteria | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Infection unlikely** | None of the below present | Investigate alternative diagnosis (aseptic loosening, instability, ALTR) |
| **Infection likely** | ≥1 of: elevated CRP (unexplained by other cause); imaging suggestive of infection; presence of new-onset synovitis or effusion with elevated synovial WBC/PMN%/leukocyte esterase below "confirmed" threshold; single positive culture of a virulent organism | Proceed to further synovial/microbiological work-up before committing to definitive surgical infection pathway; treat as infected if pretest probability high and further tests support it |
| **Infected (confirmed)** | ≥1 major criterion: **sinus tract communicating with the joint**, **visible purulence around the prosthesis**, or **≥2 positive cultures with phenotypically identical organism** — OR a combination of minor criteria (elevated synovial WBC, elevated synovial PMN%, positive alpha-defensin, positive histology, single positive culture of a virulent organism plus supportive serum/synovial markers) reaching the defined threshold | Full surgical infection pathway (DAIR/one-stage/two-stage as indicated) |

Management implication: EBJIS is preferred by many European centers for its stepwise "unlikely → likely → confirmed" logic that mirrors real clinical workflow (clinic referral → aspiration → theatre), reducing the number of patients left in a diagnostic "gray zone" compared with ICM's fixed scoring system.

### 2018 ICM/MSIS criteria (International Consensus Meeting / Musculoskeletal Infection Society)

**Major criteria (either is diagnostic alone):**
1. Two positive periprosthetic cultures with phenotypically identical organisms.
2. A sinus tract communicating with the joint.

**Minor criteria — weighted scoring system (used when major criteria are not met):**

*Preoperative:*
| Criterion | Points |
|---|---|
| Elevated CRP (≥10 mg/L) or D-dimer | 2 |
| Elevated ESR (≥30 mm/h) | 1 |
| Elevated synovial WBC (≥3,000 cells/µL) or leukocyte esterase strip ++ | 3 |
| Positive alpha-defensin | 3 |
| Elevated synovial PMN% (≥80%) | 2 |
| Elevated synovial CRP | 1 |

*Preoperative score interpretation:* **≥6 = infected**; **2–5 = inconclusive** (proceed to intraoperative criteria); **0–1 = not infected**.

*Intraoperative criteria (added to preoperative subtotal when inconclusive):*
| Criterion | Points |
|---|---|
| Positive histology (≥5 PMN/HPF in 5 fields) | 3 |
| Positive single culture | 2 |
| Purulence | 3 |

*Combined score interpretation:* **≥6 = infected**, **4–5 = inconclusive** (clinical judgment, consider NGS/repeat sampling), **≤3 = not infected**.

Management implication: the numeric scoring system is well suited to structured, reproducible decision-making and is the basis for most published outcome registries; it is also what most journals now require for study eligibility, making it the default framework for evidence appraisal. **ICM 2025** revisited and updated ~37 of the 2018 recommendations (21.5% of comparable questions changed), including strengthening the case for a **minimum 12-week total antibiotic course after debridement** and recognizing increased revision-arthroplasty infection risk after prior ipsilateral arthroscopy — practitioners should treat ICM 2018 as the diagnostic scaffold and ICM 2025 as the current management-recommendation layer on top of it.

### Tsukayama / Zimmerli timing classification

The timing classification is the operative decision-driver: it determines whether implant retention (DAIR) is biologically plausible (biofilm immature) or whether component removal is mandatory (biofilm mature).

| Type | Definition | Typical organism pattern | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Type I — Positive intraoperative culture** | ≥2 unexpected positive cultures at what was presumed aseptic revision surgery | Low-virulence organisms (coagulase-negative staph, Cutibacterium) | No re-operation needed if implant already well-fixed and patient well; treat with a full antibiotic course (targeted, typically 3 months) |
| **Type II — Early postoperative infection** | Onset <4 weeks (some series <1 month) after index arthroplasty | Virulent perioperative contaminants (S. aureus, Gram-negatives, polymicrobial) | DAIR with exchange of all modular components (liner, head/insert) — biofilm still immature, best salvage rates |
| **Type III — Acute hematogenous infection** | Sudden onset in a previously well-functioning prosthesis, from a remote bacteremic source, **symptom duration <3 weeks** at presentation | S. aureus, streptococci most common (bacteremic seeding) | DAIR with modular exchange if implant stable and symptom duration short; source control of the primary bacteremic focus mandatory |
| **Type IV — Chronic infection** | Onset/symptoms >4 weeks (postoperative) or >3 weeks (hematogenous), indolent course, often with sinus tract or radiographic loosening | Any organism, often biofilm-adapted low-virulence organisms | Implant removal required — one-stage or two-stage exchange; DAIR contraindicated (unacceptably high failure) |

(Zimmerli's clinical classification is essentially the same three/four-tier timing logic — early postoperative, late chronic, acute hematogenous — and is used interchangeably with Tsukayama in most European literature; both converge on the same operative branch-point.)

### McPherson host-grading system

A three-axis system (host physiologic grade + local extremity grade + type of infecting organism/systemic grade in some iterations) originally adapted from the Cierny-Mader osteomyelitis staging concept, applied specifically to PJI to risk-stratify before committing to a treatment strategy.

**Host physiological grade:**
| Grade | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Uncompromised host** | No systemic compromising factors | Standard eradication strategy (DAIR/one- or two-stage per timing) has best expected success |
| **B — Compromised host** | 1–2 of: rheumatoid arthritis/inflammatory arthropathy, diabetes, chronic immunosuppression/steroid use, active smoking, age extremes with frailty | Intensify medical optimization pre-op; expect intermediate success rates; consider two-stage over one-stage if additional risk factors present |
| **C — Significantly compromised host** | ≥3 of the above, or any single "severe" comorbidity: chronic renal failure/dialysis, chronic liver disease/cirrhosis, malignancy on active treatment, malnutrition, IV drug use, AIDS/severe immunosuppression | High expected failure of eradication surgery; discuss suppression-only, resection arthroplasty, or arthrodesis as primary strategy rather than aggressive exchange |

**Local extremity grade:**
| Grade | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | No local compromise | No special soft-tissue planning needed |
| **2** | Compromised soft tissue envelope (scarring, multiple prior incisions, chronic sinus, prior soft-tissue procedures) | Plan soft-tissue coverage/plastic surgery input, consider staged wound closure |
| **3** | Severely compromised (large-vessel disease, extensive scarring, chronic lymphedema, multiple failed prior surgeries) | High risk of persistent wound complications; consider free/rotational flap, or amputation discussion in extreme cases |

Management implication overall: McPherson grading (host + local grade, sometimes combined with a systemic "type of infection" axis) is the primary tool for setting realistic expectations with the patient and choosing between **eradication** (DAIR/exchange) versus **palliative/suppressive** strategies — a Host C / Local 3 patient with a low-demand joint is a rational candidate for chronic suppression or resection arthroplasty rather than a second major reconstructive attempt.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Not a definitive strategy for confirmed PJI** except in the specific subset of patients unfit for any surgery (severe medical comorbidity, limited life expectancy, patient declines surgery) — here, **long-term (often lifelong) suppressive oral antibiotic therapy** is used to control symptoms rather than eradicate infection.
- Suppression is also the standard adjunct **after** DAIR or exchange arthroplasty in selected higher-risk hosts (McPherson host C, difficult-to-treat organism, incomplete debridement) — recent cohort data (2024) show suppression is generally well tolerated without inducing resistance, though the evidence on whether it changes reinfection/failure rates versus a defined-course stop is mixed; several 2024 cohort studies found **no significant difference in outcomes between stopping suppression at a defined point versus continuing indefinitely** in carefully selected patients who were doing well, suggesting individualized rather than blanket indefinite suppression.
- Optimize modifiable host factors preoperatively regardless of the operative plan: glycemic control (HbA1c target <8%, ideally <7% if achievable without hypoglycemia risk), smoking cessation, nutritional repletion (albumin, prealbumin, total lymphocyte count), reduction/bridging of immunosuppressive medications where feasible in coordination with rheumatology, and treatment of any remote infection source before elective reconstruction.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Diagnostic aspiration** is the core "adjunctive procedure" in the PJI pathway (see Clinical Evaluation) — image-guided (fluoroscopy or ultrasound) if a dry tap is likely or if precise intra-articular placement is needed, particularly in the hip.
- **Local antibiotic delivery adjuncts at the time of surgery**: antibiotic-loaded bone cement (spacers and, in some one-stage protocols, definitive cement fixation), antibiotic-loaded calcium sulfate/hydroxyapatite beads, and intrawound vancomycin powder — used to achieve high local antibiotic concentrations exceeding biofilm MBEC (minimum biofilm eradication concentration) while limiting systemic toxicity; evidence for vancomycin powder specifically remains mixed in recent high-quality RCT data (see Recent Evidence).
- **Dilute povidone-iodine (betadine) lavage** prior to closure is widely practiced as a low-cost adjunct; a large multicenter RCT (2025–2026 data) comparing vancomycin powder, dilute povidone-iodine, combination, and saline control in high-risk primary TJA found **no significant differences in infection/wound-complication rates at 3 months or 1 year** between arms — reinforcing that this remains a reasonable-but-unproven adjunct rather than a mandated intervention.
- Therapeutic corticosteroid or hyaluronic acid injections have **no role** once PJI is suspected or confirmed and should be avoided if infection is on the differential, as they can mask signs and confound synovial fluid analysis.

### Operative

**DAIR (Debridement, Antibiotics, and Implant Retention)**

*Indications (all should generally be met):*
- Acute infection by timing classification: early postoperative (<4 weeks) or acute hematogenous with **symptom duration <3 weeks**.
- Implant well-fixed/stable on imaging and intraoperative assessment.
- Intact, closable soft-tissue envelope (no established sinus tract or extensive abscess undermining skin).
- Organism identified or presumed susceptible to an oral, anti-biofilm-active regimen (rifampin-susceptible staphylococcus is the favorable scenario).
- Host and local grade favorable enough that retention is a reasonable goal (McPherson A/B preferred).

*Technique:* Full arthrotomy/arthroscopic access is inadequate — open debridement with complete synovectomy, thorough lavage (pulsatile lavage, large volume, ± antiseptic adjunct), **exchange of all modular components** (polyethylene liner/insert, femoral head, tibial insert) even though fixed components are retained, and multiple deep cultures before starting antibiotics.

*Success predictors — validated risk scores:*

| Score | Components | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **KLIC score** (for early post-op DAIR) | **K**idney (chronic renal failure, +2), **L**iver cirrhosis (+1.5), **I**ndex surgery = revision not primary (+1.5), **C**emented prosthesis (+2), CRP >115 mg/L (+1.5) | Score <2: low failure risk (favorable for DAIR); ≥4: substantially higher failure risk — consider staged exchange instead. Note: external validation has been inconsistent (some Northern European cohorts found no predictive value), so use as one input among several, not a strict gatekeeper. |
| **CRIME80 score** (for late acute/hematogenous DAIR) | Age ≥80, Chronic renal failure, Rheumatoid/inflammatory arthritis, Male sex, cementless implant, Extended surgery (polymicrobial or difficult organism) — components vary slightly by publication | Higher score predicts DAIR failure in late-acute presentations; some validation series found CRIME80 more robust than KLIC specifically for late acute knee PJI. |

Both scores should be interpreted as **decision-support, not absolute cutoffs** — recent (2024–2025) external validation studies have shown inconsistent predictive performance across cohorts, and several found neither score reliably predicted failure at repeat DAIR. Use them to frame the discussion of expected success with the patient, alongside timing, organism, and host grade.

*Reported DAIR success:* roughly 60–90% at 2 years when strict indications are followed (early postoperative, favorable organism, good host); success falls sharply (often <50%) with S. aureus infection, symptom duration >3–4 weeks, and McPherson host C patients.

**One-stage exchange arthroplasty**

*Indications:* Chronic PJI with an **identified organism preoperatively**, adequate soft-tissue envelope for primary closure, absence of gross purulence/extensive bone loss requiring staged reconstruction, and a host fit for a single larger procedure. Historically reserved for a narrow European (originally Endo-Klinik) cohort; indications have broadened in recent registries and RCT data.

*Technique:* Single anesthetic — complete removal of all components and cement, extensive debridement with a change of gloves/instruments/draping ("clean" phase), then reimplantation of new components (often with antibiotic-loaded cement) in the same setting, guided by known organism sensitivity for local and systemic antibiotic selection.

**Two-stage exchange arthroplasty**

*Indications:* Chronic PJI without a reliably identified organism preoperatively, extensive bone/soft-tissue loss requiring interval reconstruction planning, difficult-to-treat or multidrug-resistant organisms, sinus tract requiring interval soft-tissue management, or host/local factors that favor a staged, lower-single-procedure-burden approach.

*Technique (Stage 1):* Radical debridement, removal of all components and cement, placement of an **antibiotic-loaded cement spacer** (static or articulating — articulating preferred where bone stock allows, to preserve soft-tissue length and joint motion, easing reimplantation and improving interim function). Spacer antibiotics typically combine a heat-stable agent (vancomycin) with a Gram-negative-active agent (an aminoglycoside such as tobramycin/gentamicin), tailored to culture results once available.

*Interval:* Typically **6 weeks (systemic antibiotic course, IV or highly bioavailable oral)** followed by a variable antibiotic-free "washout" window before reimplantation (commonly 2–4 weeks) with reassessment of serum markers and, where equivocal, repeat aspiration before Stage 2. Total interval is commonly quoted as 6–12 weeks total but varies by center and organism.

*Stage 2:* Reimplantation once serologic/clinical markers trend toward normal and (if performed) repeat aspiration/intraoperative findings do not suggest persistent infection; deep cultures are taken again at reimplantation regardless.

**Salvage procedures** (unsalvageable joint / failed prior eradication attempts, especially McPherson host C / local grade 3): **resection (Girdlestone) arthroplasty** for the hip, **arthrodesis** for the knee (intramedullary nail or external fixation), and **above-knee/through-knee amputation** as a last resort for uncontrolled infection threatening life or limb. These are legitimate, sometimes preferable, end points rather than "failures" in the frailest hosts — reframe with the patient as a functional/quality-of-life decision, not a defeat.

**Selection rationale — one-stage vs two-stage:** The historical dogma that two-stage is the unconditional "gold standard" is being revised. Recent meta-analyses (2024) of pooled cohort data (40 studies, ~8,700 patients) found **no significant difference in reinfection or reoperation rates** between one- and two-stage approaches overall, and a 2024 multicenter RCT presented at AAHKS reported 2-year success of **98% for one-stage vs 91% for two-stage**, including patients with resistant organisms and draining wounds — though this remains a single trial requiring confirmation and one-stage still depends heavily on preoperative organism identification and adequate soft tissue. Practical synthesis: **two-stage remains the default for unknown-organism, poor soft-tissue, or highly resistant-organism chronic PJI**; **one-stage is a reasonable, increasingly evidence-supported alternative** when the organism is known, soft tissue is adequate, and the surgical team has the experience/infrastructure (immediate intraoperative antibiogram-guided cementing) to execute it — the deciding factor is patient/organism selection, not a fixed hierarchy of stages.

**Antibiotic strategy (systemic):**
- Empiric broad-spectrum coverage (typically vancomycin ± a Gram-negative agent) at the time of debridement/explantation, narrowed once culture and sensitivities return.
- **Rifampin combination therapy** for confirmed rifampin-susceptible staphylococcal biofilm infection where implant is retained or freshly reimplanted (DAIR, one-stage, or Stage 2 of two-stage) — never as monotherapy (rapid resistance emergence), always paired with a companion agent (commonly a fluoroquinolone, or alternative per susceptibility) once the wound has stabilized/is closed.
- Minimum total systemic antibiotic duration: **12 weeks** is now the ICM 2025-endorsed floor for most PJI treated with debridement/retention or exchange strategies (longer historically quoted as 6 weeks IV + oral tail in various protocols — the field has moved toward a unified 12-week minimum framework).
- **Suppressive therapy** (see Non-operative) considered indefinitely in McPherson host C, difficult organisms, or incomplete source control, with periodic reassessment of tolerability, renal/hepatic function, and whether cessation is reasonable in a stable, asymptomatic patient.

**Prevention bundle (primary and revision arthroplasty):**
- Preoperative: screen and decolonize S. aureus nasal carriage (mupirocin ± chlorhexidine wash), optimize glycemic control, smoking cessation counseling, treat active remote infections, nutritional optimization, minimize preoperative hospital stay.
- Perioperative: weight-based IV antibiotic prophylaxis timed within 60 minutes of incision (vancomycin within 120 minutes given longer infusion time), redosing for prolonged procedures/blood loss, laminar airflow/ventilation and traffic discipline in theatre, double-gloving, dilute povidone-iodine lavage and/or intrawound vancomycin powder per surgeon/institutional protocol (evidence for incremental benefit of these specific adjuncts remains mixed in the most recent high-quality RCT data), meticulous hemostasis and tourniquet technique, minimizing operative time.
- Postoperative: appropriate VTE prophylaxis (hematoma is a major risk factor for infection), early wound surveillance, prompt evaluation and low threshold for return to theatre for any persistent drainage beyond 5–7 days, avoidance of unnecessary blood transfusion, and dental/procedural antibiotic prophylaxis counseling per current AAOS/ADA joint guidance for a defined high-risk subgroup rather than universal lifelong prophylaxis for all arthroplasty patients.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

Rehabilitation differs fundamentally by pathway (DAIR vs two-stage) and must be individualized to host grade and soft-tissue status; the following is a general framework.

**DAIR pathway:**
- **Weeks 0–2 (postoperative):** Weight-bearing as tolerated for hip/knee unless a concurrent structural procedure limits it; focus on wound protection, edema control, gentle active-assisted ROM, and early mobilization to prevent stiffness — the joint has not been reconstructed, only debrided, so formal arthroplasty precautions from the index surgery still apply.
- **Weeks 2–6:** Progressive strengthening as antibiotics continue (often still IV/early oral phase); monitor CRP trend at each follow-up as a surrogate for treatment response; therapy focuses on regaining pre-DAIR functional baseline.
- **Weeks 6–12:** Continue targeted antibiotics to the 12-week minimum; functional milestones mirror standard post-arthroplasty rehabilitation (independent ambulation, stair negotiation, return to low-impact ADLs).
- **Return to work:** sedentary work 2–4 weeks, physical/manual work typically deferred until antibiotic course completion and confirmed clinical/serologic response (often 3 months).
- **Return to sport/high-demand activity:** generally deferred until antibiotics complete and sustained clinical remission is confirmed (commonly ≥6 months from DAIR), with shared decision-making given residual reinfection risk.

**Two-stage exchange pathway:**
- **Stage 1 to reimplantation interval (weeks 0–6 to 12):** Protected weight-bearing per spacer construct stability (articulating spacers generally allow partial weight-bearing with assistive device; static spacers or significant bone loss may mandate touch-down/non-weight-bearing) — surgeon-specific based on spacer fixation and bone stock. Gentle ROM work with an articulating spacer to preserve soft-tissue excursion and ease reimplantation exposure; static spacers are managed more conservatively with immobilization/bracing.
- **Post-reimplantation (Stage 2), weeks 0–2:** Standard early post-arthroplasty protocol — weight-bearing as tolerated (hip/knee primary-style protocol) unless bone-grafting/allograft/extensile exposure dictates protected loading, cryotherapy, edema control, active-assisted ROM.
- **Weeks 2–6:** Progressive closed-chain strengthening, gait training, continued ROM progression; watch closely for any recurrent drainage, fever, or rising inflammatory markers as red flags for reinfection.
- **Weeks 6–12:** Advance to full strengthening program, proprioception/balance work, functional task-specific training (stairs, uneven terrain).
- **Months 3–6:** Continue strengthening; formal outcome/functional scoring (e.g., HOOS/KOOS, Oxford scores) at follow-up visits to track trajectory versus expected post-revision benchmarks (typically lower peak function than primary arthroplasty).
- **Return to work:** sedentary 6 weeks, moderate physical work 3 months, heavy manual labor individualized (often 4–6 months) and contingent on confirmed infection clearance.
- **Return to sport/high-demand recreational activity:** low-impact activity from ~3–4 months if progressing well; higher-impact/higher-demand activity deferred to 6–12 months with explicit counseling that revision-for-infection constructs generally carry lower functional ceilings and stricter activity modification than primary arthroplasty, given elevated re-revision risk with high-demand loading.

**Physiotherapy content common to both pathways:** early mobilization to prevent DVT/stiffness, edema and scar management, progressive ROM (avoiding aggressive early stretching over fresh soft-tissue reconstruction/flap coverage where used), closed-chain then open-chain strengthening, gait re-training with assistive device weaning per surgeon protocol, and cardiovascular conditioning appropriate to host comorbidity burden.

**Monitoring during rehabilitation:** serial CRP/ESR at defined intervals as a treatment-response surrogate (not a stand-alone "cure" criterion), wound surveillance at every visit, and a low threshold to re-image or re-aspirate if pain, swelling, drainage, or marker plateau/rebound occurs.

## Complications

- **Reinfection/persistent infection** — the central complication across all strategies; risk highest with McPherson host C, S. aureus/resistant organisms, inadequate debridement, and premature or incomplete antibiotic courses.
- **Antibiotic-related toxicity** — nephrotoxicity/ototoxicity with prolonged vancomycin/aminoglycosides, hepatotoxicity and drug interactions with rifampin (notably reducing efficacy of oral contraceptives, warfarin, and many other CYP3A4-metabolized drugs), Clostridioides difficile colitis with prolonged broad-spectrum courses, and cumulative adverse events with long-term suppressive therapy (generally well tolerated per 2024 cohort data, but requires periodic labs and clinical reassessment).
- **Spacer-related complications** (two-stage interval): spacer fracture or dislocation, femoral/tibial bone loss around a static spacer, leg-length discrepancy, stiffness (especially with static spacers or prolonged intervals), and interval periprosthetic fracture.
- **Wound-healing complications** — persistent drainage, dehiscence, need for flap coverage, particularly in McPherson local grade 2–3 hosts.
- **Recurrent aseptic loosening/mechanical failure** after reimplantation, distinct from reinfection but requiring the same diagnostic vigilance to differentiate (recurrence of infection vs new mechanical problem).
- **Amputation or permanent resection arthroplasty** as an end-stage complication in unsalvageable, recurrent, or life-threatening infection, particularly in host C patients with exhausted reconstructive options.
- **Mortality** — PJI, particularly in revision settings and frail hosts, carries measurable perioperative and 1-year mortality risk (reported in the low single digits to over 10% in the frailest cohorts undergoing multiple procedures) — an important part of shared decision-making, especially when weighing aggressive eradication versus suppression/resection in host C patients.
- **Functional deficit** — even successfully treated PJI (particularly two-stage knee) generally yields lower functional outcome scores than primary arthroplasty or aseptic revision, which should be set as a realistic expectation preoperatively.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[International Consensus Meeting on Orthopaedic Infection: Differences Between ICM 2018 and ICM 2025](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(25)01416-0/abstract)** (Journal of Arthroplasty, 2025) — direct comparison of 172 matched questions found recommendation changes in 21.5% (37 questions), including a new **minimum 12-week total antibiotic course after debridement** and recognition of elevated revision-infection risk following TKA after prior ipsilateral arthroscopy; ICM 2025 also adopts more directive, explicitly graded language than 2018 — treat ICM 2025 as the current management layer atop the still-foundational 2018 diagnostic criteria.
- **[Diagnosing periprosthetic joint infections: a comparison of infection definitions — EBJIS 2021, ICM 2018, and IDSA 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36047011/)** (Bone & Joint Research) — head-to-head comparison confirming EBJIS's stepwise "unlikely/likely/confirmed" framework reduces the proportion of patients left in a diagnostically uncertain category compared with fixed-threshold ICM scoring, supporting complementary rather than competing use of the two systems.
- **[Diagnostic accuracy of alpha-defensin ELISA and lateral flow assays for periprosthetic joint infection: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42028540/)** (2025, search through Feb 2024) — confirms ELISA format offers superior sensitivity to lateral-flow point-of-care testing while both retain high specificity; reinforces using ELISA when send-out turnaround is acceptable and reserving lateral-flow for intraoperative/rapid decision points.
- **[Comprehensive analysis of culture-negative periprosthetic joint infection with metagenomic next-generation sequencing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12098451/)** (2024–2025) — retrospective analysis of 223 ICM-criteria-positive patients demonstrates mNGS's expanding role in identifying causative organisms and characterizing risk factors in culture-negative PJI, supporting its integration into the diagnostic algorithm rather than reserving it as a last resort.
- **[Direct detection and identification of periprosthetic joint infection pathogens by metagenomic next-generation sequencing](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-35215-3)** (Scientific Reports, 2023) — quantifies the turnaround advantage of mNGS (~1.3 days) versus standard culture (~5.2 days), a practically important consideration for early antibiotic tailoring in acute presentations.
- **[Evaluation of KLIC and CRIME80 scores for analysis of the risk of failure of DAIR in acute postoperative periprosthetic joint infections of the knee](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11603435/)** (2024) and **[KLIC Score Does Not Predict Failure After Early Prosthetic Joint Infection: An External Validation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38092159/)** (2024, 153 knees/130 hips) — recent external validations show inconsistent performance of KLIC across cohorts (predictive in some, not in others), while CRIME80 performed more robustly in at least one late-acute knee cohort; both scores should inform, not dictate, the DAIR-versus-staged-exchange decision.
- **[Are KLIC and CRIME-80 scores useful to assist decision-making initially or at the moment of repeat DAIR? — a retrospective study](https://jbji.copernicus.org/articles/10/403/2025/)** (Journal of Bone and Joint Infection, 2025) — found neither score reliably predicted failure at initial or repeat DAIR in this cohort, adding to the growing signal that these tools need cohort-specific validation before being used as hard cutoffs.
- **[Do one-stage indications predict success following two-stage arthroplasty for chronic periprosthetic joint infection?](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11002939/)** (2024) — explores whether patients who would have qualified for one-stage criteria do better with two-stage, informing the ongoing recalibration of one- vs two-stage patient selection.
- **[Systematic review and meta-analysis of single-stage vs two-stage revision for periprosthetic joint infection: a call for a prospective randomized trial](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12891-024-07229-z)** (2024, 40 studies, 8,711 patients) — pooled analysis found **no significant difference in reinfection or reoperation rates** between one- and two-stage revision, reinforcing that patient/organism selection — not stage number per se — drives outcome, while explicitly calling for a definitive RCT.
- **[Success Rates Comparable between One-Stage and Two-Stage Treatment for Periprosthetic Joint Infection](https://www.aaos.org/aaosnow/2025/jan/research/research02/)** (AAOS Now reporting a 2024 AAHKS-presented multicenter RCT) — 2-year data showed **98% success for one-stage vs 91% for two-stage**, notably including patients with resistant organisms and draining wounds, the first prospective randomized comparison of its kind and a meaningful shift in the evidence base away from two-stage as an unconditional default.
- **[Prognosis after one- and two-stage revision surgery for periprosthetic joint infection: A systematic review and meta-analysis](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10225536251315973)** (2025) — further pooled comparative outcome data consistent with the narrowing gap between the two strategies, supporting individualized rather than blanket staging decisions.
- **["No differences in outcomes with stopping or continuing antibiotic suppression in periprosthetic joint infections"](https://jbji.copernicus.org/articles/9/143/2024/)** (Journal of Bone and Joint Infection, 2024) — cohort data suggests carefully selected patients doing well on suppression can have it discontinued without a significant difference in outcome versus continuing indefinitely, supporting individualized suppression duration rather than automatic lifelong therapy.
- **["Adverse events and tolerability of long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy for periprosthetic joint infection: a prospective cohort study"](https://jbji.copernicus.org/articles/11/267/2026/)** (2026) — prospective data affirming that long-term suppression is generally well tolerated without inducing clinically significant resistance, supporting its continued use as a legitimate strategy in high-risk hosts rather than a last-resort compromise.
- **[The Three-Month Wound Complication and Infection Rates After Vancomycin Powder and Dilute Povidone-Iodine Lavage for Infection Prophylaxis in High-Risk Total Joint Arthroplasty: A Multicenter Randomized Controlled Trial](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(25)00477-2/abstract)** and its **[one-year follow-up](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(26)00396-7/abstract)** (2025–2026, 2,053 high-risk primary THA/TKA patients, four-arm design) — found **no significant differences in infection/wound-complication rates** between vancomycin powder, dilute povidone-iodine, combination, or saline control at 3 months or 1 year, tempering enthusiasm for these adjuncts as reliably effective prevention measures in high-risk primary arthroplasty and reinforcing that prevention bundles should not rely on any single adjunct as a proven solution.
