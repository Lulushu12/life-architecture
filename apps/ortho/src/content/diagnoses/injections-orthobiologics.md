## Quick Reference

**Kellgren-Lawrence (K-L) grade as injection-response modifier**

| Grade | Radiographic definition | Expected response to IA injection | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | No radiographic OA | N/A — treat other diagnosis | Injection not indicated for OA per se |
| 1 | Doubtful JSN, possible osteophyte | Good, but symptom often not OA-driven — investigate further | HA/PRP reasonable trial; low expectation ceiling because pain source may not be cartilage |
| 2 | Definite osteophyte, possible JSN | **Best responders** to HA, PRP, and steroid | First-line target population for all injectable therapies; best NNT data cluster here |
| 3 | Moderate multiple osteophytes, definite JSN, some sclerosis | Moderate, shorter duration; steroid still effective for flares | Injections as bridge/palliation; counsel on diminishing returns; PRP/BMAC reasonable if surgery deferred |
| 4 | Large osteophytes, severe JSN, marked sclerosis, deformity | **Poor and unpredictable** — bone-on-bone pain often mechanical, not inflammatory | Injections rarely change trajectory; use only as a bridge or in patients unfit/unwilling for arthroplasty; set expectations explicitly |

**PRP preparations — LR-PRP vs LP-PRP**

| Feature | Leukocyte-rich PRP (LR-PRP) | Leukocyte-poor PRP (LP-PRP) |
|---|---|---|
| Leukocyte content | High (often near whole-blood WBC or concentrated) | Low/absent (buffy coat excluded) |
| Neutrophil-derived cytokines (IL-1β, MMPs) | High — more pro-inflammatory | Low — favors anti-catabolic profile |
| Platelet concentration | Variable, system-dependent (typically 3–6× baseline) | Typically 3–8× baseline (double-spin systems concentrate platelets further) |
| Best-evidence indication | Tendinopathy (lateral epicondylitis, patellar tendinopathy) — controlled local inflammatory stimulus may aid remodeling | Intra-articular use (knee OA) — lower post-injection flare/synovitis rate |
| Post-injection reaction | Higher rate of transient flare/swelling (~20–30%) | Lower flare rate |
| Practical rule | Do **not** use LR-PRP intra-articularly in an inflamed/effusive joint | Preferred for IA injection in degenerative (non-inflammatory) OA |

**Treatment ladder by diagnosis**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Before surgery / surgical trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knee OA (K-L 2–3) | Activity mod., PT, NSAIDs, weight loss | IA corticosteroid (flare) → HA (esp. if steroid-averse/diabetic) → PRP (LP) | BMAC/PRP as bridge in younger/active patients; TKA when K-L 4 + failed conservative + functional limitation |
| Knee OA (K-L 4) | Brace, NSAIDs, activity mod. | Steroid for flare only (short-lived benefit) | Arthroplasty is definitive; do not over-promise from injectables |
| Hip OA | NSAIDs, PT, weight loss | US/fluoro-guided IA corticosteroid (also diagnostic) | THA when moderate-severe radiographic OA + failed conservative care |
| Subacromial impingement/RC tendinopathy | PT (rotator cuff strengthening), NSAIDs | Subacromial corticosteroid (≤3/yr), consider PRP for partial-thickness tears | RC repair for full-thickness tear with failed conservative Rx or acute trauma in active patient |
| Adhesive capsulitis | PT, NSAIDs | IA corticosteroid (GH joint) ± hydrodilatation | MUA/capsular release if refractory >6–9 months |
| Lateral epicondylitis | Activity mod., eccentric loading, counterforce brace | LR-PRP (preferred over steroid beyond short term); avoid repeated steroid | Percutaneous/open debridement (rare, <10% of cases) |
| CMC1 (thumb base) OA | Splint, NSAIDs, activity mod. | IA corticosteroid (diagnostic + therapeutic) | LRTI/arthroplasty if steroid response short-lived and radiographic Eaton stage advances |
| Trigger finger | Splinting (mild) | US or landmark-guided A1 pulley corticosteroid (1–2 injections) | A1 pulley release if <2 injections fail or diabetic with poor injection response |
| Plantar fasciitis | Stretching, orthotics, NSAIDs | Corticosteroid (judicious, risk of fat pad atrophy/rupture) → PRP as steroid-sparing alternative | Rarely surgical (plantar fascia release) |
| Calcific tendinopathy (shoulder) | NSAIDs, PT, ESWT | US-guided barbotage (needle lavage) ± corticosteroid | Arthroscopic excision if barbotage fails ×2 or large refractory deposit |
| Radiculopathy (lumbar/cervical) | NSAIDs, PT, short course oral steroid | Fluoroscopic epidural/transforaminal steroid (diagnostic + therapeutic) | Decompression if progressive neuro deficit, cauda equina, or failed ≥6 weeks conservative + imaging-confirmed compressive lesion |
| Chronic knee OA pain, poor surgical candidate | Optimize pharmacologic/IA therapy | Diagnostic genicular nerve block (≥50% relief) → genicular RFA | Arthroplasty remains definitive if candidate becomes fit |

**Key numbers and thresholds**

- **Steroid injection frequency ceiling:** generally ≤3–4 injections per joint per year; minimum **3-month interval** between IA corticosteroid injections in the same joint.
- **Pre-arthroplasty steroid window:** avoid IA corticosteroid within **3 months** before THA (meta-analysis-level infection signal); many US/European practices use a **4-week minimum** before TKA, though delaying beyond 4 weeks does not further reduce septic revision risk per recent registry data.
- **Pre-arthroplasty HA window:** avoid HA injection within **2 months** before TKA — associated with increased periprosthetic joint infection risk.
- **Diagnostic genicular block threshold before RFA:** ≥50% pain relief (some protocols require ≥80% or dual comparative blocks).
- **Septic arthritis synovial WBC threshold (native joint):** >50,000/mm³ with >90% PMNs is classically cited, but **>25,000–50,000/mm³** with high clinical suspicion should trigger treatment; lower absolute counts do not exclude infection.
- **Prosthetic joint infection aspirate threshold (MSIS/ICM criteria):** WBC >3,000 cells/µL (chronic, >90 days post-op) or >10,000 cells/µL (acute, <90 days) plus PMN% >70–80% depending on timeframe.
- **PRP platelet concentration:** therapeutic effect generally assumed to require **≥3–5× baseline** platelet count; supraphysiologic concentrations (>8×) may be inhibitory in vitro (paradoxical dose-response).
- **CMC1 injection volume:** 0.5–1 mL total (joint capacity is small — overfilling causes pain and extravasation).
- **Knee IA injection volume:** 4–6 mL for medication ± local anesthetic; effusion aspiration first improves both diagnostic yield and injectate distribution.
- **Trigger finger steroid success:** single injection cures ~50–70% (higher for single-digit, non-diabetic); success drops in diabetics and multi-digit disease.

**Red flags — do not proceed with elective injection**

- Active local or systemic infection, cellulitis overlying planned injection site.
- Suspected septic arthritis (aspirate and send for cell count/culture/Gram stain **before** any steroid).
- Uncontrolled coagulopathy or supratherapeutic anticoagulation for deep/vascular-adjacent targets (hip, spine) without a documented risk-benefit plan.
- Injection planned within 3 months of scheduled arthroplasty in the same joint (steroid) or 2 months (HA) — reschedule or use alternative modality.
- Known allergy to injectate components (bovine-derived HA products, local anesthetic class).
- Poorly controlled diabetes — counsel on transient hyperglycemia (24–72h) after corticosteroid; consider deferring if HbA1c grossly uncontrolled and injection is elective.
- Overlying joint prosthesis with unclear diagnosis of pain — aspirate to rule out PJI before any therapeutic injection into a periprosthetic soft-tissue structure.
- Suspected tendon rupture risk: avoid direct intratendinous corticosteroid, especially Achilles, patellar tendon, and after ≥2 prior injections into the same tendon.

## Corticosteroid Injections (Intra-Articular & Soft Tissue)

**Mechanism:** Glucocorticoids suppress phospholipase A2, reducing prostaglandin/leukotriene synthesis and downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF-α) in synovium and peri-tendinous tissue. Effect is anti-inflammatory, not disease-modifying or regenerative.

**Agents and typical dosing**

| Agent | Relative potency | Typical joint dose | Duration of action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triamcinolone acetonide (particulate) | High, long-acting depot | 40 mg knee/shoulder; 20 mg wrist/ankle/elbow; 10 mg small joints/tendon sheath | 3–8 weeks clinical effect |
| Methylprednisolone acetate (particulate) | Moderate-high | 40–80 mg knee; 20–40 mg subacromial; 20 mg small joints | 2–6 weeks |
| Betamethasone (particulate/semi-soluble) | High | 6–12 mg | Rapid onset, moderate duration |
| Dexamethasone sodium phosphate (non-particulate) | Moderate | 4–10 mg | Shorter duration, but mandatory choice for **transforaminal cervical/lumbar** injections near radicular vessels (embolic risk with particulate steroids) |

**Indications:** OA flare (any joint), inflammatory arthritis flare (adjunct, not primary therapy), adhesive capsulitis, subacromial impingement, trochanteric bursitis, de Quervain's, trigger finger, CMC1 OA, plantar fasciitis (judicious), epicondylitis (short-term only — inferior to PRP/eccentric loading beyond 6–12 weeks), carpal tunnel (bridge, not definitive).

**Risks — quantified**

- **Cartilage toxicity:** Repeated intra-articular corticosteroid, particularly triamcinolone, has documented chondrotoxic effects in vitro and in a landmark 2-year RCT (McAlindon et al., JAMA 2017) showing **greater cartilage volume loss** with quarterly triamcinolone vs saline in knee OA (no functional benefit difference). This reframed practice: steroid is for symptom control in flares, not a program of scheduled quarterly injections.
- **Tendon rupture:** Peritendinous/intratendinous corticosteroid is associated with weakened tendon collagen and rupture risk, most described for Achilles, patellar, and rotator cuff tendons. Avoid direct intratendinous injection; use peritendinous or bursal targets under image guidance; limit to ≤2–3 injections per tendon lifetime in weight-bearing tendons.
- **Post-injection flare:** ~2–5% (crystal-induced synovitis from the steroid vehicle itself, self-limited 24–48h).
- **Skin/subcutaneous atrophy and hypopigmentation:** more common with superficial soft-tissue injections (lateral epicondyle, dorsal wrist) — counsel patients, especially with darker skin tones and thin subcutaneous tissue.
- **Transient hyperglycemia:** 24–72 hours in diabetics; counsel on home glucose monitoring.
- **Facial flushing:** ~10% especially with triamcinolone.
- **Infection:** true septic arthritis after IA steroid is rare (~1:10,000–1:50,000) with sterile technique, but risk rises sharply if injected into an already-infected or bacteremic host.

**Pre-arthroplasty timing window — this is a frequently tested decision point**

- Registry- and meta-analysis-level data (2022–2025) show **IA corticosteroid within 3 months before THA increases periprosthetic joint infection risk**; most arthroplasty-adjacent guidance now recommends a **minimum 3-month washout for hip**, and commonly a **4-week minimum for knee**, before elective arthroplasty in the same joint.
- Newer data suggest delaying **beyond 4 weeks does not further reduce septic revision risk** for TKA — i.e., 4 weeks may be a reasonable practical floor rather than 3 months uniformly, but hip data are more concerning and merit the longer window.
- **HA carries its own, separate window:** injections within **2 months** of TKA are independently associated with increased PJI risk — treat HA and steroid as separate clocks, not interchangeable.
- Practical rule for your consult note: any IA injection (steroid or HA) performed within the planned pre-op window should be flagged explicitly in the surgical planning note and factored into scheduling, not just documented incidentally.

**Technique pearls**

- Aspirate first if any effusion — improves accuracy, provides diagnostic fluid, and reduces post-injection pressure/pain.
- Use image guidance (US or fluoroscopy) for hip, subacromial/GH shoulder in obese patients, and any joint with a prior failed landmark-guided attempt — accuracy for landmark-guided knee injections is high (~70–90%) but drops substantially for shoulder (GH ~65–80% blind) and hip (near 0% reliably without imaging).
- Mix with local anesthetic for immediate diagnostic confirmation (pain relief within minutes implicates the injected structure) — document response as part of the diagnostic workup.

## Hyaluronic Acid Viscosupplementation

**Mechanism:** Exogenous hyaluronan supplements synovial fluid viscoelasticity; proposed additional effects include anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective signaling, though clinical benefit is modest and product-dependent.

**Formulations:** Low molecular weight (e.g., hyaluronate sodium ~500–1200 kDa, weekly ×3–5) vs high molecular weight/cross-linked (e.g., single-injection hylan G-F 20 or cross-linked hyaluronate, ~6000 kDa) — single-injection products favored for compliance; no consistent superiority of one class over another in head-to-head trials.

**Guideline positions**

- **AAOS** (2021 Clinical Practice Guideline update, most recent full CPG cycle) issued a "cannot recommend" / limited-strength statement against routine use of HA for knee OA, citing insufficient evidence of clinically important benefit over placebo in aggregate meta-analysis — a notably more skeptical position than the 2013 CPG.
- **NICE** (osteoarthritis guideline, most recent update) recommends **against** offering intra-articular HA for OA, citing lack of consistent clinically important benefit.
- **OARSI** (Bannuru et al., 2019 guideline for management of hand, hip, and knee OA) gives HA an "uncertain" recommendation for knee OA overall, conditional/appropriate in specific subgroups, reflecting substantial heterogeneity between products and trials.
- **ACR/AF** (rheumatology-oriented) similarly conditionally recommends *against* routine IA HA for knee OA.
- Net practical position: HA is **not first-line** by most current guideline bodies; it remains a reasonable option in patients who are corticosteroid-averse, diabetic (avoids hyperglycemia), or who have had a documented but short-lived response, but should not be marketed as guideline-endorsed standard care.

**Best-evidence niche:** mild-moderate OA (K-L 2–3), possibly longer symptom duration of relief (weeks-months) vs steroid in some trials, low systemic risk profile. Weakest evidence in advanced OA (K-L 4) and outside the knee (hip, shoulder, CMC1 — less robust trial data, used more empirically).

**Technique:** Similar entry points to steroid; aspirate effusion first (viscosupplement efficacy may be reduced by residual inflammatory fluid); avoid mixing with local anesthetic in the same syringe for cross-linked products per most manufacturer instructions (check specific product monograph — some are compatible).

**Risks:** Low — local reaction/pseudoseptic reaction (self-limited synovitis, more with cross-linked/avian-derived products, rare), allergic reaction (avian-derived products, screen for egg/poultry allergy), theoretical infection risk as with any IA injection. No cartilage toxicity signal comparable to steroid.

## Platelet-Rich Plasma

**Preparation classification and management implication — see Quick Reference table above.** Key operational point: **specify LR vs LP and the platelet-concentration multiple on every order/consent** — outcomes and adverse-event rates in the literature are preparation-specific, and pooling all "PRP" as one intervention is a major source of trial heterogeneity and conflicting meta-analyses.

**Indication-by-indication evidence summary**

| Indication | Evidence strength (2024–2026 synthesis) | Practical takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Knee OA (mild-moderate, K-L 2–3) | Moderate — multiple meta-analyses show PRP superior to HA and saline for pain/function at 6–12 months; network meta-analyses show statistically but often not clinically meaningful superiority over HA | Reasonable second-line option, particularly LP-PRP, in patients wanting to defer surgery; set expectations at "meaningful for some, not transformative" |
| Knee OA (severe, K-L 4) | Weak/negative | Do not offer as substitute for arthroplasty |
| Lateral epicondylitis | Moderate-strong, LR-PRP favored | Superior to steroid at 6–24 months in multiple RCTs/meta-analyses (steroid better only short-term, <6 weeks); reasonable first injectable after failed eccentric loading |
| Patellar tendinopathy | Moderate | Similar rationale to epicondylitis; LR-PRP typically used |
| Rotator cuff tendinopathy (non-tear) | Emerging, moderate | 2026 meta-analysis data show modest but durable benefit over corticosteroid |
| Rotator cuff repair augmentation | Mixed/inconclusive on retear rate | Large 2026 meta-analysis found **platelet concentration does not significantly influence clinical outcomes or retear rates** — augmentation may aid pain/healing biology signals but is not proven to reduce structural retear; also an associated **higher rate of post-injection adhesive capsulitis** reported — counsel patients |
| Plantar fasciitis | Moderate | Effective steroid-sparing alternative, slower onset of relief (weeks) but more durable |
| Hip OA | Limited | Extrapolated from knee data; fewer dedicated RCTs, image guidance mandatory |
| Achilles tendinopathy | Weak-moderate | Mixed RCT results; reasonable adjunct to eccentric loading, not a substitute |

**Technique:** Whole blood draw (typically 15–60 mL depending on system) → single or double centrifugation → buffy-coat harvest → activation (endogenous collagen/thrombin activation at injection site is standard; exogenous activation less common). Inject under US guidance for tendon targets (peritendinous, needle fenestration technique — "dry needling" through the pathologic tendon combined with PRP deposition, especially for epicondylitis/patellar tendinopathy). For IA knee use, single or 2–3 weekly series depending on protocol; no consensus on optimal series length.

**What changes management:** confirm diagnosis with imaging (US/MRI) before committing to a PRP series — PRP is time- and cost-intensive and should not substitute for a missed alternate diagnosis (e.g., treating "lateral epicondylitis" PRP-refractory pain that is actually PIN entrapment or radial tunnel syndrome).

**Adverse effects:** post-injection pain/flare (higher with LR-PRP, ~20–30% at 48–72h), theoretical infection (autologous product, very low risk), no systemic risk profile (autologous, no allergy). Reported association with adhesive capsulitis after rotator cuff PRP injection (2026 meta-analysis) — an emerging counseling point not previously emphasized.

## Bone Marrow Aspirate Concentrate (BMAC)

**Rationale:** Autologous concentrate of mesenchymal stromal cells, hematopoietic progenitors, growth factors, and platelets harvested from iliac crest (posterior superior iliac spine most common, occasionally proximal tibia) marrow, concentrated by centrifugation.

**Indications:** Knee OA (moderate, particularly as an adjunct or alternative to PRP in patients who have failed PRP, or combined with microfracture/cartilage procedures), focal chondral defects (adjunct to microfracture — "one-step" cartilage repair), early-stage osteonecrosis of the femoral head (core decompression + BMAC augmentation has reasonable supportive evidence for precollapse Ficat-Arlet stage I–II), nonunion augmentation (percutaneous BMAC injection at fracture site, historically Connolly technique, revisited with concentration technology).

**Evidence state (2024–2026):** Systematic reviews of RCTs for knee OA (Han et al., 2024) show BMAC produces clinically meaningful pain/function improvement versus baseline and is comparable to PRP in most head-to-head comparisons, without clear superiority over PRP despite higher cost and invasiveness of harvest. A 2026 RCT comparing BMAC clot vs BMAC concentrate in grade 3–4 knee OA suggests preparation technique (clot vs concentrate) matters and is an active area of protocol refinement — no single "best" preparation is yet established.

**Technique pearls:** Use a multi-hole/side-port aspiration needle with small-volume aspirates (2 mL) at multiple depths/sites rather than a single large-volume draw — large single draws dilute the marrow with peripheral blood and reduce progenitor cell yield. Anticoagulate aspirate promptly (ACD-A) to prevent clotting before processing.

**Cost/access reality:** Point-of-care BMAC concentration adds significant procedural time and equipment cost versus PRP; in most European practice this remains largely out-of-pocket/private, and Romanian/EU reimbursement pathways for BMAC in OA are not established — counsel patients accordingly and reserve for failed-PRP or specific cartilage/AVN indications rather than routine OA first-line orthobiologic.

## Prolotherapy

**Mechanism:** Injection of an irritant solution (most commonly **hypertonic dextrose 10–25%**, sometimes combined with lidocaine) into ligament/tendon insertions or joint spaces, proposed to incite a local inflammatory cascade and subsequent fibroblastic repair/proliferation ("proliferative therapy").

**Evidence (2023–2025):** Multiple small-to-moderate RCTs (Teymouri 2025, Fu 2024, Öztürk & Baygutalp 2023) show dextrose prolotherapy outperforms saline/sham for knee OA pain and function at 6–12 months, and a 2025 network meta-analysis of semi-invasive knee OA therapies places prolotherapy among the effective options alongside PRP and HA — but overall trial quality remains modest (small samples, variable blinding, heterogeneous protocols), and it has **not** been formally endorsed by AAOS, NICE, or OARSI as a guideline-recommended therapy. It is best characterized as a reasonable, low-cost, low-risk option in a shared-decision-making context rather than a guideline-driven default.

**Indications used in practice:** Knee OA (IA and periarticular ligament/insertion targets), lateral epicondylitis, chronic low back pain (sacroiliac/interspinous ligaments — weak evidence), Achilles/patellar tendinopathy (adjunct).

**Technique:** Multiple small-volume injections (0.5–1 mL per site) at ligament/tendon entheses and/or intra-articularly, series of 3–6 sessions spaced 2–6 weeks apart. Lower cost than PRP/BMAC (no processing equipment required), which is a practical draw in resource-limited settings.

**Risks:** Injection site pain (expected, part of mechanism), rare nerve injury with periarticular injections, theoretical risk of overuse in unstable joints if fibrotic response is misapplied as a substitute for correcting mechanical instability surgically.

## Genicular Nerve Blocks & Radiofrequency Ablation

**Target nerves (knee):** Superolateral genicular nerve (SLGN), superomedial genicular nerve (SMGN), inferomedial genicular nerve (IMGN) — classic three-nerve target set; some protocols add the recurrent fibular/genicular branch or infrapatellar branch of saphenous nerve for anteromedial pain coverage.

**Diagnostic block:** Fluoroscopic or US-guided local anesthetic block at the three standard targets (periosteal targets at the junction of the shaft and epicondyle/condyle on AP and lateral views) — a **≥50% reduction in pain** (some protocols require ≥80% or a dual comparative-block paradigm with placebo/saline control to reduce false positives) is required before proceeding to RFA.

**RFA technique:** Conventional thermal RFA (80–90°C, 90 seconds/lesion) or **cooled RFA** (lower tip temperature, larger lesion volume, better nerve capture rates in device-sponsored trials) at the same three anatomic targets, typically under fluoroscopic guidance.

**Evidence (2024–2026):** A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis of image-guided RCTs (Peng et al.) supports genicular RFA for meaningful pain reduction at 6 months in appropriately selected chronic knee OA patients who are poor surgical candidates or have failed/are awaiting arthroplasty. A large multicenter pragmatic trial (SKOAP, Cohen et al., Phase 2 protocol published 2026) is directly comparing intra-articular injection, genicular nerve block, and RFA head-to-head — its results, once mature, should meaningfully sharpen the treatment ladder for this indication; until then RFA remains a reasonable option for patients who are not surgical candidates or who decline/must delay arthroplasty, not a substitute for arthroplasty in an eligible candidate.

**Patient selection:** Best candidates are patients with chronic knee OA pain who (1) are medically unfit or unwilling for arthroplasty, (2) have failed conservative and injectable therapy, and (3) demonstrate a robust diagnostic block response. Poor candidates: predominantly mechanical/instability-driven pain, significant intra-articular loose body or meniscal pathology likely to require arthroscopic intervention, or pain outside the classic anteromedial/anterolateral genicular distribution.

**Complications:** Transient dysesthesia/numbness (uncommon, self-limited), skin burn (rare with proper grounding), post-procedure ecchymosis, theoretical risk of accelerated Charcot-like joint changes with sensory denervation in a joint that continues to bear mechanical load — an important long-term counseling point, particularly if RFA is used as a long-term bridge rather than a bridge to arthroplasty.

## Epidural & Selective Nerve Root Blocks (Overview)

*(Orthopedic-relevant overview; primary spine/pain-medicine procedural detail is out of scope here.)*

**Approaches:** Interlaminar epidural (broader spread, technically simpler, less precise targeting), transforaminal/selective nerve root block (more target-specific, higher diagnostic value, higher theoretical vascular injury risk), caudal epidural (lower lumbar/sacral levels, favored when interlaminar access is difficult post-fusion).

**Steroid choice — a critical safety point:** For **cervical transforaminal** injections, **use only non-particulate steroid (dexamethasone)** — particulate steroids (triamcinolone, methylprednisolone, betamethasone) carry a documented risk of catastrophic embolic infarction (spinal cord or brainstem stroke) if inadvertently injected into a radicular artery feeding the anterior spinal artery (this prompted an FDA Safety Communication and multi-society advisory). Many pain physicians extend non-particulate-only practice to **lumbar transforaminal** injections as well, given similar (lower-frequency) reports at the lumbar level; interlaminar approaches carry lower (but not zero) vascular risk and particulate steroid is more commonly used there.

**Indications (orthopedic relevance):** Radicular pain from disc herniation or foraminal stenosis, both diagnostic (confirming the symptomatic level before considering decompression) and therapeutic (bridging conservative management, deferring or avoiding surgery). Response to a selective nerve root block at a specific level is a useful confirmatory data point when imaging shows multi-level pathology and clinical localization is ambiguous.

**What it does not replace:** Progressive motor deficit, cauda equina syndrome, or bowel/bladder involvement are surgical indications regardless of injection response — do not delay decompression for a therapeutic trial in these settings.

**Evidence-based expectation setting:** Short-to-medium term (weeks-months) pain relief is well supported; effect on long-term surgical avoidance is mixed across the literature, and epidural steroid should be framed to patients as a bridge/diagnostic tool rather than a disease-modifying treatment for the underlying structural pathology.

## Barbotage (Calcific Tendinopathy)

**Definition:** Ultrasound-guided percutaneous needle aspiration and lavage (± fragmentation) of a calcific deposit, most commonly in the supraspinatus tendon (rotator cuff calcific tendinopathy), performed with one or two needles (single-needle "in-and-out" technique or two-needle lavage technique) under local anesthesia.

**Indication:** Symptomatic calcific tendinopathy (Gärtner type I "chalk-like" and type II deposits respond best; type III fluid/soft deposits sometimes resolve spontaneously and may be less amenable) that has failed NSAIDs, physiotherapy, and extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT), or as a first invasive step given its favorable risk profile relative to surgery.

**Technique pearls:** Position deposit tangential to the needle trajectory under real-time US; irrigate with saline and aspirate repeatedly until deposit resistance decreases or aspirate clears; a post-procedure subacromial corticosteroid injection is commonly added to control the post-lavage inflammatory reaction.

**Outcomes:** Short-to-medium-term success (pain/function improvement, partial-to-complete radiographic resolution of deposit) is reported in roughly 70–90% of appropriately selected patients in the barbotage literature; a subset (~10–20%) will fail and proceed to arthroscopic excision.

**Complications:** Post-procedure flare pain (common, expected, managed with the adjunct steroid and analgesia), rare infection, incomplete deposit removal requiring repeat procedure.

## Aspiration Techniques

**Diagnostic aspiration is a distinct decision point from therapeutic injection and should be considered first whenever there is diagnostic uncertainty (effusion of unclear etiology, suspected septic arthritis, suspected crystal arthropathy, suspected PJI).**

**Native joint septic arthritis workup:** Send synovial fluid for cell count with differential, Gram stain, culture (aerobic/anaerobic, consider extended culture for low-virulence organisms), and polarized light microscopy for crystals. Classic threshold >50,000/mm³ with >90% PMNs strongly suggests septic arthritis, but sensitivity is imperfect — a lower count does not exclude infection, especially early, partially treated (prior antibiotics), or in immunocompromised/prosthetic settings; treat based on the full clinical picture, not a single cutoff.

**Prosthetic joint aspiration (PJI workup):** Follow MSIS/ICM (International Consensus Meeting) criteria — synovial WBC and PMN% thresholds differ by acute (<90 days post-op: WBC >10,000/µL, PMN% >90%) versus chronic (>90 days: WBC >3,000/µL, PMN% >70–80%) presentation; combine with serum CRP/ESR, alpha-defensin or leukocyte esterase where available, and culture. Withhold antibiotics before aspiration whenever feasible (ideally ≥2 weeks off antibiotics) to maximize culture yield unless the patient is systemically unwell and requires empiric treatment.

**Crystal arthropathy:** Gout (negatively birefringent needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals) vs CPPD/pseudogout (positively birefringent or non-birefringent rhomboid calcium pyrophosphate crystals) — aspiration and polarized microscopy remains the gold-standard differentiator and should be pursued before committing to a presumptive-gout or presumptive-OA treatment pathway in an acute mono-arthritis.

**Technique pearls:** Use the largest joint-appropriate bore needle that patient comfort allows (18–20G for knee, 20–22G for smaller joints) — under-sized needles clog with fibrin/debris in purulent or hemarthrotic fluid. For hip aspiration, always use image guidance (fluoroscopy or US) — blind hip aspiration is unreliable. Send fluid promptly; delayed processing degrades crystal identification and cell count accuracy.

## Site-by-Site Technique Landmarks

**Knee (IA, superolateral approach):** Patient supine, knee extended, quadriceps relaxed. Entry point ~1 cm superior and 1 cm lateral to the superolateral corner of the patella, directed posteromedially toward the intercondylar notch/patellofemoral joint under the patella. Alternative: anteromedial/anterolateral parapatellar approach with knee flexed 90° over the table edge (higher accuracy in some studies, useful when effusion present anteriorly).

**Hip (IA, US-guided anterior approach — landmark technique is unreliable and should not be used):** Patient supine, hip neutral or slight external rotation. Linear (or curvilinear in larger patients) probe placed longitudinally over the femoral neck to visualize the femoral head-neck junction and anterior synovial recess; needle introduced in-plane from distal-lateral to proximal-medial, targeting the recess just lateral to the femoral vessels (identify and avoid the femoral neurovascular bundle medially with color Doppler before needling). Fluoroscopic guidance with contrast arthrogram is the alternative standard, particularly useful when concurrently obtaining a diagnostic arthrogram.

**Shoulder — glenohumeral (posterior approach):** Patient seated or lateral, arm neutral at side. Entry 2 cm inferior and 2 cm medial to the posterolateral corner of the acromion, directed anteriorly toward the coracoid process. US guidance from a posterior short-axis view improves accuracy, especially in adhesive capsulitis where capsule compliance is reduced.

**Shoulder — subacromial:** Patient seated, arm relaxed at side (subacromial space widens in slight extension/internal rotation — "Crass position" with hand on posterior hip). Lateral approach: entry just inferior to the midpoint of the lateral acromial edge, directed medially and slightly posteriorly/superiorly under the acromion into the subacromial bursa. US guidance strongly recommended given the consequences of an intratendinous (vs bursal) injection.

**CMC1 (first carpometacarpal, thumb base):** Identify the joint by palpating the "anatomic snuffbox" distal recess or by traction/gliding the thumb metacarpal to open the joint line; entry dorsoradial at the joint line between the trapezium and first metacarpal base, needle nearly perpendicular to skin, small volume (0.5–1 mL) — this is a tight joint space and US guidance materially improves accuracy versus landmark technique.

**Trigger finger (A1 pulley):** Landmark: A1 pulley overlies the metacarpal head at the distal palmar crease (or proximal to it for the thumb). Needle introduced at 30–45° just proximal to the palpable nodule/pulley, either directly into the flexor tendon sheath (subcutaneous, superficial to the tendon — feel for reduced resistance as the needle enters the sheath rather than the tendon substance) with injectate visibly tracking along the sheath. US guidance reduces the risk of intratendinous injection and improves single-injection success, particularly valuable in diabetics where response rates to blind injection are lower.

**Lateral epicondyle (common extensor origin, for PRP/dry needling):** Palpate the point of maximal tenderness at or just distal to the lateral epicondyle; US-guided in-plane approach allows visualization of the hypoechoic/heterogeneous tendinopathic ECRB origin, with a peppering/fenestration technique through the pathologic tissue during PRP deposition — avoid injecting deep to the tendon into the radiocapitellar joint unless joint pathology is also being addressed.

## What Not to Inject / Contraindications Summary

- **Do not** inject a joint with untreated or unexcluded septic arthritis — aspirate and send fluid first whenever there is any diagnostic doubt (new effusion, fever, disproportionate pain, immunocompromise).
- **Do not** use particulate corticosteroid for cervical (and preferentially lumbar) transforaminal epidural injections — non-particulate (dexamethasone) only, given embolic infarction risk.
- **Do not** inject directly into tendon substance (intratendinous) with corticosteroid — peritendinous/bursal targets only; avoid repeated steroid into weight-bearing tendons (Achilles, patellar) given rupture risk.
- **Do not** proceed with elective IA corticosteroid within 3 months of planned ipsilateral THA, or IA HA within 2 months of planned ipsilateral TKA, without explicit surgical-planning discussion.
- **Do not** treat presumed gout/pseudogout empirically with steroid in a new acute mono-arthritis without at least attempting aspiration and crystal analysis, given the overlap in clinical presentation with septic arthritis.
- **Do not** promise PRP/BMAC/prolotherapy as disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy to patients — current evidence supports symptomatic benefit in appropriately selected patients, not structural disease reversal.
- **Do not** offer HA or PRP as a substitute for arthroplasty in K-L grade 4 disease with mechanical symptoms and failed conservative therapy — set expectations that injectable therapy has a low ceiling of benefit at this stage.
- **Do not** perform blind (landmark-only) hip injections when image guidance is available — accuracy is unacceptably low and risks neurovascular injury.
- **Avoid** LR-PRP for intra-articular injection into an actively inflamed/effusive joint — the added pro-inflammatory cytokine load increases post-injection flare risk without clear added benefit over LP-PRP in this setting.

## Rehabilitation Protocol (Post-Injection / Post-Procedure Care, General Framework)

**Phase 1 — Immediate post-procedure (Day 0–3)**

- Relative rest of the injected joint/structure for 24–48 hours; avoid high-impact loading and heavy resistance training in the treated region.
- Ice for symptomatic post-injection flare (steroid crystal flare, PRP/prolotherapy expected inflammatory response); counsel that flare after PRP/prolotherapy is part of the expected mechanism and typically resolves in 48–72 hours.
- Avoid NSAIDs for 3–7 days after PRP or prolotherapy if feasible (theoretical blunting of the intended inflammatory/platelet-mediated cascade) — this is a meaningful practice difference from corticosteroid injection, where NSAID use is not restricted.
- Continue baseline analgesia (acetaminophen/paracetamol) as needed.

**Phase 2 — Early mobilization (Week 1–2)**

- Resume gentle active range of motion and low-load activities of daily living.
- Begin or resume formal physiotherapy focused on the underlying mechanical driver (e.g., quadriceps/hip abductor strengthening for knee OA, rotator cuff/scapular stabilizer program for subacromial pathology, eccentric loading program for tendinopathy once acute post-injection soreness settles).
- For tendinopathy PRP protocols specifically: delay initiation/resumption of eccentric loading program 5–10 days post-injection to allow the local inflammatory/remodeling phase to establish before mechanical loading.

**Phase 3 — Progressive loading and functional return (Week 2–6)**

- Progress strengthening program per underlying diagnosis; for knee/hip OA, emphasize closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining, and weight management counseling in parallel with any injection series.
- For genicular RFA: expect denervation-related numbness in the anterior knee skin distribution (expected, not a complication); resume normal activity as tolerated within days, with the therapeutic pain-relief window typically becoming apparent by 2–4 weeks and lasting, in responders, 6–12 months.
- For BMAC/cartilage-procedure combinations (e.g., microfracture augmentation): follow the primary surgical procedure's weight-bearing protocol, not a generic injection protocol — BMAC in this context is an adjunct, and rehabilitation timelines are dictated by the cartilage repair procedure (typically protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks, progressive return to impact by 3–4 months).

**Return-to-work / return-to-sport criteria (general framework across indications)**

- Sedentary work: typically same day to 2–3 days post-injection depending on procedure invasiveness (aspiration/steroid vs BMAC harvest).
- Manual labor/heavy lifting: 3–7 days post steroid/HA; 1–2 weeks post PRP/BMAC to allow the expected inflammatory phase to settle before heavy loading.
- Return to sport/high-impact activity: individualized; for tendinopathy PRP protocols, most series use a structured criterion-based return (pain-free functional testing, symmetric strength ≥90% of contralateral limb) rather than a fixed calendar date, typically not before 4–6 weeks post-injection.
- Genicular RFA/nerve block: no formal activity restriction beyond immediate post-procedure comfort; encourage resumption of prior activity level as pain allows, since the goal is functional pain control rather than tissue healing.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[Hyaluronic Acid Injections Administered Within Two Months Prior to Total Knee Arthroplasty Increase the Risk for Periprosthetic Joint Infection](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39708558/)** (Jones CM et al., *The Knee*, 2025) — establishes a distinct, independent pre-TKA washout window for HA (2 months) separate from the corticosteroid window; changes practice by requiring explicit tracking of HA timing, not just steroid timing, in pre-arthroplasty planning.
- **[Infection Risk Increases After Total Hip Arthroplasty Within 3 Months Following Intra-Articular Corticosteroid Injection: A Meta-Analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36592824/)** (Albanese J et al., *J Arthroplasty*, 2023) — meta-analysis-level confirmation supporting a 3-month steroid washout specifically before THA; reinforces that hip and knee washout recommendations should not be assumed identical.
- **[Delaying Total Knee Arthroplasty More Than 4 Weeks After Intra-Articular Knee Injection Does Not Further Decrease Risk of Septic Revision](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36538938/)** (Bains SS et al., *J Knee Surg*, 2022) — registry data suggesting a 4-week floor may be sufficient for TKA specifically, tempering older blanket 3-month rules for knee (in contrast to the hip data above); use with the caveat that this is a single registry study, not yet a formal guideline change.
- **[Does a Simple Needle Stick From a Preoperative Knee Aspiration or Non-Steroidal Injection Influence Total Knee Arthroplasty Infection Risk?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40466921/)** (Shahzad H et al., *J Arthroplasty*, 2025) — helps separate the infection signal attributable to the injectate itself (steroid, HA) from simple needle-stick/arthrocentesis, informing more nuanced pre-op counseling.
- **[Intra-articular Orthobiologics Show Statistically but Not Clinically Meaningful Improvements Compared With Viscosupplementation in Knee Osteoarthritis: A Network Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42524752/)** (Han JH et al., *Arthroscopy*, 2026) — large network meta-analysis directly comparing PRP/BMAC/prolotherapy against HA; the "statistically but not clinically meaningful" framing is an important calibration point for how these therapies should be marketed to patients.
- **[Bone Marrow Aspirate Concentrate Injections for the Treatment of Knee Osteoarthritis: A Systematic Review of Randomized Controlled Trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39640186/)** (Han JH et al., *Orthop J Sports Med*, 2024) — consolidates BMAC-specific RCT evidence for knee OA, generally showing benefit versus baseline/placebo comparable to PRP without demonstrated superiority, informing cost-benefit counseling given BMAC's added harvest morbidity.
- **[Leukocyte-rich versus leukocyte-poor platelet-rich plasma and hyaluronic acid for knee osteoarthritis: a systematic review and network meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41629990/)** (Xu B et al., *J Orthop Surg Res*, 2026) — adds further network-level support for differentiating LR- vs LP-PRP as distinct interventions rather than a single "PRP" category, reinforcing the classification's clinical relevance.
- **[Comparative Clinical Effectiveness of Intra-Articular Leukocyte-Rich Platelet-Rich Plasma and Hyaluronic Acid in Treating Knee Osteoarthritis Pain: An Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42326600/)** (Peng YN et al., *Arch Rehabil Res Clin Transl*, 2026) — updated pooled analysis continuing to favor PRP over HA for pain outcomes at intermediate follow-up, consistent with the broader 2024–2026 literature trend.
- **[Platelet-rich plasma provides modest but durable functional benefit over corticosteroid for rotator cuff tendinopathy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42021740/)** (Yuwarungsikul C et al., *Knee Surg Sports Traumatol Arthrosc*, 2026) — extends the PRP-vs-steroid durability pattern (seen earlier in epicondylitis literature) to rotator cuff tendinopathy specifically.
- **[Platelet Concentration Does Not Influence Clinical Efficacy and Retear Rates](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41947497/)** (Lim JJ et al., *Am J Sports Med*, 2026) — a significant negative/null finding for rotator cuff repair augmentation: higher platelet concentration in PRP augmentation did not improve outcomes or reduce retear rates, arguing against over-engineering PRP preparation protocols for this specific application.
- **[Platelet-Rich Plasma Injections for Rotator Cuff Pathology Have Higher Complication Rates of Adhesive Capsulitis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41946449/)** (Driscoll A et al., *Arthroscopy*, 2026) — new safety signal that should be added to informed consent discussions for shoulder PRP specifically.
- **[Genicular Nerve Radiofrequency Treatment for Chronic Knee Osteoarthritis Pain: A Systematic Review And Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials Utilizing Image Guidance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42406393/)** (Peng YN et al., *Pain Med*, 2026) — consolidates image-guided RCT evidence supporting genicular RFA efficacy, strengthening its place as a bridge option for non-surgical-candidate chronic knee OA pain.
- **[A Multicenter Randomized Pragmatic Trial Comparing Intra-Articular Injection, Genicular Nerve Block, and Radiofrequency Ablation for Knee Osteoarthritis Pain: SKOAP Phase 2 Protocol](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42397214/)** (Cohen SP et al., *Pain Med*, 2026) — protocol publication for a major pragmatic head-to-head trial; flag for follow-up as its results mature, since it will directly inform sequencing of IA injection vs block vs RFA in chronic knee OA.
- **[Comparison of the efficacy of ultrasound-guided dextrose 25% hypertonic prolotherapy and intra-articular normal saline injection on pain, functional limitation, and range of motion in patients with knee osteoarthritis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40165187/)** (Teymouri et al., *BMC Musculoskelet Disord*, 2025) — recent placebo(saline)-controlled RCT adding to the modest but growing dextrose prolotherapy evidence base for knee OA.
- **[Semi-invasive therapies for pain in knee osteoarthritis: A systematic review and network meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39267256/)** (Goh et al., *Pain Pract*, 2025) — network meta-analysis situating prolotherapy, PRP, and other semi-invasive options relative to one another, useful for a single-glance comparative ranking when counseling patients on the injectable ladder.
