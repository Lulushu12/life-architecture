## Quick Reference

**Vancouver / Unified Classification System (UCS) — periprosthetic femur fracture around a hip stem**

| Type | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| A (Ag / Al) | Trochanteric (greater/lesser) fracture | Non-op if stem stable and fragment <2 cm displacement; ORIF (claw plate/cerclage) if abductor-compromising displacement |
| B1 | Around/just distal to stem, **stem well-fixed** | ORIF (locking plate ± cerclage ± strut allograft); preserve fixed stem |
| B2 | Around/just distal to stem, **stem loose**, adequate proximal bone stock | Revision to a **long, distally fixed tapered-fluted (cementless) or cemented stem** bypassing fracture ≥2 cortical diameters; plate augmentation as needed |
| B3 | Around/distal to stem, **stem loose + poor proximal bone stock** | Revision with **distal fixation + proximal reconstruction** — impaction grafting, proximal femoral allograft/allograft-prosthetic composite, or proximal femoral replacement (megaprosthesis) in elderly/low demand |
| C | Well distal to stem tip, implant uninvolved | ORIF as for a native periprosthetic diaphyseal fracture (plate ± IM nail if compatible), stem left alone |

**Lewis-Rorabeck — periprosthetic fracture around/above a TKA femoral component**

| Type | Definition | Treatment implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Non-displaced, implant stable | Non-operative — cast/brace/hinged brace, protected weight-bearing |
| II | Displaced, implant stable | ORIF (locked plate ± retrograde nail if box design allows) |
| III | Implant loose or failing | Revision TKA (often with stemmed femoral component ± augments), fracture fixation as adjunct |

**Su — supracondylar femur fracture above a TKA (refines Lewis-Rorabeck II by location relative to the femoral component box)**

| Type | Location | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Proximal to femoral component (box) | Standard locked plating or retrograde nail (if box open-box design permits) |
| II | At the level of the component, extends to box | Retrograde nailing usually precluded by closed-box design; locked plating preferred |
| III | Distal to component, within/anterior to the box | Very limited distal fixation zone; locked plating with distal fixed-angle device, consider revision if purchase inadequate |

**Paprosky — acetabular bone defects**

| Type | Features | Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| I | Intact rim, minimal cavitary loss | Hemispherical cementless cup ± morcellized graft |
| IIA | Superior cavitary defect, intact columns | Hemispherical cup (superomedial) + bone graft |
| IIB | Superolateral cavitary defect, rim deficient <1/3 | Larger hemispherical cup with high superolateral coverage, screws, graft |
| IIC | Medial wall defect, columns intact | Hemispherical cup, medial graft, screws into intact columns |
| IIIA | Superolateral bone loss >1/3 rim, some ischial/teardrop involvement, columns compromised but some host contact remains (<50% host contact) | Jumbo cup, porous metal augment ± cage; tantalum cup-cage construct |
| IIIB | Severe loss, both columns discontinuous or near-absent host contact (<50% host bone contact), often pelvic discontinuity | Cup-cage construct, custom triflange, porous tantalum augments/cages, allograft |

**Paprosky — femoral bone defects**

| Type | Features | Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| I | Minimal metaphyseal loss, intact diaphysis | Standard primary or proximally coated cementless stem |
| II | Extensive metaphyseal damage, intact diaphysis | Extensively coated cylindrical or tapered-fluted stem with diaphyseal fixation |
| IIIA | Metaphysis non-supportive, ≥4 cm diaphyseal isthmus available for fixation | Tapered-fluted modular/monoblock stem, diaphyseal engagement |
| IIIB | Metaphysis non-supportive, <4 cm diaphyseal fixation available | Long tapered-fluted stem, consider bypassing defect by ≥2 cortical diameters |
| IV | Extensive diaphyseal damage, isthmus not supportive, wide canal | Impaction grafting with cemented stem, or proximal femoral replacement/allograft-prosthetic composite |

**AORI (Anderson Orthopaedic Research Institute) — femoral/tibial defects in revision TKA**

| Type | Features | Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Type 1 | Intact metaphyseal bone, minor cavitary defects | Cement fill or small augments |
| Type 2A | Metaphyseal bone loss in one femoral condyle or one tibial plateau | Wedge/block augment on affected side, stem for load-sharing |
| Type 2B | Metaphyseal bone loss in both condyles or circumferential tibial loss | Bilateral augments, stemmed components, consider cones/sleeves |
| Type 3 | Deficient metaphyseal segment, may involve collateral/patellar tendon attachment | Structural allograft, metaphyseal cone/sleeve, or tumor-style segmental (hinged) prosthesis |

**Compact treatment ladders**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Periprosthetic hip fracture | — (essentially always surgical if displaced) | Protected weight-bearing only if truly non-displaced, stable, asymptomatic (rare) | ORIF (B1/A/C) vs revision arthroplasty (B2/B3) per Vancouver type and stem stability |
| Periprosthetic TKA fracture | Brace/cast for non-displaced, stable-implant (Lewis-Rorabeck I) | — | Locked plating / retrograde nail (LR II) vs revision TKA (LR III) |
| Aseptic loosening | Activity modification, analgesia while awaiting surgery | — | Revision arthroplasty (component-specific: cup, stem, or both; TKA revision) |
| Polyethylene wear/osteolysis | Surveillance if asymptomatic, small lesion, stable implant | Serial imaging (CT for lesion volume) | Liner exchange + bone grafting if shell/stem stable and locking mechanism compatible; full revision if unstable or incompatible liner |
| Instability after THA | Abduction bracing, activity/precipitant modification, physiotherapy for abductor strengthening | Component-position analysis (CT) to identify correctable cause | Revision: reorient malpositioned component, increase head size/offset, dual-mobility or constrained liner, address abductor deficiency |
| Stiffness after TKA | Aggressive supervised physiotherapy, static/dynamic splinting (up to 6–12 weeks post-op) | Manipulation under anesthesia (best 6 weeks–3 months post-op) | Arthroscopic or open lysis of adhesions; revision TKA if component malposition/oversizing/arthrofibrosis refractory |
| Metal-related ARMD | Annual clinical review + serum Co/Cr for all MoM bearings | Cross-sectional imaging (MARS-MRI or US) if symptomatic or ions elevated | Revision to non-MoM bearing if ARMD, rising ions, pseudotumor, or symptoms |

**Red flags:** acute inability to weight-bear after prior arthroplasty with thigh/groin pain (assume fracture until proven otherwise); rapid-onset effusion + pain out of proportion (rule out infection before assuming aseptic loosening — always aspirate); recurrent dislocation within 3 months (suspect malposition, not "bad luck"); rising cobalt >7 µg/L (MHRA action threshold) or >20 µg/L (high-risk) with a metal-on-metal bearing; sudden new pain in a previously well-functioning TKA/THA (rule out fracture, infection, or component fracture).

**Key numbers:** cortical bypass for revision stem past a fracture = **≥2 cortical diameters**; Paprosky IIIA/IIIB femoral diaphyseal fixation cutoff = **4 cm** of scratch-fit isthmus; acetabular host-bone contact cutoff for cup-cage/custom triflange consideration = **<50%**; MUA optimal window = **6 weeks to 3 months** post-TKA (diminishing returns beyond 12 weeks, essentially ineffective beyond ~1 year); flexion contracture requiring intervention generally >15°; MHRA blood metal ion threshold for concern = **7 µg/L** Co or Cr; osteolytic lesion volume threshold prompting proactive surgery (asymptomatic) commonly cited around **≥5–6 cm³** or any lesion with cortical breach/progressive enlargement on serial imaging.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Periprosthetic femur fracture (hip)

Occurs at the stem-bone-cement/implant interface, most commonly at or just distal to the stem tip where stress-riser effects concentrate (transition from a stiff intramedullary implant to native, more compliant bone). Risk factors: osteolysis, aseptic/septic loosening (creates a mobile stem that transmits torque to the fracture site rather than absorbing it), osteoporosis, revision surgery (prior cortical perforations, stress risers from screw holes), stem geometry (straight, cementless, extensively coated stems have historically shown different periprosthetic fracture profiles than cemented polished tapered stems — the latter are associated disproportionately with Vancouver B2 fractures because subsidence-dependent fixation predisposes to loosening under torsional load), and low-energy falls in the elderly. Intraoperative fractures occur during broach/stem insertion (calcar split, greater trochanter avulsion) especially with cementless press-fit stems in stovepipe/Dorr type C femurs. Overall incidence after primary THA is roughly 1% (higher, 2–4%, with cementless fixation and revision surgery), rising with each subsequent revision as bone stock is progressively consumed.

### Periprosthetic fracture around TKA

Supracondylar femur is the most frequent site; occurs at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction just proximal to the femoral component flange, a classic stress-riser zone especially with anterior femoral notching (>3 mm notch increases fracture risk substantially — some series report a 3-fold increase with any notching and higher with deeper/more anterior notches). Osteolysis from polyethylene debris, osteoporosis, and neurologic conditions (seizure, Parkinson's) are contributory. Tibial periprosthetic fractures are less common and often relate to stress-shielding under a well-fixed stemmed tibial component or intraoperative plateau fracture during broaching/impaction in osteoporotic bone. Patellar periprosthetic fractures are rarer still, usually stress- or maltracking-related in a resurfaced patella.

### Aseptic loosening

The end-stage of a biological cascade: micromotion at the bone-implant interface (mechanical loosening, often from initial malalignment, undersizing, or inadequate initial fixation) or particle-induced osteolysis (biological loosening) both lead to progressive radiolucency, subsidence, and mechanical failure. Distinguishing aseptic from septic loosening is the central diagnostic task — biofilm-mediated low-grade infection (particularly with less-virulent organisms such as *Cutibacterium acnes*) can mimic aseptic loosening radiographically and clinically, with normal or only mildly elevated inflammatory markers. Loosening remains the leading indication for revision THA and revision TKA in most contemporary registries, ahead of infection and instability.

### Polyethylene wear & osteolysis

Third-body and adhesive/abrasive wear generate submicron polyethylene particles (<1 µm most biologically active) that are phagocytosed by macrophages, triggering a RANKL-mediated osteoclastic cascade → periprosthetic osteolysis. Highly cross-linked polyethylene (HXLPE) has reduced volumetric wear rates by roughly 50–90% versus conventional UHMWPE and correspondingly reduced (but not eliminated) osteolysis incidence; metal and ceramic debris (from modular junctions, trunnionosis, or MoM bearings) can trigger an analogous but immunologically distinct ARMD response. Osteolytic lesions are frequently clinically silent until either a pathologic fracture, a mechanical failure of the ingrowth surface, or an incidental finding on routine surveillance radiographs — which is the rationale for lifelong periodic imaging follow-up after arthroplasty, particularly with conventional polyethylene bearings implanted before the HXLPE era.

### Instability after THA

Multifactorial: component malposition (cup anteversion/inclination outside the safe combined range, femoral component malrotation), soft-tissue deficiency (abductor insufficiency, capsular deficiency from prior surgery, particularly posterior approach without adequate repair), impingement (bony or prosthetic, including from retained osteophytes or a proud liner), leg length/offset discrepancy, and patient factors (neurocognitive disease, spinopelvic stiffness — a fused or stiff lumbar spine reduces functional acetabular anteversion changes with position, dramatically raising dislocation risk, "hip-spine syndrome"). Recurrent instability requires a directed work-up rather than empiric constraint; instability is one of the top-three indications for revision THA in most registries, and roughly half of first-time dislocations recur without a structural correction.

### Stiffness after TKA

Arthrofibrosis is the pathologic accumulation of dense fibrous scar in the anterior interval, suprapatellar pouch, and posterior capsule, driven by an exaggerated fibroproliferative response to surgical trauma. Contributing surgical factors: component malposition/oversizing (femoral component flexion or oversizing, elevated joint line, tibial slope errors), undercorrected flexion/extension gap balance, patella baja, and infection (always exclude). Patient factors: pre-existing stiffness, diabetes, CRPS-prone phenotype, low pain threshold with poor early engagement in physiotherapy. Definitions vary but stiffness is commonly defined as flexion <90° and/or a flexion contracture >15° beyond the expected postoperative timeline (generally by 6–12 weeks).

### Metal-related ARMD

Specific to metal-on-metal (MoM) bearings (resurfacing and MoM THA) and, to a lesser extent, modular metal junctions (head-neck trunnions) in metal-on-polyethylene or ceramic-on-poly THA ("trunnionosis"). Cobalt-chromium wear particles and ions provoke a lymphocyte-dominated (ALVAL — aseptic lymphocytic vasculitis-associated lesion) hypersensitivity-type response, causing pseudotumor formation, soft-tissue/muscle necrosis, and osteolysis. Large-head MoM designs (>36 mm, including hip resurfacing in females and small femoral heads) carry the highest ARMD risk due to greater sliding distance and edge-loading potential. Female sex, small component size, and high cup inclination/anteversion outside target zones are consistently identified as risk amplifiers for edge-loading and accelerated wear.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History:** mechanism (low- vs high-energy), time since index arthroplasty, pain character (activity-related mechanical pain suggests loosening; rest/night pain raises infection concern), prior revision history, systemic symptoms (fever, malaise — infection), and functional trajectory (sudden new instability episode vs gradual increasing pain).

**Examination:** inspect for prior incisions/sinus tracts, assess leg length and rotational deformity (fracture), abductor strength and Trendelenburg (THA instability/abductor insufficiency), knee ROM and extensor mechanism integrity (TKA stiffness), and a thorough neurovascular exam (mandatory before and after any periprosthetic fracture fixation).

**Imaging — first line:** orthopedic-quality AP pelvis + full-length femur or tibia/fibula radiographs (not just the joint — periprosthetic fractures and stem tip stress risers are frequently missed on joint-only views), with comparison to prior films to assess new radiolucency, subsidence, or cortical change indicating pre-existing loosening at the time of fracture (critical for Vancouver B1 vs B2 discrimination). CT is invaluable for: acetabular/femoral bone defect quantification pre-revision (Paprosky grading), fracture comminution/extension planning, osteolytic lesion volume, and component rotational malposition workup in TKA stiffness/instability. Metal artifact reduction sequences (MARS-MRI) or ultrasound are first-line for ARMD screening/pseudotumor detection. Bone scan/PET has a limited but occasional role when infection versus aseptic loosening remains equivocal after aspiration.

**Laboratory:** ESR/CRP as first-line infection screen (with awareness of the ICM 2018/2025 threshold framework); synovial fluid aspiration for cell count/differential, culture, and alpha-defensin or leukocyte esterase when infection is a differential — this step is mandatory before labeling a loose implant "aseptic," since occult PJI can present identically. Serum cobalt/chromium ion levels are mandatory surveillance for all MoM bearings and useful adjuncts when ARMD or trunnionosis is suspected in mixed-bearing THA.

**Fracture-specific work-up:** confirm implant stability by comparing current films to the earliest available post-index-arthroplasty series — any interval subsidence, new circumferential radiolucent line, or cement mantle fracture predating the acute injury reclassifies an apparent B1 pattern as B2. Document the fracture's relationship to the stem tip/component box precisely (drives Vancouver/Lewis-Rorabeck/Su subtype and, in turn, fixation-versus-nail feasibility).

**Loosening/osteolysis work-up:** serial (not single-timepoint) radiographs are far more sensitive than one film for detecting progressive migration/subsidence or lesion enlargement; measure osteolytic lesion volume on CT when a lesion is identified, since progressive enlargement or cortical breach is itself an indication to proceed to surgery even in an oligosymptomatic patient, to avoid presenting later as a pathologic fracture.

**Instability-specific work-up:** standing and supine radiographs, CT for combined anteversion (cup + stem), spinopelvic imaging (standing/sitting lateral radiographs or EOS) when spinopelvic stiffness/hip-spine syndrome is suspected, and exam under fluoroscopy if impingement mechanism is unclear.

**Stiffness-specific work-up:** lateral radiograph for flexion/extension arc and patella height, CT for femoral/tibial component rotation (malrotation is a major, correctable driver of stiffness and pain), and infection screen in every case before attributing stiffness to arthrofibrosis alone.

**ARMD-specific work-up:** paired serum cobalt and chromium levels (cobalt is generally the more sensitive marker for soft-tissue reaction), MARS-MRI or ultrasound for pseudotumor/fluid collection characterization and grading (solid vs cystic components influence urgency), and hip abductor muscle assessment (fatty infiltration/atrophy on MRI predicts post-revision function and dislocation risk).

**Differentiating aseptic loosening from low-grade infection (ICM-framework screening):**

| Feature | Favors aseptic loosening | Favors infection/PJI |
|---|---|---|
| Pain pattern | Mechanical, activity-related, gradual onset | Rest/night pain, may be more acute or unremitting |
| Serum ESR/CRP | Normal or mildly elevated | Elevated (though low-virulence organisms can present with only mild elevation) |
| Synovial WBC/differential | Low count, low neutrophil % | Elevated count and neutrophil % (thresholds differ for acute vs chronic presentation per ICM criteria) |
| Alpha-defensin / leukocyte esterase | Negative | Positive |
| Radiographic pattern | Progressive, well-demarcated radiolucency/osteolysis without periostitis | May show aggressive lucency, periostitis, or endosteal scalloping |
| Intraoperative findings | Clear/serous fluid, no purulence | Purulence, elevated intraoperative frozen-section neutrophil count |

Any ambiguity on this table mandates a full infection work-up (aspiration with culture held ≥14 days for slow-growing organisms, histopathology) before proceeding with a purely "aseptic" revision plan — misclassifying an infected loose implant as aseptic is a well-documented cause of early revision failure.

## Classification

*(Full classification tables with management implications are provided in Quick Reference above; the narrative below adds nuance not captured in tabular form.)*

**Vancouver/UCS nuance:**
- The critical branch point is intraoperative/radiographic stem stability, not fracture pattern alone — a B1 fracture treated as B2 (i.e., revised unnecessarily) exposes the patient to added morbidity, while a B2 treated as B1 (ORIF on a loose stem) reliably fails.
- Intraoperative confirmation of fixation (manual stress testing of the stem after exposure) is the gold standard when preoperative imaging is equivocal.
- A recent comparative analysis (Danderyd Hospital cohort, 157 B2 fractures) found ORIF around cemented polished tapered stems can achieve acceptable results in carefully selected cases where the cement mantle and stem remain grossly intact despite radiolucency, reflecting an evolving, more nuanced application of the B1/B2 boundary rather than a rigid binary rule — but stem revision remains the default for a truly loose stem.
- The Unified Classification System (UCS) extends Vancouver-type logic (location + stability + bone stock) across all periprosthetic fractures around any implant (hip, knee, shoulder), making the same A/B1/B2/B3/C logic transferable when counseling on periprosthetic fractures outside the hip.

**Lewis-Rorabeck/Su nuance:**
- Su further stratifies fracture location relative to the femoral component's intercondylar box, which determines whether retrograde intramedullary nailing is mechanically feasible (open-box posterior-stabilized/cruciate-retaining designs with adequate box clearance) versus precluded (closed-box designs, or fracture line through the box), steering the surgeon toward locked plating in most Su II/III patterns.
- Always confirm the specific implant's box geometry from operative records or manufacturer specifications before committing to a nailing strategy — assuming compatibility based on component appearance on plain film is an avoidable planning error.

**Paprosky nuance (femur):**
- The key intraoperative decision point is whether ≥4 cm of intact diaphyseal "scratch fit" isthmus is available distal to the defect — this threshold separates a standard-length tapered-fluted stem (IIIA) from a long tapered-fluted stem requiring extended diaphyseal engagement or supplementary strut allograft/plate (IIIB).
- Type IV femurs (stovepipe canals, non-supportive diaphysis) generally require either impaction bone grafting with a cemented stem or a proximal femoral replacement/allograft-prosthetic composite, particularly in low-demand elderly patients where restoring bone stock is less important than immediate stable function.

**Paprosky nuance (acetabulum):**
- The practical branch point is host bone contact — ≥50% supportive host contact allows a jumbo cup ± augments; <50% or pelvic discontinuity mandates a construct that bridges columns (cup-cage, custom triflange, or a porous tantalum revision shell with multiple screw fixation points).
- Pelvic discontinuity (a separate but often co-existing finding, most common with IIIB defects) requires specific recognition on CT/plain film (a horizontal lucent line separating superior and inferior hemipelvis) since it changes the reconstruction goal from cup fixation alone to restoring pelvic column continuity before/during cup placement.

**AORI nuance:**
- AORI grading drives the reconstructive ladder in revision TKA — Type 1 defects are cement-filled, Type 2 defects use metal augments (wedges/blocks) with stemmed components for load transfer past the deficient zone, and Type 3 defects require either bone-substituting metaphyseal cones/sleeves (preferred where host bone quality allows osseointegration) or, in the most severe/compromised cases, a segmental (rotating hinge/tumor-style) reconstruction.
- Grade the femur and tibia independently — asymmetric defects (e.g., AORI 1 tibia with AORI 3 femur) are common and the reconstruction plan for each side should be selected on its own merits rather than matched to the more severe side by default.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Periprosthetic hip/TKA fracture:** non-operative only in the narrow scenario of a genuinely non-displaced fracture with a confirmed stable implant (Vancouver A with minimal displacement in a low-demand patient; Lewis-Rorabeck I) — protected weight-bearing, hinged bracing for TKA fractures, and close serial radiographic follow-up (2, 6, 12 weeks) to detect secondary displacement.
- **Aseptic loosening / osteolysis:** non-operatively "managed" only as a bridge (analgesia, activity modification, walking aid) while awaiting definitive revision, since neither reverses without surgery; asymptomatic, radiographically stable small osteolytic lesions with a well-fixed implant may be surveilled with serial imaging rather than immediately revised.
- **Instability after THA:** responds to non-operative management (abduction bracing 6–12 weeks, activity/precipitant avoidance, targeted abductor strengthening) primarily for first-time, atraumatic, non-recurrent dislocations without an identifiable major structural cause; recurrent instability rarely resolves without addressing the underlying mechanical driver.
- **Stiffness after TKA:** managed non-operatively first-line with an aggressive, supervised physiotherapy program and, in the acute post-op window, dynamic or static progressive splinting; this is the appropriate first step in essentially all cases before considering MUA.
- **Metal-related ARMD:** surveillance (serial clinical review, ion levels, and imaging per MHRA-style protocols) is itself the non-operative "treatment" for asymptomatic patients with normal or borderline ion levels and no pseudotumor.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

Intra-articular corticosteroid injection has a limited, largely diagnostic-adjunct role in this cluster (it does not treat loosening, instability, fracture, or established arthrofibrosis, and carries meaningful risk of seeding a prosthetic joint if infection has not first been excluded — never inject a painful arthroplasty without first ruling out PJI). Diagnostic aspiration itself doubles as both a work-up step and, when large sympathetic effusions are present, a symptomatic adjunct while definitive surgical planning proceeds. The clinically relevant adjunctive procedures in this cluster are primarily intraoperative/surgical adjuncts rather than office injections: cell-saver/tranexamic acid protocols for revision blood management (revision arthroplasty carries substantially higher blood loss than primary surgery, particularly with extended trochanteric osteotomy or extensive membrane debridement), and — specific to stiffness — manipulation under anesthesia is best conceptualized as a closed adjunctive procedure distinct from open revision, discussed in detail under Operative below given its procedural nature and specific timing window.

**Multidisciplinary/perioperative optimization pearls:** every displaced periprosthetic hip fracture in a patient over 65 should trigger the same orthogeriatric co-management pathway used for native hip fractures (same-admission surgery target, delirium prevention, VTE prophylaxis, bone-health assessment and osteoporosis treatment initiation) given the comparable mortality burden; every case being labeled "aseptic" loosening should have infection formally excluded per the differentiating table above before theatre time and implants are committed; and every ARMD revision should be planned with tissue banking (histopathology, culture) and a discussion of possible staged reconstruction if soft-tissue destruction is extensive.

### Operative

**Periprosthetic femur fracture (hip) — ORIF vs revision, decision by stem stability.**
- **Vancouver A:** ORIF only if displacement threatens abductor function or fragment size is large (e.g., claw plate/cable-grip system for greater trochanter, tension band/cerclage for lesser trochanter with lag screw fixation into the calcar if needed); most are treated non-operatively.
- **Vancouver B1 (stem stable):** ORIF — typically a locking compression plate spanning the fracture with a combination of locking screws proximally (unicortical around the stem, or cable/cerclage where the stem occupies the canal) and bicortical screws distally, often augmented with cerclage cables and/or cortical strut allograft for added rotational stability. Pitfall: misjudging stem stability preoperatively — always be prepared to convert to revision intraoperatively if the stem is found to be loose on manual testing.
- **Vancouver B2 (stem loose, adequate bone stock):** revision arthroplasty with a **long, distally fixed tapered-fluted stem** — either cementless (modular or monoblock titanium tapered-fluted, preferred for most active patients and larger/irregular canals) or a long cemented stem in select lower-demand patients — bypassing the fracture by **≥2 cortical diameters** to avoid a new stress riser at the plate/stem junction. Cerclage wires/cables and/or a lateral plate are frequently added for additional fracture-site compression and rotational control. Comparative data (multiple 2024–2025 cohorts) show tapered-fluted stems achieve reliable diaphyseal purchase with lower intraoperative fracture rates and better functional outcomes than extensively coated cylindrical designs in this setting; cemented long-stem revision in appropriately selected B2 fractures around a previously cemented polished tapered stem has shown shorter fracture healing time and lower dislocation/re-loosening rates than uncemented revision in some comparative series, reflecting patient- and bone-quality-specific decision-making rather than a universal preference.
- **Vancouver B3 (stem loose, poor bone stock):** revision with distal fixation (long tapered-fluted stem bypassing the defect) plus proximal reconstruction — impaction bone grafting, proximal femoral allograft-prosthetic composite, or (in low-demand/elderly patients, or where reconstruction is not durable) a proximal femoral replacement (megaprosthesis) accepting a salvage-type solution that prioritizes immediate stability and early mobilization over bone stock restoration.
- **Vancouver C:** treat as a native periprosthetic diaphyseal fracture distal to the implant — locked plating (± minimally invasive technique) is standard; retrograde or antegrade IM nailing can be considered if implant geometry allows adequate proximal overlap without interfering with the existing stem.
- **Technical pearls:** always obtain full-length femur imaging pre-operatively to plan implant overlap/bypass; use a combined hip-and-trauma approach (extended trochanteric osteotomy is frequently required for cement/stem removal in B2/B3 revisions); cable-plate constructs around a revision stem provide better rotational control than cables alone; in the elderly frail patient, consider the survival benefit of early full weight-bearing achievable with a well-fixed distally engaging stem versus the slower-to-load construct of ORIF alone.

**Periprosthetic fracture around TKA.**
- **Lewis-Rorabeck I:** hinged knee brace, protected weight-bearing, serial films.
- **Lewis-Rorabeck II (implant stable):** ORIF — locked distal femoral plating is the workhorse; retrograde intramedullary nailing is an option only when the femoral component has an open (posterior-stabilized box or cruciate-retaining) design permitting nail passage (Su type I, occasionally II) — confirm compatibility with implant records/imaging before planning a nail. Dual-plating (medial + lateral) or plate-nail combinations are used for comminuted or osteoporotic patterns needing added stability.
- **Lewis-Rorabeck III (implant loose/failing):** revision TKA, typically with a stemmed femoral component bridging the fracture, metaphyseal augments/cones for associated bone loss, and fracture-specific fixation (cerclage, plate) as an adjunct to the revision construct; distal femoral replacement (hinged tumor-style prosthesis) is reserved for unreconstructable comminution, severe bone loss, or the frail elderly patient in whom immediate full weight-bearing is the priority.
- **Technical pearls:** avoid anterior femoral notching at the index TKA (the single most modifiable risk factor); in revision for LR III, restore the joint line and use a rotating-hinge design when collateral ligament integrity cannot be confirmed or restored.

**Aseptic loosening.**
- Revision strategy depends on which component(s) are loose (confirm with targeted fluoroscopic-guided injection/aspiration if radiographic findings are ambiguous, and always exclude infection first — including intraoperative frozen section and cultures even when preoperative work-up is reassuring).
- Acetabular revision uses the Paprosky-based reconstruction ladder above; femoral revision uses the femoral Paprosky ladder with **tapered-fluted stems as the current workhorse** for most Type II–IIIB defects given reliable diaphyseal scratch-fit fixation, versatility across canal shapes, and modularity to restore length/offset independently of fixation.
- Impaction bone grafting is reserved for Type IV femurs or younger patients where bone stock restoration is prioritized over the simplicity of a tapered-fluted stem.
- Pitfall: underestimating the extent of osteolysis on plain films — CT consistently reveals larger cavitary defects than radiographs, and undersizing the planned reconstruction intraoperatively is a common avoidable error.

**Polyethylene wear & osteolysis.**
- If the acetabular shell (hip) or tibial/femoral components (knee) are well-fixed and the locking mechanism is compatible with a modern liner, **isolated liner exchange** with bone grafting of osteolytic cavities (via existing screw holes or a limited cortical window) avoids the morbidity of full component revision.
- Full revision is required when the shell/tray is loose, malpositioned (uncorrectable by liner change alone), the locking mechanism is obsolete/incompatible, or the osteolytic defect compromises structural support requiring reconstruction.
- Pitfall: isolated liner exchange in a well-fixed but malpositioned cup treats the wear but not the underlying instability risk — assess combined anteversion before committing to liner-only exchange, and consider upsizing to a larger femoral head or dual-mobility liner at the time of exchange given the low incremental morbidity and meaningful reduction in subsequent dislocation risk.

**Instability after THA.**
- Directed revision targets the identified cause: reorient a malpositioned cup or stem (the single most common correctable cause); increase femoral head diameter and/or restore offset; convert to a **dual-mobility** construct.
- Dual mobility is increasingly favored first-line for recurrent instability without a clearly correctable structural cause, and in revision settings generally, given effectiveness data and lower reported dislocation rates versus constrained liners in comparative series through 2025–2026.
- Reserve a **constrained liner** for cases with abductor deficiency/absence or recurrent instability after dual mobility/optimized component position has failed, since constrained constructs carry a mechanical-failure/dissociation risk of their own and constrain native hip biomechanics.
- Address abductor insufficiency directly (soft-tissue reconstruction, allograft/mesh augmentation, or acceptance of dual-mobility/constraint as compensation) when present.
- In hip-spine syndrome, favor increased combined anteversion tolerance (dual mobility) rather than attempting to "chase" an ideal fixed cup position that a stiff spine will not tolerate through its functional range.
- Pitfall: revising an unstable THA without a clear mechanistic diagnosis (i.e., simply "upsizing the head" without addressing malposition or soft-tissue deficiency) has a materially higher failure rate than a directed revision.

**Stiffness after TKA.**
- **Manipulation under anesthesia (MUA):** best performed in the **6-week to 3-month** window post-primary TKA, under regional/general anesthesia with controlled, gentle supracondylar-lever technique to avoid periprosthetic fracture or extensor mechanism rupture; efficacy drops substantially beyond 3 months and MUA is largely ineffective beyond ~1 year, at which point mature fibrosis no longer yields to closed manipulation. Recent multicenter randomized data (Ranawat Award-winning trial) evaluated adjuvant anti-inflammatory strategies around MUA to improve sustained ROM gains; failure risk after MUA is associated with older age, cruciate-retaining implant design, and lower ROM achieved at the 2-week post-MUA check, per 2025 predictor analyses.
- **Arthroscopic lysis of adhesions (aLOA):** an option for patients who failed a prior MUA, particularly for anterior interval/suprapatellar scarring; open surgical lysis is reserved for extensive or posterior capsular scarring not accessible arthroscopically.
- **Revision TKA:** indicated when component malposition/malrotation, oversizing, joint-line elevation, or flexion/extension gap mismatch is identified as the structural driver of stiffness — addressing the mechanical cause is essential, since revising a well-positioned implant purely for "idiopathic" arthrofibrosis without correcting an underlying technical error yields inconsistent results. A 2025 registry-linked analysis found manipulation and/or lysis of adhesions performed before a subsequent revision TKA is itself associated with increased risk of further revision, underscoring that stiffness requiring intervention is a marker of a more complex knee and warrants careful causal work-up rather than reflexive MUA.

**Metal-related ARMD.**
- Revision is indicated for symptomatic ARMD, pseudotumor on imaging, progressively rising ion levels, or asymptomatic but high-risk implant/ion combinations per MHRA-style thresholds (historically 7 µg/L as the action threshold, with growing evidence supporting lower thresholds in some analyses).
- Revision converts the bearing away from metal-on-metal (typically to ceramic-on-polyethylene or metal-on-highly-cross-linked-polyethylene), requires debridement of necrotic/pseudotumor tissue, and must address any associated abductor/soft-tissue damage.
- Outcomes are worse than routine aseptic revision, with higher complication rates reported when significant soft-tissue destruction is present, so surgery should not be delayed once revision criteria are met — waiting for symptoms to worsen only increases the extent of irreversible soft-tissue loss.
- Pitfall: failing to send tissue for histopathology (ALVAL grading) and culture at revision — distinguishing ARMD from low-grade PJI, and grading the extent of tissue damage, both inform prognosis and the need for staged versus single-stage reconstruction.

#### Constraint ladder in revision TKA

Constraint is added only to the degree required by the residual soft-tissue/ligamentous deficiency confirmed intraoperatively — over-constraining transmits more stress to the bone-implant interface and accelerates aseptic loosening, while under-constraining leaves the knee unstable.

- **Posterior-stabilized (PS) primary-style articulation:** sufficient when both collateral ligaments are competent and only PCL substitution is needed (most routine revisions for isolated component wear/loosening with preserved collaterals).
- **Varus-valgus constrained (VVC) / condylar-constrained knee (CCK):** the first step up when there is partial collateral incompetence or coronal-plane laxity that a PS poly cannot control; a deeper, more conforming tibial post/femoral box resists varus-valgus stress while still permitting some native rotation. Contemporary registry data (2025 National Joint Registry analysis) show CCK achieves favorable survivorship as a first-time aseptic revision construct, supporting its position as the default constraint step before hinge.
- **Rotating-hinge:** reserved for global ligamentous instability (both collaterals deficient/absent), extensor mechanism compromise requiring a linked construct, severe flexion-extension gap mismatch unmanageable by soft-tissue balancing, or salvage after failed lower-constraint revision. A rotating (rather than fixed) hinge articulation reduces transmitted torsional stress at the bone-implant interface compared with older fixed-hinge designs. Meta-analytic pooling shows CCK and rotating-hinge achieve broadly similar ~90% 5-year survivorship, so the choice is driven primarily by the degree of ligamentous deficiency present, not by an assumed durability advantage of either design.
- **Distal femoral replacement (segmental/tumor-style):** the final rung, for unreconstructable bone loss (AORI Type 3 with unsalvageable metaphysis) or failed hinge revision, prioritizing immediate stable function over further bone-preserving reconstruction attempts.

#### Tapered-fluted stems, cones & sleeves — technical notes

- **Tapered-fluted stems** achieve axial and rotational stability through distal diaphyseal "scratch-fit" engagement rather than proximal metaphyseal fit, making them the workhorse implant for both periprosthetic fracture revision and aseptic loosening revision with compromised proximal bone. Modular versions allow independent intraoperative optimization of version, offset, and length; monoblock versions sacrifice that modularity for a lower risk of junction-related fracture/corrosion, generally preferred when a very long distal fixation segment is needed.
- **Metaphyseal cones and sleeves** (porous tantalum or highly porous titanium) bridge severe metaphyseal bone loss (AORI Type 2B/3 in the knee; analogous severe metaphyseal deficiency in proximal femoral reconstruction) by providing a biologic scaffold for osseointegration onto which a standard stemmed revision component is then cemented or press-fit. Cones generally provide more robust fixation in the most severe defects and can be combined with structural allograft; sleeves are more commonly used for moderate defects with a more standardized, less custom-contoured fit. Reported cone-specific survivorship is excellent (implant-specific revision rates often <5% at 5–10 years in contemporary series), even though overall reoperation-for-any-reason rates in these complex reconstructions remain meaningfully higher.
- Pitfall: under-reaming/under-preparing the metaphyseal defect for a cone risks incomplete seating and early loosening; conversely, over-reaming sacrifices host bone unnecessarily — templating from preoperative CT is standard practice before committing to cone/sleeve size.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Periprosthetic hip fracture — ORIF (B1/A/C):**
- **Weeks 0–6:** protected weight-bearing (toe-touch to 50% partial) pending radiographic union evidence; hip precautions per approach used; formal physiotherapy for gait training, isometric strengthening, and edema control begins immediately.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progression to full weight-bearing by **10–12 weeks** if callus/bridging present on serial films; active strengthening of abductors/core and gait normalization.
- **Weeks 12–16:** union typically expected; delayed union warrants endocrine/metabolic work-up (vitamin D, calcium, parathyroid function) and consideration of bone-stimulation adjuncts beyond 4–6 months if progress stalls.

**Periprosthetic hip fracture — revision (B2/B3):**
- **Weeks 0–2:** a well-fixed tapered-fluted stem with solid diaphyseal scratch-fit generally allows **weight-bearing as tolerated** immediately — a key advantage over ORIF, particularly in frail elderly patients where prolonged restricted weight-bearing carries its own mortality risk. Impaction grafting or allograft-prosthetic composite reconstructions instead require **protected/partial weight-bearing for 6–12 weeks** to allow graft incorporation.
- **Weeks 2–12:** physiotherapy emphasizes early mobilization, fall-prevention strategy, and abductor/core strengthening; assistive device weaning guided by strength and balance testing.
- **Months 3–6:** functional recovery continues; anticipate a slower overall trajectory than primary arthroplasty given the reconstructive complexity, particularly after proximal femoral replacement or allograft-prosthetic composite.

**Periprosthetic TKA fracture — ORIF:**
- **Weeks 0–4:** hinged brace locked in extension for ambulation, partial weight-bearing; ROM exercises (passive/active-assisted) begin early (within days) to avoid superimposed stiffness while balancing fracture protection — a genuine tension requiring individualized pacing with the treating surgeon.
- **Weeks 4–8:** progression to full weight-bearing with radiographic progress; brace unlocked incrementally as ROM and fracture stability allow.
- **Weeks 8+:** continued ROM progression targeting functional flexion (≥90–100°); union assessment at 3 months.

**Periprosthetic TKA fracture — revision:** rehabilitation follows standard revision TKA principles — weight-bearing as tolerated if stemmed components achieve solid diaphyseal fixation, ROM progression targeting 90° flexion by 6 weeks, full recovery trajectory 6–12 months.

**Aseptic loosening / wear-osteolysis revision (hip or knee):** weight-bearing status individualized to fixation achieved (cementless press-fit generally protected 4–6 weeks; cemented fixation allows earlier full weight-bearing); physiotherapy follows standard revision arthroplasty protocols with emphasis on regaining strength lost during the pre-revision symptomatic period. Return to low-impact activity typically 3 months; return to higher-demand activity 6–12 months, contingent on component ingrowth.

**Instability after THA (post-revision):** hip precautions (avoid the specific provocative position — posterior for posterior approach, anterior for anterior approach) strictly maintained for **6–12 weeks** minimum, longer if a constrained liner or major soft-tissue reconstruction was performed; abductor strengthening progresses gradually starting around 6 weeks once soft-tissue healing allows; return to unrestricted activity generally 3–4 months with a stable, well-positioned reconstruction.

**Stiffness after TKA (post-MUA):**
- **Day 0:** immediate continuous passive motion or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM program starting the same day as manipulation is essential — ROM gains achieved intraoperatively are lost rapidly without immediate reinforcement.
- **Weeks 0–2:** formal physiotherapy 3–5 times weekly, static/dynamic splinting overnight to hold end-range extension/flexion.
- **Week 2 checkpoint:** ROM at this visit is a validated predictor of ultimate success; patients failing to maintain gains by 2 weeks are candidates for repeat intervention (aLOA) rather than prolonged observation.
- **Weeks 2–6:** continued intensive physiotherapy, transition to home program with periodic supervised reassessment.

**Return to work/sport (general, this cluster):** sedentary work typically resumes 2–6 weeks post-uncomplicated revision or ORIF; physical/manual labor 3–6 months contingent on fixation and healing; low-impact recreational sport (swimming, cycling, golf) 3–4 months; higher-demand activity is individualized and often more conservative than after primary arthroplasty given the compromised bone stock and construct in revision settings — many revision surgeons counsel permanent avoidance of high-impact/pivoting sport regardless of timeline.

## Complications

**Periprosthetic fracture (hip/knee):**
- Nonunion/malunion — higher with ORIF around a loose or osteoporotic construct, or when fixation does not achieve adequate mechanical stability across the fracture.
- Hardware failure and recurrent periprosthetic fracture at the proximal end of a long stem/plate (a new stress riser created by the revision construct itself), dislocation (hip), and periprosthetic joint infection.
- A critical population-level point: **elevated 1-year mortality**, with the PIPPAS multicenter prospective study (2025) specifically characterizing risk factors and mortality outcomes in Vancouver type C fractures. Periprosthetic hip fractures in the elderly carry hip-fracture-equivalent mortality risk and should be treated with the same medical optimization urgency (same-admission surgery where feasible, orthogeriatric co-management, VTE prophylaxis, osteoporosis treatment initiation).

**Revision arthroplasty (general):**
- Dislocation — highest immediately post-revision, particularly hip revision with extensive soft-tissue release.
- Aseptic re-loosening and periprosthetic joint infection — both elevated versus primary arthroplasty due to longer surgical time, more extensive dissection, and prior surgical history/scar burden.
- Leg length discrepancy, nerve palsy (peroneal nerve in knee revision with correction of a fixed valgus/flexion deformity; sciatic/femoral in hip revision, particularly with limb lengthening), and intraoperative fracture during component removal or broaching.

**Constrained/hinge constructs:**
- Mechanical failure at the constraint mechanism itself (bushing wear, post fracture, dissociation).
- Higher aseptic loosening rates transmitted to the bone-implant interface from the constrained kinematics — the fundamental trade-off of adding constraint.
- Contemporary comparative data show condylar-constrained and rotating-hinge implants achieve broadly similar mid-term survivorship (roughly 90% at 5 years in recent meta-analytic pooling) when applied appropriately by indication, reassuring that appropriately selected constraint is durable rather than a short-term compromise.

**Metaphyseal cones/sleeves:**
- Generally excellent early-to-mid-term survivorship for the cone/sleeve construct itself (implant-specific revision rates low, often <5% at 5–10 years).
- Overall reoperation-for-any-reason rates in the broader reconstruction are meaningfully higher, reflecting that cones are typically used in the most complex, bone-deficient reconstructions where the whole construct — not just the cone — is at elevated risk from infection, instability, or extensor mechanism complications.

**ARMD-specific:**
- Higher revision complication rates than routine aseptic revision due to soft-tissue/abductor destruction from the metal debris reaction itself.
- Elevated dislocation risk post-revision from compromised soft-tissue tensioning, and the need for close post-revision surveillance of metal ion trends to confirm resolution of the underlying process.

**Stiffness pathway:**
- Periprosthetic fracture during aggressive manipulation (rare but reported, particularly in osteoporotic bone or with excessive force applied through a long lever arm).
- Extensor mechanism injury (patellar tendon avulsion, quadriceps rupture) — the most feared complication of overly forceful manipulation.
- An association between prior MUA/lysis of adhesions and subsequent higher revision rates, suggesting the intervention itself is a marker of a more complex knee trajectory rather than a fully benign, isolated procedure — reinforcing the importance of identifying and correcting a structural cause when one is present rather than repeating closed interventions indefinitely.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[Open reduction and internal fixation offers lower hip-related complications compared to stem revision in Vancouver B2 fractures around cemented polished tapered femoral stems](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39957836/)** (2025) — a comparative cohort challenging the traditional B2-always-means-revision doctrine specifically for cemented polished tapered stems, finding fewer hip-related complications with ORIF in carefully selected cases; supports a more individualized, stem-design-specific approach to the B1/B2 boundary rather than a rigid rule.
- **[Comparative outcomes of uncemented and cemented stem revision in managing periprosthetic femoral fractures: a retrospective cohort study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11258106/)** (2024) — reports cemented revision stems with lower dislocation (8.9% vs 22.5%), lower stem loosening (0.6% vs 9.3%), and faster fracture healing (11.4 vs 16.7 weeks) than uncemented revision, informing stem-type selection in B2/B3 revision by patient bone quality and demand.
- **[Periprosthetic Fracture After Cementless Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty with a Tapered, Fluted Monobloc Stem: A Retrospective Long-Term Analysis of 121 Cases](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11989893/)** (2024) — long-term (up to 23.8 years) data reporting a cumulative 5.2% risk of subsequent periprosthetic fracture after monobloc tapered-fluted stem revision, providing durable benchmark survivorship data for this workhorse revision implant.
- **[Risk Factors and Predictors of 1-Year Mortality in 262 Vancouver Type C Periprosthetic Femoral Fractures: Insights from the PIPPAS Prospective Multicenter Observational Study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12428945/)** (2025) — the largest prospective multicenter dataset specifically on Vancouver C fractures, characterizing 1-year mortality predictors and reinforcing that even implant-uninvolved periprosthetic fractures require hip-fracture-level perioperative medical optimization.
- **[Periprosthetic Femur Fractures Managed by Revision Arthroplasty](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12317294/)** (2024–2025) — contemporary series detailing outcomes of revision arthroplasty as definitive management for periprosthetic femur fractures, adding to the evidence base guiding stem selection and reconstruction strategy in B2/B3 injuries.
- **[Condylar-constrained prostheses are associated with favourable implant survival following first-time aseptic revision knee arthroplasty: An analysis from the National Joint Registry](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.70548?af=R)** (2025) — large national-registry confirmation that CCK implants perform favorably as a first-line constraint step in aseptic revision TKA, supporting their place at the base of the constraint ladder before progressing to hinge.
- **[Survivorship and Complications in Revision Total Knee Arthroplasty With a Constrained Condylar Knee Implant: A Minimum 10-Year Follow-Up Study](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(25)00607-2/abstract)** (2025) — long-term (10–23 year) CCK survivorship data in 345 revision TKAs, with aseptic loosening, infection, malalignment, and instability as the leading indications for further revision — useful for counseling patients on durability expectations.
- **[Condylar constrained and rotating hinged implants in revision knee arthroplasty show similar survivorship and clinical outcome: a systematic review and meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37747534/)** (2023) — pooled analysis showing CCK and rotating-hinge implants achieve similar ~90% 5-year survivorship, supporting selection primarily by the degree of collateral ligament deficiency rather than a presumed durability advantage of one design.
- **[Incidence of constrained condylar and hinged knee implants and mid- to long-term survivorship: a register-based study from the Nordic Arthroplasty Register Association (NARA)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39912759/)** (2025) — Nordic registry data tracking utilization trends and mid/long-term survivorship of high-constraint implants across national registries, providing population-level benchmarking for the top of the constraint ladder.
- **[Dual Mobility vs Constrained Liners in Managing Instability After Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty: A Systematic Review and Exploratory Cost-Effectiveness Model](https://www.cureus.com/articles/482997-dual-mobility-vs-constrained-liners-in-managing-instability-after-revision-total-hip-arthroplasty-a-systematic-review-and-exploratory-cost-effectiveness-model)** (2025–2026) — systematic review of 2,046 revision hips (1,237 dual mobility, 809 constrained liner) finding dual mobility outperforms constrained liners on dislocation and re-revision outcomes; corroborated by a parallel 2024 meta-analysis of dual-mobility versus fixed-bearing components in revision THA, together supporting dual mobility as the preferred first escalation step for THA instability over constrained liners.
- **[The Chitranjan S. Ranawat Award: Manipulation Under Anesthesia to Treat Postoperative Stiffness After Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Multicenter Randomized Clinical Trial](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(24)00131-1/fulltext)** (2024) — award-winning multicenter RCT on adjuvant strategies to improve MUA outcomes, the highest-quality prospective evidence to date on optimizing this intervention.
- **[Failure Incidence and Predictors Following Manipulation Under Anesthesia for the Stiff Total Knee Arthroplasty](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(26)00248-2/abstract)** (2026) — identifies older age, cruciate-retaining implant design, and 2-week post-MUA ROM as independent predictors of MUA failure, refining patient counseling and follow-up triggers for escalation to aLOA.
- **[Manipulation under anaesthesia and/or lysis of adhesions prior to revision total knee arthroplasty increases the risk of subsequent revision](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.70211)** (2025) — signals that a history of MUA/lysis is itself a marker for a more complex, higher-revision-risk knee, reinforcing the need to identify and correct an underlying structural cause of stiffness rather than treating MUA as definitive.
- **[Porous Tantalum Tibial Metaphyseal Cones in Revision Total Knee Arthroplasty: Excellent 10-Year Survivorship](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(24)00382-6/fulltext)** (2024) — long-term data showing durable cone-specific fixation (implant-specific survivorship far exceeding overall reoperation-free survivorship of ~66% at 10 years); a companion 2024 mid-term series (100% cone-specific, 83.8% reoperation-free at 5 years) confirms cones are now the standard for AORI type 3 metaphyseal defects, with cone failure itself rare even when the broader reconstruction later requires reoperation.
- **[International Consensus Meeting on Orthopaedic Infection: Differences Between ICM 2018 and ICM 2025](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(25)01416-0/abstract)** (2025) — updates the diagnostic consensus criteria surgeons must apply before labeling any loose or painful implant "aseptic," directly relevant to the mandatory infection work-up preceding aseptic loosening/osteolysis revision in this cluster.
