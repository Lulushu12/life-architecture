## Quick Reference

**Femoral shaft fracture — treatment ladder**

| Line | Option | When |
|---|---|---|
| First-line | Reamed antegrade statically-locked IM nail | Essentially all skeletally mature diaphyseal fractures (AO/OTA 32-A/B/C), stable or resuscitated polytrauma |
| Bridge (damage control) | Temporary external fixator (bridging knee/hip if needed) | Physiologically unstable polytrauma ("borderline"/"in extremis"), severe soft-tissue injury, damage-control laparotomy pending |
| Alternative | Retrograde nail | Ipsilateral femoral neck fracture, ipsilateral acetabular/pelvic fracture needing supine positioning, floating knee, morbid obesity, bilateral simultaneous nailing, pregnancy |
| Alternative | Bridge/locked plating | Very proximal or very distal extension precluding nail interlock purchase, periprosthetic, pediatric/open physis |
| Non-operative | Traction ± spica cast | Essentially obsolete in adults; reserved for non-ambulatory patients, extreme unfitness for anesthesia, or resource-limited settings |

**Distal femur fracture — treatment ladder**

| Line | Option | When |
|---|---|---|
| First-line (extra-articular/simple articular, 33-A, 33-B, simple 33-C1) | Retrograde IM nail | Good metaphyseal bone stock, minimal comminution, allows earlier weight bearing |
| First-line (complex intra-articular / comminuted metaphyseal, 33-C2/C3) | Locked lateral distal femoral plate (LISS/anatomic locking plate) | Articular reconstruction needed, coronal (Hoffa) fragments, deficient medial column |
| Combined construct | Nail-plate ("nail-and-plate") or dual plating | Very short distal segment, severe metaphyseal comminution, periarticular nonunion risk, varus collapse risk in osteoporotic bone |
| Elderly/frail salvage | Distal femoral replacement (DFR, hinged prosthesis) | ≥ 65-75 y with severe comminution, osteoporosis, periprosthetic fracture around failed TKA, or fracture in a knee already destined for arthroplasty |
| Non-operative | Hinged brace + protected weight bearing | Truly nondisplaced, or patient unfit for any anesthesia/surgery (rare) |

**Bilateral femur fractures — key points**

- High-energy mechanism by definition; treat as **major trauma**, assume additional injuries (chest, pelvis, spine, abdominal) until excluded.
- Fat embolism syndrome incidence is disproportionately higher than unilateral fractures (reported ~4.8-7.5% vs ~1-3% for isolated shaft fractures).
- Early definitive fixation (<24 h) is associated with significantly lower FES risk (adjusted OR ≈0.42) in physiologically stable patients — reinforces early total care when the patient tolerates it.
- Sequential same-session bilateral reamed nailing is acceptable in a resuscitated, hemodynamically stable patient; damage-control staged approach (bilateral ex-fix → staged conversion) in the unstable/borderline patient.

**Red flags**

- Ipsilateral femoral neck fracture (missed in up to 1/3 of cases without dedicated fine-cut CT) — mandates screening pelvis/hip CT or fine-cut protocol in all femoral shaft fractures.
- Vascular injury: absent/asymmetric pulses, expanding hematoma, especially in distal-third shaft or supracondylar fractures near the adductor hiatus (popliteal artery tethered).
- Compartment syndrome of the thigh (less common than leg, but occurs, especially with vascular injury or prolonged traction).
- Open fracture — treat per BOAST/AO open fracture principles: IV antibiotics within 1 hour, photograph and cover, urgent debridement (ideally <12-24 h, combined ortho-plastics for Gustilo IIIB/C).
- Floating knee (ipsilateral femur + tibia fracture) — fix both, usually same anesthetic, nail-nail construct preferred when feasible.
- Elderly low-energy distal femur fracture = fragility fracture — treat with the same urgency/pathway as hip fracture (BOAST: within 36 h, orthogeriatric co-management).

**Key numbers**

- Acceptable coronal/sagittal alignment after femoral shaft nailing: <5-10° angulation, <10-15° malrotation, <1.5-2 cm shortening.
- Distal femur nail: minimum 2 interlocking screws (ideally divergent/multiplanar) in the distal fragment; nail should engage as much of the distal metaphysis as possible.
- Reported union rates with reamed antegrade nailing of closed diaphyseal fractures: **90-98%** (recent multicenter cohort: 96.7% at 6 months, 98.9% at 1 year); nonunion rates in larger series 4-8%, higher with unreamed nails, smoking, NSAID use, open fractures, high BMI, diabetes.
- Damage-control external fixation typically converted to definitive nailing within **7-14 days** once physiology normalizes (lactate normalized, base deficit corrected, coagulopathy resolved).
- Fat embolism syndrome: incidence ~1-7.9% after femoral shaft fracture; case-fatality up to ~10-17% when clinically manifest; risk reduced with early stabilization.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Femoral shaft.** Extends from 5 cm distal to the lesser trochanter to 5-8 cm proximal to the knee joint (the metaphyseal flare). It is the largest tubular bone, with a gentle anterior bow (radius of curvature ~120 cm) that must be respected by IM nail design — using an undersized or a straight nail in a bowed canal risks anterior cortical perforation. Blood supply is predominantly endosteal via the nutrient artery (a branch of the profunda femoris entering posteromedially) plus periosteal contribution; reaming temporarily disrupts endosteal flow but periosteal supply compensates and reaming stimulates osteogenic response (the "internal bone graft" effect), which is part of why reamed nailing achieves faster, more reliable union than unreamed nailing in closed diaphyseal fractures. The deforming muscular forces are classic and predictable: iliopsoas flexes/externally rotates the proximal fragment, abductors abduct it, adductors and hamstrings shorten and vary the distal fragment, gastrocnemius extends/flexes the distal fragment in supracondylar fractures producing apex-posterior angulation with the distal fragment tilted posteriorly by the gastrocnemius origin — this posterior sag is the hallmark deforming force in distal femur fractures and the main reason distal fixation must control the sagittal plane.

**Distal femur.** The metaphyseal-epiphyseal region is trapezoidal in the coronal plane (medial condyle distal and wider than lateral) and the articular surface is angled ~9-10° into valgus relative to the anatomic femoral shaft axis (mechanical axis roughly perpendicular to the joint line) — a critical reference for restoring the distal femoral valgus (anatomic-axis) angle intraoperatively (normal ~81° lateral distal femoral angle, LDFA, or equivalently ~7-9° of anatomic valgus). Coronal-plane (Hoffa) fragments occur because the posterior condyles are relatively unsupported and shear off in a coronal plane with axial load through a flexed knee — frequently missed on plain films, require CT for detection and preoperative planning. The popliteal artery is tethered at the adductor hiatus proximally and the soleal arch distally, making it vulnerable to stretch/injury in displaced supracondylar (33-A3) and complete articular (33-C) fractures — always document a careful vascular exam and have a low threshold for ankle-brachial index (ABI) or CT angiography if ABI <0.9 or exam is equivocal.

The genicular artery anastomotic ring (superior medial/lateral, inferior medial/lateral, and middle genicular branches off the popliteal artery, plus the descending genicular from the femoral artery) supplies the distal femoral epiphysis and condyles independent of the medullary/nutrient supply that dominates the shaft — this rich peri-articular collateral network is why isolated Hoffa fragments, though frequently comminuted, rarely progress to avascular necrosis if fixed promptly, and why extensive posterior/circumferential soft-tissue stripping around the condyles during open fixation should still be minimized wherever possible to protect this supply.

**Bilateral femur fractures** occur in 2-9.5% of femur fracture patients, essentially always high-energy (motor vehicle collision, fall from height, crush). Physiologically, bilateral long-bone fracture creates a larger embolic/inflammatory load (fat, marrow contents, inflammatory mediators — IL-6, complement activation) than a unilateral injury, which is the mechanistic basis for the higher observed rates of fat embolism syndrome and ARDS, and underlies the rationale for the "second hit" concept in damage-control decision-making. Associated injury patterns cluster predictably with the causative mechanism: dashboard-type frontal motor vehicle collisions producing bilateral femoral shaft fractures frequently co-occur with posterior wall/column acetabular fractures, patellar fractures, and posterior cruciate ligament injury from the knee striking the dashboard — all of which must be actively sought, not assumed absent, once the femurs are identified.

**Ipsilateral femoral neck + shaft fractures ("Hip-Shaft" pattern).** Occurs in roughly 1-9% of femoral shaft fractures, typically from an axial-load high-energy mechanism transmitted up the shaft. The femoral neck component is frequently vertically oriented (Pauwels III) and nondisplaced, which is precisely why it is easy to miss on a shaft-focused trauma series — the neck fracture is a stress riser that does not follow the classic Garden pattern of a low-energy fall. This anatomic reality is the direct justification for mandatory fine-cut CT or dedicated internal-rotation hip radiographs before any antegrade nail is reamed.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

- **Primary survey first** (ATLS) — femoral shaft fracture can cause 1-1.5 L of concealed blood loss per side (more if bilateral or open); do not attribute hemodynamic instability to the femur alone without excluding chest/abdomen/pelvis sources.
- **Full ipsilateral limb exam**: skin/soft-tissue (open wound classification, degloving, compartment tension), distal neurovascular status (peroneal/tibial function, pedal pulses, capillary refill, ABI if any asymmetry), knee ligamentous exam once fracture is provisionally splinted (occult ligament injury is common with distal femur/floating knee patterns but exam is often deferred until after fixation).
- **Screen for ipsilateral femoral neck fracture** in every femoral shaft fracture — historically missed in up to 19-31% of cases on initial films. Obtain a dedicated fine-cut (2 mm) CT of the femoral neck, or a true AP hip/pelvis film with internal rotation, in all shaft fractures before nailing; if using an antegrade start point, confirm neck integrity before reaming disrupts a nondisplaced neck fracture line.
- **Imaging**: AP/lateral full-length femur including hip and knee joints above and below. For distal femur fractures, add dedicated knee AP/lateral/oblique and CT with coronal/sagittal reconstruction to characterize articular comminution and coronal (Hoffa) fragments — CT changes management (fixation strategy, need for combined construct) in a substantial proportion of intra-articular cases and is considered standard of care pre-operatively for any AO/OTA 33-C fracture.
- **Bilateral injuries**: full trauma CT (pan-scan) is standard given mechanism; serial physiologic monitoring (lactate, base deficit, core temperature, coagulation panel, platelet count) to stage damage-control decision-making.
- **Compartment syndrome** of the thigh: clinical diagnosis (pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, tense compartments); thigh compartment pressures can be measured but threshold data are less robust than for the leg — maintain high suspicion with vascular injury, crush mechanism, or prolonged ischemia/traction time.
- **Baseline labs**: full blood count/hemoglobin (trend serially given the concealed blood loss potential), coagulation panel, group and save/crossmatch, lactate and base deficit (both to guide resuscitation and to inform damage-control staging decisions), renal function, and creatine kinase if a crush/prolonged extrication mechanism is present.
- **VTE risk stratification** on admission per institutional/national protocol (e.g., a validated trauma VTE risk score); femoral shaft and distal femur fractures are inherently high-risk injuries for VTE independent of any additional scoring.
- **Distal femur-specific exam**: assess knee effusion, patellar tracking, and ligamentous stability once the fracture is stabilized enough to permit safe stress testing — occult multi-ligament knee injury (including knee dislocation with spontaneously reduced popliteal artery injury) is under-recognized in high-energy supracondylar fracture patterns and changes both vascular surveillance intensity and eventual rehabilitation planning.
- **Bilateral femur fracture-specific**: because tourniquet/compression dressing decisions, positioning, and total blood loss estimation differ when both limbs are simultaneously injured, coordinate imaging, resuscitation targets, and OR sequencing as a single integrated trauma-team decision rather than treating each limb in isolation.

## Classification

### Winquist-Hansen Classification (femoral shaft comminution)

Describes the degree of cortical comminution at the fracture site and was historically used to guide reaming/interlocking strategy for IM nailing.

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Type 0 | No comminution (simple transverse/short oblique) | Stable after nailing; static or dynamic locking both acceptable |
| Type I | Minimal comminution, small butterfly fragment, >75-90% cortical contact maintained | Rotationally and axially stable; static locking still routine but dynamization can be considered later |
| Type II | Comminuted fragment ≤50% of the cortical circumference, 25-50% cortical contact | Some rotational instability; **static interlocking recommended** |
| Type III | Comminuted fragment involves 50-100% of the circumference, <25% cortical contact | Significant rotational and axial instability; **static interlocking mandatory**, larger diameter nail preferred |
| Type IV | Segmental comminution, no cortical contact between proximal and distal fragments | Nail alone bears full load; **static interlocking mandatory**, consider larger/stronger nail, watch closely for shortening/nonunion, exchange nailing or augmentation if healing stalls |

Practical takeaway: in modern practice essentially all femoral shaft fractures are statically locked regardless of Winquist type (dynamic locking primarily reserved for later dynamization of a healing but slow-progressing fracture), but Winquist-Hansen remains useful shorthand for communicating comminution severity, anticipated stability, and nonunion/shortening risk.

### AO/OTA Classification — 32 (Femoral Shaft) and 33 (Distal Femur)

**32 — Femoral diaphysis**

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 32-A | Simple fracture (spiral, oblique ≥30°, or transverse <30° obliquity) | Straightforward reamed nailing; excellent union expected |
| 32-B | Wedge fracture (spiral wedge, bending wedge, or fragmented wedge — wedge fragment(s) present but after reduction some cortical contact between main fragments is restorable) | Nail with static locking; wedge fragment left biologically undisturbed (indirect reduction) |
| 32-C | Complex fracture (spiral, segmental, or irregular — no cortical contact restorable between main fragments) | Static locking mandatory; higher nonunion/malalignment risk; consider larger nail diameter, blocking (Poller) screws for segmental patterns, and closer follow-up |

**33 — Distal femur**

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 33-A1 | Extra-articular, simple (two-part supracondylar) | Retrograde nail or locked plate; both effective; nail favored for its load-sharing biomechanics and faster mobilization when bone stock adequate |
| 33-A2 | Extra-articular, metaphyseal wedge fragment | Similar to A1; static locking/multiple distal interlocks recommended given comminution |
| 33-A3 | Extra-articular, metaphyseal complex/comminuted | Nail (if adequate distal purchase achievable) or locked plate; combined nail-plate if very short distal segment or gross instability |
| 33-B1 | Partial articular, lateral condyle (sagittal) | ORIF with lag screws ± buttress plate; anatomic articular reduction is the priority |
| 33-B2 | Partial articular, medial condyle (sagittal) | ORIF, medial plating/screws; assess for associated coronal shear |
| 33-B3 | Partial articular, coronal plane (Hoffa fragment) | Frequently missed on plain film; requires CT; fixed with anteroposterior lag screws (often headless compression screws) buried beneath cartilage, sometimes posterior buttress plate |
| 33-C1 | Complete articular, simple articular + simple metaphyseal | Reduce and provisionally fix articular block, then nail (retrograde) or plate the metaphyseal component |
| 33-C2 | Complete articular, simple articular + comminuted metaphyseal | Locked plate (LISS-type) most common; nail-plate combination increasingly used for added stability |
| 33-C3 | Complete articular, comminuted articular + comminuted metaphyseal | Most complex pattern; often requires combined nail-plate or dual-plate construct for stable fixation, especially with medial comminution/deficiency; in frail elderly with unreconstructable comminution, primary distal femoral replacement is a valid alternative to prolonged non-weight-bearing ORIF |

Selection principle across 33-A/B/C: articular reduction must be anatomic (≤1-2 mm step-off) regardless of fixation method chosen for the metaphyseal/diaphyseal component; the choice between nail, plate, or combined construct is driven primarily by metaphyseal comminution, bone quality, and the length/stability of the distal segment rather than by the articular pattern alone.

**Distal femur implant selection at a glance**

| Factor | Retrograde nail | Lateral locked plate | Nail-plate combined | Distal femoral replacement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Biomechanics | Load-sharing, central axis, good for axial/rotational control | Load-bearing/fixed-angle, superior articular reconstruction access | Load-sharing + fixed-angle; most rigid construct against varus collapse | Removes fixation biology question entirely; immediate mechanical stability |
| Best fracture pattern | 33-A, simple 33-C1 | 33-A3 with intact medial column, 33-B, complex articular needing open reduction | 33-C2/C3 with medial comminution, short distal segment, nonunion salvage | 33-C3 unreconstructable, periprosthetic (Su II/III with loose component), very frail bone |
| Typical early weight bearing | WBAT if stable construct | Often protected (toe-touch) 6-8 weeks if comminuted | Earlier progressive WB supported by added rigidity | Immediate full WB |
| Main technical risk | Malrotation, inadequate distal purchase in very short segments | Varus collapse with medial deficiency, malunion in comminution | Increased soft-tissue dissection/OR time vs single implant | Long-term revision burden, infection, dislocation |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- Adult femoral shaft and distal femur fractures are managed non-operatively only in narrow circumstances: patients who are non-ambulatory at baseline with minimal expected functional demand, patients medically unfit for any anesthesia even regional, or in resource-limited settings without surgical access — skeletal traction followed by cast-bracing or spica casting is the classic non-operative pathway but carries high rates of malunion, shortening, joint stiffness, and prolonged immobilization-related complications (DVT, pressure sores, deconditioning) and is not standard of care where surgical treatment is available.
- Truly nondisplaced/minimally displaced distal femur fractures (rare) can be managed in a hinged knee brace with protected, progressive weight bearing and close radiographic follow-up (weekly for the first 3-4 weeks) to detect early displacement, at which point conversion to surgical fixation is indicated.
- Nonoperative management is never appropriate for open fractures, fractures with vascular compromise, or floating knee patterns.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

Not applicable as a primary treatment modality for acute femoral shaft or distal femur fractures. The relevant adjunctive procedures are peri-fracture pain-control blocks (fascia iliaca compartment block or femoral nerve block for pre-operative analgesia in shaft fractures) rather than intra-articular or biologic injections; these are analgesic adjuncts, not fracture treatments, and are covered under perioperative pain management rather than as a distinct treatment line.

### Operative

**Femoral shaft fracture — reamed antegrade nailing (workhorse technique)**

- **Positioning/start point**: supine on a fracture table (traction) or lateral decubitus; piriformis fossa or greater trochanteric tip start point — trochanteric entry nails have a lateral bow matched to the proximal femur and reduce the risk of iatrogenic varus malreduction and femoral neck fracture compared with piriformis entry, and are generally preferred, especially in patients with a narrow proximal canal or concomitant femoral neck fracture requiring cephalomedullary fixation.
- **Reaming**: sequential reaming to 1-1.5 mm over the selected nail diameter improves endosteal contact, allows a larger/stronger nail, and is associated with higher union rates and lower nonunion/implant-failure rates than unreamed nailing in closed diaphyseal fractures — this is one of the most consistent findings in the femoral nailing literature. Unreamed nailing is reserved for specific damage-control or severe pulmonary-compromise scenarios (see below) given theoretical concerns about fat/marrow embolization during reaming, though this concern is now considered largely manageable with reaming irrigator-aspirator (RIA) systems that reduce intramedullary pressure and embolic load.
- **Reduction technique**: traction-table longitudinal traction for most fractures; for segmental or comminuted (Winquist III/IV) patterns, manual/semi-open reduction with a reduction (ball-spike, femoral distractor, or provisional plate) technique or blocking (Poller) screws to control alignment before nail passage, since traction alone may not correct rotational or coronal deformity in comminuted zones.
- **Locking**: static interlocking (proximal and distal) is now standard for essentially all closed diaphyseal nailings regardless of comminution grade — it controls rotation and length and is not associated with meaningfully higher nonunion rates than dynamic locking when reaming is adequate; dynamization (removal of one set of interlocking screws) is reserved for delayed union without progression at 3-6 months on serial films, not performed prophylactically.
- **Fluoroscopic rotational assessment**: compare cortical diameter ratios (lesser trochanter profile) or use the "perfect circle" technique of the contralateral distal femur intraoperatively; malrotation >15° is symptomatic and a recognized, largely preventable complication.
- **Pearls**: confirm the guidewire and reamers track the anterior bow of the femur (avoid anterior perforation, particularly with long or stiff reamers in short-statured patients); check leg length and rotation against the contralateral limb before final locking; obtain full-length fluoroscopic or radiographic confirmation of alignment before leaving the OR.
- **Pitfalls**: missed ipsilateral femoral neck fracture (screen with CT before reaming/nailing); malrotation (most common preventable technical error); anterior cortical perforation with mismatched nail bow; iatrogenic comminution at the entry site with trochanteric start points if guidewire placement is imprecise.
- **Anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients**: femoral shaft fixation is not a "life-over-limb" emergency in most closed injuries and can typically be delayed 24-48 h to allow reversal or clearance of anticoagulation (per agent-specific reversal protocols) without meaningfully worsening outcome, provided the limb is well splinted/traction-applied and neurovascular status is monitored; open fractures, vascular compromise, or compartment syndrome override this and require urgent intervention with multidisciplinary hematology/anesthesia input on reversal strategy.

**Distal femur fracture — nail vs plate vs combined construct**

*Retrograde intramedullary nailing.* Indicated for extra-articular (33-A) and simple articular (33-C1, occasionally C2) fractures with adequate distal bone stock to accept multiple interlocking screws and enough intact metaphysis for the nail to gain fixed-angle-like stability through its screws. Advantages: load-sharing (favorable in comminuted/osteoporotic diaphyseal-metaphyseal junction fractures, ipsilateral shaft extension, floating knee, bilateral injuries needing rapid sequential fixation), smaller incision, less soft-tissue stripping, earlier comfortable weight bearing in many series. Entry point is just anterior to the PCL origin, in line with Blumensaat's line on the lateral view; the joint is violated, so this is generally avoided (or used cautiously) with active septic arthritis, and requires reasonably preserved articular anatomy to allow safe passage. Requires a minimum of 2 (ideally more, in divergent/multiplanar configuration using modern multi-planar distal interlocking nails) screws for rotational and coronal control.

*Locked lateral distal femoral plating (LISS/anatomic locking plate).* Indicated for comminuted metaphyseal (33-A3) and, especially, complex intra-articular fractures (33-C2/C3) where articular reconstruction with lag screws must precede metaphyseal bridging, and where a nail cannot achieve adequate distal fixation because the distal segment is too short or too comminuted. Locking-plate technology (fixed-angle screws) restored favorable outcomes compared with older non-locking condylar plates/blade plates, particularly in osteoporotic bone, by resisting the classic varus-collapse failure mode. Submuscular, minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (MIPO) technique preserves the biology of the comminuted metaphyseal zone by indirect reduction and bridging rather than anatomic exposure of every fragment. Medial column comminution/void is the Achilles heel of isolated lateral locked plating — a deficient medial buttress predisposes to varus collapse and nonunion even with a locked lateral plate, which is precisely the scenario that favors either a medial-assist construct (medial plate or nail-plate combination) or primary bone grafting of the medial defect.

*Combined nail-plate ("nail-and-plate") construct.* Increasingly used and increasingly supported by comparative series (2024-2025 literature) for the most demanding patterns: very short/comminuted distal segments (33-C2/C3), periprosthetic distal femur fractures with poor bone stock, and fractures at high risk of varus collapse or nonunion with a single-implant strategy. Technique: a retrograde nail (often shorter, "mini" or standard length) placed first for central/axial and rotational control, augmented with a lateral locking plate placed for additional coronal-plane rigidity, particularly countering medial comminution. Comparative studies (2024-2025, including a multicenter study of AO 33-C fractures) show nail-plate constructs achieve comparable or superior knee alignment, functional outcomes, and quality-of-life scores compared with dual (medial + lateral) plating, with some series favoring nail-plate for lower operative time/blood loss versus dual plating, while others show dual plating providing similarly robust mechanical control — the evidence base (multiple 2024-2025 comparative series, still mostly retrospective) increasingly supports nail-plate as at least equivalent, and often preferred, for AO/OTA 33-C fractures with severe comminution or medial bone loss. Selection rationale: **nail alone** for simple to moderately comminuted extra-/simple-intra-articular fractures with good bone stock; **plate alone** for complex intra-articular fractures needing precise articular reconstruction, especially with adequate medial column integrity; **nail-plate combined** for the most unstable, comminuted, osteoporotic, or medial-deficient patterns, or in periarticular nonunion salvage.

**Perioperative and blood management considerations**

- **Tranexamic acid (TXA)**: increasingly used as an adjunct in femoral fracture surgery (IV at induction ± topical at closure) to reduce blood loss and transfusion requirement, extrapolated from strong evidence in hip and knee arthroplasty and supported by pragmatic trauma trials; contraindications (active thromboembolic disease, seizure disorder at high dose) should be screened as in any TXA protocol.
- **Anesthesia choice**: general anesthesia is standard for fracture-table antegrade nailing; regional/neuraxial techniques combined with peripheral nerve blocks (fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block) are valuable adjuncts for perioperative analgesia and opioid-sparing, particularly relevant in elderly distal femur fracture and DFR patients where minimizing systemic opioid load reduces delirium risk.
- **Positioning-related risk**: fracture-table traction carries a recognized, largely preventable risk of pudendal nerve palsy and perineal soft-tissue injury from the perineal post — use a well-padded post, limit traction time, and consider a flat-table (radiolucent table without traction) or lateral technique in prolonged or difficult reductions.
- **Blood loss expectations**: closed femoral shaft fracture typically 1-1.5 L of blood loss (more if open, bilateral, or associated with vascular injury); distal femur fracture fixation blood loss is generally lower for nailing than for open plating of comminuted patterns, which is one practical factor favoring nailing when the fracture pattern allows either option.

**Damage-control timing in polytrauma**

- Concept: in a physiologically unstable or "borderline" polytrauma patient, definitive prolonged femoral nailing (with reaming, embolic load, and operative time/blood loss) can act as a "second hit," worsening the systemic inflammatory response, coagulopathy, and risk of ARDS/multi-organ failure. Damage-control orthopedics (DCO) substitutes rapid, low-physiologic-cost temporary external fixation for definitive fixation, deferring the latter until the patient is resuscitated.
- **Classification of patient status** (commonly used framework): stable → early total care (ETC, definitive nailing within 24 h reasonable); borderline (multiple long-bone + thoracic/abdominal injury, hypotension responsive to resuscitation, coagulopathy, hypothermia, lactate/base-deficit not yet normalized) → favor DCO; unstable/in extremis (ongoing hemodynamic instability, severe coagulopathy, severe hypothermia <32-35°C, lactate persistently elevated) → DCO mandatory, resuscitation and hemorrhage control take priority over any long-bone fixation beyond splinting/external fixation.
- **Technique**: spanning or non-spanning external fixator applied rapidly (typically <30-45 minutes), restoring gross length/alignment, allowing ICU resuscitation, imaging, and further surgery (laparotomy, thoracotomy, neurosurgery) to proceed without the femur competing for OR time or physiologic reserve.
- **Conversion to definitive fixation**: once physiologic parameters normalize (lactate <2-2.5 mmol/L, base deficit corrected, core temperature normal, coagulopathy resolved, typically within the first several days), convert to reamed IM nailing — most literature and practice favor conversion within roughly **day 2-14**, with many centers targeting under 2 weeks to minimize pin-tract infection risk, which rises appreciably with external fixator dwell time beyond 2-3 weeks.
- **Current evidence position (2023-2025)**: the DCO-versus-ETC debate remains unresolved by high-quality randomized evidence; current literature (including a 2024 study using a porcine polytrauma model) suggests DCO induces less trauma-induced coagulopathy than ETC, and a 2025 TraumaRegister DGU propensity-matched analysis reassessed external fixation's role in polytrauma patients with concomitant chest trauma and femur fracture, generally supporting a physiology-adapted (not protocol-rigid) approach — the operative decision should be individualized to the patient's real-time physiologic status rather than applied as a blanket rule based on injury severity score alone.
- **Bilateral femur fractures specifically**: in a resuscitated, hemodynamically stable patient, same-session bilateral reamed antegrade nailing is safe and preferred (avoids a second anesthetic, allows symmetric early mobilization); in the unstable/borderline patient, bilateral damage-control external fixation followed by staged sequential (or simultaneous, if physiology allows) conversion nailing is the appropriate strategy. Early definitive stabilization (<24 h) when tolerated is associated with a significantly lower risk of fat embolism syndrome.

**Distal femoral replacement (DFR) in the elderly**

- Indication: elderly (generally ≥65-75 years, individualized by physiologic age/bone quality) patients with severely comminuted, unreconstructable native distal femur fractures (33-C3), particularly with osteoporotic bone unlikely to hold fixed-angle implants reliably; periprosthetic distal femur fractures around a failed or loose total knee arthroplasty where revision arthroplasty is indicated anyway; and salvage after failed ORIF/nonunion in an elderly patient.
- Rationale: DFR (a hinged distal femoral endoprosthesis, using the same implant families as oncologic megaprosthesis reconstruction) allows immediate full weight bearing, avoiding the prolonged partial/non-weight-bearing period required after ORIF, which in a frail elderly population carries substantial risk of deconditioning, loss of independence, pneumonia, and mortality — analogous to the rationale for arthroplasty over fixation in displaced femoral neck fractures in the elderly.
- Recent evidence (2024-2026): a systematic review and meta-analysis comparing **primary DFR** (performed as the index procedure) versus **salvage DFR** (performed after failed ORIF) found primary DFR patients were older (mean ~79 vs ~65 years) and more comorbid, yet primary DFR was associated with significantly **lower reoperation** (12.2% vs 23.5%) **and complication rates** (15.7% vs 43.1%) compared with salvage DFR — supporting a lower threshold to proceed directly to DFR in appropriately selected frail elderly patients with unreconstructable fractures rather than attempting ORIF first and reserving DFR for failure. A 2025 five-year district general hospital cohort (24 patients, native and periprosthetic) reported DFR safely deliverable outside tertiary/oncologic centers, with surgical site infection in 25%, 90-day mortality 8.3%, and 1-year mortality 16.7% — comparable to published tertiary-center data and to hip fracture mortality benchmarks, reinforcing that these patients need the same orthogeriatric, MDT-based perioperative pathway as fragility hip fractures.
- Technical/selection pearls: preserve as much of the collateral ligament and extensor mechanism attachments as possible; address leg-length and rotational alignment intraoperatively as in any hinged knee arthroplasty; involve orthogeriatrics/anesthesia early given the comorbidity burden of this population; counsel patients and families that DFR trades a shorter, higher-mortality-risk-adjusted recovery for a permanent large metal implant with its own long-term revision burden (aseptic loosening, infection, periprosthetic fracture around the stem).
- **Periprosthetic distal femur fractures around TKA** are conceptually staged by the Su classification (Type I: fracture proximal to the femoral component, treat as a standard distal femur fracture; Type II: fracture originating at or near the proximal edge of the femoral component, most challenging for fixation given limited distal purchase; Type III: fracture at or distal to the component with the component itself in question) — Type II/III patterns with a loose or worn component, and Type II/III in a low-demand elderly patient with poor bone stock, are the group in whom revision to a hinged distal femoral replacement (rather than ORIF around a retained well-fixed component) is most strongly favored.

**Vascular injury with distal femur fracture**

- A hard sign of vascular injury (absent pulse, active hemorrhage, expanding hematoma, palpable thrill/bruit, or a cold pulseless limb) mandates immediate vascular surgery involvement and does not wait for definitive fracture fixation — temporary intraluminal shunting to restore perfusion followed by rapid skeletal stabilization (external fixator, not a lengthy definitive nailing/plating procedure) to protect the vascular repair, then formal vascular reconstruction, is the standard damage-control sequence. Warm ischemia time beyond roughly 6 hours substantially increases amputation risk, so sequencing must prioritize speed of revascularization over anatomic perfection of the initial fixation.
- Soft signs (diminished but present pulse, small stable hematoma, peripheral nerve deficit in the distribution of an adjacent nerve) warrant ABI measurement; ABI <0.9 or asymmetry between limbs should prompt CT angiography even without hard signs, given the recognized association between displaced supracondylar/distal femur fracture patterns and popliteal artery stretch injury.

**Floating knee and combined open injuries**

- **Floating knee** (ipsilateral femoral and tibial fracture) mandates fixation of both bones, generally under the same anesthetic once physiology allows — antegrade femoral nail plus tibial nail ("nail-nail") is the most biomechanically favorable combination when both fracture patterns permit it; retrograde femoral nailing is a practical alternative that allows a single incision/positioning strategy with the tibial nail. Functional outcomes are driven more by the articular (knee/ankle) injury components than by the diaphyseal fixation choice, so any intra-articular extension at either the knee or the ankle must be identified and anatomically reduced.
- **Open femoral shaft/distal femur fractures**: manage per BOAST/AO open fracture principles — IV antibiotics within 1 hour of injury (typically a first-generation cephalosporin, with gram-negative and anaerobic coverage added for higher Gustilo grades or heavily contaminated wounds), photograph the wound and cover with a saline-soaked dressing rather than repeated exposure, provisional splinting/realignment, and formal debridement performed urgently (same-day, ideally within 12-24 h) rather than as an emergency in the middle of the night unless grossly contaminated or associated with vascular compromise. Gustilo-Anderson IIIB/C injuries require a combined orthoplastic approach at a center with soft-tissue coverage capability, per BOAST Open Fracture Management standards. Definitive fixation (reamed nailing) can generally proceed at the index or an early staged debridement once the wound is judged adequately clean; damage-control external fixation is used when definitive fixation must be deferred for soft-tissue or physiologic reasons.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Femoral shaft fracture (post reamed statically locked nail)**

- **0-2 weeks**: mobilize out of bed day 1 post-op when medically stable; weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is standard after a statically locked reamed nail in a simple-to-moderately comminuted pattern (Winquist 0-II); protected/toe-touch weight bearing considered for Winquist III-IV or segmental patterns with tenuous fixation, per surgeon judgment and intraoperative stability assessment. Early hip and knee active-assisted ROM, quadriceps sets, ankle pumps for DVT prophylaxis alongside pharmacologic prophylaxis (LMWH or equivalent per institutional VTE protocol).
- **2-6 weeks**: progress weight bearing to full as tolerated by pain/comfort in most patterns; gait training progressing from walker/crutches to cane; active ROM hip/knee, closed-chain strengthening as tolerated; radiographic check at ~6 weeks to assess early callus.
- **6-12 weeks**: progressive resistive strengthening of hip abductors/quadriceps/hamstrings; proprioceptive and balance training; single-leg stance work; radiographic union typically progressing, with most simple patterns showing solid bridging callus by 12-16 weeks.
- **3-6 months**: return to full unrestricted activity, running, and impact sport generally deferred until radiographic union (bridging callus on ≥3 of 4 cortices) and clinical union (painless full weight bearing, no motion/tenderness at fracture site), typically achieved by 4-6 months in uncomplicated cases (union rates ~90-98% by 6-12 months in reamed nailing series).
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often possible by 6-8 weeks; physically demanding/manual labor typically requires 4-6+ months and confirmed radiographic union.
- **Return to sport**: pivoting/contact sport deferred until full radiographic union and restoration of at least ~90% of contralateral strength on isokinetic or functional testing, typically 5-9 months.

**Distal femur fracture (post nail, plate, or nail-plate)**

- **0-2 weeks**: knee ROM started early (0-90°) when fixation stability allows, to prevent arthrofibrosis — this is the single highest-yield rehab intervention for distal femur fractures given the joint's propensity to stiffen; weight-bearing status is implant- and comminution-dependent — many stable retrograde-nail constructs allow WBAT, whereas comminuted metaphyseal patterns fixed with a single lateral locked plate are often kept touch-down/toe-touch weight bearing (10-15 kg) for 6-8 weeks given documented varus-collapse risk with early loading of comminuted, medially-deficient constructs; nail-plate combined constructs, by virtue of added stability, increasingly allow earlier progressive weight bearing in several 2024-2025 series.
- **2-6 weeks**: progressive knee flexion toward 90-110°, quadriceps activation (initially isometric, progressing to active), patellar mobilization to prevent adhesion; continue protected weight bearing per fracture pattern/construct until radiographic evidence of healing progression.
- **6-12 weeks**: progress weight bearing to full as callus/healing progresses on serial radiographs (typically begun around 6-8 weeks for comminuted patterns, earlier for simple patterns or DFR); continue ROM progression toward functional range (generally ≥110-120° flexion); begin closed-chain strengthening.
- **3-6 months**: full weight bearing, strengthening progression, gait normalization; union in distal femur fractures is generally slower and less certain than shaft fractures — union rates in comparative nail vs plate series cluster around 85-95%, with nonunion more frequent after plating of severely comminuted patterns without adequate medial support (reported nonunion 10-17% in some nail vs plate comparative cohorts, higher for plate-only constructs with medial deficiency).
- **Return to work**: sedentary 6-10 weeks; manual labor 4-6+ months, contingent on both union and restoration of functional knee ROM/strength.
- **Return to sport**: high-demand/pivoting sport generally deferred 6-9 months and contingent on radiographic union, painless full ROM, and quadriceps/hamstring strength ≥85-90% of the contralateral limb.

**Distal femoral replacement (DFR)**

- Immediate full weight bearing as tolerated is the principal advantage of DFR and should be encouraged from day 1-2 post-op as pain and medical status allow, following standard hinged total knee arthroplasty rehabilitation protocols (early ROM, quadriceps activation, gait training with walker progressing to cane).
- Orthogeriatric/MDT perioperative pathway mirrors hip fracture care (same-day/next-day mobilization, delirium prevention, nutrition, bone-health/osteoporosis treatment initiation, falls assessment) given the shared frail elderly population and comparable mortality risk profile.

**Bilateral femur fractures**

- Rehabilitation follows the same principles as unilateral injury but requires bilateral simultaneous mobilization planning (bilateral walker/frame use, increased physiotherapy intensity, higher fall risk) and closer monitoring for the systemic complications (FES, ARDS, VTE) to which this cohort is disproportionately exposed in the first 48-72 hours.
- Standing/transfer training typically requires two physiotherapists or a hoist in the first 1-2 weeks given the absence of a "good leg" to pivot on; a wheeled frame or platform walker is often more practical initially than standard crutches, and discharge planning should anticipate a longer inpatient rehabilitation stay than an equivalent unilateral injury.

**Physiotherapy content common to all diagnoses**

- **Early phase**: cryotherapy/edema control, ankle pump and calf-activation exercises for VTE prophylaxis, quadriceps and gluteal isometric sets, patellar mobilization (distal femur), respiratory physiotherapy in polytrauma/bedbound patients to reduce pneumonia risk.
- **Mid phase**: progressive open- and closed-chain strengthening (quadriceps, hamstrings, hip abductors/extensors), stationary cycling once flexion allows, gait re-education focusing on symmetric stance time and elimination of compensatory Trendelenburg/hip-hike patterns, proprioceptive/balance training on progressively unstable surfaces.
- **Late phase**: functional and sport-specific drills (step-downs, single-leg squat, lateral agility), plyometric progression only after strength and union milestones are met, formal isokinetic or functional hop/strength testing before clearance to pivoting sport or heavy manual labor.

## Complications

- **Nonunion/delayed union**: femoral shaft ~4-8% after reamed nailing (higher with unreamed nailing, smoking, NSAID use, high BMI, diabetes, open fracture, inadequate reduction/high-energy comminution); distal femur nonunion reported higher, roughly 10-17% in some nail-vs-plate comparative series, particularly with plate-only fixation of medially-comminuted patterns. Management: exchange reamed nailing (shaft), or revision fixation with biologic augmentation/bone grafting ± conversion to a more stable (e.g., nail-plate) construct (distal femur); persistent atrophic nonunion may need vascularized or structural autograft.
- **Malunion**: malrotation is the most common preventable technical complication of femoral nailing (>15° is clinically apparent and symptomatic); coronal/sagittal malalignment more common in distal femur fractures owing to the deforming gastrocnemius/adductor forces — meticulous intraoperative alignment checks (fluoroscopic cortical-diameter/"perfect circle" technique, contralateral-limb comparison) are the primary prevention strategy.
- **Infection**: deep infection after closed femoral shaft nailing is low (well under 1-2% in most series); rates rise substantially with open fracture severity (low single digits for Gustilo I-II, into the double digits for IIIB/C) and with damage-control staged protocols with prolonged external fixator dwell time (pin-tract infection risk rises notably beyond 2-3 weeks); deep infection after nailing or plating requires debridement, culture-directed antibiotics, and implant retention vs staged exchange depending on stability and chronicity.
- **Fat embolism syndrome / ARDS**: incidence ~1-7.9% after femoral shaft fracture, higher (up to ~7.5%) after bilateral fractures; classic triad of hypoxemia, neurologic changes, and petechial rash; case-fatality reported up to ~10-17% in symptomatic cases. Early fracture stabilization (<24 h in the physiologically tolerant patient) is protective.
- **VTE (DVT/PE)**: femoral fractures are high-risk for VTE; mechanical (IPC) plus pharmacologic prophylaxis per institutional/national protocol from admission through the immobile period.
- **Vascular injury**: popliteal artery injury most feared with displaced supracondylar/distal femur fractures; missed injury risks limb loss — maintain vigilance with serial vascular exams/ABI.
- **Knee stiffness/arthrofibrosis**: the most common functional complication after distal femur fracture fixation; driven by intra-articular injury itself, prolonged immobilization, and quadriceps scarring to the fracture site — mitigated by early, protected ROM and aggressive physiotherapy engagement.
- **Post-traumatic osteoarthritis**: expected sequela of any intra-articular (33-B/C) fracture proportional to residual articular incongruity; anatomic articular reduction is the single best modifiable prevention factor.
- **Implant failure**: varus collapse of isolated lateral locked plates in medially-deficient comminuted distal femur fractures is a well-recognized, largely construct-driven failure mode — mitigated by nail-plate/dual-plate constructs or medial bone grafting in high-risk patterns.
- **Periprosthetic fracture around DFR/TKA stems**: a long-term risk of any endoprosthetic reconstruction; requires the same vigilance and surgical planning as any periprosthetic fracture.
- **Leg-length discrepancy**: can occur with comminuted patterns treated by nailing without careful intraoperative length restoration (traction table over-distraction or under-reduction); confirm against contralateral limb intraoperatively.
- **Heterotopic ossification**: reported at low but nonzero rates after distal femur fixation, particularly with extensive quadriceps/vastus lateralis dissection; minimally invasive plating technique reduces this risk relative to extensile open approaches.
- **Missed femoral neck fracture** progressing to displacement, avascular necrosis, or nonunion of the neck if not identified before antegrade nail reaming — the single most consequential "avoidable" complication discussed in this monograph, reinforcing the mandatory pre-nailing neck-screening step described above.
- **Systemic complications of prolonged immobility/damage-control staging**: pin-tract infection with prolonged external fixator dwell (rises appreciably beyond 2-3 weeks), pneumonia, pressure injury, and deconditioning — all argue for converting external fixation to definitive fixation as soon as physiology allows rather than defaulting to a fixed calendar-day protocol.

**Exchange nailing for femoral shaft nonunion.** When a diaphyseal fracture fails to progress radiographically by 6 months (or shows no interval change on two consecutive visits 6-8 weeks apart), exchange reamed nailing is the first-line surgical salvage for a well-aligned, hypertrophic/oligotrophic nonunion: remove the existing nail, ream sequentially to 1-2 mm larger than the prior nail diameter to maximize endosteal stimulation and mechanical fill, and place a larger-diameter statically locked nail. Reported union rates after exchange nailing for femoral shaft nonunion are favorable (commonly cited in the 80-90% range) when the nonunion is aligned and infection has been excluded; a rotationally or angularly malaligned nonunion, an infected nonunion, or an atrophic nonunion with bone loss instead requires deformity correction, infection eradication (staged with an antibiotic spacer if septic), and often adjunctive bone grafting (autograft, RIA-harvested graft, or vascularized fibula for large segmental defects) rather than exchange nailing alone.

## Recent Evidence

- **2025 — Retrospective multicenter cohort, reamed IM nailing of femoral diaphyseal fractures** ([PMC12029105](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12029105/)): 91 patients across two centers (2020-2024) treated with IM nailing achieved a 96.7% bone-healing rate within 6 months and 98.9% within 1 year, with a low complication rate (3.26%) — reinforces reamed antegrade nailing as a highly reliable first-line treatment and provides contemporary real-world union benchmarks for patient counseling.
- **2024 — 200-patient cohort on nonunion risk factors after IM nailing** (referenced via search of femoral nailing literature): reported an 8% nonunion incidence with identified risk factors including higher BMI, diabetes mellitus, NSAID use, unreamed nailing, and delayed weight-bearing initiation — directly supports the practice of reamed nailing plus early weight bearing and NSAID avoidance/limitation in the perioperative period to reduce nonunion risk.
- **2025 — Systematic review/meta-analysis, retrograde IM nailing vs locking plate for distal femur fractures**, *Orthopedic Reviews* ([openmedicalpublishing.org](https://orthopedicreviews.openmedicalpublishing.org/article/140710)): pooled three comparative studies (345 patients, 2022-2025 window) found nonunion in 17/173 (nail) vs 29/172 (plate), with a pooled estimate favoring nailing though not reaching statistical certainty — signals a possible union advantage for nailing in appropriately selected distal femur fractures but underscores the continued lack of high-quality RCT evidence, which should temper firm guideline-level recommendations either way.
- **2025 — Nail-plate combination vs single traditional constructs, systematic review/meta-analysis**, *Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery* ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00402-024-05723-6)): synthesizes comparative data supporting nail-plate combined constructs as at least equivalent, and in several outcome domains superior, to single-implant fixation for complex distal femur fractures — an important shift toward combined-construct thinking for AO/OTA 33-C patterns with comminution or medial deficiency.
- **2025 — Multicenter study, double plating vs nail-plate construct in AO 33-C distal femur fractures** ([PMC11965052](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11965052/); also published in *Journal of Orthopaedics and Traumatology*, [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s10195-025-00834-1)): treatment choice (double plating vs nail-plate) meaningfully affected knee alignment, clinical outcomes, and quality of life — provides some of the first robust multicenter comparative data directly informing construct selection in the most complex distal femur fracture subgroup.
- **2025 — Comparison of combined femoral nail-plate fixation vs dual-plate fixation in AO/OTA 33-C fractures**, *Scientific Reports* ([nature.com](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14999-6); also PMC12331908, [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12331908/)): 78 patients (2017-2022) compared nail-plate (n=42) vs dual-plate (n=36) constructs across operative time, blood loss, time to weight-bearing, union time, and functional/complication outcomes — adds further comparative granularity supporting nail-plate as a technically efficient alternative to dual plating for the most comminuted intra-articular distal femur fractures.
- **2026 — Systematic review/meta-analysis, primary vs salvage distal femoral endoprosthetic replacement (DFR) following native distal femur fracture** ([PMC12809088](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12809088/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352344125003267)): 12 studies, 281 patients (230 primary DFR, 51 salvage DFR); primary DFR patients were older (mean 79.3 vs 64.9 years) and more comorbid, yet primary DFR showed significantly lower reoperation (12.2% vs 23.5%) and complication rates (15.7% vs 43.1%) than salvage DFR performed after failed ORIF — direct evidence supporting earlier, primary use of DFR in appropriately selected frail elderly patients with unreconstructable distal femur fractures rather than defaulting to ORIF-first.
- **2025 — Five-year retrospective cohort, DFR for fragility and periprosthetic fractures in a district general hospital setting** ([PMC12718647](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12718647/)): 24 patients (2019-2024, native and periprosthetic distal femur fractures) treated with DFR outside a tertiary/oncologic center; 25% surgical site infection, 8.3% 90-day mortality, 16.7% 1-year mortality — comparable to tertiary-center benchmarks, supporting the feasibility of DFR programs in non-specialist trauma units provided appropriate orthogeriatric perioperative pathways are in place.
- **2024 (PMC10728770 / PubMed 38116023) — Distal femur replacement as an option for osteoporotic fractures in the elderly** ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10728770/), [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38116023/)): reinforces the rationale for DFR over fixation in severely osteoporotic distal femur fractures, emphasizing that DFR permits immediate weight bearing and faster functional recovery compared with fixation strategies that mandate protected weight bearing — a key counseling point for frail patients where prolonged restricted weight bearing carries its own mortality/morbidity risk.
- **2024 — Porcine polytrauma model, damage control orthopedics vs early total care and trauma-induced coagulopathy**: found DCO induced less trauma-induced coagulopathy than ETC, providing mechanistic support for physiology-adapted staged management in the borderline/unstable polytrauma patient with femoral fracture — adds experimental mechanistic weight to the clinical DCO literature, though translation to a firm clinical threshold remains an area of ongoing study.
- **2025 — TraumaRegister DGU propensity-matched analysis reassessing external fixation in polytrauma with chest trauma and femur fracture** ([PubMed 40986042](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40986042/)): large registry-based propensity-matched analysis re-examining the role of external fixation (damage control) specifically in severely injured patients with concomitant chest trauma and femur fracture — contemporary registry evidence refining which polytrauma subgroups derive the clearest benefit from a staged DCO strategy versus early definitive fixation.
- **2025-2026 — Fat embolism syndrome incidence/mortality in long bone fractures, trauma referral center cohort (Bogotá)** ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41925786/); [Springer/European Journal of Trauma and Emergency Surgery](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00068-026-03104-9)): 3,475 patients with long bone fractures (2016-2019 and 2022-2023 cohorts) found FES incidence 4.3%, with 5.4% in-hospital mortality among affected patients — contemporary, large-cohort quantification of FES risk that supports continued emphasis on early stabilization protocols, particularly for bilateral/multiple long-bone injury.
- **2025 — Emerging trends in distal femur fracture treatment modalities, narrative review** ([PMC11907132](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11907132/)): synthesizes the shift in distal femur fracture management toward individualized construct selection (nail, plate, combined nail-plate, or arthroplasty) based on comminution pattern, bone quality, and patient physiologic reserve rather than a one-size-fits-all implant choice — consolidates the practice-pattern evolution reflected across the other 2024-2025 comparative studies above.
