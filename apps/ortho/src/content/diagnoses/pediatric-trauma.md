## Quick Reference

**Elbow ossification order (CRITOE)** — Capitellum (1y), Radial head (3-5y), Internal/medial epicondyle (5-7y), Trochlea (9-10y), Olecranon (9-10y), External/lateral epicondyle (10-11y). Use to avoid mistaking an avulsed medial epicondyle for a loose body in the joint on a trans-positioned film.

**Key numeric thresholds**

| Parameter | Normal / threshold | Clinical use |
|---|---|---|
| Baumann angle (humerocapitellar angle) | 64–81°, side-to-side difference <5° | Coronal-plane reduction check in supracondylar fx |
| Anterior humeral line | Passes through middle third of capitellum | Screening for extension-type supracondylar fx on lateral film |
| Carrying angle | 5–15° valgus (boys < girls) | Baseline for detecting cubitus varus |
| Lateral condyle displacement | ≤2 mm = nonop; >2 mm = operative | Milch/Jakob-based decision point |
| Radial neck angulation (Judet) | ≤30° generally accepted after closed means; up to 45–60° tolerated in <10y with good motion | Threshold for percutaneous vs open |
| Tibial spine displacement | Meyers-McKeever II reducible on extension = nonop; ≥III or block to reduction = operative | Extension-cast trial vs arthroscopy |
| Distal radius buckle | No displacement by definition | Removable splint, self-directed care (FORCE trial) |
| Both-bone forearm angulation (acceptable, distal 1/3, <9y) | Up to 15–20° | See remodeling table below |
| Femur shortening acceptable (spica, <6y) | ≤2 cm (some accept ≤1.5–2 cm bayonet) | Determines closed spica vs traction/nail |
| Compartment pressure (delta-P) | <30 mmHg from diastolic | Threshold to fasciotomy in forearm/leg compartment syndrome |

**Red flags — act immediately**

- **Pink pulseless hand** after supracondylar reduction with a perfused, capillary-refilling hand but absent radial pulse and no Doppler signal — algorithm below.
- **White pulseless hand** (dysvascular) — surgical emergency, immediate reduction ± exploration.
- Forearm compartment syndrome signs: pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment, paresthesia — do **not** wait for pulselessness.
- Floating elbow (ipsilateral supracondylar + forearm fracture) — higher NV injury and compartment syndrome risk; lower threshold for both fixation.
- Non-ambulatory infant with a femur fracture, especially <12 months and no reported trauma or a history inconsistent with the injury pattern — mandatory NAI workup.
- Classic metaphyseal lesions (corner/bucket-handle), posterior/lateral rib fractures, multiple fractures at different healing stages, spinous process, scapular or sternal fractures — high specificity for abuse.
- Open fracture, neurovascular deficit, or floating joint in any pediatric long-bone injury — same damage-control principles as adults, scaled to size.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Physis and growth plate biology

The **physis** is the site of longitudinal bone growth and the biomechanically weakest structural link in a child's musculoskeletal system — ligaments and joint capsule are relatively stronger than the growth plate, so forces that would sprain an adult joint instead fracture the child's physis. Histologically the physis has four zones (reserve, proliferative, hypertrophic, zone of provisional calcification); the **zone of hypertrophy** is the mechanically weakest layer and is where most Salter-Harris fracture lines propagate. The **perichondrial ring of Ranvier** and **groove of Ranvier** contribute peripheral growth (latitudinal) and stability; injury here (e.g., an unrecognized SH III/IV fragment, or repeated screw crossing) risks a peripheral bony bar and angular growth arrest. Blood supply is epiphyseal (via epiphyseal vessels penetrating the secondary ossification center) and metaphyseal; the reserve zone is nourished by epiphyseal vessels, making SH III/IV/V fractures (which disrupt epiphyseal vascularity) more prone to osteonecrosis and growth disturbance than SH I/II.

### Elbow region

The distal humerus develops through six secondary ossification centers appearing in the CRITOE sequence above; this order is essential for distinguishing a true fracture fragment from a normal, not-yet-fused apophysis (classically the medial epicondyle mistaken for a loose intra-articular body after elbow dislocation — if the trochlea has ossified but the medial epicondyle is not visible in its expected location, suspect intra-articular incarceration). The distal humeral metaphysis is relatively thin (the "supracondylar waist"/olecranon fossa) between the medial and lateral columns — the anatomic basis for the high frequency of supracondylar fractures in the 5–7-year age group when this region is thinnest relative to load. The brachial artery and median nerve lie directly anterior to the distal humerus, coursing just medial to the biceps tendon; in **extension-type** supracondylar fractures the distal fragment displaces posteriorly and the proximal spike can tent, kink, or entrap the brachial artery and the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) — the most commonly injured nerve overall. In **flexion-type** fractures (much rarer, ~2–5%) the ulnar nerve is at greatest risk. The lateral condyle physis has a watershed, largely intra-articular blood supply through the posterior soft-tissue attachments (the "lateral condyle blood supply enters posteriorly") — this is why displacement, stripping of posterior periosteum, and non-anatomic reduction predispose to avascular change and nonunion, and why the fracture is treated with a lower displacement threshold for surgery than most other physeal injuries. The radial neck physis is intracapsular and the radial head is a secondary ossification center supplied by an anastomotic ring that is vulnerable to disruption with high-energy displacement or aggressive open manipulation — the basis for avoiding open reduction of the radial neck whenever a percutaneous option exists.

### Forearm and wrist

The distal radius and ulna account for the largest share of all childhood fractures owing to the FOOSH mechanism and a metaphyseal region rich in cancellous bone with a thick, biologically active periosteum. Remodeling capacity is greatest close to a fast-growing physis (the distal radius contributes ~75-80% of radial growth) and in the sagittal plane aligned with the adjacent joint's motion arc; it is essentially absent for rotational malalignment at any age and diminishes rapidly after roughly age 10 in girls / 12 in boys as growth remaining declines.

### Femur

The femoral shaft is supplied by the profunda femoris perforators and nutrient artery; the proximal femoral epiphysis is vulnerable to iatrogenic osteonecrosis from piriformis/greater-trochanteric-tip entry nailing before the trochanteric apophysis is mature — the rationale for lateral (trochanteric)-entry, not piriformis-entry, nails in children, and for elastic nail insertion from the distal metaphysis in younger patients. **Overgrowth** of the injured femur (typically 0.5–1.5 cm, from post-traumatic hyperemia) is expected in children under ~10 and is factored into acceptable shortening targets (mild bayonet apposition is intentionally accepted in the 2-10y bracket).

### Knee

The tibial spine (intercondylar eminence) is the tibial insertion of the ACL; in skeletally immature patients the ACL midsubstance is stronger than the incompletely ossified spine, so a valgus/hyperextension or deceleration force that would tear an adult's ACL instead avulses the tibial spine as a sleeve of bone (an important age-dependent injury-equivalence concept). The anterior horns of the menisci and the intermeniscal ligament can become entrapped beneath an avulsed, flipped fragment and block anatomic reduction.

### Ankle

The distal tibial physis closes in a predictable, asymmetric centripetal pattern beginning centrally, then medially, and lastly anterolaterally (Chaput/Tillaux region), over roughly 18 months around ages 12–14. During this transitional 12–18-month window, a partially fused physis produces two classic fracture patterns: the **Tillaux fracture** (SH III avulsion of the anterolateral epiphysis by the anterior-inferior tibiofibular ligament, physis already closed elsewhere) and the **triplane fracture** (a combined SH II+III+IV pattern with fracture lines in three planes — sagittal through the epiphysis, axial through the physis, coronal through the metaphysis — producing 2, 3, or 4 fragments depending on the plane of imaging and rotational forces).

### Non-accidental injury biomechanics

Fracture patterns with high specificity for abuse reflect mechanisms unlikely to occur accidentally in a pre-ambulatory or newly-ambulatory child — shaking/traction (classic metaphyseal lesions), direct chest compression (posteromedial rib fractures), and forceful grabbing/swinging. The **classic metaphyseal lesion (CML)**, seen at the corner of a long-bone metaphysis, is a planar fracture through the zone of provisional calcification produced by shearing/traction forces applied to the limb — its biomechanics require an acceleration-deceleration or twisting force well outside the range of normal handling, feeding, or diapering, which is why it carries such high specificity (though not 100% sensitivity — it can be subtle or missed on a single-view radiograph) for inflicted injury.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

### History and developmental context

Mechanism, developmental/ambulatory stage (a femur fracture in a non-ambulatory infant is abuse until proven otherwise), time since injury, delay in presentation, and consistency of the history with the injury and the child's developmental capability are all part of the orthopedic exam — this is not solely a social-work task.

### Elbow trauma exam

Document the full NV exam **before and after** any manipulation — AIN (thumb-index "OK sign"/FPL and FDP-index flexion, no sensory component), PIN (finger/thumb extension, no sensory component), median nerve sensory, ulnar nerve (interosseous/FDP small finger, sensory little finger), radial pulse, and capillary refill. Compare to the contralateral side. Assess skin puckering/dimpling anteriorly and anteromedially, which signals a severely displaced (often flexion-rotation, "pucker sign") fracture with soft-tissue/brachialis entrapment and warrants a lower threshold for open reduction.

### Imaging by region

AP and true lateral of the elbow (posterior fat pad sign = occult fracture until proven otherwise, even with normal alignment — treat as nondisplaced/Gartland I); Baumann angle and anterior humeral line on the lateral for supracondylar fractures; internal oblique view increases sensitivity for lateral condyle fractures and helps stage Milch/Jakob-type displacement; contralateral comparison views are used selectively (medial epicondyle ossification timing, subtle physeal widening) but should not be routine/reflexive. Elbow arthrography or intraoperative fluoroscopic arthrogram is useful to assess the cartilaginous articular step-off in lateral condyle fractures, since the visible bony fragment underestimates the true (mostly cartilaginous) fracture size in young children. MRI is reserved for occult SH fractures with normal radiographs and focal physeal tenderness, tibial spine fracture characterization (fragment size, meniscal entrapment, associated ACL/meniscal injury — present in up to 40%), and equivocal transitional ankle fractures. CT with 1-2 mm cuts is the imaging standard for surgical planning in Tillaux and triplane fractures — it more accurately quantifies articular gap/step (the 2 mm operative threshold is a CT, not plain-film, measurement) and defines fragment number/orientation.

### Femur exam

Assess thigh compartment tension, associated hip/knee exam, and screen the whole child for other injuries in high-energy trauma (femur fractures from low-energy falls in infants/toddlers vs. high-energy mechanisms in school-age children carry different injury-pattern implications).

### Knee exam (tibial spine)

A large hemarthrosis with block to full extension, positive Lachman with a soft/absent endpoint (ACL midsubstance is usually intact — laxity is from the bony avulsion, not a true ACL tear), and inability to actively extend the knee (extensor lag from the anterior fat pad/fragment blocking terminal extension) are characteristic.

### Skeletal survey and NAI-specific workup

Full skeletal survey (per ACR/SPR technical standard) in all children <2 years with a fracture concerning for abuse, with a follow-up (repeat) skeletal survey at 2 weeks to detect healing fractures missed on the index study (increases yield by ~10-20%). CT head (with or without MRI) for children <1 year with any fracture concerning for abuse, given the high co-occurrence of occult abusive head trauma. Dating fractures radiographically has wide, overlapping confidence intervals — avoid over-precise verbal dating in documentation or testimony.

**Fracture/history features that shift suspicion toward inflicted injury**

| Feature | Lower suspicion (typical accidental pattern) | Higher suspicion (concerning for NAI) |
|---|---|---|
| Age/developmental stage | Ambulatory child, mechanism matches motor skill | Non-ambulatory infant with any long-bone fracture, or mechanism exceeds developmental capability |
| History | Consistent across caregivers, consistent with fracture pattern, witnessed | Absent, vague, changing between tellings, or delayed presentation without explanation |
| Fracture location | Distal radius, supracondylar humerus, toddler tibia — common accidental patterns | Classic metaphyseal lesion, posteromedial rib fractures, scapular/sternal/spinous process fractures |
| Number/stage | Single fracture, single stage of healing | Multiple fractures, especially at different healing stages, or bilateral symmetric injuries |
| Associated findings | None | Bruising patterns inconsistent with mobility (e.g., bruising in a pre-cruising infant), oral/frenulum injury, retinal hemorrhage |



## Classification

### Salter-Harris classification (physeal fractures)

| Type | Fracture line | Definition | Prognosis | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | Through physis only | Complete separation of epiphysis from metaphysis along the physis; often radiographically normal except widening/soft-tissue swelling | Generally good; low growth-arrest risk unless high-energy (e.g., distal femur) | Closed reduction if displaced, cast/splint; distal femur SH I is an exception with higher arrest risk |
| II | Through physis + metaphyseal fragment (Thurston-Holland fragment) | Most common type overall (~75%) | Good in most sites | Closed reduction + cast usually sufficient; CRPP if unstable |
| III | Through physis + epiphysis (intra-articular) | Fracture exits through the joint surface | Growth-arrest risk moderate; articular incongruity risk | Anatomic reduction required; ORIF/CRPP if >2 mm articular step |
| IV | Through metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis (vertical, intra-articular) | Crosses all three zones | Highest growth-arrest risk of I-IV if not anatomically reduced | Anatomic reduction mandatory — precise physeal alignment across the full fracture, usually ORIF |
| V | Crush injury to physis, no visible fracture line | Diagnosis often retrospective (growth arrest discovered later); axial load mechanism | Poor — high growth arrest risk | No acute treatment changes outcome; counsel family, serial follow-up for arrest/bar formation |
| VI (Rang, not universally used) | Injury to peripheral perichondrial ring (Ranvier) | Peripheral physeal/periosteal injury, often open wounds, lawnmower injuries | Risk of peripheral bony bar → angular deformity | Debridement, soft-tissue coverage, long-term surveillance |

Growth arrest risk correlates more with **fracture displacement, the specific physis involved, and quality of reduction** than with SH type alone — the distal femur and proximal tibia physes carry disproportionately high arrest risk even for SH I/II due to their undulating physeal contour.

### Gartland classification (extension-type supracondylar humerus fracture, + Wilkins/flexion-type modifiers)

| Type | Definition | Posterior cortex | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Nondisplaced or minimally displaced; posterior fat pad sign may be only finding | Intact | Long-arm cast/splint 3 weeks, no reduction |
| II | Displaced with intact posterior cortex (hinge); anterior humeral line may still intersect capitellum abnormally | Intact, acts as a hinge | Closed reduction; cast if stable and no rotational malalignment; CRPP if any instability, IIA with rotation, or comminution ("IIA" = angulated only; "IIB" per some schemes = angulated + rotated, treated as unstable) |
| III | Completely displaced, no cortical contact; subdivided by direction (posteromedial vs posterolateral) | Disrupted | CRPP, urgent (within 24h; emergent if vascular compromise) |
| IV (Leitch/multidirectional) | Periosteal hinge disrupted circumferentially — fracture is unstable/multidirectional under fluoroscopy in both flexion and extension, often only recognized intraoperatively | Disrupted, no stabilizing hinge in any plane | CRPP with careful fluoroscopic assessment in multiple positions; higher threshold for open reduction if reduction not maintainable percutaneously |

Posteromedial type III fractures carry the highest risk of AIN injury and, less commonly, brachial artery injury (the proximal fragment's spike points anteromedially toward the neurovascular bundle); posterolateral fractures carry higher radial nerve risk.

### Milch / Jakob / Weiss classification (lateral condyle fracture)

Two classification systems are used together in practice — **Milch** (fracture-line location relative to the trochlear groove) and **Jakob/Weiss** (displacement staging, which drives treatment).

| System | Grade | Definition | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Milch I | Fracture line lateral to the trochlear groove (through the capitello-trochlear groove) | Salter-Harris IV equivalent; elbow remains stable (trochlea intact) | Less common; more stable pattern |
| Milch II | Fracture line extends medial to the trochlear groove, into the trochlea | Salter-Harris II equivalent functionally; elbow can be unstable because part of the trochlear articulation is on the fragment | More common (~85%); higher instability |
| Jakob/Weiss I | <2 mm displacement, articular cartilage hinge intact | Stable | Cast immobilization, weekly radiographs x 2-3 weeks to catch late displacement |
| Jakob/Weiss II | 2–4 mm displacement, or displacement seen on one view only; articular surface may be partially disrupted | Potentially unstable | Individualized — many treat as operative given propensity for interval displacement; arthrogram helpful |
| Jakob/Weiss III | >4 mm displacement, often rotated/flipped fragment, articular surface disrupted | Unstable | CRPP or ORIF with screw or K-wire fixation |

The **2 mm** threshold (rather than 4 mm) is used by many pediatric centers as the operative cutoff because true (cartilaginous) displacement is routinely underestimated on plain radiographs relative to arthrographic/intraoperative findings, and because delayed/interval displacement occurs in a meaningful minority of "stable" fractures followed nonoperatively.

### Judet classification (radial neck fracture)

| Type | Angulation | Definition | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | <30° | Minimal displacement | Sling/cast, early motion at 1-2 weeks |
| II | 30–60° | Moderate angulation, translation may be present | Closed reduction (manipulation ± percutaneous flexible-nail-assisted reduction) if >30-45° or per functional threshold |
| III | 60–90° | Significant angulation/translation | Closed reduction with percutaneous techniques (e.g., Métaizeau intramedullary reduction/"Israeli technique" using a nail passed retrograde to lever the head); avoid open reduction if at all possible |
| IV | >90° or complete displacement/dislocation of the radial head | Severe, may be associated with elbow dislocation or Monteggia-equivalent | Percutaneous reduction preferred even at this grade; open reduction only if percutaneous fails — highest AVN/synostosis/stiffness risk of any subtype |

Functional outcome correlates with **residual angulation and associated injury** more than absolute Judet grade — up to 30-45° residual angulation is often accepted in children under ~10 years with preserved forearm rotation rather than pursuing open reduction, because remodeling of the radial neck (unlike malrotation) is real and open surgery carries a disproportionately high complication rate (AVN in up to 20-30% after open reduction of severely displaced fractures).

### Meyers-McKeever classification (tibial spine avulsion), + Zaricznyj type IV addition

| Type | Definition | Management |
|---|---|---|
| I | Nondisplaced or minimally displaced fragment | Immobilize in extension (or slight hyperextension) cylinder/long-leg cast 4-6 weeks; aspirate tense hemarthrosis for comfort and to improve extension |
| II | Anterior 1/3–1/2 of fragment elevated, posterior hinge intact ("beak" appearance on lateral) | Attempt closed reduction with knee extension (reduces the anterior fat pad against the fragment); cast in extension if reduced; arthroscopy if a mechanical block (meniscus, intermeniscal ligament) prevents reduction |
| III | Complete separation of fragment from underlying bone bed; commonly subclassified IIIA (no rotation) vs IIIB (rotated/displaced fragment) | Operative — arthroscopic reduction and fixation (suture through the ACL base vs. screw fixation of the bony fragment, technique chosen by fragment size/comminution) |
| IV (Zaricznyj) | Comminuted fragment | Operative, usually suture fixation (screw fixation often not feasible through comminuted bone); most technically demanding subtype |

Extension, not flexion, is the reducing position for tibial spine fractures (opposite of the intuitive "flexion relaxes the ACL" reasoning) because the intact anterior horns of the menisci and the intercondylar roof push the fragment down into its bed as the knee extends.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Physeal fractures (SH I-II) and nondisplaced/minimally displaced fractures generally**: closed reduction under sedation or hematoma block when angulation exceeds age-based remodeling thresholds (see table above), followed by cast immobilization spanning the joint above and below; SH I is often treated presumptively when radiographs are normal but physeal tenderness is focal.

**Supracondylar humerus, Gartland I**: long-arm posterior splint or cast at 90° flexion, neutral rotation, for ~3 weeks; repeat films at 1 week to confirm no interval displacement is reasonable practice though not universal.

**Supracondylar humerus, Gartland II (select cases)**: closed reduction with the elbow hyperflexed (typically >120°) and forearm pronated (for extension-type fractures) to tension the intact posterior periosteal hinge, then long-arm casting with close (weekly) radiographic follow-up for the first 3 weeks; requires an intact hinge, no rotational component, and a reliable family for follow-up. Many centers now pin most type II fractures given a measurable, if modest, rate of loss of reduction with casting alone — this is a genuine practice-pattern variation, not a single correct answer.

**Lateral condyle, Jakob/Weiss I**: long-arm cast in neutral-to-slight-pronation, weekly radiographs for 2-3 weeks (the period of highest risk for interval displacement), then cast to clinical/radiographic union (typically 4-6 weeks total).

**Medial epicondyle, minimally displaced (<2 mm), no incarceration, no elbow instability**: brief immobilization (7-10 days) followed by early active motion; functional outcomes are traditionally reported as good regardless of bony union given the epicondyle's extra-articular position — but see Recent Evidence for a 2025 RCT materially challenging this assumption for athletes.

**Radial neck, Judet I-II**: sling or above-elbow cast in a position of comfort for 1-2 weeks, then early motion; forearm rotation, not extension/flexion, is the functional parameter to track.

**Both-bone forearm fracture**: closed reduction (traction-countertraction, often with an assistant, under conscious sedation or hematoma block) followed by a well-molded long-arm cast in the rotational position that best opposes the deformity (supination for apex-volar/distal-third fractures, neutral-to-pronation for more proximal fractures) with a three-point mold; weekly films for the first 3 weeks are standard given a meaningful redisplacement rate (~20-30% in unstable patterns), particularly in the first 1-2 weeks.

**Distal radius buckle/torus and minimally angulated greenstick fractures**: this is the fracture pattern with the strongest recent-evidence shift toward minimal intervention — a removable prefabricated wrist splint or simple soft bandage, with instructions for self-directed weaning as symptoms allow (typically 3 weeks), is now equivalent in outcomes to rigid casting and does not require orthopedic follow-up in isolated, radiographically definitional buckle fractures (see FORCE trial, Recent Evidence). True greenstick fractures with angulation (as opposed to buckle without cortical disruption) still generally warrant a molded cast, and may need reduction if angulation exceeds remodeling thresholds for age.

**Pediatric femur fracture, age-based ladder**:
- **<6 months**: Pavlik harness (flexion-abduction), tolerates significant shortening/angulation given enormous remaining remodeling; typically united in 3-4 weeks.
- **6 months–5 years** (roughly <25-30 kg): early hip spica cast, single-leg or 90-90 (one-and-a-half), applied in the ED/OR under sedation; traction (skin or skeletal) for 3-5 days pre-spica if shortening exceeds ~2-3 cm or the fracture is comminuted/length-unstable and closed reduction under sedation alone will not hold length.
- **5–11 years**: flexible (elastic stable) intramedullary nailing (ESIN/Nancy nails) is first-line for most length-stable diaphyseal patterns; spica casting remains an option for younger, lighter children in this bracket with transverse or short-oblique patterns and acceptable initial shortening.
- **>11 years or >45-50 kg, or length-unstable pattern at any age in this bracket**: flexible nailing has an unacceptably high complication rate (malunion, painful nail prominence) in heavier/older children — rigid, trochanteric-entry locked IM nail (or submuscular plating, particularly for very proximal or distal metaphyseal fractures) is preferred.

**Tibial spine, Meyers-McKeever I and reducible II**: joint aspiration of a tense hemarthrosis, then closed reduction by extending the knee (with gentle pressure over the fragment) and immobilizing in extension or slight hyperextension in a cylinder or long-leg cast for 4-6 weeks; serial radiographs at 1 and 2 weeks to confirm maintained reduction.

**Toddler fracture** (spiral, nondisplaced tibial shaft fracture in ambulatory 1-3 year olds, classically the "CAST" fracture pattern often not visible on initial radiographs): short-leg walking cast or a removable pre-fabricated walking boot for 2-3 weeks is sufficient; weight-bearing as tolerated is generally permitted from the outset given the inherent stability of the pattern. Bone scan or MRI is rarely needed but can confirm an occult fracture when clinical suspicion is high with normal initial films (repeat films at 10-14 days often show periosteal reaction confirming the diagnosis without further imaging).

**Transitional ankle fractures with <2 mm displacement on CT**: non-weight-bearing short-leg or long-leg cast for 4-6 weeks; given the mechanism occurs near skeletal maturity, growth arrest is a low but non-zero concern and follow-up to confirm maintained reduction and eventual physeal closure is standard.

**Non-accidental injury**: the fracture itself is treated according to its own merits using the ladders above — NAI status does not, by itself, change fixation strategy — but nonoperative treatment (splinting/casting) is favored where clinically adequate to minimize additional intervention in a child already undergoing a traumatic child-protection process.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Hematoma block** (intra-fracture site local anesthetic injection) is a validated, opioid-sparing adjunct for closed reduction of distal forearm fractures in the ED, particularly buckle/greenstick and both-bone fractures, and reduces reliance on procedural sedation resources.
- **Procedural sedation** (ketamine, nitrous oxide, or combination regimens per institutional protocol) remains the mainstay for reduction of displaced supracondylar, both-bone forearm, and distal radius fractures in the ED prior to casting or as a bridge to OR.
- **Regional/peripheral nerve blocks** for postoperative analgesia (supraclavicular/axillary block for upper extremity, femoral/adductor canal or fascia iliaca block for femur surgery) are effective and widely used, but a **single-shot block placed before or immediately after supracondylar humerus fixation in a limb at risk for compartment syndrome is controversial** — a dense block can mask the earliest, most sensitive sign (pain out of proportion/pain with passive stretch); many pediatric centers avoid regional blockade in Gartland III/IV fractures with concerning presentation, or use catheter techniques with a defined, closely monitored dosing regimen instead of a dense single-shot block.
- **Aspiration of tense knee hemarthrosis** in tibial spine fractures is both diagnostic (fat globules confirm intra-articular fracture) and therapeutic (improves comfort and enables clinical extension assessment for closed reduction candidacy).
- **Bier block** (intravenous regional anesthesia) is a reasonable alternative to procedural sedation for isolated forearm/wrist reductions in older, cooperative children in some ED settings, though used less commonly than hematoma block or sedation in most North American/European pediatric centers.

### Operative

**Supracondylar humerus, Gartland III/IV — closed reduction and percutaneous pinning (CRPP)**: reduction restores the anterior humeral line and Baumann angle; pin fixation options are **lateral-entry divergent pins (2-3 pins)** versus **crossed medial-lateral pins**. Lateral-entry-only constructs avoid iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury (a real risk with medial pin placement, historically reported up to 3-8%) and are the default in most unstable/type III fractures when biomechanically adequate pin spread and divergence at the fracture site can be achieved (avoid parallel, non-divergent pins clustered in a single column — this is a common technical pitfall that reduces construct rigidity). **Medial-lateral crossed pinning provides superior biomechanical stability** in highly unstable, comminuted, or type IV fractures and is favored when three well-spread lateral pins cannot be achieved or the fracture is markedly unstable in extension as well as flexion (the hallmark of Gartland IV) — placed via a **mini-open medial approach or with the elbow in less flexion** to move the ulnar nerve posteriorly and reduce injury risk, with direct palpation/protection of the nerve strongly recommended. **Open reduction** is indicated for: failure to achieve an acceptable closed reduction, an irreducible fracture (button-holed brachialis/periosteum, the "pucker sign"), open fractures, and the pulseless-white hand requiring exploration (see algorithm below). A **medial approach** is generally preferred when brachial artery/median nerve exploration is needed; the anterior approach is an alternative giving direct access to the neurovascular bundle.

*Pink pulseless hand vascular algorithm*: after closed reduction and pinning, if the hand is **well-perfused (pink, warm, brisk capillary refill, normal function) but the radial pulse remains absent** by palpation and Doppler — this represents vascular spasm, kinking, or entrapment of the brachial artery with adequate collateral perfusion, and can be observed with serial vascular checks in most cases rather than mandating immediate exploration, provided perfusion remains clinically excellent; a substantial fraction of these arteries remain occluded on later imaging yet are clinically asymptomatic, and immediate exploration in a well-perfused limb has not been shown to improve outcomes and adds surgical risk. If the hand is **poorly perfused/dysvascular (white, cool, delayed or absent capillary refill)** despite reduction — this is a vascular emergency requiring immediate surgical exploration and, if needed, vessel repair/reconstruction; do not delay for imaging. Any hand that transitions from pink-pulseless to signs of poor perfusion, or that fails to improve after re-reduction of a malaligned fracture, mandates exploration. Serial, structured perfusion reassessment (documented at set intervals) after initial fixation is the safety net in the "observe" pathway; the threshold for exploring a persistently pulseless-but-perfused hand varies across centers and remains an area of active practice variation (see Recent Evidence).

**Lateral condyle**: percutaneous or open reduction with **K-wire fixation (2-3 divergent smooth pins)** for younger children/smaller fragments, or **cannulated screw fixation** (a single or two partially-threaded screws providing interfragmentary compression) for older children/larger fragments where growth remaining is limited enough that a screw crossing the physis carries acceptable arrest risk (or when the screw can be placed largely within the epiphyseal fragment). Open reduction (posterolateral or lateral approach, taking care to preserve the posterior soft-tissue hinge that supplies the fragment's blood supply — **do not strip posteriorly**) is used for displaced (Jakob III), rotated, or irreducible fragments; intraoperative arthrogram confirms articular congruity. Immobilize 4-6 weeks; K-wires are typically removed in clinic at 4-6 weeks once union is confirmed, screws may be retained or removed depending on symptoms/surgeon preference.

**Medial epicondyle**: indications for ORIF are converging toward a broader group than historically taught — classic indications are an **incarcerated fragment within the joint** (after elbow dislocation-reduction), **open fracture**, **ulnar nerve dysfunction with a displaced fragment**, and gross **valgus instability**; displacement thresholds historically cited (2 mm, 5 mm, 10-15 mm) have never been well-validated and vary by author. A 2025 multicenter RCT (see Recent Evidence) found a markedly higher nonunion rate with cast treatment versus surgery, which is prompting many centers to lower the threshold for fixation, particularly in adolescent throwing/overhead athletes and gymnasts for whom valgus load tolerance and nonunion-related instability carry real functional consequences. Fixation is with a single partially-threaded screw (most common) or heavy suture fixation through drill tunnels for small/fragmented pieces; the ulnar nerve should be identified and protected during exposure and can be transposed if there is preoperative ulnar nerve dysfunction or if it subluxates with elbow flexion intraoperatively.

**Radial neck**: percutaneous closed reduction techniques are preferred whenever feasible — manipulation with a percutaneous joystick/elevator, or the **Métaizeau technique** (an intramedullary flexible nail passed retrograde from the distal radial metaphysis, advanced across the fracture, then rotated/used as a lever to reduce the radial head, and left in place as an intramedullary splint for 4-6 weeks). Open reduction (via a lateral/Kocher approach) is reserved for failed percutaneous attempts because it substantially increases the risk of radial head avascular necrosis, radioulnar synostosis, and stiffness; when open reduction is unavoidable, minimize periosteal stripping and preserve the annular ligament and posterior soft-tissue attachments as much as possible.

**Both-bone forearm shaft fracture**: **elastic stable intramedullary nailing (ESIN)** via retrograde radius and antegrade or retrograde ulna nails is the workhorse operative technique for unstable/unacceptable-alignment fractures in school-age children — it is load-sharing, preserves the periosteal biology, and allows earlier functional cast-free (or lightly protected) mobilization than plating. **Plate fixation** is favored for adolescents nearing skeletal maturity (where remodeling potential is exhausted and anatomic, rotationally exact reduction is required), refracture, comminuted/segmental patterns, and cases where ESIN cannot achieve/maintain adequate rotational and length control. A single-bone (radius-only) nail with the ulna reduced and cast-controlled is an option in some length-stable both-bone patterns but most surgeons nail both bones for reliable rotational control.

**Femur, operative techniques**: flexible IM nailing is inserted retrograde through medial and lateral distal metaphyseal entry points, crossing the fracture in a symmetric "C-C" or "S-S" configuration for maximal stability, with nail diameter selected per bone (each nail ~40% of the canal diameter, two nails together approximating 80%) — pitfalls include nails cut too long (soft-tissue irritation, a leading complication requiring elective removal) and inadequate divergence at the fracture site. Rigid trochanteric-entry locked nailing is used in older/heavier children and length-unstable patterns, entering lateral to the piriformis fossa/greater trochanteric tip to avoid the lateral ascending cervical vessels supplying the femoral head. Submuscular bridge plating is an alternative for very proximal or distal metaphyseal fractures poorly suited to flexible nails. External fixation is reserved for open fractures, severe soft-tissue injury, or polytrauma requiring damage-control stabilization, with conversion to definitive fixation once the soft-tissue envelope allows.

**Tibial spine, arthroscopic reduction and fixation**: arthroscopy allows direct visualization to free entrapped meniscal or intermeniscal-ligament tissue (present in a meaningful minority of type III/IV fractures and the most common cause of an irreducible fragment), anatomic reduction, and fixation with either **transosseous/epiphyseal suture** (passed around or through the base of the ACL and tied over an anterior bony bridge or button — avoids drilling across the physis, preferred in the least skeletally mature patients) or **screw fixation** (a single partially-threaded screw or two divergent screws through the fragment into the tibial metaphysis, providing strong compression but requiring adequate fragment size/bone stock and carrying a physeal-crossing consideration in less mature patients). Comminuted (Zaricznyj IV) fragments are usually not amenable to screw purchase and are managed with suture fixation. Postoperative bracing/ROM restriction protects the repair while avoiding prolonged immobilization-related arthrofibrosis (see Rehabilitation).

**Transitional ankle fractures**: for displacement >2 mm on CT, or evidence of syndesmotic diastasis, closed or open reduction with **percutaneous epiphyseal screw fixation** placed parallel to the residual open physis (never crossing an open portion of physis if it can be avoided, given the imminent-but-incomplete closure) restores the articular surface; a fully transverse triplane fracture with a large metaphyseal component may additionally need a metaphyseal screw or plate. Because these injuries occur near the end of growth, growth arrest is a comparatively minor concern relative to articular step-off, which is the primary driver of late post-traumatic ankle arthritis if left uncorrected.

**Non-accidental injury**: operative decisions follow the fracture-specific indications above; what differs is process — mandatory hospital child-protection team involvement, skeletal survey (± repeat survey at 2 weeks) and, in infants, neuroimaging as above, thorough contemporaneous documentation (photographs, verbatim history from each caregiver taken separately when possible), and formal reporting to child-protective/social services and, where legally mandated, law enforcement, per the reporting physician's jurisdiction — every physician evaluating a child with a suspicious fracture in Romania and across the EU operates under a **mandatory reporting obligation for suspected child abuse**; confirm the current local statutory pathway and hospital safeguarding protocol, as specific reporting mechanisms and thresholds are set at the national/institutional level and are outside the scope of orthopedic guidelines per se.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Timeline at a glance**

| Diagnosis | Immobilization | Motion/loading milestone | Return to sport |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elbow fractures (supracondylar, lateral condyle, medial epicondyle, radial neck) | 3-4 wk cast/splint, pins out at 3-4 wk | Active ROM begins immediately after pin/cast removal | 6-8 wk (3-4 mo for throwing after medial epicondyle ORIF) |
| Both-bone forearm | 6 wk cast (nonop) or 2-4 wk protection (ESIN) | Rotation tracked as key milestone | 3 mo post-ESIN, nail removal 4-9 mo |
| Distal radius buckle/greenstick | 2-3 wk splint/bandage, self-weaned | No formal milestone needed | Days to ~2 wk after discontinuation |
| Femur fracture | 4-6 wk spica (young), 2-4 wk protected WB (ESIN) | Self-limited WB progression in young children | 3-4 mo (ESIN), adult-pattern progression after rigid nailing |
| Tibial spine | 4-6 wk brace/cast in extension, earlier protected flexion if fixation stable | Quad sets from day 1; ROM progression at 2-6 wk | 4-6 mo, later if extension deficit/laxity persists |
| Toddler fracture | 2-3 wk cast/boot | WBAT from diagnosis | No restriction after cast removal |
| Transitional ankle | 4-6 wk NWB | Progressive WB in boot after NWB period | 8-10 wk running, 3-4 mo cutting/pivoting |

**Supracondylar humerus / lateral condyle / medial epicondyle / radial neck (elbow region), operative**: immobilize in a well-padded long-arm splint or cast for 3-4 weeks; pins are typically removed in clinic at 3-4 weeks once radiographic union/callus is present, followed by removal of the cast/splint and initiation of active, unassisted ROM (avoid passive stretching or formal physiotherapy in the first several weeks post-pin-removal in most uncomplicated cases — children reliably regain motion on their own, and aggressive early passive therapy in this age group is associated with increased pain and heterotopic bone formation rather than faster recovery). Expect motion to lag 2-4 weeks behind the contralateral side initially, with the great majority regaining full or near-full motion by 3 months; residual mild flexion contracture (<10-15°) is common and usually resolves by 6-12 months without intervention. Formal physiotherapy is reserved for motion that plateaus well below expected milestones by 6-8 weeks post-cast-removal. Return to contact sports/gymnastics/monkey bars is typically permitted at 6-8 weeks once radiographic union is confirmed and functional strength/motion has returned; return to competitive throwing after medial epicondyle fixation is usually delayed to 3-4 months with a structured, gradual throwing progression given the valgus-stress environment of the healed fixation.

**Both-bone forearm fracture**: cast immobilization (nonoperative) 6 weeks total, transitioning from long-arm to short-arm at 3-4 weeks in many protocols; after ESIN, a short period of cast/splint protection (2-4 weeks) is common though some centers mobilize early with nails alone providing sufficient stability. Forearm rotation (pronation/supination) is the functional milestone to track and typically lags the longest; unrestricted sport/monkey-bar activity is generally permitted at 3 months post-ESIN once union is confirmed, with nail removal electively at 4-9 months (removal not mandatory in all cases but standard in most pediatric practice given the small nail diameter and growing bone).

**Distal radius buckle/greenstick**: functional splint/bandage discontinued at the patient/family's discretion once comfortable (median ~2-3 weeks); no formal PT needed; full activity typically resumes within days to a couple of weeks of splint discontinuation without a graduated protocol.

**Femur fracture**: spica cast maintained 4-6 weeks (younger/faster-healing children toward the shorter end), then transitioned to a removable brace or simply observed for progressive weight-bearing as pain allows; children are remarkably good at self-limiting weight-bearing and formal weight-bearing restriction protocols are less rigid than in adults. After flexible nailing, protected weight-bearing (crutches/walker) for 2-4 weeks then progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, with return to running/sport typically at 3-4 months once solid radiographic union (bridging callus on 3 of 4 cortices) is present; after rigid nailing in adolescents, weight-bearing progression follows adult-pattern protocols more closely (partial weight-bearing early, progressing over 6-12 weeks based on union). Formal physiotherapy is used more often after femur fracture than elbow fracture, particularly for adolescents with rigid fixation, to address quadriceps/hip-abductor strength deficits.

**Tibial spine fracture**: cast or hinged-brace immobilization in near-extension for 4-6 weeks (whether treated nonoperatively or with fixation robust enough to permit brace rather than cast), with quadriceps-setting/isometric exercises begun immediately to limit atrophy; progressive ROM (working toward full flexion) begins at 4-6 weeks once the fixation/healing fragment can tolerate load, with the single greatest rehabilitation risk being **arthrofibrosis/flexion contracture from prolonged extension immobilization** — balancing protection of the reduction against motion loss is the central tension of this rehab protocol, and most contemporary protocols favor earlier (2-3 week) initiation of protected flexion once fixation is judged stable rather than the traditional full 6-week extension cast. Return to pivoting sport is typically deferred to 4-6 months, later if residual anterior knee laxity or extension deficit persists, and formal ACL-injury-prevention/neuromuscular training is reasonable before clearance given the shared mechanism with ACL tears.

**Toddler fracture**: weight-bearing as tolerated from diagnosis in most cases; cast/boot discontinued at 2-3 weeks with no restriction thereafter and no formal PT required.

**Transitional ankle fracture**: non-weight-bearing 4-6 weeks (longer, closer to 6 weeks, for fixed/operative triplane fractures with a large metaphyseal component), then progressive weight-bearing in a walking boot with physiotherapy focused on ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion ROM and gait normalization; return to running typically at 8-10 weeks and full return to cutting/pivoting sport at 3-4 months once radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing are achieved.

**Return-to-school/return-to-sport, general principles**: return to school is typically immediate to within a few days for upper-extremity injuries treated nonoperatively (accommodating for dominant-hand writing limitations), and 1-2 weeks for lower-extremity injuries once comfortable with assistive devices/spica mobility aids. Return-to-sport clearance across all these diagnoses should combine radiographic union, near-symmetric strength and ROM, and (for lower-extremity/pivoting-sport injuries) functional hop or agility testing rather than time alone.

## Complications

- **Volkmann's ischemic contracture / compartment syndrome**: the most feared complication of supracondylar humerus and both-bone forearm fractures; results from missed or delayed-recognized vascular compromise or missed compartment syndrome, producing flexion contracture of the wrist/fingers from forearm flexor muscle infarction and fibrosis. Prevention is through timely reduction/fixation and vigilant, structured serial neurovascular examination in the first 24-48 hours, particularly in floating-elbow injuries.
- **Cubitus varus ("gunstock deformity")**: the classic late complication of supracondylar humerus fracture from inadequately corrected coronal-plane (varus) malunion — a cosmetic deformity historically taught as not functionally limiting, though contemporary series report a nontrivial rate of subsequent lateral condyle fracture and tardy posterolateral rotatory instability in the malaligned elbow; prevention is intraoperative Baumann angle/rotational control, not a "fix later" problem, since remodeling of coronal-plane elbow malalignment is essentially absent.
- **Nerve injury**: AIN is the most common nerve injury overall in extension-type supracondylar fractures (motor-only, typically neurapraxic, recovers in the large majority within 3-6 months); ulnar nerve injury is most associated with flexion-type fractures and with medial pin placement (iatrogenic) during CRPP; radial nerve injury is associated with posterolateral-displaced fractures. Most closed nerve injuries in supracondylar fractures are neurapraxic and recover spontaneously — observation with EMG/referral reserved for injuries not improving by 3 months.
- **Lateral condyle nonunion / delayed union**: occurs in a meaningful minority of fractures treated nonoperatively that were initially judged "stable," from interval displacement or inherently tenuous blood supply; nonunion can progress to a symptomatic lateral spur, cubitus valgus, and **tardy ulnar nerve palsy** (a delayed ulnar neuropathy from chronic valgus stretch, occurring years after the index injury) — a classic long-term complication specific to this fracture.
- **Medial epicondyle nonunion**: historically considered benign/asymptomatic given the extrasynovial location, but recent RCT-level evidence (Recent Evidence, below) demonstrates a markedly higher nonunion rate after cast treatment and raises real questions about long-term valgus stability and throwing performance in that subgroup.
- **Radial neck AVN, radioulnar synostosis, and stiffness**: the dominant complications after radial neck fracture, disproportionately associated with open reduction and with higher initial Judet grade; loss of forearm rotation is often the more functionally limiting sequela than residual angulation itself.
- **Forearm malunion, cross-union (radioulnar synostosis), and refracture**: malunion (usually rotational) causes loss of pronosupination; synostosis is a rare but serious complication most associated with both-bone fractures at the same level with substantial initial displacement/comminution and single-incision same-level surgical approaches; refracture risk is elevated in the first 6 months after cast removal, particularly with incomplete remodeling of angulation or premature return to high-risk activity.
- **Femoral leg-length discrepancy and malunion**: overgrowth (expected, usually 0.5-1.5 cm, more pronounced in younger children and more proximal/mid-shaft fractures) is the most common length issue; malunion (rotational or angular) from inadequately controlled fixation is more common with under-constrained flexible nailing in unstable fracture patterns. Symptomatic leg-length discrepancy beyond ~2 cm at maturity may warrant contralateral epiphysiodesis planning.
- **Tibial spine arthrofibrosis and residual laxity**: arthrofibrosis (extension loss) is the most common complication, historically from prolonged extension-cast immobilization — driving the shift toward earlier protected motion; residual anterior laxity (from stretched-out healing or malreduction) is less common but can produce functional instability, particularly in pivoting athletes, and is distinct from a true ACL tear.
- **Toddler fracture — missed diagnosis**: initial radiographs can be normal in a genuine toddler's fracture; a limping/refusing-to-bear-weight toddler with normal initial films and no other cause identified should be immobilized empirically and reimaged at 10-14 days if symptoms persist, rather than pursuing an extensive alternative workup prematurely.
- **Transitional ankle fracture — growth arrest and post-traumatic arthritis**: growth arrest is uncommon given the near-complete physis at time of injury, but articular incongruity (>2 mm) left uncorrected is a recognized driver of early post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis — the primary rationale for the operative displacement threshold.
- **Non-accidental injury — missed diagnosis and recurrent/escalating harm**: the single most consequential complication in this entire cluster is a **missed diagnosis of abuse**, which carries a well-documented risk of recurrent injury and, in a meaningful minority of cases, death on re-presentation; conversely, false-positive labeling carries severe familial and legal harm — both failure modes underscore why a structured, protocolized, multidisciplinary workflow (rather than individual gestalt) is the standard of care.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2022 (foundational, still driving 2023-2026 practice change)** — [Perry et al., "Immobilisation of torus fractures of the wrist in children (FORCE): a randomised controlled equivalence trial," Lancet 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35780790/) and the companion [Health Technology Assessment report](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35904496/) — the practice-changing multicenter UK RCT (965 children, ages 4-15) establishing that a soft bandage with self-directed removal is clinically equivalent to rigid splint/cast immobilization for distal radius torus (buckle) fractures, with no orthopedic follow-up required; this remains the evidentiary basis for the "soft-cast/self-care" pathway now recommended by BOAST and adopted widely across UK, European, and increasingly North American pediatric EDs, and is the single largest practice shift in this cluster over the review period.
- **2025** — [Grahn et al., "Operative vs. Nonoperative Treatment for Pediatric Medial Epicondyle Fractures," JAMA Network Open 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40327343/) — a randomized trial reporting nonunion in only 1/37 (2.7%) surgically-fixed medial epicondyle fractures versus 24/35 (68.6%) cast-treated fractures; this is a striking result relative to the traditional teaching that medial epicondyle nonunion is a benign radiographic curiosity, and is prompting reassessment of nonoperative thresholds, particularly in throwing athletes and adolescents with higher valgus demand — treat as hypothesis-generating and practice-influential rather than yet fully guideline-incorporated, and weigh functional/valgus-instability outcomes (not just union) as they mature in follow-up publications from this cohort.
- **2024** — [Achten et al., "SCIENCE: Surgery versus Cast Immobilisation for dEep displacemeNt of paediatriC mEdial epicondyle fractures — protocol," Bone & Joint Open 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38269598/) — a UK multicenter RCT protocol (building on the earlier trial infrastructure) formally testing surgery vs. cast for displaced medial epicondyle fractures; its results, expected in the 2025-2027 window, will materially inform the displacement threshold for ORIF discussed in Treatment above — flag this as a trial to track, since results may further shift practice beyond the Grahn 2025 signal.
- **2024** — [Goh et al., "Systematic review of paediatric pulseless pink humerus supracondylar fractures," Journal of Pediatric Orthopaedics B 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38189745/) — a contemporary systematic review synthesizing outcomes of the "observe" strategy for the pink pulseless hand, supporting the practice of serial monitoring without mandatory immediate exploration when the hand remains well-perfused after reduction/pinning, while highlighting the persistent heterogeneity in reported exploration thresholds across centers — useful as the most current summary reference for the vascular algorithm in this monograph.
- **2024** — [Phan et al., "Treatment of Pink Pulseless Hand Following Supracondylar Fractures of the Humerus in Children," Journal of Hand Surgery (Asian-Pacific Volume) 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39544037/) and [Kanumuri et al., "Open Exploration and Reduction of Paediatric Supracondylar Humerus Fracture with Pink, Pulseless Hand in Resource-Limited Settings," J Hand Surg Asian Pac Vol 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38494170/) — additional 2024 case-series-level data reinforcing that outcomes after observation of the pink-pulseless hand are generally favorable, while the resource-limited-settings series is a reminder that the "observe" pathway assumes access to reliable serial vascular reassessment and rapid escalation capability — not a universal default irrespective of monitoring capacity.
- **2023** — [Xing, Dong, Che, "Medial-lateral versus lateral-only pinning fixation in children with displaced supracondylar humeral fractures: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials," Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36647086/) — pooled RCT-level evidence continuing to support lateral-entry pinning as non-inferior for stability with a lower ulnar nerve injury rate than crossed pinning in the broad Gartland III population, reinforcing lateral-entry as the default in fractures where adequate pin spread/divergence is achievable, while crossed pinning remains reserved for the most unstable subset (as detailed in Operative Treatment above).
- **2023** — [Cummings et al., multicenter study of lateral condyle humerus fracture fixation methods (762 fractures), Journal of Pediatric Orthopedics 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36634213/) and [Cho, Kang, Kang, "K-wire versus screw fixation for lateral condylar fracture of the humerus in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis," BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37573303/) — large multicenter and pooled meta-analytic data confirming comparable union and complication rates between K-wire and cannulated screw fixation for displaced lateral condyle fractures, supporting a fixation-method choice driven by fragment size/age (as above) rather than a uniform superiority of one implant.
- **2025** — [Chang et al., percutaneous screw fixation for delayed union of lateral condyle fractures, Journal of Pediatric Orthopedics](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39523729/) — reinforces that early recognition of interval displacement/delayed union in lateral condyle fractures (the rationale for the weekly-film follow-up protocol in nonoperative management above) allows salvage with percutaneous screw fixation before progression to established nonunion.
- **2022** — [Trottier et al., "Incidence of secondary interventions after early spica casting for diaphyseal femur fractures in young children," Canadian Journal of Surgery 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35790240/) — real-world outcome data on AAOS-CPG-concordant early spica casting in children 6 months-5 years, quantifying the secondary-intervention (re-manipulation/conversion) rate and providing contemporary benchmark figures for counseling families considering spica versus flexible nailing at the younger end of the age-ladder.
- **2025** — [Shankar et al., "Clinical Outcome of Arthroscopic Reduction and Fixation by Pull-Through Suture Technique in Tibial Spine Fractures," Cureus 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40171339/) — contemporary case series supporting all-suture (pull-through) arthroscopic fixation as a reliable, physeal-sparing technique for tibial spine avulsion, consistent with the growing preference for suture over screw fixation in less skeletally mature patients described in Operative Treatment above.
- **Ongoing gap flagged for the reader**: high-quality, recent (2023-2026) comparative trial data specific to both-bone forearm remodeling thresholds, toddler fracture management, transitional ankle fracture imaging/threshold refinement, and updated non-accidental-injury skeletal survey protocols were **not** identified in this review's literature search; management guidance for those four diagnoses in this monograph rests on established society guidance and classic literature rather than a newly identified 2023-2026 primary study — verify against your own institution's current protocol and any interim society updates before treating this monograph as the final word on those four topics specifically.
