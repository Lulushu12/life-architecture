## Quick Reference

**Compartment nomenclature**: medial tibiofemoral (MTF), lateral tibiofemoral (LTF), patellofemoral (PF). Isolated single-compartment disease is the entry criterion for osteotomy, UKA, and PFA; multi-compartment disease defaults to TKA.

### Classification snapshot

| Classification | What it grades | Key grades | Management pivot |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kellgren-Lawrence (KL)** | Global radiographic OA severity (AP weight-bearing view) | 0–4 | KL0-1: non-op only. KL2-3 + isolated compartment + correctable deformity: HTO/UKA candidate. KL4: TKA workhorse (UKA only if strictly unicompartmental with intact ACL/collaterals). |
| **Ahlbäck** | Compartment-specific bone loss/subluxation (used for HTO/UKA candidacy) | I–V | I–II (JSN to bone contact): ideal for HTO/UKA. III–IV (attrition 0–10 mm): UKA/HTO feasible but higher technical demand, consider augments. V (>10 mm attrition + subluxation): usually TKA. |
| **CPAK** | Constitutional coronal phenotype (aHKA × aJLO) | I–IX | Types I/II (varus + non-neutral JLO) are the classic HTO/varus-OA population; CPAK guides whether kinematic/restricted-kinematic vs mechanical alignment better restores native joint line in TKA. |

### Treatment ladder by diagnosis

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Knee OA (general) | Weight loss ≥5–10%, structured exercise/PT, activity modification, topical/oral NSAIDs, acetaminophen (limited benefit) | Bracing (unloader for unicompartmental), intra-articular corticosteroid (short-term flare control), HA (selected patients), PRP (selected, out-of-pocket in most systems) | HTO / UKA / TKA per compartment pattern and age/activity |
| Symptomatic varus MTF OA, young/active | Non-op ladder as above | Unloader brace, single CS trial | **HTO** if age <60, BMI <30–32, correctable deformity, KL ≤3, flexion >120°, flexion contracture <10–15°, ROM arc intact, no fixed subluxation |
| Isolated MTF OA, older/lower demand | Non-op ladder | CS/HA injection | **Medial UKA** if intact ACL, correctable/passively correctable deformity, <15° flexion contracture, >90–100° flexion, thin patients preferred (obesity is a relative, not absolute, contraindication in modern series) |
| Tricompartmental / inflammatory / fixed deformity / ACL-deficient | Non-op ladder | CS injection for symptom bridging pre-op | **TKA** |
| Isolated patellofemoral OA (Iwano III–IV, no dysplasia driving instability) | Quadriceps/VMO strengthening, activity modification, patellar taping/bracing, NSAIDs | CS/HA injection, consider tibial tuberosity offloading if malalignment | **PFA** (young, isolated, no maltracking needing realignment) or TKA (older, diffuse disease, inflammatory arthropathy) |

### Red flags / contraindications to compartment-sparing surgery

- **Inflammatory arthropathy** (RA, psoriatic, crystal) — TKA, not HTO/UKA/PFA.
- **Active or recent joint sepsis**, uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c >8%), active skin/soft-tissue infection near the knee.
- **ACL insufficiency with instability** — relative contraindication to medial UKA (many high-volume centers still proceed with concomitant/staged ACL reconstruction, but outcomes are implant/surgeon-dependent).
- **Fixed varus/valgus deformity not passively correctable**, flexion contracture >15–20°, arc of motion <90°.
- **Full-thickness cartilage loss in the "spared" compartment** on MRI/arthroscopy — converts a UKA/HTO candidate to TKA.
- **Patellofemoral OA with lateral maltracking/instability** as the dominant driver — needs realignment (not isolated PFA).

### Key numbers

- **Mechanical axis / HKA angle**: neutral ≈180° (varus if <180°, valgus if >180°); classic mechanical-alignment TKA target = 180° ± 3°.
- **HTO correction target (Fujisawa point)**: weight-bearing line at **62–66% of tibial plateau width from medial edge** (mild overcorrection, mechanical axis 3–6° valgus, MPTA ~93–95°).
- **HTO magnitude**: typically 1° correction per ~1 mm opening-wedge gap at the osteotomy (varies with technique); avoid overcorrection >5° valgus (accelerates lateral compartment wear).
- **CPAK aHKA bands**: varus <−2°, neutral −2° to +2°, valgus >+2°. **aJLO bands**: apex-distal <177°, neutral 177–183°, apex-proximal >183°.
- **UKA flexion contracture cutoff**: generally <10–15°; **UKA minimum flexion**: >90–100°.
- **PRP/HA peak clinical effect**: 3–6 months post-injection.
- **Cementless TKA US utilization**: ~20% of primary TKAs by mid-2020s (up from ~3% in 2015) — see Evidence section.
- **UKA vs TKA revision**: registry pooled UKA revision ≈ 2× TKA revision rate (aggregate ~11% vs ~5.6% at comparable follow-up across worldwide registers) — counterbalanced by lower morbidity, faster recovery, and easier revision-to-TKA if it occurs.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The knee is a **tricompartmental joint**: medial and lateral tibiofemoral compartments (separated by the intercondylar eminence and cruciate ligaments) and the patellofemoral compartment. Osteoarthritis is a **whole-organ disease** — progressive loss of hyaline cartilage matrix (proteoglycan depletion, collagen network disruption), subchondral bone remodeling (sclerosis, cyst formation, osteophyte formation at the margins), synovial inflammation (low-grade, cytokine-driven — IL-1β, TNF-α, matrix metalloproteinases), and meniscal degeneration/extrusion that mechanically accelerates compartment-specific wear.

**Load distribution** matters more in the knee than almost any other joint: roughly **60–70% of tibiofemoral load passes through the medial compartment** in a neutrally aligned limb during stance phase, which is why the medial compartment is affected in the large majority of primary (non-inflammatory) knee OA, and why **varus malalignment self-propagates** — cartilage/meniscal loss shifts the mechanical axis further medially, which increases medial load, which accelerates further loss (the "vicious cycle" that HTO and unloader bracing interrupt).

Coronal alignment is defined by three key angles on a full-length, weight-bearing hip-to-ankle radiograph:
- **MPTA** (medial proximal tibial angle) — normal ≈87° ± 3°.
- **LDFA** (lateral distal femoral angle) — normal ≈88° ± 3°.
- **HKA angle** (hip-knee-ankle, mechanical axis) — neutral ≈180°.
- **Joint line convergence angle (JLCA)** — the residual coronal laxity gap; a large JLCA relative to bony deformity suggests soft-tissue (ligamentous) contribution to deformity, relevant when planning correction magnitude for HTO or soft-tissue balancing in TKA.

**Patellofemoral disease** has a distinct pathomechanics axis dominated by the **Q-angle, trochlear morphology (dysplasia), patellar height (Caton-Deschamps/Insall-Salvati), and TT-TG distance** — maltracking and instability drive a meaningful subset of PF OA, distinguishing it from simple "wear" and dictating whether realignment (tibial tubercle osteotomy, MPFL reconstruction) must accompany or substitute for arthroplasty.

### Per-diagnosis anatomic emphasis

- **Knee OA (general)**: whole-organ disease as above; the compartment pattern on imaging (medial vs lateral vs patellofemoral vs global) is the single most important anatomic variable driving the entire downstream treatment-ladder decision.
- **HTO candidates**: the deformity must be primarily intra-articular/tibial in origin (or at least correctable at the proximal tibia); a femoral-based deformity (distal femoral varus/valgus) requires a **distal femoral osteotomy** instead — differentiate by measuring MPTA vs LDFA independently, not just the composite HKA angle.
- **UKA candidates**: cruciate-ligament anatomy is decisive — the ACL functions as the "fourth ligament" of a UKA construct; ACL insufficiency changes tibiofemoral kinematics enough that most surgeons treat it as a relative contraindication to medial UKA without concomitant reconstruction.
- **TKA**: posterior condylar offset, trochlear geometry, and native rotational landmarks (transepicondylar axis, posterior condylar axis, Whiteside's line) are the anatomic references for femoral component rotation regardless of alignment philosophy chosen.
- **Patellofemoral arthritis**: trochlear dysplasia (Dejour classification) and patellar height/tilt are the anatomic drivers that must be assessed independently of cartilage loss severity, since dysplasia-driven instability changes the surgical plan from arthroplasty alone to combined realignment.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: activity-related mechanical pain (vs rest/night pain suggesting inflammatory or advanced disease), localization (medial joint-line vs anterior/patellofemoral vs diffuse), instability/giving-way (suggests ACL insufficiency or patellar instability), effusion pattern, prior meniscectomy/ligament surgery, occupational and recreational demand (critical for HTO vs UKA vs TKA selection), comorbidities (BMI, diabetes control, smoking, inflammatory arthropathy history).

**Physical exam**: gait — look for a **varus (or lateral) thrust**, which is a relative contraindication/complicating factor for isolated compartment surgery unless it corrects with valgus stress. Coronal deformity, passive correctability, ROM (flexion arc, flexion contracture), ligamentous stability (varus/valgus stress in extension and 20–30° flexion, Lachman/pivot-shift for ACL), patellar tracking and apprehension, quadriceps strength, and a full hip/spine screen to exclude referred pain.

**Imaging**:
- **Weight-bearing AP** (standing, extension) — standard KL grading view.
- **Rosenberg (45° flexion weight-bearing PA) view** — more sensitive for early joint-space narrowing than standing extension AP; the workhorse screening view for early/moderate OA.
- **Skyline/Merchant view** — patellofemoral joint space, subluxation, tilt.
- **Lateral view** — patellar height, posterior tibial slope, osteophyte pattern.
- **Full-length hip-to-ankle standing radiograph** — mandatory for any osteotomy or arthroplasty pre-operative plan; measures HKA, MPTA, LDFA, JLCA.
- **MRI** — not required for advanced radiographic OA, but valuable in early/equivocal disease, to assess the "spared" compartment cartilage/meniscus before committing to HTO/UKA/PFA, and to assess ACL/collateral integrity.
- **CT** — increasingly used pre-operatively for robotic-assisted TKA/UKA planning (3D bone models) and for rotational alignment assessment.
- **Long-leg CT scanograms** are an acceptable alternative to radiographic HKA measurement where available and are less susceptible to rotational malpositioning artifact.

### Diagnosis-specific evaluation pearls

- **HTO work-up**: mandatory full-length standing films with MPTA/LDFA/HKA/JLCA measurement, Rosenberg view for cartilage status of both compartments, and stress views if a large JLCA suggests soft-tissue laxity contributing to deformity (affects planned correction magnitude).
- **UKA work-up**: confirm full-thickness cartilage loss confined to one compartment (MRI or diagnostic arthroscopy if radiographs are equivocal), document ACL status clinically (Lachman/pivot-shift) since MRI ACL assessment can be unreliable in an arthritic, osteophyte-laden joint.
- **TKA work-up**: full-length alignment films for pre-operative planning regardless of alignment philosophy chosen; assess flexion contracture and coronal laxity under exam, as these drive intra-operative balancing strategy more than the static radiograph alone.
- **PF arthritis work-up**: skyline/Merchant view plus CT or MRI measurement of TT-TG distance and trochlear dysplasia (Dejour A–D) whenever instability/maltracking is part of the symptom complex — isolated wear without instability is treated differently from dysplasia-driven arthrosis.

## Classification

### Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade

The original (1957) and still most widely used radiographic OA severity scale, based on the standing AP knee radiograph.

| Grade | Radiographic findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | No radiographic features of OA | Reassurance; address other pain generators |
| **1** | Doubtful joint space narrowing (JSN); possible osteophytic lipping | Non-op ladder (exercise, weight management); no injections/surgery indicated on imaging alone |
| **2** | Definite osteophytes; possible JSN | Non-op ladder; injections reasonable for symptom control; HTO can be considered if malalignment is the dominant driver even at this grade |
| **3** | Multiple, moderate osteophytes; definite JSN; some subchondral sclerosis; possible bony contour deformity | Prime zone for **HTO or UKA** if compartmental and deformity-correctable; injections as bridge/adjunct |
| **4** | Large osteophytes; marked JSN; severe subchondral sclerosis; definite bony deformity | **TKA** workhorse indication; isolated-compartment KL4 with intact ACL/collaterals and neutral-to-mild deformity in the unaffected compartments may still support UKA in appropriately selected patients |

### Ahlbäck classification

Described by Ahlbäck (1968) specifically to grade **compartmental bone loss and subluxation**, and remains the reference scale in the HTO/UKA literature (particularly Scandinavian and European series) because it stratifies severity beyond simple joint-space narrowing.

| Stage | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Joint space narrowing (<50% of normal width, roughly <3 mm) | Ideal candidate for HTO or UKA; minimal bone loss to accommodate |
| **II** | Joint space obliteration (bone-on-bone contact), no bone attrition | Still favorable HTO/UKA candidate |
| **III** | Minor bone attrition (0–5 mm) | HTO/UKA feasible; expect slightly more complex balancing; UKA component sizing/augmentation rarely needed |
| **IV** | Moderate bone attrition (5–10 mm) | HTO/UKA more technically demanding; consider augments for UKA; counsel on higher revision risk vs TKA |
| **V** | Major bone attrition (>10 mm) with subluxation of the tibia on the femur, often with contralateral compartment/ligament involvement | Usually exceeds HTO/UKA envelope — **TKA** preferred due to bone loss, subluxation, and frequent multicompartmental extension |

### Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee (CPAK) classification

Introduced by Rivière/MacDessi et al. (2021) to describe **constitutional (native, pre-arthritic) coronal knee phenotype** rather than deformity per se, using two calculated parameters derived from long-leg radiographs:

- **Arithmetic HKA (aHKA) = MPTA − LDFA** — approximates the native limb alignment before joint-space loss. Bands: **varus** <−2°, **neutral** −2° to +2°, **valgus** >+2°.
- **Arithmetic joint line obliquity (aJLO) = MPTA + LDFA** — describes whether the joint line is oriented apex-distal (varus-leaning tibia relative to femur), neutral, or apex-proximal. Bands: **apex-distal** <177°, **neutral** 177–183°, **apex-proximal** >183°.

Crossing the two 3-level parameters yields **nine phenotypes (CPAK I–IX)**:

| CPAK type | aHKA | aJLO | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Varus | Apex-distal | The classic "varus knee" phenotype — most prevalent pattern in varus tibiofemoral OA cohorts; the population HTO and varus-correcting kinematic TKA techniques were built around |
| **II** | Varus | Neutral | Common varus phenotype without joint-line obliquity exaggeration |
| **III** | Varus | Apex-proximal | Less common; varus limb with valgus-oriented joint line — mechanical alignment risks over-constraining the native joint line here |
| **IV** | Neutral | Apex-distal | Neutral overall limb but obliquely oriented joint line (common in females with a wider pelvis/more valgus femur combined with varus tibia) |
| **V** | Neutral | Neutral | The "textbook" constitutional knee that classic mechanical alignment (180° HKA, both component axes perpendicular to mechanical axis) best reproduces |
| **VI** | Neutral | Apex-proximal | Neutral limb, valgus-leaning joint line |
| **VII** | Valgus | Apex-distal | Uncommon combination |
| **VIII** | Valgus | Neutral | Common valgus phenotype |
| **IX** | Valgus | Apex-proximal | Classic "valgus knee" phenotype, analogous role to Type I on the valgus side |

**Management implication**: CPAK does not by itself dictate a surgical alignment strategy, but it is the framework used to plan **kinematic and functional alignment** TKA — the goal in restricted-kinematic and functional-alignment techniques is to respect the patient's native aJLO (joint line obliquity) rather than forcing every knee to a neutral mechanical axis, while still staying within a "safe" restriction boundary (commonly ±5° from neutral HKA and defined MPTA/LDFA boundaries) to avoid outlier component positions associated with early loosening or instability. Robotic platforms now report intra-operative and post-operative CPAK type to audit how well a chosen alignment philosophy reproduced (or intentionally shifted) the constitutional phenotype.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

Every major society (**AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline**, **OARSI**, **NICE NG226/CG177**, **ESCEO**, **ACR/Arthritis Foundation**) converges on the same core non-operative ladder for knee OA before any injection or surgery is considered:

- **Structured land-based exercise therapy** (strengthening + aerobic + neuromuscular training) — strong/consistent recommendation across all guidelines; effect sizes for pain/function are moderate and durable with adherence.
- **Weight loss** for overweight/obese patients — target ≥5–10% body weight; dose-dependent symptomatic benefit, and mechanically reduces medial compartment load.
- **Self-management education, activity modification.**
- **Topical NSAIDs** — preferred first-line pharmacologic agent in NICE and ACR guidance, particularly in older patients, given a superior safety profile to oral NSAIDs.
- **Oral NSAIDs** — effective for pain but require GI/renal/cardiovascular risk stratification; use lowest effective dose, shortest duration.
- **Acetaminophen/paracetamol** — guideline support has weakened over the past decade (small effect size); still used as an adjunct, not monotherapy, in most guidelines.
- **Bracing** — unloader (valgus-directing) braces for symptomatic medial compartment OA have moderate evidence for pain reduction and are a reasonable bridge for patients not yet ready for HTO/UKA, or a non-operative alternative in those who are poor surgical candidates.
- **Assistive devices** (cane in contralateral hand, appropriate footwear).
- Explicitly **not recommended** across most current guidelines: glucosamine/chondroitin (no consistent benefit beyond placebo in high-quality trials), routine long-term opioid use.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

Injection evidence and guideline positions remain genuinely discordant between societies, which is clinically important to communicate to patients:

- **Intra-articular corticosteroid (CS)**: reliable short-term (2–6 week) pain relief, modest effect size; **OARSI conditionally recommends** it, **AAOS gives it a moderate-strength recommendation**. Evidence on chondrotoxic effect with repeated dosing is mixed but has led most guidelines to caution against injections more frequent than every 3 months and to avoid unlimited repeat dosing before arthroplasty (some series suggest higher peri-prosthetic infection risk with CS injection within ~3 months of TKA, motivating a pre-operative "washout" interval in many practices).
- **Hyaluronic acid (HA)**: the most guideline-discordant injection in orthopaedics. AAOS's 2013 CPG issued a **strong recommendation against** HA; subsequent OARSI (2019) and VA/DoD (2020) guidance moved toward conditional/qualified endorsement, and the current AAOS CPG has moderated to a moderate-strength recommendation supporting its use in symptomatic knee OA. ESCEO's expert consensus continues to support HA as part of a stepped-care approach, particularly in patients with contraindications to NSAIDs. Clinically: small-to-moderate pain/function benefit, **peak effect at 3–6 months**, more consistently beneficial in early-to-moderate (KL2-3) disease than end-stage disease.
- **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)**: the fastest-moving evidence base in this space. Recent meta-analyses (2024–2026) show PRP — particularly **leukocyte-poor PRP** — outperforming both HA and corticosteroid for pain and function at 6–12 months in mild-to-moderate (KL I–III) OA, with effect correlating with **total deliverable platelet dose**. Access is limited in many European health systems (out-of-pocket), and standardization of preparation protocols remains a limitation for guideline bodies to issue strong blanket recommendations; ESSKA and several national societies now offer conditional support for PRP in early-stage disease.
- **Prolotherapy / other biologics (BMAC, stem-cell products)**: insufficient high-quality evidence for routine guideline endorsement; use should be considered investigational outside of clinical trials or informed shared decision-making discussions.
- **Viscosupplementation is not a substitute for correcting mechanical deformity** — in a patient with varus malalignment and compartmental OA who is a surgical candidate, injections should be framed as a bridge, not a durable alternative to HTO/UKA/TKA.

### Operative

#### High tibial osteotomy (HTO)

**Indications**: symptomatic isolated medial compartment OA (KL2-3, Ahlbäck I-III preferred) in a relatively young (typically <60 years), active/high-demand patient with a correctable varus deformity, intact ACL (or reconstructable concurrently/staged), flexion arc >120°, flexion contracture <10–15°, and preserved lateral and patellofemoral compartments. Also indicated to correct varus malalignment concurrently with meniscal transplant, cartilage restoration procedures, or as a "joint-preserving" strategy to delay arthroplasty in a young patient who understands they will very likely need eventual TKA.

**Technique options**:
- **Medial opening-wedge HTO** — current dominant technique; single osteotomy cut proximal to the tibial tubercle, wedge opened to the pre-planned correction, fixed with an angle-stable locking plate (e.g., TomoFix-type constructs), gap filled with allograft/synthetic bone substitute or left to heal via guided bone regeneration in smaller corrections. Advantages: single-cut simplicity, easier concurrent ligament/meniscal procedures, avoids fibular osteotomy and peroneal nerve risk. Disadvantage: alters posterior tibial slope (tends to increase it) if the opening is not carefully biplanar-controlled, and lengthens the limb slightly.
- **Lateral closing-wedge HTO** — classic technique; more biomechanically stable construct with faster bone healing at the closing surface, but requires a fibular osteotomy/proximal tibiofibular joint disruption (peroneal nerve risk) and shortens the limb.
- Correction target: the **Fujisawa point**, weight-bearing line passing through **62–66% of tibial plateau width** from medial to lateral, corresponding to a mechanical axis of roughly **3–6° valgus overcorrection** — deliberate mild overcorrection unloads the medial compartment durably.

**Pearls/pitfalls**: control the posterior slope intentionally (each mm of anterior/posterior wedge asymmetry changes slope); avoid overcorrection >5° valgus, which accelerates lateral compartment degeneration; a lateral hinge fracture is the most common intra-operative complication of opening-wedge HTO (avoid by controlled, slow osteotomy and hinge protection, consider a hinge screw); reported **union rates >90–95%** with modern locking plates; delayed union more likely with larger corrections (>10° or gaps >10 mm) — some surgeons use bone graft substitute routinely above this threshold.

#### Unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA)

**Indications** (classic Kozinn-Scott criteria, liberalized substantially in modern high-volume series): isolated medial (far more common) or lateral compartment OA, intact ACL, correctable/passively correctable deformity, minimal flexion contracture (<10–15°), flexion arc >90–100°, and — contrary to older teaching — obesity and age extremes are now considered **relative rather than absolute** contraindications in contemporary series, though outcomes remain best in appropriately selected patients.

**Implant/technique options**:
- **Fixed-bearing vs mobile-bearing** — mobile-bearing designs (e.g., Oxford) theoretically reduce polyethylene wear via conforming contact but carry a specific risk of **bearing dislocation** (historically 1–2%) requiring surgeon experience with the technique; fixed-bearing designs are more forgiving of positioning error and dominate US practice.
- **Cemented (standard)** vs **cementless UKA** — cementless fixation (porous/HA-coated) has grown in adoption for medial UKA with registry data showing comparable or improved aseptic loosening rates versus cemented fixation in some cohorts, addressing the historically higher tibial component loosening rate that was UKA's Achilles heel.
- **Robotic-assisted UKA** — among the earliest and most validated robotic arthroplasty applications; improves component positioning precision and soft-tissue balance reproducibility versus manual technique, with registry and comparative series suggesting lower revision rates in robotic cohorts, though long-term (>10-year) comparative survivorship data are still maturing.

**HTO vs UKA selection** (the central decision in the medial-compartment-OA patient): recent large-scale meta-analyses (2025, pooling >55,000 patients) show **UKA achieves better WOMAC/functional outcome scores**, while **HTO achieves better KSS-function and Tegner activity scores** — i.e., HTO patients tend to return to higher-impact activity/sport more successfully, while UKA delivers more reliable symptom relief with a shorter recovery. Practical decision framework:
- Favor **HTO**: younger (<55–60), high physical/occupational demand, desire to return to impact sport, wish to preserve bone stock/avoid a prosthesis, willingness to accept a second surgery (HTO-to-TKA conversion) later.
- Favor **UKA**: older (>60), lower-impact activity goals, desire for faster, more reliable pain relief and recovery, less tolerance for a staged surgical strategy.
- Both procedures can fail forward to TKA; **UKA conversion to TKA is generally more straightforward than HTO conversion to TKA** (HTO alters proximal tibial anatomy, joint line height, and can complicate later component sizing/patella baja), which factors into counseling younger patients about lifetime surgical burden.

**Registry survivorship**: pooled worldwide registry data show UKA revision rates roughly **~2x** TKA at comparable follow-up (aggregate ~11% vs ~5.6%), but UKA's revision is lower morbidity and easier to convert, and modern robotic-assisted cohorts are narrowing this gap.

#### Total knee arthroplasty (TKA)

**Indications**: multicompartmental or inflammatory OA, failed non-operative management and/or failed joint-preserving surgery, fixed deformity, ligamentous insufficiency incompatible with UKA/HTO, or diffuse KL4 disease.

**Alignment philosophy** — the central controversy/decision point in modern primary TKA:
- **Mechanical alignment (MA)**: both femoral and tibial components cut perpendicular to their respective mechanical axes, targeting a neutral (180° ± 3°) overall HKA regardless of the patient's native anatomy. Decades of registry track record, predictable technique, but forces ligament releases in a substantial minority of knees whose native anatomy is not neutral (i.e., most CPAK types other than V), and is associated with a subset of "well-aligned but unhappy" patients (~15–20% patient dissatisfaction historically reported with MA-TKA despite good objective alignment).
- **Kinematic alignment (KA)**: resects bone to restore the patient's pre-arthritic native joint line and rotational axes (using pre-arthritic femoral/tibial cut references), without a fixed alignment target, aiming to reproduce natural soft-tissue tension and avoid releases. Early- and mid-term RCT data (e.g., multiple RCTs and pooled meta-analyses) show KA at least equivalent, and in several trials superior, patient-reported outcome scores versus MA at 2–5 years, with debate ongoing about long-term implant survivorship in more extreme (outlier) native alignments.
- **Restricted kinematic alignment (rKA) / functional alignment (FA)**: hybrid approaches — rKA restores native joint-line obliquity but caps how far any single parameter can deviate from neutral (commonly ±5° boundaries on HKA, MPTA, LDFA) to avoid the theoretical instability/wear risk of extreme unrestricted KA; FA (typically robotic-enabled) prioritizes balanced flexion/extension gaps and soft-tissue tension intra-operatively over any pre-set target, adjusting bone cuts in real time. CPAK phenotyping is the operational language used to plan and audit these hybrid strategies — e.g., robot-assisted kinematic techniques in CPAK type I knees achieve extension-gap balance without soft-tissue release more often than mechanical alignment in comparative series.
- **Practical synthesis**: no single philosophy has yet demonstrated definitively superior long-term revision-free survivorship in high-quality comparative registry data; the field's current center of gravity favors **restricted kinematic/functional alignment within a safe boundary** over unrestricted native-anatomy KA or rigid classic MA, particularly when using robotic or navigated platforms that allow real-time boundary-checked planning.

**Implant/technique decisions**:
- **Cruciate-retaining (CR) vs posterior-stabilized (PS) vs medial-pivot/ultracongruent** — CR preserves the PCL (requires PCL competence and careful balancing), PS substitutes a cam-post mechanism for PCL function (more forgiving in PCL-deficient or over-released knees, easier to balance in larger deformities), medial-pivot/ultracongruent designs aim to reproduce more natural kinematics (medial ball-in-socket, lateral rollback) and have gained popularity with kinematic/functional alignment techniques. No RCT-level evidence shows a clear, consistent PROM advantage of one bearing philosophy over another; selection is largely surgeon/deformity-driven.
- **Cementless TKA** — the major fixation-technique trend of the past decade. US utilization rose from roughly **3% (2015) to approximately 9% (2021)**, a ~242% relative increase (registry analyses), and by the mid-2020s roughly **one-fifth of primary TKAs in the US are cementless**. Modern porous/highly-porous metal-backed designs with mechanical interlock plus biologic ongrowth show mid-term survivorship comparable to cemented fixation in registry and RCT data; a randomized paired trial noted a signal of **cementless patellar component migration**, reinforcing that most surgeons still cement the patellar button even in an otherwise cementless (hybrid) construct. Cementless fixation is favored in younger, higher-bone-quality patients; cemented fixation remains preferred in osteoporotic bone, markedly deformed proximal tibiae, and revision settings.
- **Robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA)**: contemporary meta-analyses of RCTs (2024–2025, pooling >20 RCTs, ~2,700 patients) consistently show RA-TKA achieves **significantly fewer mechanical-axis outliers** (risk ratio ~0.33 for outlier alignment) and smaller mean deviation from the planned axis (~1° tighter) versus conventional jig-based TKA. However, **clinical/functional PROMs and complication rates remain statistically similar** between robotic and conventional TKA in pooled RCT data through mid-term follow-up — i.e., robotics reliably improves the surrogate (alignment precision) without yet demonstrating a definitive clinical outcome advantage, though it is the enabling technology that makes functional/restricted-kinematic alignment practically reproducible at scale, and registry signals in some cementless/robotic cohorts suggest lower early revision.

**Patellar resurfacing**: remains surgeon/region-dependent (near-universal in the US, more selective in parts of Europe); meta-analyses show no consistent difference in revision-for-any-reason but a modestly lower rate of secondary patellar resurfacing/anterior knee pain revision when resurfaced primarily in patients with pre-operative patellofemoral arthritis.

#### Patellofemoral arthritis and patellofemoral arthroplasty (PFA)

**Indications**: isolated, symptomatic patellofemoral OA (Iwano grade III–IV) with preserved tibiofemoral compartments, typically in patients who are younger than the usual TKA population and whose maltracking (if any) has already been or can be concurrently addressed. PFA is **not** appropriate when instability/maltracking is the dominant driver without correction, nor in inflammatory arthropathy, nor with any meaningful tibiofemoral chondral disease (progresses to need TKA conversion).

**Technique options**: **inlay** designs (trochlear component recessed flush with native cartilage, less dependent on precise rotational alignment, more "cartilage-replacement" philosophy) vs **onlay** designs (resurface and extend proximally onto the anterior femoral cortex, more anatomically constrained trochlear geometry, requires more precise rotational positioning analogous to TKA femoral component placement). Recent pooled meta-analysis (76 studies, >4,000 patients, search through January 2025) found both design families deliver good implant-specific survivorship, with technique/generation of implant mattering more than the inlay/onlay distinction per se.

**Outcomes**: modern (second-generation) PFA implants have markedly improved on the poor track record of first-generation designs. International registry data show approximately **90% survival at 5 years and 82% at 10 years**; a 2025 series in isolated Iwano grade IV disease reported **86% survival at 10 years**. Most PFA revisions occur early (within ~2.5 years), predominantly for unresolved pain or disease progression to the tibiofemoral compartments; when revision is required it is generally converted to TKA with outcomes comparable to primary TKA. Comparative GRADE-assessed evidence (2025) supports PFA over TKA in appropriately selected isolated PF OA on the basis of better early functional recovery, while acknowledging TKA's superior long-term implant survivorship as a trade-off to discuss with patients.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

### High tibial osteotomy

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protection | 0–2 weeks | Toe-touch/partial (per plate stability and gap-fill graft use) | Gentle passive ROM 0–90°, avoid forced extension against the osteotomy | Pain/swelling control, quad activation (straight-leg raise once achievable), DVT prophylaxis |
| Progressive loading | 2–6 weeks | Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated by 6 weeks if radiographs show healing progression | Advance to full flexion as tolerated | Closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining off crutches |
| Union consolidation | 6–12 weeks | Full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks once radiographic union criteria met | Full ROM goal | Progressive resistance training, proprioception/balance work |
| Return to activity | 3–6 months | — | — | Return to low-impact sport ~3–4 months; **return to impact/pivoting sport typically 6–9 months**, contingent on confirmed radiographic union and functional testing |
| Return to work | Variable | — | — | Sedentary work 2–4 weeks; physical/manual labor 3–4 months |

### UKA

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acute | 0–2 weeks | Weight-bearing as tolerated immediately (cemented and most cementless designs) | 0–90° by 2 weeks | Quad activation, cryotherapy, gait training off walker/cane |
| Early recovery | 2–6 weeks | Full weight-bearing, wean assistive device | Progress toward full flexion | Closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike |
| Functional recovery | 6–12 weeks | Full | Full functional ROM (typically ≥120°) | Return to low-impact recreational activity, driving by 2–4 weeks |
| Return to work/sport | 6–12 weeks (desk work often 1–2 weeks) | — | — | Manual labor 8–12 weeks; low-impact sport (golf, cycling, doubles tennis) 6–12 weeks — UKA's faster recovery profile versus TKA is one of its principal selling points to active patients |

### TKA

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peri-operative | Day 0–1 | Weight-bearing as tolerated same-day/next-day (modern rapid-recovery/ERAS pathways) | Begin CPM or active-assisted ROM per protocol | Multimodal analgesia, early mobilization, DVT prophylaxis initiation |
| Acute rehab | 0–2 weeks | Full WBAT with assistive device weaned as tolerated | Goal ≥90° flexion by 2 weeks, full extension | Gait training, quad sets, staircase training |
| Subacute | 2–6 weeks | Independent ambulation | Progress toward 110–120° flexion | Outpatient PT 2–3x/week, strengthening, edema control |
| Strengthening | 6–12 weeks | — | Functional ROM target 0–120°+ | Progressive resistance, balance/proprioception, low-impact conditioning |
| Return to work | Variable | — | — | Sedentary 3–6 weeks; physical labor 3–4 months |
| Return to activity/sport | 3–6 months | — | — | Low-impact sport (walking, swimming, cycling, golf) 3 months; higher-demand recreational activity by 6 months; guideline consensus discourages high-impact/pivoting sport indefinitely after TKA given polyethylene wear/loosening risk |

### PFA

Generally follows a **UKA-like accelerated pathway** (immediate weight-bearing as tolerated, early ROM, faster functional recovery than TKA) given the absence of tibiofemoral bone resection — return to low-impact activity by 6–8 weeks, full recovery by 3 months in most series.

## Complications

**HTO**: nonunion/delayed union (higher with larger corrections/gaps >10 mm without grafting), **lateral hinge fracture** (most common intra-operative complication of opening-wedge technique), loss of correction/hardware failure, peroneal nerve injury (higher risk with lateral closing-wedge/fibular osteotomy), overcorrection accelerating lateral compartment OA, undercorrection with recurrent medial symptoms, compartment syndrome (rare), and altered posterior slope affecting subsequent ACL reconstruction or TKA conversion biomechanics.

**UKA**: aseptic loosening (historically the leading failure mode, particularly tibial component; improving with cementless designs), **bearing dislocation** (mobile-bearing designs specifically), progression of osteoarthritis in unresurfaced compartments, polyethylene wear, periprosthetic fracture, infection (lower absolute rate than TKA given smaller implant/less bone exposure), and unexplained pain (a recognized, sometimes idiopathic, cause of early revision).

**TKA**: **periprosthetic joint infection (PJI)** — the leading cause of early revision, incidence ~1–2% in primary TKA, higher with diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, prior knee surgery; management follows MSIS/ICM criteria for diagnosis. Broad treatment-selection logic:

| Scenario | Typical strategy |
|---|---|
| Acute (<4–6 weeks from index surgery or acute hematogenous, stable implant, susceptible organism) | **DAIR** (debridement, antibiotics, implant retention) with modular component exchange |
| Chronic infection, well-fixed components, favorable host/organism | **One-stage revision** (increasingly favored in select European centers with strong culture-directed antibiotic protocols) |
| Chronic infection, difficult organism, compromised host, loose components | **Two-stage revision** (antibiotic spacer, organism-directed IV antibiotics, reimplantation once cleared) — remains the reference standard in most of the US and much of Europe for chronic PJI |

Aseptic loosening, stiffness/arthrofibrosis (manipulation under anesthesia typically considered if <90° flexion by 6–12 weeks), instability (flexion or extension gap mismatch), periprosthetic fracture, extensor mechanism disruption (patellar tendon/quadriceps tendon rupture, patellar fracture), venous thromboembolism (prophylaxis per AAOS/ACCP guidance — aspirin now supported as adequate prophylaxis for standard-risk patients in most contemporary protocols), and persistent unexplained pain (~10–20% of patients report some degree of residual dissatisfaction despite a technically well-functioning implant, underscoring the alignment-philosophy debate above).

**PFA**: progression of tibiofemoral compartment OA requiring conversion to TKA (most common reason for late revision), persistent anterior knee pain (leading cause of early revision), patellar maltracking/instability if trochlear component malrotated, and — importantly — **PFA to TKA conversion carries a small but real risk of inferior outcomes versus primary TKA** if patellar bone stock or trochlear geometry is compromised, reinforcing the importance of strict patient selection up front.

### Peri-operative Optimization & Special Considerations

- **Obesity**: BMI is a modifiable risk factor for PJI, wound complications, and revision; many centers use a soft BMI threshold (commonly cited ≥40) to trigger a pre-operative weight-optimization discussion before elective TKA/UKA, though this is applied with clinical judgment rather than as an absolute cutoff, and HTO is often favored over UKA in higher-BMI younger patients seeking joint preservation.
- **Diabetes**: target HbA1c <7.5–8% and perioperative glucose control before elective arthroplasty; poor control is one of the strongest modifiable PJI risk factors.
- **Smoking cessation**: recommended ≥4 weeks pre-operatively; associated with reduced wound and infection complications.
- **Pre-operative corticosteroid injection washout**: many arthroplasty surgeons enforce a ≥3-month interval between the last intra-articular CS injection and elective TKA given signals of increased early PJI risk with more recent injection, though the evidence quality behind a precise "safe" interval is moderate.
- **Staged vs simultaneous bilateral TKA**: simultaneous bilateral TKA reduces total hospital/rehab burden but carries higher perioperative cardiopulmonary and mortality risk, particularly in patients >70 or with cardiac comorbidity — most guidance favors staged bilateral TKA in higher-risk patients.
- **VTE prophylaxis**: contemporary AAOS/ACCP-aligned protocols support **aspirin as adequate prophylaxis for standard-risk patients** after TKA/UKA, reserving more potent anticoagulation (LMWH, DOACs) for patients with elevated VTE risk factors (prior VTE, malignancy, hypercoagulable state, immobility).
- **Shared decision-making**: for the HTO-vs-UKA-vs-TKA decision in a borderline candidate, explicitly discuss the trade-off between lifetime number of surgeries (younger patients choosing HTO/UKA may eventually need conversion to TKA), functional/activity goals, and each option's registry-quantified revision risk so the patient can weigh durability against recovery speed and joint preservation.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **HA guideline discordance persists but is trending toward endorsement**: AAOS's original 2013 strong recommendation against hyaluronic acid has been progressively softened; OARSI (2019) and VA/DoD (2020) conditionally support HA, and current stabilizing-utilization analyses (2025–2026) document how conflicting guidance has shaped real-world prescribing patterns — see [Intra-articular Hyaluronic Acid for Knee Osteoarthritis: Stabilizing Utilization Trends Amid Conflicting Clinical Practice Guidelines](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12806583/). Practice takeaway: HA is a defensible, guideline-supported option in symptomatic KL2-3 OA, particularly where NSAIDs are contraindicated.
- **2025 systematic review/expert opinion on injectable therapies** confirms literature trends are shifting toward more favorable evidence for cell-based and PRP therapies relative to older HA/CS data, while flagging continued heterogeneity in preparation protocols — [Corticosteroids, hyaluronic acid, platelet-rich plasma, and cell-based therapies for knee osteoarthritis (2025)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14712598.2025.2465833).
- **PRP dose-response confirmed (2026)**: a systematic review/meta-analysis found PRP clinical benefit is directly associated with the **total deliverable platelet dose** delivered, not merely PRP use per se — a key practical variable for protocol selection — [Hooper et al., PM&R (2026)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/pmrj.13455).
- **PRP superiority over HA/corticosteroid reaffirmed in mild-moderate disease**: multiple 2024–2025 meta-analyses show leukocyte-poor PRP outperforming HA and CS for pain/function, especially in KL I–III disease, with clinically significant improvement through 12 months — [PRP Injections for Knee Osteoarthritis: Clinically Significant Improvement Influenced by Platelet Concentration (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11874499/).
- **CPAK refinement for restricted kinematic alignment**: a 2024 proposal for a "restriction-boundary-based CPAK" (Rb-CPAK) shows the original CPAK boundaries do not map directly onto rKA-TKA safe zones, prompting a refined classification specifically for surgeons using restricted kinematic alignment — [A Restriction Boundary-Based CPAK Classification for Restricted Kinematic Alignment TKA (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11584355/).
- **Postoperative CPAK drift does not clearly hurt outcomes**: a 2025 study found that changes in CPAK type after restricted kinematic alignment TKA did not significantly influence patient-reported functional outcomes, reassuring surgeons that exact phenotype reproduction may be less critical than gap balance — [Changes in CPAK classification do not significantly influence outcomes after rKA-TKA (2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12709653/).
- **CPAK now applied to HTO outcome prediction**: a 2025 study specifically examined the effect of CPAK phenotype on clinical outcomes after HTO, extending the classification's use beyond arthroplasty into osteotomy planning — [Effect of CPAK Classification on Clinical Outcomes After High Tibial Osteotomy (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12957569/).
- **Robotic TKA alignment-precision benefit confirmed at scale, clinical benefit still unproven**: a February 2025 meta-analysis of 21 RCTs (2,692 patients) found RA-TKA reduced mechanical-axis outlier risk (RR 0.33) and mean axis deviation (−0.93°) versus conventional TKA, but PROMs and complications remained comparable — [Robotic-assisted versus conventional TKA: systematic review and meta-analysis of alignment accuracy and clinical outcomes (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40110313/).
- **Cementless TKA adoption continuing to rise**: nationwide US analysis confirms cementless TKA utilization rose 242% (2015–2021, ~3% to ~9%, CAGR +20%) across all demographic subgroups, with the trend continuing into the mid-2020s toward ~1 in 5 primary TKAs — [Trend of using cementless total knee arthroplasty: a nationwide analysis from 2015 to 2021 (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10998332/).
- **Cementless fixation outcomes reaffirmed at mid-term**: 2024–2025 comparative literature and registry-based analyses (including Michigan Arthroplasty Registry Collaborative Quality Initiative data) continue to show comparable survivorship for modern cementless versus cemented TKA fixation, while noting device-specific patellar component migration signals in paired RCT data warranting continued surveillance — [Cemented Versus Cementless Total Knee Arthroplasty: Analysis of the Latest Literature Data (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40630819/); [Uncemented TKA is on the Rise — MARCQI report (2024)](https://www.arthroplastytoday.org/article/S2352-3441(24)00184-5/fulltext).
- **HTO vs UKA large-scale updated meta-analysis (2025)**: pooled analysis of ~56,000 patients across 39 studies found UKA delivers significantly better WOMAC scores while HTO delivers better KSS-function and Tegner activity scores, formalizing the activity-level-based selection framework — [Comparison of UKA vs HTO for Medial Knee Osteoarthritis: Updated Meta-Analysis of 56,000 Patients (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12404872/).
- **UNIKORN RCT progressing toward maturity**: the first head-to-head randomized trial directly comparing UKA and HTO for medial knee OA (estimated completion 2024–2025) will provide the first Level I evidence to resolve the current meta-analysis-only comparative literature — [UNIKORN: Unicompartmental Knee Arthroplasty vs. High Tibial Osteotomy for Medial Knee Osteoarthritis, study protocol](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05442242).
- **UKA registry revision-rate benchmarking expanded (2025)**: the AJRR 2025 Annual Report Supplement analyzed >101,000 UKA cases (2012–2024) — the largest device-specific UKA survivorship dataset to date — enabling implant-specific counseling on revision risk — [American Joint Replacement Registry 2025 Annual Report Supplement Preview](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/american-joint-replacement-registry-2025-annual-report-supplement-preview/).
- **Worldwide registry pooling confirms UKA revision ~2x TKA**: a 2024 meta-analysis of clinical studies and worldwide arthroplasty registers found mean overall revision rates of 5.63% (TKA) vs 11.04% (UKA), i.e., UKA revision risk ~1.96x TKA — a benchmark figure for informed consent discussions — [Correlation of revision rate of UKA with TKA: a meta-analysis of worldwide arthroplasty registers (2024)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00402-024-05574-1).
- **PFA vs TKA GRADE-assessed comparative recommendations published (2025)**: a systematic review with formal GRADE assessment issued evidence-based recommendations favoring PFA over TKA in appropriately selected isolated patellofemoral OA, balancing better early function against TKA's longer-term survivorship — [Patellofemoral vs. total knee arthroplasty for isolated patellofemoral osteoarthritis: evidence-based recommendations with GRADE assessment (2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12901212/).
- **Modern PFA implant survivorship reaffirmed**: a 2025 series in isolated Iwano grade IV disease reported 86% survival at 10 years, consistent with contemporary international registry benchmarks (~90% at 5 years, ~82% at 10 years) for second-generation implants — [Patellofemoral arthroplasty in isolated Iwano grade IV patellofemoral osteoarthritis: 86% 10-year survival (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12661219/).
