## Quick Reference

**Red flags requiring urgent escalation**
- Warm, swollen, erythematous diabetic foot with intact skin and normal inflammatory markers → **assume Charcot until proven otherwise**, not cellulitis. Temperature differential >2 °C vs. contralateral limb is the most useful bedside sign.
- Any diabetic foot wound with probe-to-bone positive → treat as osteomyelitis until cultured/imaged otherwise (high positive predictive value in high-prevalence settings).
- Ischemic rest pain, absent pulses, or WIfI ischemia grade ≥2 with a wound → same-week vascular surgery referral; do not debride an ischemic wound without a perfusion plan.
- Rapidly progressive rigid cavovarus in an adult, especially asymmetric or with a normal foot on the contralateral side → screen for a **spinal cord/peripheral nerve lesion** (tethered cord, tumor, CMT) with MRI neuraxis before treating as idiopathic.
- Acute-on-chronic Achilles pain with a palpable gap → rule out acute rupture through a chronic tendinopathy before committing to elective tendinoscopy.

**Compact treatment ladders**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| PCFD (flexible, no arthritis) | Orthotic (UCBL/custom arch support), PT for PTT strengthening, activity modification | Ankle-foot orthosis (Arizona brace) if orthotic fails | Reconstruction ladder (osteotomy + tendon transfer ± lateral column lengthening ± medial column stabilization) |
| PCFD (rigid or arthritic) | Rigid/semi-rigid brace, shoe modification | Cast trial for pain control | Arthrodesis (triple, double, TTC, or pantalar) |
| Cavovarus (flexible) | Lateral-wedge/full-length orthotic, lateral ankle bracing | AFO if peroneal/dorsiflexor weakness | Peroneus longus-to-brevis transfer, dorsiflexion osteotomy of 1st MT, plantar fascia release |
| Cavovarus (rigid) | Bracing, footwear modification | — | Calcaneal osteotomy (Dwyer/lateralizing), tendon transfers, ± triple arthrodesis if arthritic |
| Ankle OA | Activity modification, NSAIDs, lace-up/Arizona brace, weight loss | Intra-articular steroid (diagnostic + short-term relief) | Supramalleolar osteotomy (pre-arthritic malalignment) → TAR or arthrodesis (end-stage) |
| OLT | Protected WB 6–12 weeks, NSAIDs | Repeat MRI/WBCT if unresolved at 3 months | Bone marrow stimulation vs. OAT/ACI vs. allograft by size (see below) |
| Plantar fasciitis | Plantar fascia-specific stretching, night splints, OTC/custom orthotic, NSAIDs | ESWT after 6 weeks failed conservative care | Gastrocnemius recession ± partial plantar fasciotomy after ≥6–12 months failed non-op |
| Insertional Achilles | Heel lift, activity modification, eccentric loading (modified, avoiding deep DF) | ESWT | Haglund resection + debridement ± reattachment (>50% detachment) or Zadek osteotomy |
| Midportion Achilles | Alfredson eccentric protocol, heel lift | ESWT, PRP (mixed evidence) | Debridement ± FDL/FHL augmentation if >50% cross-section involved |
| Posterior ankle impingement | Activity modification (avoid forced plantarflexion), NSAIDs, PT | US-guided FHL sheath/posterior joint injection | Two-portal posterior hindfoot endoscopy (os trigonum/Stieda excision ± FHL release) |
| Tarsal coalition (adult) | Orthotic/UCBL, activity modification | Cast trial (diagnostic + therapeutic) | Coalition resection + interposition (if <50% facet, no arthritis) vs. arthrodesis |
| Charcot (active) | Strict offloading — TCC gold standard | Bracing transition once consolidated | Surgery only if unstable/ulcerated/infected/deformed beyond bracing |
| Diabetic ulcer | Sharp debridement, TCC offloading, glycemic control | Advanced dressings, revascularization if ischemic | Exostectomy, tendon balancing (TAL/gastroc recession), or amputation by level |
| TPD Stage I–II | Orthotic, PT (posterior tibial strengthening), NSAIDs | Immobilization trial | FDL transfer + calcaneal osteotomy ± lateral column lengthening/Cotton |
| TPD Stage III–IV | Rigid bracing | — | Triple/double arthrodesis ± deltoid reconstruction |

**Key numbers**
- PCFD: talonavicular uncoverage >40% on AP foot → consider lateral column lengthening; hindfoot moment arm/valgus on WBCT increasingly replaces plain-film measures.
- Meary's angle (lateral talo-1st MT angle) normal ≈0°; apex-plantar break >4° suggests cavus, apex-dorsal breaks suggest planus.
- Calcaneal pitch normal 17–32°; low pitch supports flatfoot, high pitch (cavus) often >30°.
- OLT size threshold for primary bone marrow stimulation: **<100–150 mm² (~<10–15 mm diameter)**; larger or cystic lesions favor restorative cartilage/bone procedures.
- Plantar fascia thickness on US/MRI >4 mm (some cite >4.5 mm) supports diagnosis of plantar fasciitis/fasciopathy.
- Corticosteroid injections for plantar fasciitis: limit to **≤2–3 lifetime injections**, spaced ≥3 months apart — repeated injection raises fat-pad atrophy and fascia rupture risk.
- Charcot temperature asymmetry >2 °C = active disease; resolution to <1–2 °C differential over serial visits signals transition to Eichenholtz stage 3.
- TCC change interval: weekly in active/ulcerated Charcot, every 1–2 weeks for diabetic ulcer offloading; average forefoot ulcer healing 6–8 weeks with good offloading compliance.
- ABI <0.9 abnormal, <0.4 or absolute toe pressure <30 mmHg / TcPO2 <30 mmHg = critical limb ischemia threshold triggering revascularization work-up before elective debridement.
- Ankle arthrodesis nonunion ~5–10% (isolated tibiotalar), rising to 15–30% in Charcot/neuropathic or TTC/pantalar fusions.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Hindfoot alignment and the PTT–spring ligament–deltoid complex
The hindfoot functions as a coupled system: the **tibialis posterior tendon (PTT)** inverts/adducts the hindfoot and supports the medial longitudinal arch during stance; the **spring ligament complex** (superomedial, medioplantar oblique, inferoplantar longitudinal bands) supports the talar head from beneath; and the **deltoid ligament** (superficial and deep bands) restrains talar tilt and eversion at the tibiotalar joint. Progressive failure of this chain — PTT tendinosis/rupture → spring ligament attenuation → peritalar subluxation at the talonavicular joint → secondary deltoid insufficiency and ankle valgus — is the pathomechanical basis of **progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD)**, the term now preferred over "adult acquired flatfoot deformity" because it reflects the multiplanar, progressive nature of the disease rather than a single tendon's failure.

### Cavovarus mechanics
Cavovarus results from a muscular imbalance, most commonly relative overpull of **peroneus longus over tibialis anterior** and **tibialis posterior over peroneus brevis**, plantarflexing the first ray and inverting the hindfoot. Roughly 20–30% of adult cavovarus feet have an identifiable neurologic cause — **Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease (CMT1A most common)**, other hereditary motor-sensory neuropathies, spinal dysraphism, tethered cord, or prior compartment syndrome/peroneal nerve injury — making neurologic screening mandatory in any adult presenting with new or asymmetric cavovarus.

### Ankle joint and osteoarthritis
Unlike the hip and knee, most ankle OA (≈70–80%) is **post-traumatic** (rotational ankle fracture, chronic instability, pilon fracture) rather than primary/idiopathic, reflecting the ankle's smaller contact area and higher peak cartilage stress per unit load. This shifts the epidemiology younger and makes deformity correction (supramalleolar osteotomy) a viable pre-arthritic strategy that has no true analog in hip/knee OA.

### Osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT)
Most lesions occur at the **posteromedial talar dome** (deeper, cup-shaped, often chronic/atraumatic) or **anterolateral talar dome** (shallower, wafer-shaped, typically traumatic/inversion injury). Pathophysiology involves subchondral bone microtrauma, vascular compromise, and cyst formation beneath an intact or fissured cartilage cap; healing potential correlates with subchondral bone integrity more than cartilage appearance alone.

### Plantar fascia
The plantar fascia originates from the medial calcaneal tuberosity and is a **degenerative (fasciopathy/fasciosis)** rather than purely inflammatory process in chronic cases — histology shows myxoid degeneration, collagen disorganization, and neovascularization rather than acute inflammatory infiltrate, which explains the shift in evidence away from repeated corticosteroid injection toward mechanotherapy-based treatments.

### Achilles tendon
The Achilles has a hypovascular **watershed zone 2–6 cm proximal to the insertion**, the site of most midportion tendinopathy/rupture. **Insertional** disease occurs at the calcaneal enthesis, frequently with a Haglund deformity (prominent posterosuperior calcaneal tuberosity) and retrocalcaneal bursitis, and behaves as a distinct entity from midportion disease with different loading biomechanics (deep dorsiflexion loads the insertion adversely, hence modified eccentric protocols).

### Posterior ankle impingement
Bony (os trigonum, prominent Stieda process of the lateral talar tubercle) or soft-tissue (posterior capsule/synovium, FHL tenosynovitis) structures become compressed between the posterior tibial plafond and calcaneal tuberosity during forced plantarflexion — classically in ballet dancers and downhill/plantarflexion-loading athletes ("dancer's/footballer's ankle").

### Tarsal coalition
A congenital fibrous, cartilaginous, or osseous bridge — most commonly **calcaneonavicular (middle facet region anteriorly)** or **talocalcaneal (middle facet)** — restricts subtalar/midtarsal motion. Adults present later than the classic adolescent "peroneal spastic flatfoot" either because a fibrous/cartilaginous coalition ossifies with age or because coalitions were previously subclinical and decompensate after trauma or with age-related degeneration of compensatory joints.

### Charcot neuroarthropathy
Combines the **neurotraumatic theory** (repetitive unperceived microtrauma in an insensate foot) and **neurovascular theory** (autonomic dysfunction causes increased blood flow and osteoclast activation via **RANKL/OPG pathway dysregulation**, producing localized osteolysis and bone fragmentation) — most authors now accept both mechanisms act together in a genetically susceptible, densely neuropathic patient, typically with intact/increased perfusion (distinguishing it from ischemic diabetic complications).

### Diabetic foot ulcer
Ulceration results from the triad of **peripheral neuropathy** (loss of protective sensation), **peripheral arterial disease** (present in up to 50% of diabetic foot ulcers), and **repetitive mechanical stress** over a bony prominence, with **structural deformity** (Charcot, hallux valgus, claw toes, prior amputation) concentrating pressure at specific sites.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

- **PCFD/TPD**: single-leg heel rise (loss/weakness = PTT dysfunction), "too-many-toes" sign from behind, double-leg heel rise for hindfoot flexibility, standing exam for forefoot abduction and talonavicular uncoverage, Silfverskiöld test to distinguish gastrocnemius-only vs. gastroc-soleus equinus contracture, manual medial column stability testing (dorsiflexion of first ray).
- **Cavovarus**: **Coleman block test** (see Classification), peroneal strength testing, careful heel alignment assessment from behind, full neurologic exam (deep tendon reflexes, intrinsic hand muscle wasting, pes cavus in family members), consider EMG/nerve conduction studies and spine/neuraxis MRI when idiopathic diagnosis is not clearly established or exam is asymmetric.
- **Ankle OA**: gait assessment for coronal malalignment, weightbearing AP/lateral/mortise radiographs, **hindfoot alignment (Saltzman) view**, CT for deformity/bone stock planning, consider adjacent joint (subtalar) assessment since combined ankle+hindfoot arthritis changes the surgical plan.
- **OLT**: MRI is the primary modality for cartilage/subchondral bone edema and staging (Hepple); **weightbearing CT (WBCT)** increasingly used for accurate lesion size/depth/containment mapping and surgical planning, superior to conventional CT for true weightbearing geometry.
- **Plantar fasciitis**: largely clinical — plantar medial heel pain worst with first steps in the morning or after rest ("post-static dyspnia"), tenderness at the medial calcaneal tuberosity; ultrasound or MRI (fascia thickness >4 mm) reserved for atypical presentation, failure to improve, or to rule out fascia rupture/tumor/stress fracture.
- **Achilles**: palpation for insertional vs. midportion tenderness, Haglund prominence, Thompson test to exclude rupture, single-leg heel raise, ultrasound (dynamic, real-time, operator-dependent) or MRI (better for surgical planning/percentage cross-sectional involvement) to grade tendinosis extent.
- **Posterior impingement**: forced passive plantarflexion reproducing posterior pain ("posterior impingement test"), FHL resisted great toe flexion with ankle plantarflexed, diagnostic US-guided local anesthetic injection into the posterior recess is both diagnostic and prognostic for scope success.
- **Tarsal coalition**: restricted/painful subtalar motion, peroneal spasm (classic but often absent in adults), "C-sign" on lateral radiograph (talocalcaneal coalition), anteater nose sign (calcaneonavicular), **CT is the gold standard** for bony coalitions, MRI better for fibrous/cartilaginous coalitions.
- **Charcot/diabetic foot**: **loss of protective sensation** via 10 g Semmes-Weinstein monofilament (4 of 10 standard sites) and 128 Hz vibration/tuning fork, palpate pulses (dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial), **ABI/toe-brachial index**, probe-to-bone test for suspected osteomyelitis, plain radiographs first-line, MRI to distinguish Charcot from osteomyelitis when radiographs are equivocal (though both can produce marrow edema — clinical correlation and lack of sinus tract/ulcer favors Charcot), inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP/procalcitonin) support but do not confirm infection.

**Differential diagnosis pitfalls**

| Presentation | Mimics/confounders to exclude |
|---|---|
| Warm, swollen, non-ulcerated diabetic foot | Charcot vs. cellulitis vs. gout vs. deep vein thrombosis — MRI/nuclear imaging and clinical trend (response to elevation/offloading) help differentiate when radiographs are equivocal |
| Medial heel pain | Plantar fasciitis vs. tarsal tunnel syndrome vs. calcaneal stress fracture vs. fat pad atrophy vs. S1 radiculopathy |
| Posterior ankle pain | Posterior impingement vs. Achilles tendinopathy vs. FHL tenosynovitis vs. os trigonum fracture vs. subtle Achilles partial tear |
| Progressive flatfoot | PCFD/TPD vs. Charcot (always check sensation/HbA1c) vs. inflammatory arthropathy (RA, seronegative) vs. tarsal coalition decompensation |
| New adult cavovarus | Idiopathic vs. CMT/hereditary neuropathy vs. spinal cord tumor/tethered cord vs. residual clubfoot vs. peroneal nerve injury |

## Classification

### PCFD consensus classification (replacing Johnson-Strom/Myerson)
The **2020 international consensus** (de Cesar Netto et al., *Foot & Ankle International*) replaced the linear **Johnson & Strom stages I–IV** (and Myerson's addition of stage IIIB/IV subtypes for ankle valgus) with a **multiplanar, component-based system**. Rather than a single stage number, each foot is described across independent deformity axes, each graded present/absent and flexible/rigid, plus arthritis status. This better captures the reality that patients mix-and-match deformity components and guides an à la carte reconstruction rather than a fixed stage-based algorithm.

| Deformity component | What is assessed | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Hindfoot valgus | Standing heel valgus, subtalar joint alignment | Drives need for medializing calcaneal osteotomy (flexible) or subtalar fusion (rigid/arthritic) |
| Peritalar subluxation / talonavicular (TN) uncoverage | % TN uncoverage on AP foot or WBCT | >40% uncoverage favors adding lateral column lengthening; severe uncoverage with arthritis favors TN or triple fusion |
| Forefoot abduction | Talo-1st MT angle on AP, talonavicular coverage angle | Guides lateral column lengthening (Evans osteotomy or calcaneocuboid distraction arthrodesis) |
| Forefoot varus / medial ray instability | Dorsiflexion of 1st ray relative to hindfoot with hindfoot corrected | Guides Cotton osteotomy (medial cuneiform opening wedge) or 1st TMT/naviculocuneiform fusion |
| Ankle valgus / deltoid insufficiency | Tibiotalar tilt on mortise/WBCT, deltoid integrity on MRI | Flexible: deltoid reconstruction alongside hindfoot correction. Rigid/arthritic: consider TTC or pantalar fusion |
| Degenerative changes | Subtalar, talonavicular, calcaneocuboid, ankle joint arthritis on imaging | Presence of arthritis converts a joint-preserving plan (osteotomies/tendon transfer) into a fusion plan for that joint |

**Practical translation**: a "flexible, no-arthritis" foot with isolated hindfoot valgus and mild TN uncoverage is managed with **MCO + FDL transfer**; add lateral column lengthening as uncoverage increases; add Cotton osteotomy/medial column fusion when forefoot varus/medial instability is present; add deltoid reconstruction when ankle valgus is flexible; any joint with fixed deformity or arthritis is fused rather than osteotomized. The old Johnson-Strom/Myerson stages remain useful shorthand in conversation (Stage I = tendinosis, no deformity; Stage II = flexible deformity; Stage III = rigid deformity/arthritis of hindfoot; Stage IV = ankle valgus/deltoid involvement) but are no longer precise enough to plan surgery from alone.

### Coleman block test interpretation
A block (typically 2.5–4 cm) is placed under the lateral foot from the heel to just proximal to the first metatarsal head, allowing the first ray to hang freely off the medial edge.

| Result | Interpretation | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Hindfoot corrects to neutral or valgus with block | **Flexible hindfoot** — cavus is forefoot-driven (plantarflexed first ray from peroneus longus overpull) | Forefoot-only correction: first ray dorsiflexion osteotomy, peroneus longus-to-brevis transfer, plantar fascia release. No calcaneal osteotomy needed |
| Hindfoot remains varus with block | **Fixed/rigid hindfoot deformity** independent of the forefoot | Requires hindfoot correction: lateralizing or Dwyer calcaneal osteotomy, ± tendon transfers; triple arthrodesis if arthritic |

Limitations: unreliable in severe/rigid deformity, requires an examiner experienced in observing subtle correction, and does not substitute for WBCT-based 3D assessment increasingly used pre-operatively.

### Eichenholtz classification (Charcot)
| Stage | Name | Radiographic/clinical findings | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (added by later authors, not original Eichenholtz) | Prodromal/inflammatory | Normal or near-normal radiographs, but clinical warmth/swelling/edema; MRI shows bone marrow edema | Highest index of suspicion stage — begin strict offloading (NWB or TCC) immediately even before radiographic changes appear, as this is when deformity can still be prevented |
| I | Development (fragmentation) | Osseous fragmentation, joint subluxation/dislocation, debris formation | Strict immobilization, non-weightbearing progressing to TCC, no elective surgery unless limb-threatening |
| II | Coalescence | Absorption of debris, early new bone formation, sclerosis, fusion of larger fragments begins | Continue offloading, begin cautious progression toward weightbearing in TCC as stability improves |
| III | Reconstruction (consolidation) | Consolidation, remodeling, arch/joint contours re-establish (often with residual deformity) | Transition to bracing (CROW walker), gradual return to protected weightbearing and therapeutic footwear; elective reconstructive surgery, if needed, is best performed here |

Clinical resolution of warmth (temperature differential normalizing) and radiographic consolidation both guide stage transitions — treat the clinical exam trend, not a single radiograph.

### Brodsky classification (anatomic, Charcot)
| Type | Location | Notes | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Midfoot (tarsometatarsal/naviculocuneiform, Lisfranc region) | Most common (~60–70%) | Rocker-bottom deformity risk; if plantigrade, brace (CROW); exostectomy for ulcerating plantar prominence; realignment arthrodesis if unstable/recurrent ulcer |
| 2 | Hindfoot (subtalar, talonavicular, calcaneocuboid) | Less common but higher instability | Higher risk of progressive collapse and instability — lower threshold for surgical stabilization (beaming/arthrodesis) |
| 3A | Ankle | Least common, most unstable | Highest risk of limb-threatening instability; often needs TTC arthrodesis with intramedullary nail or ring fixator |
| 3B | Calcaneus (insufficiency fracture of tuberosity) | Distinct mechanism (fracture, not joint collapse) | Usually managed with offloading/immobilization; surgery reserved for severe displacement |

### Hepple / Berndt-Harty classification (osteochondral lesion of the talus)
**Berndt-Harty (1959, plain radiograph-based)**: Stage I compression of subchondral bone; II incomplete avulsion fragment; III complete avulsion, non-displaced; IV displaced fragment; (V, added later) subchondral cyst.

**Hepple (MRI-based, 1999 — preferred modern staging)**
| Stage | MRI findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Articular cartilage damage only | Trial of non-operative management (protected WB, NSAIDs, activity modification) |
| 2a | Cartilage injury with underlying subchondral fracture and surrounding bone marrow edema | Non-op trial reasonable; MRI edema suggests instability risk — shorter non-op trial before considering scope |
| 2b | Same as 2a but without surrounding edema | More stable pattern; longer non-op trial reasonable |
| 3 | Cartilage injury with an underlying detached, non-displaced osteochondral fragment | Failed non-op → surgery; fragment may be amenable to fixation if large/acute |
| 4 | Detached and displaced fragment | Surgical (fragment usually not salvageable — treat as a cartilage defect: BMS or restorative procedure) |
| 5 | Subchondral cyst formation | Cystic/large lesions favor restorative procedures (OAT, ACI, bone grafting) over bone marrow stimulation alone |

### IDSA/IWGDF and WIfI classification (diabetic foot)

**IDSA/IWGDF infection severity grading** — applied once a wound is present; grades local and systemic infection to set the treatment site and antibiotic route.

| Grade | Clinical findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Uninfected | No purulence or inflammation | No antibiotics; local wound care and offloading only |
| Mild | Local infection involving only skin/subcutaneous tissue, erythema 0.5–2 cm around the wound | Outpatient management, narrow-spectrum oral antibiotics, close follow-up |
| Moderate | Local infection with erythema >2 cm, or involving deeper structures (tendon, muscle, joint, bone), no systemic inflammatory response | Usually requires surgical debridement/source control; oral or outpatient IV antibiotics depending on extent; imaging (MRI or bone biopsy) if osteomyelitis suspected |
| Severe | Local infection plus systemic inflammatory response (SIRS: fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, leukocytosis) or metabolic instability | Hospital admission, IV antibiotics, urgent surgical debridement/drainage, sepsis workup — limb- and life-threatening |

**WIfI classification (Society for Vascular Surgery)** — grades **W**ound, **I**schemia, and foot **I**nfection each 0–3, combined into a clinical stage that predicts 1-year amputation risk and the expected benefit of revascularization.

| Component | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wound (W) | No ulcer/gangrene | Small, shallow ulcer, no gangrene | Deeper ulcer exposing bone/joint/tendon, or limited forefoot gangrene | Extensive, full-thickness ulcer or extensive gangrene |
| Ischemia (I) | ABI ≥0.8, toe pressure ≥60 mmHg | ABI 0.6–0.79, toe pressure 40–59 mmHg | ABI 0.4–0.59, toe pressure 30–39 mmHg | ABI <0.4, toe pressure <30 mmHg |
| foot Infection (fI) | None | Mild (per IDSA above) | Moderate | Severe |

- **Management implication**: the combined clinical stage (1 = very low, through 4 = high risk of major amputation at 1 year) determines urgency of revascularization and the aggressiveness of local wound management — a high W/fI grade with low ischemia can often be managed with debridement/offloading alone, whereas a high I grade (regardless of wound size) mandates a vascular work-up and likely revascularization before or concurrent with any debridement, since wound healing potential is perfusion-limited above all else.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **PCFD/TPD (flexible, Stage I–II equivalent)**: custom UCBL or semi-rigid orthotic with medial posting, gastroc-soleus stretching, formal PT for eccentric/concentric PTT strengthening (evidence-based protocols such as Kulig et al. show improved pain and function), NSAIDs for tenosynovitis; progress to an articulated or solid-ankle AFO (Arizona brace) if orthotic insufficient. A 3–6 month structured trial is reasonable before considering surgery in a compliant patient without arthritis.
- **Cavovarus (flexible)**: lateral-wedge or full-length orthotic to offload the lateral column, ankle stability bracing for recurrent sprains, AFO for significant peroneal/dorsiflexor weakness (foot drop component), footwear with a wide toe box for claw toe accommodation.
- **Ankle OA**: activity modification, weight loss, NSAIDs, unloader-style lace-up ankle brace or an Arizona-style AFO to reduce coronal/sagittal motion through the arthritic joint, assistive devices (cane contralateral hand). There is no ankle-specific OARSI guideline equivalent to knee/hip OA, but the general OA principles (exercise, weight management, bracing before injection before surgery) are extrapolated by most foot & ankle societies.
- **OLT**: protected weightbearing (boot ± crutches) for 6–12 weeks, activity modification, NSAIDs; reasonable first-line for Hepple 1–2b lesions, especially in low-demand or first-presentation patients.
- **Plantar fasciitis — evidence ladder**: (1) **plantar fascia-specific stretching** (DiGiovanni protocol) has the strongest evidence base among conservative measures and should be first-line for essentially all patients; (2) Achilles/gastroc-soleus stretching and night splints; (3) OTC or custom orthotics; (4) NSAIDs for short-term pain control (2–4 weeks); (5) avoid repeated corticosteroid injection as primary therapy — reserve for refractory pain limiting participation in stretching, and cap at 2–3 lifetime injections given fat-pad atrophy/fascia rupture risk; (6) low-level laser and iontophoresis have weaker, more mixed evidence. Roughly 80–90% of patients improve with structured conservative care within 6–12 months.
- **Achilles (insertional and midportion)**: **eccentric loading is first-line** — the classic **Alfredson heavy-load eccentric protocol** (twice daily, 3×15 reps, both knee-straight and knee-bent, 12 weeks) has the strongest evidence for **midportion** disease; for **insertional** disease the protocol is modified to avoid deep dorsiflexion beyond neutral (which compresses the insertion against the calcaneus) — heel lifts and a floor-level (not stair-edge) eccentric protocol are preferred. NSAIDs for short courses, activity modification, and correction of training errors.
- **Posterior ankle impingement**: activity modification avoiding forced/repetitive plantarflexion, NSAIDs, PT focusing on FHL flexibility and posterior capsule mobility; often effective in non-athletes or those able to modify activity.
- **Tarsal coalition (adult)**: orthotic/UCBL to limit subtalar motion, activity modification, NSAIDs; a **cast trial (4–6 weeks NWB or WB cast)** is both diagnostic (symptom relief supports the coalition as pain generator) and can occasionally provide durable relief.
- **Charcot (active)**: **strict offloading is the cornerstone of treatment** — total contact casting (TCC) remains the gold standard, changed weekly initially (more frequently if significant edema reduction expected), continued until clinical (temperature, edema) and radiographic consolidation (Eichenholtz III). Bisphosphonates have been studied as an adjunct to reduce bone turnover but evidence for altering the disease course remains inconsistent and they are not a substitute for offloading.
- **Diabetic foot ulcer**: sharp debridement of nonviable tissue, moisture-balanced dressings, **offloading is essential — TCC is the gold-standard, most effective offloading modality for plantar forefoot ulcers** per IWGDF; removable cast walkers rendered non-removable ("instant TCC") are a reasonable alternative when TCC expertise/resources are unavailable; glycemic optimization; smoking cessation; nutrition optimization; treat infection per IDSA/IWGDF severity grading (below).
- **TPD Stage III–IV**: rigid bracing (double upright AFO, solid-ankle AFO) when surgery is declined/contraindicated.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Corticosteroid injection**: useful for short-term pain relief in plantar fasciitis and as a diagnostic/therapeutic tool in ankle OA and posterior impingement (US-guided posterior recess/FHL sheath injection); **avoid repeated plantar fascia injections** — cumulative risk of fat-pad atrophy and fascia rupture is well described; limit to 2–3 total, ultrasound-guided when feasible.
- **Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT)**: reasonable second-line option for **chronic plantar fasciitis** (>6 weeks failed conservative care) and for chronic **Achilles tendinopathy** (both insertional and midportion) refractory to eccentric loading; meta-analyses through 2024 (Cortés-Pérez et al., *Clin Rehabil* 2024) show ESWT performs comparably to or better than corticosteroid injection for plantar fasciitis at intermediate-term follow-up with a better safety profile, though a large sham-controlled trial (Heide et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2024) tempers enthusiasm by showing a smaller effect size over sham than earlier open-label studies suggested — counsel patients that ESWT modestly outperforms placebo, is not curative, and typically requires 3+ sessions.
- **PRP**: mixed and generally underwhelming evidence for both plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathy compared with ESWT or standard care (Daher et al., *Foot Ankle Int* 2024) — reasonable as an adjunct/alternative in refractory cases with informed shared decision-making, not first-line.
- **Viscosupplementation**: limited and inconsistent evidence in the ankle (extrapolated from knee OA literature); not broadly recommended by AAOS or specialty societies for ankle OA.
- **US-guided diagnostic/therapeutic injections**: valuable for posterior impingement (posterior recess or FHL sheath) both to confirm the pain generator and to provide symptomatic relief, and for peritendinous (not intratendinous) Achilles injections in selected refractory cases.

### Operative

**PCFD reconstruction ladder** (build additively based on which consensus-classification components are present and whether flexible or rigid):
1. **Base construct (flexible, no arthritis)**: medializing calcaneal osteotomy (MCO) + FDL tendon transfer to the navicular ± gastrocnemius recession or Achilles tendon lengthening for equinus contracture.
2. **Add spring ligament repair/reconstruction** when imaging/intraoperative exam shows attenuation contributing to talar head plantar-medial sag.
3. **Add lateral column lengthening** (Evans osteotomy through the anterior calcaneus, or calcaneocuboid distraction arthrodesis if CC joint arthritic) when talonavicular uncoverage is significant (>40%) or forefoot abduction persists after MCO.
4. **Add medial column stabilization** — Cotton osteotomy (dorsal opening-wedge medial cuneiform osteotomy) for flexible forefoot varus, or first TMT/naviculocuneiform fusion (modified Lapidus-type) for medial column instability/hypermobility.
5. **Add deltoid ligament reconstruction** (allograft or FDL-based) when ankle valgus is flexible and the deltoid is attenuated but the ankle joint is preserved.
6. **Convert to arthrodesis** for any joint with fixed/rigid deformity or arthritis: isolated talonavicular fusion (often sufficient — TN motion drives most hindfoot motion), double (TN+ST) or **triple arthrodesis** for combined subtalar/talonavicular/calcaneocuboid disease, **tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC)** fusion when ankle arthritis/valgus coexists with rigid hindfoot disease, or **pantalar fusion** for combined ankle+hindfoot+midfoot arthritis (rare, high morbidity, reserved for salvage).
- Pearls: undercorrection of forefoot abduction/medial column instability is the most common cause of recurrent deformity after isolated MCO+FDL transfer — do not skip lateral column lengthening or medial stabilization when indicated by the consensus classification. Achilles/gastroc equinus must be addressed or correction will recur.

**Cavovarus foot surgery**: driven by Coleman block result and whether the deformity is idiopathic/neurologic and progressive.
- **Flexible, forefoot-driven (block corrects)**: peroneus longus-to-brevis transfer (removes the deforming force and augments eversion), dorsiflexion (closing-wedge) osteotomy of the first metatarsal to correct the plantarflexed ray, plantar fascia release for a tight/contracted fascia, tibialis anterior/posterior transfer for muscle imbalance in neurologic cases (e.g., CMT), claw toe correction (flexor-to-extensor transfer, interphalangeal fusion) as needed.
- **Fixed hindfoot varus (block does not correct)**: lateralizing or **Dwyer closing-wedge calcaneal osteotomy** added to the forefoot procedures above; **lateral ligament reconstruction** if chronic instability coexists (common given the varus-driven inversion sprain pattern).
- **Rigid/arthritic or severe/neglected deformity**: **triple arthrodesis** with concurrent soft-tissue balancing, occasionally combined with a first metatarsal or midfoot osteotomy for residual forefoot deformity.
- Pearls: always correct the deforming muscular imbalance (tendon transfer) in the same setting as bony correction in neurologic cavovarus (e.g., CMT) or the deformity will recur; screen and counsel for progressive underlying neurologic disease preoperatively, as it affects both prognosis and the durability of soft-tissue balancing.

**Supramalleolar osteotomy** (pre-arthritic or early ankle OA with coronal malalignment): realigns the tibial plafond to unload the damaged compartment (medial opening-wedge for varus malalignment/medial OA, lateral closing-wedge for valgus) — a joint-preserving option in younger, active patients with early/moderate OA and a correctable deformity, deferring arthrodesis/arthroplasty by years; often combined with fibular osteotomy, ligament balancing, or concurrent hindfoot correction (PCFD/cavovarus) when the deformity originates outside the ankle itself.

**Total ankle replacement vs. arthrodesis** (end-stage ankle OA):
- **Arthrodesis** (open compression screw fixation, or arthroscopic for minimal deformity) remains the reliable reference standard — predictable pain relief, fusion rates typically 85–95% in appropriately selected patients (lower in smokers, AVN, obesity, revision settings), at the cost of stiffness and accelerated adjacent-joint (subtalar, midfoot) arthritis over 10–20 years.
- **Total ankle replacement (TAR)** preserves motion and reduces adjacent-joint stress; best candidates are older (>50–55 years), lower-demand patients with preserved/correctable coronal alignment (generally <10–15° deformity with modern designs able to address more via concurrent osteotomy/ligament balancing), adequate bone stock, and no significant talar avascular necrosis, Charcot, or uncontrolled diabetic neuropathy (relative/absolute contraindications). Modern fixed- and mobile-bearing designs report ~90% implant survivorship at 10 years in registries and selected series, though revision surgery when needed is more complex than revision knee/hip arthroplasty.
- **TARVA trial** (UK multicenter RCT, Goldberg et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2022; HTA report 2023): randomized ~303 patients aged 50–85 to TAR vs. arthrodesis; both groups improved substantially and similarly on the MOXFQ at 3 years, but with different complication and reoperation profiles between arms; the accompanying HTA monograph explicitly describes the trial as **inconclusive regarding superiority of either procedure**, reinforcing that the choice should be individualized (age, activity demand, deformity, bone quality, adjacent-joint status) through shared decision-making rather than dictated by a single trial.
- Pearls: correct coronal/sagittal deformity concurrently (with osteotomy or ligament balancing) rather than relying on the implant alone to accommodate malalignment; address hindfoot deformity (PCFD/cavovarus) before or simultaneously with TAR — an uncorrected hindfoot is a leading cause of edge-loading and early failure.

**OLT treatment by size**:
- **Primary lesion <100–150 mm² (~<10–15 mm diameter), Hepple 1–2b or failed non-op**: arthroscopic **bone marrow stimulation** (microfracture/drilling) — reliable first-line surgery with good short/mid-term outcomes; a 2024 10-year survivorship study (Rikken et al., *JBJS Am*) confirms durable but not universal benefit, with a meaningful subset requiring secondary procedures over time, reinforcing size/lesion-quality selection.
- **≥100–150 mm², cystic (Hepple 5), or failed prior BMS**: restorative options — **osteochondral autograft transfer (OATS/mosaicplasty)** from the ipsilateral knee, **particulated juvenile allograft cartilage**, or **autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI/MACI)**, typically requiring a medial or lateral malleolar osteotomy for posterior/central lesion access.
- **Large lesions with subchondral bone loss**: bone grafting with or without BMS ("sandwich" technique), or **fresh osteochondral allograft** for very large or revision lesions where autograft would create unacceptable donor-site morbidity.
- **Hepple 3 (acute, displaceable fragment of adequate size)**: primary **fixation** (headless compression screws or bioabsorbable pins) rather than excision, preserving native cartilage — best results in acute, large, reducible fragments.
- Pearls: WBCT-based lesion mapping increasingly guides the size/containment decision more accurately than conventional CT; always assess and correct concurrent hindfoot malalignment (varus/valgus) or chronic ankle instability, as uncorrected malalignment is a major driver of OLT recurrence/progression.

**Achilles surgery**:
- **Insertional**: Haglund exostectomy + debridement of the diseased insertion through a central-splitting or medial approach; if **>50% of the insertion is detached** during debridement, reattach with suture anchors (single- or double-row technique — systematic review data show comparable outcomes between single- and double-row constructs, with double-row offering a biomechanically stronger footprint) ± **FHL transfer** for augmentation in extensive debridement, revision cases, or lower-demand/elderly patients needing tendon strength support. **Zadek osteotomy** (dorsal closing-wedge calcaneal osteotomy) is an increasingly used alternative that leaves the tendon insertion undisturbed, unloading the insertion indirectly — 2024 series report good outcomes and a defined learning curve, with earlier weightbearing than detachment-reattachment techniques, though data on long-term recurrence remain more limited than for traditional debridement.
- **Midportion**: open or percutaneous longitudinal tenotomy/debridement of degenerative tissue; **gastrocnemius recession** (isolated gastroc contracture on Silfverskiöld testing) unloads the tendon and is increasingly used as an adjunct or alternative to direct tendon debridement; **FHL transfer** when >50% of tendon cross-section is debrided or in revision/chronic degenerative cases.
- A 2025 meta-analysis (Chen et al., *J Exp Orthop*) comparing endoscopic vs. open surgery for insertional Achilles tendinopathy found comparable clinical outcomes with reduced wound complication rates favoring endoscopic/minimally invasive approaches, supporting a shift toward less invasive techniques where the surgeon has the requisite experience.

**Posterior ankle impingement**: **two-portal posterior hindfoot endoscopy** (van Dijk technique, prone position, posterolateral and posteromedial portals) for excision of os trigonum/prominent Stieda process, debridement of posterior soft tissue impingement, and FHL release/debridement when concurrent FHL tenosynovitis is present; contemporary reviews (Anastasio et al., *Foot Ankle Int* 2024) confirm this as the standard of care with high satisfaction and rapid return to sport in athletes, reserving open posteromedial or posterolateral approaches for large fracture fragments, revision cases, or concurrent procedures not amenable to endoscopy.

**Tarsal coalition (adult)**: after failed conservative management, **coalition resection with interposition** (fat graft, extensor digitorum brevis muscle, or bone wax) for calcaneonavicular coalitions and for talocalcaneal coalitions involving **<50% of the posterior facet without significant secondary arthritis**; for coalitions >50% of the facet, extensive degenerative change, or failed prior resection, proceed directly to **subtalar or triple arthrodesis** rather than resection.

**Charcot surgery**: indications are recurrent or non-healing ulceration over a bony prominence, gross instability precluding safe bracing, deformity precluding shoe wear, and infection — surgery is otherwise avoided during active (Eichenholtz 0–II) disease except when limb-threatening. Elective reconstruction is ideally deferred to **Eichenholtz stage III (consolidation)**. Techniques: **exostectomy** alone for a stable foot with an isolated plantar bony prominence; **realignment arthrodesis using the "superconstruct" principle** (Sammarco) — fusion extended beyond the zone of injury to healthy bone, maximally rigid fixation (axial/beaming intramedullary screws, locking plates, combined internal + external fixation), generous bone grafting, and prolonged immobilization/protected weightbearing, reflecting the impaired bone-healing biology of the neuropathic host; **circular external fixation** for unstable, infected, or severely compromised soft-tissue cases, often as a staged adjunct to internal fixation; **TTC arthrodesis with a retrograde intramedullary nail** for combined ankle + hindfoot Charcot (Brodsky 3A) given the instability and limb-threat associated with this pattern.

**Diabetic foot ulcer/infection surgery and limb salvage**: sharp debridement to bleeding, viable tissue; revascularization (endovascular or open bypass) before or concurrent with debridement when WIfI ischemia grade indicates benefit; **surgical resection of infected/necrotic bone with culture-directed antibiotics** for osteomyelitis, though a growing body of evidence supports that some cases can be managed with prolonged antibiotics alone when radical resection is not feasible or would be excessively morbid; **Achilles tendon lengthening or gastrocnemius recession** to reduce forefoot plantar pressure and promote healing/prevent recurrence of recalcitrant forefoot ulcers (a well-established adjunct, with the trade-off of a small risk of calcaneal gait/heel ulceration from overlengthening); amputation level selection (toe, ray, transmetatarsal, below-knee, above-knee) balances perfusion, tissue viability, function, and likelihood of achieving a durable, shoeable/prosthetic-fitting residual limb. **Limb salvage is a multidisciplinary team effort** — vascular surgery, infectious disease, endocrinology/diabetology, orthopedic/podiatric foot & ankle surgery, wound care nursing, and orthotics/prosthetics — the "toe and flow" model (aggressive local wound/bone management plus aggressive perfusion optimization) is associated with reduced major amputation rates in cohort data.

**TPD operative** (mirrors the PCFD ladder since TPD is the tendon-driven subset of PCFD): Stage I (tenosynovitis, no deformity) failing non-op → tenosynovectomy/debridement; Stage II (flexible deformity) → FDL transfer + MCO ± lateral column lengthening ± Cotton osteotomy/medial column fusion ± spring ligament repair, exactly per the PCFD ladder above; Stage III (rigid/arthritic) → triple or double arthrodesis; Stage IV (ankle valgus/deltoid insufficiency) → add deltoid reconstruction to a Stage III construct, or proceed to TTC/pantalar fusion when ankle arthritis is also present.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

- **Hindfoot osteotomy/tendon transfer (MCO+FDL ± lateral column lengthening)**: 0–2 weeks splint, non-weightbearing (NWB), elevation; 2–6 weeks NWB in cast or removable boot with early passive/active-assisted ankle ROM initiated around 2–4 weeks if soft-tissue healing allows; 6–10/12 weeks progressive weightbearing in a controlled ankle motion (CAM) boot as radiographs show osteotomy consolidation; 10–12 weeks transition to supportive shoewear with orthotic/brace, begin formal PT for gait retraining, PTT/gastroc-soleus strengthening; return to low-impact work/activity by 3–4 months; return to sport/high-demand activity 5–6 months, contingent on radiographic union and functional strength symmetry.
- **Arthrodesis (triple, TTC, pantalar)**: 2 weeks splint NWB; 6 weeks NWB in cast (non-Charcot) — **for Charcot/neuropathic patients extend NWB to 8–12+ weeks** given impaired bone healing biology; serial radiographs (± CT if union uncertain) before progressing; gradual weightbearing progression over 4–6 weeks once union is confirmed; brace/orthotic support and PT for 3–6 months; return to sedentary work ~2–3 months, physical work/sport typically 6–9 months and only after solid union.
- **Total ankle replacement**: protocol varies by implant/approach but a common pathway is splint NWB 2 weeks, then transition to a CAM boot with progressive weightbearing beginning around 2–4 weeks (faster than arthrodesis due to press-fit/cemented fixation not requiring bony union across a joint), early ROM exercises started once the wound is stable; formal PT emphasizing ankle ROM, gait training, and proprioception from 4–6 weeks; return to low-impact activity 3–4 months; avoid high-impact/running activity indefinitely per most surgeons given implant longevity concerns.
- **OLT — bone marrow stimulation**: NWB or touch-down WB 2–6 weeks depending on lesion size/location, early passive ROM/CPM encouraged to promote fibrocartilage organization, progressive WB from 6 weeks, full activity 3–4 months. **Restorative procedures (OAT/ACI)**: more conservative — NWB 6 weeks, protected WB to 10–12 weeks, full activity typically not before 6–9 months given the biology of graft incorporation/maturation.
- **Plantar fasciitis (conservative)**: structured stretching program minimum 8–12 weeks before escalating (fascia-specific stretching 3×/day, 10 reps held 10 seconds, per DiGiovanni protocol), reassess for ESWT candidacy if inadequately improved at 6 weeks, surgical candidacy generally not considered before 6–12 months of documented compliant conservative care.
- **Achilles repair (insertional detachment-reattachment)**: splint/NWB 2 weeks, boot with heel wedge NWB to partial WB 2–6 weeks (bone-tendon healing at the calcaneal insertion is slower than midsubstance repair), progressive WB 6–10 weeks as wedges are gradually removed, formal PT with progressive ROM and eccentric strengthening from ~8–10 weeks, return to running/sport 4–6 months. **Zadek osteotomy** allows comparatively earlier progressive WB (often boot WB from 2 weeks) since the insertion itself is undisturbed, with osteotomy union guiding progression (~6 weeks).
- **Posterior hindfoot endoscopy**: WBAT in a supportive boot or shoe within days, early ROM, return to sport typically 4–8 weeks depending on sport demands and whether concurrent FHL or ligament work was performed.
- **Charcot (active)**: TCC weekly initially, transitioning as edema/swelling stabilizes; strict NWB or minimal WB throughout Eichenholtz 0–II; once stage III consolidation is confirmed, transition to a **CROW (Charcot restraint orthotic walker) or rigid AFO**, gradual progression to therapeutic footwear over subsequent months; many patients require **lifelong bracing** to prevent recurrence/re-ulceration, particularly with midfoot (Brodsky 1) rocker-bottom residual deformity.
- **Diabetic ulcer offloading**: TCC or instant-TCC changed weekly to every 2 weeks; typical healing trajectory for a well-offloaded, adequately perfused plantar forefoot ulcer is 6–8 weeks; failure to reduce wound area by ≥40–50% at 4 weeks predicts non-healing and should trigger reassessment (perfusion, infection, offloading compliance, adjunctive therapy).
- **Cavovarus surgery (calcaneal osteotomy ± tendon transfer)**: splint NWB 2 weeks, cast/boot NWB to 6 weeks pending osteotomy consolidation, progressive WB 6–10 weeks, formal PT for peroneal/eversion strengthening and proprioceptive/balance training (critical given the associated chronic instability), bracing for residual weakness in neurologic (CMT) cases often continues indefinitely; return to sport 4–6 months.
- **Return-to-work/sport general principles**: sedentary work resumes earliest (as soon as pain/elevation needs allow, often 2–6 weeks post-op depending on procedure); standing/walking-intensive work requires solid union/wound healing plus demonstrated weightbearing tolerance; return to impact sport requires radiographic union (osteotomy/fusion), full painless ROM, and strength ≥85–90% of the contralateral limb on functional testing.

## Complications

- **PCFD/TPD reconstruction**: undercorrection/recurrence of deformity (most common failure mode when lateral column lengthening or medial column stabilization is omitted when indicated), sural nerve injury (calcaneal osteotomy), nonunion of osteotomy/fusion sites, overcorrection to varus (lateral column lengthening), wound complications especially with concurrent multiple incisions.
- **Ankle arthrodesis**: nonunion ~5–10% isolated tibiotalar in healthy hosts, rising substantially (15–30%) in smokers, AVN, Charcot/neuropathic patients, or TTC/pantalar constructs; progressive adjacent-joint (subtalar, midfoot) arthritis over 10–20 years is near-universal and the principal long-term trade-off vs. TAR.
- **Total ankle replacement**: periprosthetic fracture, component subsidence/loosening, syndesmotic nonunion (in designs requiring syndesmotic fixation), wound healing complications (thin anterior soft-tissue envelope), deep infection (~1–2%, higher revision-surgery morbidity than nonunion after failed fusion), progressive coronal malalignment/edge loading if deformity undercorrected at index surgery, and eventual revision (bearing exchange or conversion to fusion) in a meaningful minority by 10–15 years.
- **OLT surgery**: persistent pain/incomplete relief after bone marrow stimulation in a meaningful minority even at long-term follow-up (10-year survivorship data confirm durable but incomplete success), donor-site morbidity with OATS (ipsilateral knee pain), graft delamination/failure with ACI, subchondral cyst recurrence.
- **Plantar fasciitis treatment**: fascia rupture and fat-pad atrophy from repeated corticosteroid injection, lateral column overload/pain after aggressive plantar fasciotomy (especially complete release), incomplete relief after gastrocnemius recession alone in patients without a true equinus contracture.
- **Achilles surgery**: wound dehiscence/infection (notably higher with traditional detachment-reattachment insertional approaches — a driver of interest in minimally invasive/Zadek alternatives), sural nerve injury, re-rupture, persistent insertional pain from inadequate debridement or heterotopic bone reformation.
- **Posterior impingement surgery**: sural nerve or FHL neurovascular bundle injury (technically demanding portal placement), recurrent impingement from incomplete resection.
- **Cavovarus surgery**: recurrence of deformity if the underlying muscular imbalance or progressive neurologic disease (CMT) is not addressed alongside bony correction, overcorrection to valgus (Dwyer/lateralizing osteotomy), peroneal tendon injury/subluxation, persistent lateral instability if ligament reconstruction is omitted when indicated.
- **Supramalleolar osteotomy**: nonunion/delayed union, undercorrection, progression to arthritis eventually requiring conversion to arthrodesis or TAR (osteotomy is a deferral strategy, not a permanent cure, in most patients with established cartilage loss).
- **Tarsal coalition surgery**: recurrent coalition/fibrosis after resection, incomplete pain relief if arthritis was underappreciated preoperatively, need for conversion to arthrodesis.
- **Charcot surgery**: highest complication burden in foot & ankle surgery — wound complications 20–50%, hardware failure/breakage given prolonged abnormal loading, deep infection, nonunion requiring revision with even more aggressive fixation, and a persistent risk of **recurrent or contralateral Charcot** (cohort data through 2024 suggest roughly 20–40% of patients develop recurrent or new contralateral disease over their lifetime, reinforcing the need for lifelong surveillance and bracing).
- **Diabetic foot ulcer/infection**: recurrence rate after healing is high (up to 40% at 1 year, 60%+ at 3–5 years) without ongoing offloading/footwear intervention, progression to osteomyelitis, and major amputation in patients with uncontrolled infection or critical limb ischemia not amenable to revascularization — underscoring why offloading and footwear are lifelong, not episodic, interventions.

## Recent Evidence

- **[Barbachan Mansur et al., Foot Ankle Int, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36639923/)** — demonstrates that weightbearing CT measurements materially change component-by-component classification of PCFD compared with conventional radiographs, reinforcing WBCT as an increasingly standard adjunct (not yet a mandatory requirement) when applying the consensus classification.
- **[Hong et al., J Am Acad Orthop Surg, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40523163/)** — a 2025 overview reinforcing and clarifying the PCFD consensus nomenclature and how it should be communicated and applied in practice relative to legacy Johnson-Strom/Myerson staging.
- **[Poutoglidou et al., Foot Ankle Surg, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38514277/)** — systematic review correlating patient-reported outcomes with radiographic correction in PCFD reconstruction, finding imperfect correlation and supporting outcome measurement beyond radiographs alone when counseling patients on expected benefit.
- **[Montoya-delaTorre et al., Foot Ankle Int, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40391801/)** — porous titanium wedge implants for lateral column lengthening/osteotomy augmentation show high rates of osseointegration and maintained radiographic correction, an evolving alternative to structural allograft.
- **[Jaiswal et al., Foot (Edinb), 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40349380/)** — describes a technique for combined deltoid-spring ligament complex reconstruction in PCFD, reflecting growing surgical attention to concurrent medial ligamentous reconstruction rather than bony correction alone.
- **[Goldberg et al., Ann Intern Med, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36375147/) and the associated [Health Technology Assessment report, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37022932/)** — the **TARVA RCT** primary and full-cost-effectiveness results: comparable 3-year MOXFQ improvement between TAR and arthrodesis, trial explicitly reported as **inconclusive on superiority**, the key modern evidence base clinicians should cite when counseling patients that either option is reasonable depending on individual factors.
- **[Rikken & Kerkhoffs, Foot Ankle Clin, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38679438/)** — review confirming no single OLT treatment has been established as categorically superior, and that lesion/patient-specific decision-making (size, containment, cyst presence, prior surgery) should continue to drive the size-based ladder described above.
- **[Rikken et al., J Bone Joint Surg Am, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38728384/)** — 10-year survivorship data for arthroscopic bone marrow stimulation confirming durable benefit for most patients but a meaningful failure/reoperation rate over time, supporting careful size-based patient selection rather than universal first-line use.
- **[Efrima et al., Knee Surg Sports Traumatol Arthrosc, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38591657/)** — validates weightbearing CT distance mapping as a more accurate method of quantifying OLT size/location than conventional (non-weightbearing) CT, likely to become standard pre-operative planning going forward.
- **[Cortés-Pérez et al., Clin Rehabil, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38738305/)** — meta-analysis showing ESWT is at least comparable to, and in several outcomes superior to, corticosteroid injection for chronic plantar fasciitis, with a more favorable safety profile — supports positioning ESWT ahead of repeat steroid injection in refractory cases.
- **[Heide et al., Br J Sports Med, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38904119/)** — sham-controlled trial of radial ESWT showing a smaller true treatment effect over placebo than earlier unblinded studies suggested, an important calibration for counseling patients on realistic expected benefit from ESWT.
- **[Daher et al., Foot Ankle Int, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38419209/)** — PRP shows no consistent advantage over ESWT for plantar fasciitis at 3–6 months, reinforcing ESWT (not PRP) as the preferred second-line adjunct where available.
- **[Hörterer et al., Arch Orthop Trauma Surg, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38085325/)** and **[Hall et al., Foot Ankle Int, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39219246/) / [Arch Orthop Trauma Surg, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38926196/)** — contemporary recurrence-rate and learning-curve data for surgically treated insertional Achilles tendinopathy and for the **Zadek osteotomy**, supporting Zadek as a viable, less-invasive alternative to traditional detachment-reattachment in appropriately selected patients.
- **[Chen et al., J Exp Orthop, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40708889/)** — meta-analysis finding endoscopic/minimally invasive surgery for insertional Achilles tendinopathy achieves comparable outcomes to open surgery with fewer wound complications, supporting a shift toward minimally invasive technique where surgeon experience allows.
- **[Anastasio et al., Foot Ankle Int, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37905829/)** — contemporary review confirming two-portal posterior hindfoot endoscopy as the standard approach for posterior ankle impingement/os trigonum syndrome, with a companion **[systematic review in Am J Sports Med, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38348483/)** on minimally invasive os trigonum excision showing high satisfaction and rapid return to sport.
- **[Senneville et al., Clin Infect Dis / Diabetes Metab Res Rev, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37779323/)** — the updated **IWGDF/IDSA diabetic foot infection guideline**, refining infection severity grading and antibiotic duration/route recommendations (favoring shorter courses and more selective use of IV therapy than older practice patterns).
- **[Monteiro-Soares et al., Diabetes Metab Res Rev, 2023/2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37179483/)** — updated **IWGDF risk classification guideline** for diabetic foot ulceration, refining screening intervals and risk stratification used to trigger preventive foot care and footwear referral.
- **[Raspovic et al., Diabetes Metab Res Rev, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37179484/)** — systematic review underpinning updated IWGDF guidance on diagnosis and treatment of **active Charcot neuro-osteoarthropathy**, reaffirming offloading (TCC) as the primary evidence-based intervention and highlighting the continued absence of strong evidence for any pharmacologic disease-modifying therapy.
- **[Prem et al., SAGE Open Med, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39691864/)** — systematic review comparing weightbearing vs. non-weightbearing TCC protocols in active Charcot, finding insufficient high-quality evidence to mandate strict non-weightbearing in all cases, though most centers continue to favor initial non-/minimal-weightbearing pending further trial data.
- **[Cheong et al., J Foot Ankle Res, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39550635/)** — scoping review of risk factors for recurrent and contralateral Charcot neuroarthropathy, informing the counseling point that roughly a fifth to two-fifths of patients experience recurrence or new contralateral disease, supporting lifelong surveillance/bracing.
- **[Sakkab et al., J Foot Ankle Surg, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38097009/)** — minimum 2-year patient-reported outcomes after triple arthrodesis for adult acquired flatfoot deformity, providing contemporary benchmark data for counseling patients considering hindfoot fusion for rigid/arthritic PCFD.
