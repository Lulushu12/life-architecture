## Quick Reference

**Open fracture — treatment ladder**

| Step | Action | Timing target |
|---|---|---|
| First-line (ED) | IV antibiotics, photograph wound, remove gross contamination, splint, cover with saline-soaked gauze, tetanus prophylaxis, do NOT probe/irrigate repeatedly in ED | Antibiotics ≤ 1 hour from presentation (ideally ≤ 66 min from injury per NTN/BOAST) |
| Second-line | Formal surgical debridement ± provisional stabilization (ex-fix or IM nail) | ≤ 24 h from injury (not a strict 6-h rule); same-day if highly contaminated (agricultural, aquatic, sewage) |
| Definitive | Skeletal fixation + soft tissue coverage (fix-and-flap or staged fix-then-flap) | Coverage within 72 h ideally, acceptable up to 7 days at a specialist orthoplastic centre; delay >7 days sharply increases infection |

**Acute compartment syndrome — treatment ladder**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| First-line | High clinical suspicion + serial exams; remove all circumferential dressings/casts, limb at heart level, correct hypotension |
| Second-line | Continuous/repeated intracompartmental pressure monitoring if exam equivocal, obtunded patient, or regional/neuraxial block in place; ΔP (diastolic BP − ICP) < 30 mmHg supports diagnosis |
| Surgical | Emergent 4-compartment fasciotomy (leg) — no role for pressure monitoring once irreversible neuromuscular/vascular damage is clinically evident |

**Polytrauma — decision ladder**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| First-line | ATLS primary survey, physiologic staging (lactate, base deficit, coagulopathy, temperature, hemodynamic stability) |
| Second-line | Damage Control Orthopaedics (DCO): external fixation of long-bone/pelvic fractures, hemorrhage control, ICU resuscitation |
| Definitive | Early Total Care (ETC) if physiologically stable/borderline; staged conversion to definitive fixation once resuscitated (typically day 2–10) |

**Nonunion — treatment ladder**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| First-line | Confirm type (hypertrophic/oligotrophic/atrophic) on imaging; optimize modifiable risk factors (smoking, diabetes, NSAID use, vitamin D, nutrition) |
| Second-line | Hypertrophic → mechanical stabilization alone (revision plating/nailing, dynamization) usually sufficient |
| Surgical/biologic | Atrophic/oligotrophic → Diamond Concept: revision fixation + autologous bone graft (± BMP, concentrated bone marrow aspirate); infected/segmental defect → Masquelet or bone transport |

**Segmental bone loss — treatment ladder**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| First-line | Radical debridement to bleeding bone, culture-directed antibiotics, temporary antibiotic cement spacer + stable fixation |
| Second-line | Defect < 4–5 cm: acute shortening ± relengthening, or autograft/vascularized graft |
| Surgical | Defect ≥ 4–5 cm (critical-size, roughly >62 mm reported threshold for Masquelet reliability): Masquelet induced-membrane technique or distraction osteogenesis (bone transport); vascularized fibula for very long defects |

**Fat embolism syndrome — treatment ladder**

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| First-line | Early fracture stabilization, adequate oxygenation, hemodynamic support, minimize hypoxia/hypovolemia |
| Second-line | Supportive ICU care — mechanical ventilation (lung-protective), fluid/vasopressor titration, DVT prophylaxis |
| No proven pharmacologic cure | Corticosteroids not routinely recommended (evidence weak/mixed); treatment is prevention + supportive care |

**Scoring shortcuts for the on-call surgeon**
- ISS ≥ 16 → treat as major trauma/polytrauma pathway activation.
- Hannover/Pape "borderline" patient → favor DCO unless resuscitation trends clearly favor early definitive fixation within the first 24 h.
- Gurd's criteria (1 major + 4 minor, or weighted equivalents) → working diagnosis of fat embolism syndrome pending exclusion of other causes (PE, sepsis, TBI).

**Red flags**
- Pain out of proportion / pain on passive stretch, especially after regional block masking symptoms → assume compartment syndrome until proven otherwise
- Open fracture with vascular compromise → "life before limb," restore perfusion within 6 h ischemia time, consider temporary shunt
- Petechial rash + confusion + hypoxemia 24–72 h post long-bone fracture → fat embolism syndrome
- Polytrauma patient with rising lactate/falling temperature/coagulopathy → abandon ETC, revert to DCO
- Segmental defect with active infection → do NOT graft until infection eradicated (two-stage protocol mandatory)

**Antibiotic regimen quick table**

| Gustilo-Anderson grade | First-line agent | Add-on | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / II | Cefazolin (Gram-positive cover) | None needed per SIS 2022 | ≤ 24 h after wound closure |
| IIIA / IIIB / IIIC | Cefazolin or broader beta-lactam per local protocol | Aminoglycoside or extended Gram-negative agent | ≤ 24 h after each debridement/procedure, never > 72 h total absent infection |
| Any grade + soil/farm/aquatic/fecal contamination | As above | High-dose penicillin or metronidazole (anti-clostridial cover) | Same as underlying grade |
| Penicillin-allergic patient | Clindamycin or vancomycin substitute for Gram-positive cover | Gram-negative agent as above if indicated | As above |

**Key numbers**
- Antibiotic timing: ideally ≤ 1 h from injury/presentation; Gram-positive cephalosporin for Gustilo I/II; add Gram-negative coverage for III; add penicillin for soil/fecal/aquatic contamination (anti-clostridial)
- Duration: 24 h post-wound-closure for I/II; up to 72 h (or 24 h after each subsequent procedure) for III per SIS 2022 update — do not exceed 72 h regardless
- Debridement: within 24 h (not the old dogmatic "6-hour rule")
- Flap coverage: within 72 h optimal; up to 7 days acceptable at specialist centres; >7 days = significantly higher infection risk
- Compartment syndrome: ΔP < 30 mmHg (diastolic − intracompartmental pressure) supports fasciotomy; absolute ICP > 30 mmHg supportive but less specific
- Ischemia tolerance: warm ischemia ~6 h before irreversible muscle damage
- Nonunion definition: no radiographic progression over 3 consecutive months, and/or failure to heal by 6–9 months post-injury
- Critical-size bone defect: generally ≥ 2–2.5 cm in some series, ≥ 4–5 cm in most practical algorithms, with >62 mm cited as a threshold where Masquelet success drops
- Masquelet union rate ~86%; bone transport union rate ~95% (meta-analysis, comparable functional outcomes, transport = higher complication burden and longer external fixation time)
- FES mortality with supportive management < 10%; incidence reported 1–30% depending on diagnostic criteria used (Gurd, Lindeque, Schonfeld)

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

This cluster spans a shared injury continuum: a high-energy mechanism produces an **open fracture** with a defined zone of soft-tissue injury; the same energy transfer, or the swelling that follows, can precipitate **acute compartment syndrome** in the same limb; the patient sustaining that fracture is frequently a **polytrauma** patient whose systemic physiology dictates how and when the limb injury can safely be definitively treated; the marrow disruption from the fracture itself (and from subsequent instrumentation) is the trigger for **fat embolism syndrome**; and the downstream healing trajectory of the fracture — shaped by the initial bone and soft-tissue loss, fixation strategy, and biological environment — determines whether the patient ends up with **normal union**, **malunion**, **nonunion**, or a **segmental bone defect** requiring reconstruction. Understanding each diagnosis in isolation is necessary but insufficient; the operative decisions at each stage (debridement extent, fixation strategy, timing of definitive surgery) directly shape the probability of the downstream healing complications covered later in this monograph.

**Open fracture.** A fracture with a wound communicating the fracture hematoma with the external environment. Severity is determined less by the skin wound size than by the zone of injury: periosteal stripping, muscle devitalization, contamination burden, and vascular compromise. The wound is a marker of the energy delivered to the limb, not a measure of it — a small puncture from a high-velocity injury can overlie extensive deep soft-tissue and periosteal destruction ("inside-out" vs "outside-in" mechanism matters for prognosis).

**Acute compartment syndrome (ACS).** Elevated pressure within a closed osteofascial compartment exceeds capillary perfusion pressure, causing a vicious cycle: venous outflow obstruction → increased hydrostatic pressure → further edema → arteriolar collapse → muscle and nerve ischemia. Irreversible muscle necrosis begins around 4–6 hours of complete ischemia; nerve conduction failure occurs earlier and is often the first reversible warning sign. The leg (4 compartments: anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, deep posterior) is the most common site; forearm, thigh, foot, hand, and gluteal compartments also at risk. Regional anesthesia/neuraxial blocks blunt the cardinal pain symptom and are a recognized risk factor for delayed diagnosis.

**Polytrauma.** Multiple, potentially life-threatening injuries in at least two body regions, with a systemic inflammatory/immunologic response ("second hit" phenomenon) that determines tolerance for major surgery. The "two-hit" model: the initial trauma primes the innate immune system; a poorly timed major surgical "second hit" (e.g., prolonged IM nailing with reaming, bilateral femur fixation) can precipitate ARDS, MODS, or death in a physiologically unprepared patient. Injury Severity Score (ISS), lactate clearance, base deficit, core temperature, coagulation status, and platelet count are the physiologic parameters used to stage a patient's readiness for definitive fixation.

**Nonunion.** Failure of a fracture to unite with cessation of the biological healing process, confirmed clinically (motion/pain at fracture site) and radiographically (no progressive callus over 3 consecutive months). Pathophysiology bifurcates into a biological problem (poor blood supply, infection, excessive gap, soft-tissue interposition, patient factors: smoking, NSAIDs, diabetes, malnutrition) versus a mechanical problem (excessive strain at the fracture site from inadequate fixation stability).

**Malunion.** Fracture healing in a non-anatomic position — angular, rotational, or translational deformity, or leg-length discrepancy — beyond an accepted threshold, resulting in altered joint mechanics, adjacent-joint degeneration risk, and functional/cosmetic deficit.

**Segmental bone loss.** Loss of a length of diaphyseal or metaphyseal bone (traumatic avulsion, debridement of nonviable bone, tumor resection) that exceeds the biological capacity for spontaneous bridging, requiring active reconstruction of both the biological (vascularity, osteogenic potential) and mechanical (stable scaffold) environment.

**Fat embolism syndrome (FES).** Classically explained by two mechanisms: (1) the mechanical theory — marrow fat is forced into torn medullary venous sinusoids by intramedullary pressure at the time of fracture or instrumentation (reaming, nailing), embolizing to lung and, via patent foramen ovale/paradoxical embolism or pulmonary capillary transit, to brain, skin, retina; (2) the biochemical theory — circulating free fatty acids, released by systemic inflammatory lipase activity, cause direct toxic injury to pulmonary and cerebral capillary endothelium, producing a syndrome that outlasts and outweighs simple mechanical obstruction. Long bone (especially femoral) and pelvic fractures, particularly with reamed IM nailing, are highest risk.

**Scoring systems used across the cluster**: Injury Severity Score (ISS ≥ 16 defines "major trauma"/polytrauma in most registries), New Injury Severity Score (NISS), Mangled Extremity Severity Score (MESS, historically used to support amputation counseling though not a strict cutoff decision tool), Hannover Polytrauma Classification / Pape physiologic staging (stable / borderline / unstable / in extremis), and ASAMI (Association for the Study and Application of the Method of Ilizarov) scoring for bone/functional results after segmental reconstruction.

**Bone healing biology underlying all reconstructive decisions.** Secondary (indirect) bone healing — the default pathway under relative stability (IM nailing, bridge plating, external fixation) — proceeds through hematoma formation, inflammatory phase, soft callus (cartilaginous) formation, hard callus (woven bone) formation, and remodeling to lamellar bone; visible periosteal callus on radiographs is the hallmark. Primary (direct) bone healing occurs only under absolute stability with anatomic reduction and compression (typically lag-screw/compression-plate constructs) via osteonal (cutting cone) remodeling across the fracture line without visible callus; the absence of callus in this setting is expected and should not be mistaken for delayed union. Strain theory (Perren) explains why a given fixation construct heals or fails to heal: interfragmentary strain above roughly 10% at a simple fracture gap disrupts bridging callus formation (favoring nonunion), while strain in the 2–10% range favors robust secondary healing — this is the mechanistic basis for why an overly rigid construct with a persistent gap (relative stability without adequate compression, and without enough working length to allow controlled micromotion) can paradoxically produce an oligotrophic nonunion.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

- **Primary survey first**: ATLS approach — life- and limb-threatening hemorrhage control takes priority over detailed fracture assessment in the polytrauma patient.
- **Open fracture**: document neurovascular status before and after any manipulation; photograph the wound once (per BOAST guidance — for the record and to avoid repeated dressing removal in ED); assess contamination source (soil, water, sewage — informs antibiotic choice); assess soft-tissue "zone of injury," degloving, and compartment status; remove gross debris only, cover with saline-soaked dressing, splint, do not repeatedly irrigate/probe in the emergency department (this is done once, definitively, in theatre).
- **Physical exam pearls**: examine and document distal pulses, capillary refill, and a full peripheral nerve exam (motor and sensory, each named nerve individually) before any reduction/splinting and again after — a change in exam after manipulation is a red flag requiring immediate re-imaging or exploration, not observation; in the obtunded polytrauma patient, absence of a reliable exam is itself an indication to lower the threshold for adjunctive monitoring (e.g., ICP monitoring for possible compartment syndrome).
- **Vascular assessment**: ABI (ankle-brachial index) < 0.9 or asymmetric pulses mandates CT angiography; hard signs (absent pulse, expanding hematoma, pulsatile bleeding, bruit/thrill, or signs of distal ischemia) mandate immediate surgical exploration without imaging delay.
- **Imaging**: orthogonal plain radiographs including joint above/below; CT for articular extension, pelvic/acetabular injuries, or pre-operative planning; whole-body CT ("pan-scan") is standard of care in hemodynamically stable polytrauma patients at most Level 1 centres.
- **Acute compartment syndrome**: clinical diagnosis in the alert patient — the "6 P's" (pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, paresthesia, pallor, poikilothermia, pulselessness) — but pulselessness and pallor are late/unreliable signs; pain on passive stretch and paresthesia are earliest reliable findings. In obtunded, sedated, intubated, or regionally-blocked patients, use continuous intracompartmental pressure (ICP) monitoring — AAOS 2025 CPG: ΔP (diastolic BP − ICP) < 30 mmHg is the best-supported threshold to help rule in ACS when combined with clinical picture; absolute ICP > 30 mmHg is supportive but non-specific.
- **Nonunion/malunion**: serial radiographs at fixed intervals (6, 12, then every 4–6 weeks) to assess bridging callus/progression; CT with metal-artifact reduction to confirm bridging across ≥ 3 of 4 cortices when plain films are equivocal; assess for infection (ESR/CRP, WBC, consider aspiration/biopsy with hold on antibiotics ≥ 2 weeks before sampling) before committing to a biologic-augmentation strategy — treating an infected nonunion as aseptic is a common and costly error.
- **Fat embolism syndrome**: primarily a clinical diagnosis of exclusion using Gurd's criteria (1 major + 4 minor, or the classic weighted system) — major: petechial rash, respiratory symptoms with bilateral infiltrates, cerebral signs unrelated to head injury; minor: tachycardia, pyrexia, retinal fat emboli, fat in urine/sputum, thrombocytopenia, falling hematocrit, raised ESR. CT chest may show ground-glass/"crazy paving"; MRI brain (diffusion-weighted) shows characteristic "starfield" pattern of scattered punctate hyperintensities in cerebral fat embolism.
- **Polytrauma workup**: primary survey (ATLS ABCDE) with early identification of hemorrhage source; secondary survey head-to-toe; whole-body CT in the stable/borderline patient; serial physiologic parameters (arterial lactate, base excess, core temperature, INR/fibrinogen/platelets, hemodynamics) drive DCO-vs-ETC staging decisions and should be reassessed at fixed intervals, not just once on admission; FAST/eFAST for occult hemothorax/intra-abdominal bleeding informs sequencing of orthopedic vs general surgical priorities.
- **Malunion assessment**: clinical alignment (rotational profile, limb-length measurement), long-leg standing (mechanical axis) radiographs for lower-limb deformity, CT rotational profile (comparing femoral/tibial torsion to the contralateral limb) when rotational malunion is suspected clinically but not obvious on plain films; deformity analysis (center of rotation of angulation, CORA) is mandatory before planning corrective osteotomy.

**Critical-size defect concept.** A segmental defect is termed "critical size" when it will not heal spontaneously regardless of stabilization quality because the gap exceeds the biological capacity for bridging — commonly cited thresholds are roughly 2–2.5× the diaphyseal diameter, or in practical clinical terms defects ≥ 4–5 cm in the tibia/femur. Below this size, acute shortening-relengthening or bulk autograft alone may succeed; above it, active biological/mechanical reconstruction (Masquelet, bone transport, vascularized graft) is required.

## Classification

**Gustilo-Anderson classification (open fractures)** — assessed intra-operatively after debridement, not in the ED

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Wound < 1 cm, clean, minimal soft-tissue damage, simple fracture pattern | Low infection risk (~0–2%); Gram-positive coverage (cefazolin) suffices; often amenable to primary IM nailing after debridement |
| II | Wound 1–10 cm, moderate soft-tissue damage, no extensive stripping, moderate contamination | Infection risk ~2–7%; Gram-positive coverage adequate per SIS 2022; standard debridement + fixation |
| IIIA | Extensive soft-tissue laceration/damage but adequate soft-tissue coverage of bone achievable; segmental or severely comminuted fracture regardless of wound size | Add Gram-negative coverage (aminoglycoside or equivalent); usually locally closable without free tissue transfer |
| IIIB | Extensive soft-tissue loss with periosteal stripping and bone exposure requiring flap coverage (local or free); usually highly contaminated | Highest infection risk of the reconstructable group (~10–25%); mandates orthoplastic (combined ortho + plastics) input, early fix-and-flap planning |
| IIIC | Any open fracture with an arterial injury requiring repair for limb viability | Vascular repair supersedes skeletal priorities in sequencing; highest amputation risk (~25–50% in some series); shortest acceptable ischemia window (~6 h warm) drives OR sequencing |

**Tscherne classification (closed soft-tissue injury)** — for closed fractures, complements Gustilo-Anderson for open

| Grade | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| C0 | No/minimal soft-tissue injury, simple fracture pattern | Standard fixation, no special soft-tissue precautions |
| C1 | Superficial abrasion or contusion from indirect injury | Watch for delayed swelling; can usually proceed with planned fixation timing |
| C2 | Deep abrasion with local skin/muscle contusion, direct trauma, impending compartment syndrome possible | Consider staged fixation; monitor closely for evolving compartment syndrome; delay definitive plating until swelling permits |
| C3 | Extensive skin contusion/crushing, subcutaneous degloving, established or manifest compartment syndrome, decompensated vascular injury | Damage control (external fixation) mandatory; definitive fixation delayed until soft-tissue envelope recovers (often 10–21 days) |

**OTA Open Fracture Classification (OTA-OFC)** — five components scored separately (more granular than Gustilo-Anderson, better inter-observer reliability); each graded 1–3

| Component | Grade 1 | Grade 2 | Grade 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Skin | Can be closed | Cannot be closed primarily | Extensive degloving |
| Muscle | No obvious non-viable muscle | Some loss, muscle function preserved | Dead muscle, loss of function, missing muscle groups |
| Arterial injury | None | Injury without ischemia | Injury with distal ischemia |
| Contamination | None/minimal | Surface contamination easily removed | High-energy contamination (soil, organic, foreign body) deeply embedded, or ongoing exposure (farm, sewage, aquatic) |
| Bone loss | None | Bone missing/devascularized but reconstructable length/rotation retainable | Segmental bone loss precluding standard reconstruction |

Management implication: OTA-OFC's individual-component scoring predicts infection and amputation risk more granularly than the aggregate Gustilo-Anderson grade and is increasingly used in registries and trials to stratify for reconstructive planning (e.g., a fracture with severe arterial and bone-loss components but modest skin injury drives amputation counseling even if the skin wound looks "IIIA").

**Non-union types**

| Type | Radiographic appearance | Biology | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophic ("elephant's foot" / "horse's hoof") | Abundant callus, flared fracture ends, "lucent gap" persists | Good biology, inadequate mechanical stability | Rigid fixation alone (revision plating/nailing, exchange nailing, dynamization) — do NOT need bone graft |
| Oligotrophic | Minimal callus, fracture ends not resorbed, gap persists | Reduced biology + some mechanical instability, often from displaced/distracted fixation | Improve stability AND biology — reduce the gap, compression, consider limited grafting |
| Atrophic | No callus, rounded/resorbed osteopenic bone ends, sometimes a fibrous non-osseous gap | Poor/absent biology (avascular, often after excessive stripping, infection, or NSAID/smoking-impaired healing) | Requires biological augmentation — autologous bone graft (iliac crest, RIA reamings), consider BMP/concentrated bone marrow aspirate, correction of systemic factors, ± vascularized bone if avascular bed |

**Practical staging table — DCO vs ETC decision inputs**

| Parameter | Favors ETC (definitive fixation now) | Favors DCO (external fixation, stage later) |
|---|---|---|
| Hemodynamics | Stable, responsive to modest resuscitation | Ongoing hemorrhage, pressor-dependent |
| Lactate / base deficit | Normalizing / near-normal | Persistently elevated, not clearing |
| Temperature | Normothermic | Hypothermic (< 35 °C) |
| Coagulation | Normal INR/platelets/fibrinogen | Coagulopathic |
| Chest injury | Absent/minor | Significant pulmonary contusion, high risk for ARDS with reamed nailing |
| Head injury | Absent/minor, no rising ICP | Significant TBI where a long anesthetic/hypotensive episode is poorly tolerated |
| Number/complexity of injuries | Isolated or limited long-bone injury | Multiple long-bone + pelvic + visceral injuries |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Malunion**: accepted for angular/rotational deformity within functional tolerance thresholds — general orthopaedic teaching accepts up to ~5° varus/valgus and ~10° antero-posterior angulation in the tibial diaphysis, up to ~10–15° rotational malalignment in the femur before symptomatic dysfunction, though thresholds are lower near joints (articular malunion has essentially no tolerance and mandates surgical correction). Asymptomatic, functionally tolerated malunions in low-demand patients are observed with activity modification and monitoring for adjacent-joint degeneration.
- **Fat embolism syndrome**: no non-operative "treatment" beyond supportive care — see Operative/Rehabilitation sections; prevention is the operative decision (early fracture stabilization) rather than a conservative option.
- **Minimally displaced closed fractures at risk of ACS**: cast splitting/bivalving, limb elevation to heart level (not above, which can reduce arterial perfusion pressure), serial neurovascular checks — appropriate only when clinical suspicion is low; any suspicion of established ACS is a surgical emergency, not a trial of conservative measures.
- **Selected hypertrophic nonunions with acceptable alignment** in low-demand patients: functional bracing plus controlled axial loading (weight-bearing stimulates callus in mechanically stable but under-loaded constructs) may occasionally suffice, but most hypertrophic nonunions still require operative stabilization since the underlying problem (inadequate rigidity) rarely self-corrects.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Concentrated bone marrow aspirate (BMA) / bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC)** injection into the fracture site — percutaneous iliac crest harvest, used as a biologic adjunct for hypertrophic-to-oligotrophic nonunions or to augment grafting in atrophic nonunions; moderate evidence for improved union rates when combined with adequate mechanical stability, weak alone.
- **PRP (platelet-rich plasma)** — case-report and small-series evidence in refractory nonunion (including as salvage after multiple prior surgeries); not a substitute for mechanical stability correction; evidence quality remains low-to-moderate.
- **Recombinant BMP-2/BMP-7** — adjunct in selected atrophic nonunions and acute high-energy open tibial fractures with segmental bone loss where autograft volume is inadequate; used off-label/case-by-case in most European centres given cost and regulatory variation; strongest evidence base remains in acute Gustilo IIIA/B open tibial fractures (BESTT trial, historical) and augmentation of Masquelet second-stage grafting.
- **Local/regional anesthetic nerve blocks** are an important perioperative adjunct for pain control in polytrauma and post-fasciotomy management, but must be used cautiously (or avoided) when compartment syndrome surveillance is ongoing, since dense blocks mask the cardinal pain symptom.

### Operative

**Open fracture — sequencing and technique**
- **Antibiotics**: administer as soon as possible after injury/on ED arrival, target ≤ 1 hour (BOAST/NTN 2024 guideline; SIS 2022 update). First-generation cephalosporin (e.g., cefazolin) for Gustilo I/II; add Gram-negative coverage (aminoglycoside, or a broader beta-lactam per local protocol) for type III; add high-dose penicillin (or metronidazole) for farm/soil/aquatic/fecal contamination to cover Clostridium. Duration: stop 24 h after wound closure for I/II; do not exceed 24 h post-injury/post-each-procedure for type III, and never continue beyond 72 h total in the absence of documented infection (SIS 2022; supported by 2023 AAOS review) — prolonged "prophylaxis" increases resistant organism selection without reducing infection rate.
- **Debridement**: formal, systematic, layer-by-layer excision of all non-viable tissue in theatre; current evidence does not support a rigid "6-hour rule" — debridement within 24 hours is the accepted target for most wounds, though heavily contaminated wounds (agricultural, aquatic, sewage) should still go emergently. Irrigation: low-pressure, high-volume normal saline is preferred over high-pressure pulsatile lavage (FLOW trial showed no benefit and possible harm from high pressure or additives).
- **Skeletal stabilization**: provisional external fixation (spanning or fracture-specific) for damage control in contaminated/complex wounds or the physiologically unstable patient; definitive fixation (IM nailing, plating) can be performed acutely at the same setting in Gustilo I–IIIA wounds if adequately debrided and the patient is stable; in IIIB/IIIC, coordinate skeletal fixation with the soft-tissue reconstruction plan.
- **Fix-and-flap vs staged fix-then-flap**: single-stage "fix and flap" (definitive fixation and free/local flap coverage in the same anesthetic, ideally within 72 h) at a combined orthoplastic centre reduces infection, nonunion, and number of procedures compared with staged approaches, per BOAST/BAPRAS joint standards. When a specialist combined unit/team isn't immediately available, staged fix-then-flap with early transfer to a Major Trauma Centre within 72 h (and coverage completed by day 7) is the accepted fallback; recent cohort data confirm delay beyond 7 days significantly raises deep infection risk regardless of strategy. Intramedullary nailing prior to flap coverage in IIIB/IIIC does not appear to increase complication rates compared with nailing after flap coverage, per recent retrospective data — offering more flexibility in sequencing than historically assumed.
- **Vascular injury (IIIC)**: temporary shunt to restore perfusion first if skeletal stabilization will otherwise prolong ischemia beyond ~6 h; definitive vascular repair after or with skeletal stabilization depending on shunt use; early, honest discussion of primary amputation is appropriate for a mangled extremity with combined severe nerve, vascular, bone, and soft-tissue injury (MESS and similar scores are adjuncts to, not substitutes for, clinical judgment and shared decision-making). Factors weighing toward primary amputation include warm ischemia time > 6 h, combined tibial nerve transection (anticipated insensate plantar foot), extensive multi-compartment crush/muscle loss, and patient factors making prolonged reconstruction unsafe; functional outcomes between successful limb salvage and amputation with modern prosthetics were broadly comparable at 2 years in the historical LEAP cohort, supporting shared decision-making over a reflexive salvage-at-all-costs approach.
- **Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT)**: acceptable temporizing dressing between debridement and definitive coverage, but is not a substitute for timely debridement/coverage and should not be used to justify delaying flap coverage beyond the target window.

**Acute compartment syndrome**
- Emergent four-compartment fasciotomy of the leg via the standard two-incision (medial + lateral) technique — release anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, deep posterior compartments; leave wounds open, dress, and plan delayed primary closure or split-thickness skin graft at 5–10 days once swelling subsides (staged closure techniques: shoelace, vessel-loop, gradual approximation).
- Time from onset to fasciotomy is the dominant prognostic factor; fasciotomy performed within 6 h of onset gives the best functional outcomes; beyond 8–12 h risk of myonecrosis, contracture, and need for delayed debridement/amputation rises sharply — this is a "no delay for imaging" emergency in the clinically obvious case.
- Pitfall: an incomplete fasciotomy (missing the deep posterior compartment via the medial incision) is a common technical error — the deep posterior compartment (behind the soleus, tibialis posterior/FDL/FHL) is the most frequently under-released and re-explored compartment.

**Polytrauma — DCO vs ETC**
- **Early Total Care (ETC)**: definitive fixation of major long-bone and pelvic/acetabular fractures within the first 24–36 hours, reserved for physiologically stable or "borderline" patients (per Pape/Hannover classification of patient status: stable, borderline, unstable, in extremis) — reduces pulmonary complications, ICU stay, and cost when appropriately selected.
- **Damage Control Orthopaedics (DCO)**: temporary external fixation of long bones and pelvis, hemorrhage control (including pelvic binder/REBOA/angioembolization as indicated), and ICU resuscitation to correct the "lethal triad" (hypothermia, coagulopathy, acidosis) before committing to definitive surgery, in unstable or in-extremis patients, or those with severe chest/head injury where a prolonged "second hit" procedure (reamed nailing, especially bilateral femoral) risks precipitating ARDS/MODS.
- Decision-making is physiology-driven, not purely time-driven: recent literature (Stevens & Tejwani, J Trauma Acute Care Surg, 2024) reaffirms there is no single validated algorithm proven superior in RCTs; serial reassessment of lactate clearance, base deficit, temperature, coagulation, and hemodynamic trend — not a fixed "golden window" — should dictate conversion timing from external fixation to definitive fixation, typically feasible around day 2–10 once the patient has normalized.
- Reaming for IM nailing in the multiply injured patient with significant chest injury is used cautiously — unreamed or minimally reamed nailing, or staged conversion from external fixation, is favored when pulmonary reserve is compromised.
- **Damage control resuscitation** runs in parallel with DCO: permissive hypotension (in the absence of TBI) until hemorrhage control, balanced 1:1:1 transfusion ratio (packed red cells:FFP:platelets) for massive transfusion, tranexamic acid within 3 h of injury (CRASH-2 framework), and correction of hypothermia/acidosis before any elective conversion to definitive fixation — orthopedic staging decisions cannot be made in isolation from this resuscitation trajectory.
- Pelvic ring injuries in the polytrauma patient: mechanical binder/sheet at the greater trochanters as immediate temporizing measure, followed by exam under anesthesia and definitive fixation (percutaneous iliosacral screws, anterior external fixation, or open reduction) once hemodynamically controlled; angioembolization or pre-peritoneal packing addresses ongoing pelvic hemorrhage refractory to mechanical stabilization.

**Nonunion — Diamond Concept operative execution**
- The Diamond Concept (Giannoudis et al.) frames nonunion treatment around simultaneously optimizing: (1) osteogenic cells, (2) osteoconductive scaffold, (3) growth factors, and (4) mechanical stability, plus vascularity as the underlying platform.
- Practically: revise fixation to achieve absolute or relative stability appropriate to the nonunion type (compression plating, exchange reamed nailing with a larger nail for diaphyseal nonunions, or dual-plating/blocking screws for challenging metaphyseal patterns), combined with autologous iliac crest bone graft or Reamer-Irrigator-Aspirator (RIA) graft harvest, ± BMP or concentrated bone marrow aspirate for biologically deficient (atrophic) nonunions.
- Recent large-defect series applying the Diamond Concept to femoral/tibial defects ≥ 5 cm report union around 64% within 2 years (Bone & Joint Open, 2024/2025) — reflecting that very large defects behave more like segmental bone loss than simple nonunion and often need staged Masquelet/transport strategies layered onto the diamond framework.
- Always exclude/treat infection first (culture-directed debridement, implant removal or exchange, antibiotic cement spacer, delayed grafting) — grafting an infected nonunion without eradicating infection reliably fails.

**Segmental bone loss — Masquelet vs bone transport**
- **Masquelet (induced membrane) technique**: Stage 1 — radical debridement, stable fixation (plate or nail), insertion of an antibiotic-loaded PMMA cement spacer that induces a vascularized biological membrane (pseudo-periosteum) around it over 6–8 weeks. Stage 2 — spacer removal preserving the membrane, filling the defect with autologous (± allograft-extended) cancellous bone graft. Advantages: technically simpler, shorter time to full weight-bearing after stage 2, lower direct hospitalization cost, better reported quality-of-life scores in comparative series; limitations: reliable up to roughly 4–6 cm defects reproducibly (success drops for defects >6 cm, with 62 mm cited as an approximate critical threshold in some series), donor-site morbidity from graft harvest for larger volumes.
- **Bone transport (distraction osteogenesis, Ilizarov principle)**: acute or gradual bone resection at the defect margins with corticotomy at a separate metaphyseal site, followed by gradual distraction (~1 mm/day, typically in 0.25 mm increments 4×/day) transporting a bone segment to fill the defect ("docking site"), often requiring later docking-site bone grafting/exchange nailing. Advantages: not limited by defect size, avoids large-volume graft harvest, higher reported union rates (~95% in comparative meta-analyses); disadvantages: prolonged external fixation time (frame often worn many months, "external fixation index" ~1–1.5 months/cm), pin-site infection burden, docking-site nonunion requiring secondary grafting, greater patient burden/complexity.
- Selection rationale: Masquelet favored for shorter defects, patients who cannot tolerate prolonged external fixation, or when soft-tissue coverage is already achieved; bone transport favored for very large (>6 cm) or combined bone-and-soft-tissue defects (transport can simultaneously resurface soft tissue via bone-transport-associated soft-tissue stretch), and centres with Ilizarov/circular-frame expertise. Vascularized fibular graft (free or pedicled) is an alternative/adjunct for very long diaphyseal defects (>8–10 cm), especially in the humerus/forearm or when local biology is severely compromised (post-osteomyelitis).

**Fat embolism syndrome — operative implications**
- Early definitive stabilization of long-bone fractures (within 24 h in the stable patient) reduces the incidence of FES compared with prolonged non-operative or delayed management — this is the single most impactful "operative treatment."
- In the multiply injured or physiologically unstable patient, damage-control external fixation still reduces marrow embolization risk compared with prolonged non-operative traction, while deferring the pulmonary burden of reamed nailing until the patient is stable (see DCO discussion above) — the FES and ARDS risk calculus overlaps substantially with the broader DCO-vs-ETC decision.
- Unreamed nailing or lower-pressure reaming systems may modestly reduce intramedullary pressure spikes during nailing of at-risk patients (large canal fractures, bilateral femurs), though evidence for clinical FES reduction from technique modification alone is limited.

**Malunion — corrective osteotomy**
- Indications: symptomatic deformity beyond functional tolerance (see Non-operative thresholds above), progressive adjacent-joint arthrosis, unacceptable cosmesis/limb-length discrepancy, or gait dysfunction.
- Preoperative planning: full deformity analysis (magnitude, plane, level, translation, rotation) using long-leg films/CT rotational profile; identify the CORA and plan osteotomy at or near that level whenever possible to avoid secondary translational deformity.
- Techniques: acute correction with osteotomy and internal fixation (plate or IM nail) for single-plane deformities correctable in one stage; gradual correction with a circular/hexapod external fixator (e.g., Taylor Spatial Frame) for multiplanar deformity, concurrent limb-length discrepancy, or when soft-tissue/neurovascular structures cannot tolerate acute correction (risk of acute nerve stretch injury, particularly the peroneal nerve with acute valgus/lengthening correction).
- Pearl: correct rotation and length gradually if the deformity is longstanding — acute correction of chronic soft-tissue contracture around nerves/vessels risks iatrogenic neurovascular injury; when in doubt, prefer a staged/gradual correction strategy.
- Combined bone-loss malunions (shortened, malaligned, with a segmental deficit) are managed with the same Masquelet/bone-transport framework described above, often combined with deformity correction in the same frame construct.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Open fracture (lower limb, post fix-and-flap)**
- Weeks 0–2: flap monitoring (Doppler/clinical), strict limb elevation, non-weight-bearing, ankle/foot pumps and passive ROM of uninvolved joints to prevent stiffness/DVT; dangling protocol for free flaps typically starts around day 5–7 per plastics team.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive mobilization out of bed, touch-down to partial weight-bearing per fixation stability and fracture pattern (nailed diaphyseal fractures often allowed partial weight-bearing earlier than plated metaphyseal/articular fractures), physiotherapy for joint ROM, edema control (compression garments once flap is robust), scar/graft site desensitization.
- Weeks 6–12: progression to full weight-bearing guided by radiographic callus and clinical exam (no pain/tenderness at fracture site); strengthening, gait re-training, proprioception work.
- Months 3–6+: return to work — sedentary work often feasible by 6–8 weeks once pain-controlled and mobile; manual labor/heavy lifting typically deferred until radiographic union confirmed and functional strength ≥ 80–90% of contralateral limb, often 4–6 months for IIIB injuries; return to impact sport individualized, generally not before 6–9 months and full radiographic union with satisfactory strength/hop testing where applicable.

**Post-fasciotomy**
- Early (days 0–5): dressing changes, staged wound closure (shoelace technique or serial VAC-assisted closure), active/passive ankle and toe ROM to limit contracture, elevation.
- Weeks 1–3: definitive wound closure or split-thickness skin graft once edema resolves; progressive weight-bearing as underlying fracture fixation allows; desensitization and scar management once healed.
- Months 1–3+: strength and gait rehabilitation; residual sensory deficit or mild motor weakness is common and should be addressed with targeted physiotherapy and, if persistent beyond 3–6 months, nerve conduction studies.

**Polytrauma (general framework)**
- ICU/acute phase: chest physiotherapy, early mobilization as permitted by injury complex and hemodynamic status (early mobilization protocols reduce ICU-acquired weakness and pulmonary complications), DVT prophylaxis.
- Subacute (weeks 2–8): fracture-specific weight-bearing/ROM protocols run in parallel across injured segments — sequencing prioritized by the limiting injury; interdisciplinary rehabilitation (PT/OT, sometimes rehabilitation medicine consult) is standard for multi-limb injury.
- Nutritional optimization (adequate protein intake, vitamin D repletion, correction of anemia) should be addressed early in the polytrauma admission — catabolic stress from major trauma directly impairs fracture-healing biology and is a modifiable contributor to delayed union/nonunion risk across every fracture in a multi-injury patient.
- Return to work/sport: driven by the most functionally limiting individual injury; formal functional capacity evaluation useful before clearing manual-labor occupations after major polytrauma.

**Nonunion/segmental defect reconstruction (Masquelet/transport)**
- Post-stage 1 (spacer/frame placement): non- to touch-weight-bearing, ROM of adjacent joints maintained, pin-site care education if external fixation used.
- Distraction phase (bone transport): typically starts 5–7 days post-corticotomy, ~1 mm/day distraction; weight-bearing as tolerated through the frame is often encouraged (axial loading stimulates regenerate bone formation) unless contraindicated by fixation type.
- Post-graft (Masquelet stage 2) or post-docking: protected weight-bearing progressing over 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic incorporation; full weight-bearing typically not before 3–4 months and often later for large-defect reconstructions.
- Return to work/sport is defined case-by-case and is typically delayed 9–18 months for large segmental reconstructions given the prolonged biological remodeling timeline.

**Malunion post-corrective osteotomy**
- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing per fixation method (acute plate/nail fixation may allow earlier progressive loading than a frame under active correction); frame pin-site care if applicable; ROM of adjacent joints maintained throughout.
- Correction phase (if gradual/frame-based): typically 0.5–1 mm/day distraction or angular correction rate, with neurovascular checks at each clinic visit; overcorrection is sometimes intentionally planned for rotational deformities to offset expected settling.
- Consolidation phase: frame retained or fixation left in place until radiographic union at the osteotomy site, generally 3–5 months depending on bone quality and correction magnitude; progressive weight-bearing as consolidation index improves.
- Return to sport/heavy work typically 6–12 months after corrective osteotomy, contingent on solid union and restored strength/proprioception.

**Fat embolism syndrome — ICU/ward course**
- Acute phase (24–72 h): mechanical ventilation with lung-protective strategy if ARDS develops, hemodynamic support, neurological monitoring (serial GCS, consider ICP monitoring if cerebral FES with reduced consciousness); most patients who survive the acute pulmonary/cerebral phase begin measurable improvement within 3–7 days.
- Subacute phase (weeks 1–3): weaning from ventilatory support, cognitive reassessment if cerebral involvement occurred, early mobilization once hemodynamically stable, coordinated with the fracture-specific rehabilitation plan for the inciting injury.
- Longer-term: neuropsychological follow-up for patients with cerebral fat embolism, as cognitive deficits can persist for months; otherwise rehabilitation trajectory follows the underlying fracture's own protocol once the systemic syndrome resolves.

## Complications

- **Fracture-related infection (FRI)** — the dominant complication of open fractures; risk scales with Gustilo-Anderson grade (roughly 0–2% type I, up to 25–50%+ type IIIC), delayed debridement/antibiotics, and delayed soft-tissue coverage beyond 7 days.
- **Compartment syndrome sequelae** if diagnosis/fasciotomy delayed: Volkmann's ischemic contracture, permanent nerve palsy (foot/wrist drop), myonecrosis requiring debridement or amputation, rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury.
- **Nonunion/malunion** as sequelae of both open fractures (infection, bone loss) and polytrauma damage-control staging (prolonged external fixation, delayed conversion).
- **Amputation** — mangled extremity with combined vascular, nerve, bone, and soft-tissue injury (Gustilo IIIC in particular); early honest counseling reduces prolonged futile limb-salvage attempts with worse ultimate functional/psychological outcomes in some series (historic LEAP study framework still broadly cited).
- **ARDS/MODS** in polytrauma — precipitated by inappropriately timed "second hit" major fixation surgery in a physiologically unprepared patient; the core rationale for DCO.
- **Fat embolism syndrome** — up to 10% mortality even with modern supportive care; neurological sequelae (cognitive deficit) can persist in severe cerebral fat embolism.
- **Donor-site morbidity** from iliac crest bone graft harvest (chronic pain, sensory nerve injury, hematoma) — a real trade-off in Diamond Concept/Masquelet planning.
- **Pin-site infection and frame-related complications** in bone transport — the most common reason patients/surgeons favor Masquelet when defect size allows.
- **Docking-site nonunion** in bone transport, frequently requiring secondary bone grafting/exchange nailing at the docking site even after successful transport.
- **Adjacent joint degeneration** from malunion — angular or rotational malalignment increasing joint contact-pressure asymmetry over years.
- **Delayed union** — a distinct entity from established nonunion (healing is slower than expected but still progressing); management is watchful waiting with optimization of modifiable factors and consideration of dynamization or adjunct biologics before committing to revision surgery, since many delayed unions still go on to heal without further intervention.
- **Deep vein thrombosis / pulmonary embolism** — elevated risk across this entire cluster (immobility, pelvic/lower-limb trauma, prolonged surgery); mechanical prophylaxis started early, pharmacologic prophylaxis timed around any planned procedures and bleeding risk, particularly relevant in polytrauma with concurrent TBI or solid-organ injury where anticoagulation timing is a frequent multidisciplinary discussion point.
- **Heterotopic ossification** — more common after blast/high-energy polytrauma, TBI-associated fractures, and extensive soft-tissue/muscle injury; can limit joint ROM and complicate secondary reconstructive procedures.
- **Chronic regional pain syndrome and post-traumatic stiffness** — under-recognized long-term complications across open fracture and polytrauma populations; early active mobilization and multimodal analgesia reduce risk.
- **Psychological morbidity** (PTSD, depression) is common after major polytrauma and open fracture with prolonged reconstruction; screening and early referral improve functional and return-to-work outcomes and should be part of routine follow-up, not an afterthought.
- **Implant-related complications** — screw/plate loosening or breakage at a nonunion site (mechanical failure signals inadequate initial stability and should prompt revision rather than repeat attempts with the same construct), and stress-shielding under overly rigid plating in some diaphyseal constructs.
- **Growth of resistant organisms** from prolonged "just in case" antibiotic courses beyond the 72-hour ceiling — a stewardship-relevant complication distinct from the injury itself but directly influenced by surgeon prescribing habits.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- [Northern Trauma Network / BOAST Open Fracture Management Executive Summary (2024)](https://traumanorth.org.uk/files/pdf/ntn-open-fractures-guideline-2024.pdf) — reaffirms and updates UK national standards: photograph wounds once, antibiotics as early as possible, debridement within 24 h (not a strict 6-hour rule), and transfer/definitive management pathways for combined orthoplastic care; useful as a concrete operational template even outside the UK.
- [Orthoplastics Management of Open Lower Limb Fractures at a Major Trauma Centre: Audit of Adherence to BOAST4 Guidelines, JPRAS Open/PMC (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11415631/) — real-world audit found 81% debrided within 24 h and 75% definitively closed within 72 h, confirming these targets are achievable but still leave a meaningful compliance gap worth auditing locally.
- [Surgical Infection Society: 2022 Updated Guidelines for Antibiotic Use in Open Extremity Fractures, Surg Infect (Larchmt), PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36350736/) — Gram-positive coverage alone is sufficient for type I/II fractures (no added benefit from extended-spectrum agents); type III antibiotics should not exceed 24 h after wound closure/each subsequent procedure and never beyond 72 h total absent documented infection — this narrows practice away from prolonged "prophylactic" courses still common in some centres.
- [SEXTANT trial — Evaluation of a New Strategy for Protocolized Antibiotic Care for Severe Open Fractures, ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04678154) — ongoing multicenter RCT (primary completion estimated 2026) testing a protocolized antibiotic strategy for severe open fractures; watch for results that may further standardize type III antibiotic duration/selection.
- [2025 AAOS Updated Clinical Practice Guideline: Management of Acute Compartment Syndrome, Guideline Central](https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/9783/) — updates the evidence base: moderate evidence supports continuous/repeated ICP monitoring using a ΔP < 30 mmHg threshold to help rule out ACS, and explicitly flags that neuraxial/regional anesthesia can mask clinical diagnosis, warranting increased monitoring vigilance when blocks are used in at-risk patients.
- [Pressure trends and diagnosis of acute compartment syndrome, PMC (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12860628/) and [Diagnosis of acute compartment syndrome: current diagnostic parameters, Injury (2025)](https://www.injuryjournal.com/article/S0020-1383(25)00631-X/fulltext) — recent reappraisals of ICP thresholds reinforce that single absolute-pressure cutoffs are less reliable than trend/ΔP-based monitoring, supporting a move toward continuous rather than single-point pressure measurement in equivocal cases.
- [Stevens JM, Tejwani NC. Damage-control orthopedics or early total care: what you need to know. J Trauma Acute Care Surg (2024)](https://journals.lww.com/jtrauma/fulltext/2024/05000/damage_control_orthopedics_or_early_total_care_.2.aspx) — reaffirms that no RCT-level evidence proves DCO superior to ETC across the board; physiologic staging (not a rigid timing rule) should drive the decision, and "window of opportunity" thinking is being de-emphasized in favor of continuous physiologic reassessment.
- [External Fixators as a Tool for Damage Control Orthopedics in Severely Injured or Polytrauma Patients, PMC (2024)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11470837/) — contemporary review reinforcing external fixation's continued central role in DCO staging strategy and practical frame-application pearls.
- [Surgical treatment of critical size bone defects with Masquelet technique versus bone transport: a systematic review and meta-analysis, Arch Orthop Trauma Surg / PubMed (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37695386/) — pooled comparative data (6 studies, 364 patients): no significant overall outcome difference except better ASAMI bone scores and fewer residual deformities favoring one technique in subgroup analysis; Masquelet showed lower cost, shorter time-to-full-load after final surgery, and better quality-of-life scores, while bone transport showed higher union rates (~95% vs ~86%) — supports individualized rather than protocol-driven selection.
- [Modified Masquelet technique coupled with vascularized fibular transplantation for ultra-long structural bone defects following osteomyelitis debridement, BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders (2025)](https://bmcmusculoskeletdisord.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12891-025-09090-0) — combined technique achieved union in all cases (mean 3.8 months) for very long infected defects, suggesting a hybrid approach for cases exceeding standard Masquelet critical-size limits.
- [Surgical nonunion treatment of large-sized defects of femur and tibia based on the diamond concept, Bone & Joint Open (2024/2025)](https://boneandjoint.org.uk/Article/10.1302/2633-1462.61.BJO-2024-0096.R1) — in defects ≥5 cm treated under the Diamond Concept framework, union achieved in 64.3% within 2 years, tempering the historically cited "100% success" figure and clarifying that very large defects need defect-specific (Masquelet/transport) strategies layered onto diamond-concept biology optimization.
- [State of the Nonunion: A review of the latest literature, PMC (2024/2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11807701/) — consolidated contemporary review of nonunion risk factors, diagnostic thresholds, and the evidence tiers behind BMAC, PRP, and BMP adjuncts.
- [Does the Timing, Type, and Method of Flap Coverage After Open Tibia Fracture Fixation Influence the Rate of Deep Infection?, Microsurgery / Wiley (2025)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/micr.70110) and [Does timing of flap coverage in open fractures affect the risk of fracture-related infections during the index hospital stay? A nationwide US analysis, Injury (2025)](https://www.injuryjournal.com/article/S0020-1383(25)00770-3/abstract) — both reinforce that coverage delay (particularly beyond 7 days) is associated with higher deep infection rates, supporting the fix-and-flap/orthoplastic-pathway timing targets above.
- [Intramedullary nail prior to flap coverage may not increase complications in Gustilo-Anderson Grade IIIB and IIIC open tibial fractures, PMC (2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12616495/) — challenges the strict simultaneity dogma of "fix and flap," suggesting nailing before coverage is a viable sequencing option when a combined single-stage procedure isn't logistically feasible.
- [Fat embolism syndrome after trauma: what you need to know, J Trauma Acute Care Surg (2024)](https://journals.lww.com/jtrauma/fulltext/2024/10000/fat_embolism_syndrome_after_trauma__what_you_need.4.aspx) — updated clinician-facing synthesis reaffirming Gurd's criteria as the practical diagnostic standard and early fracture fixation plus meticulous supportive/ventilatory care as the only evidence-supported management, with mortality <10% when managed supportively.
- [Fat Embolism Syndrome: Evolving Perspectives on Diagnosis and Care, PMC (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12679895/) — recent review highlighting continued underdiagnosis due to lack of a single validated criterion set and reinforcing that no pharmacologic therapy (including corticosteroids) has robust evidence for altering outcomes.

**Common technical pitfalls across this cluster**
- Treating the skin wound size as a proxy for injury severity rather than formally grading after debridement (Gustilo-Anderson is an intra-operative, not an ED, classification).
- Repeatedly removing ED dressings to "look at" an open wound — this increases contamination and pain without changing management; photograph once, cover, splint, move to theatre.
- Applying a rigid "6-hour rule" for debridement and thereby either rushing an unprepared team or, conversely, assuming any wound outside 6 hours is automatically high-risk — current evidence supports a 24-hour window with faster action only for heavily contaminated wounds.
- Missing the deep posterior compartment on leg fasciotomy — always confirm all four compartments are released and documented.
- Grafting an infected nonunion before eradicating infection — culture, debride, and use a staged antibiotic-spacer protocol first.
- Choosing bone transport or Masquelet by habit rather than by defect size, soft-tissue status, and patient tolerance for prolonged external fixation — both are valid, complementary tools.
- Committing a hemodynamically unstable polytrauma patient to a long definitive fixation ("second hit") instead of damage-control external fixation — physiology, not a fixed time window, should drive this decision.
