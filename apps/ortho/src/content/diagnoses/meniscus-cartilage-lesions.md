## Quick Reference

**Core principle: save the meniscus.** Meniscectomy (even partial) accelerates cartilage wear and OA progression proportional to resection volume; repair-first is the default whenever tissue quality, tear pattern, and patient factors allow it. Alignment, ligament stability, and (for cartilage lesions) subchondral bone health must be corrected concurrently — a repair or cartilage graft placed into a malaligned or unstable knee fails.

### Treatment ladder by diagnosis

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Degenerative meniscus tear (no mechanical block) | Structured exercise therapy 12–16 wk (ESCAPE/METEOR-equivalent) | NSAIDs, activity modification, weight loss, ± intra-articular steroid | APM only if locking/failed PT ≥3 mo; no benefit over PT for pain alone |
| Traumatic peripheral longitudinal/bucket-handle tear (young, red-red/red-white zone) | — (mechanical symptoms mandate surgery) | — | Repair (inside-out/all-inside/outside-in) |
| Root tear (acute, traumatic, minimal extrusion, low OA) | — | — | Transtibial pull-out or suture-anchor repair |

| Ramp lesion (with ACL tear, unstable on probing) | — | — | Repair concurrent with ACLR (inside-out or all-inside meniscocapsular) |
| Discoid meniscus, asymptomatic | Observation | — | — |
| Discoid meniscus, symptomatic/torn | — | — | Arthroscopic saucerization ± repair (preserve peripheral rim) |

### Key numbers

- **Meniscectomy contact-pressure effect**: total meniscectomy increases tibiofemoral contact pressure by 235%; partial meniscectomy (medial) increases contact pressure roughly in proportion to % resected (~65% removal → ~70–90% pressure increase).
- **Root tear biomechanics**: a complete root tear is functionally equivalent to a total meniscectomy in contact pressure and joint kinematics.
- **Root repair candidacy cutoffs**: meniscal extrusion >3 mm, Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≥3, and varus malalignment >5° each independently predict poor repair outcome — HTO or arthroplasty pathway favored.
- **Ramp lesion prevalence**: up to 17–42% of ACL-injured knees (wide range by detection method — arthroscopic medial gutter view via notch or posteromedial portal roughly doubles detection vs. standard anterior-portal view).
- **ESCAPE/METEOR takeaway**: no clinically relevant difference between APM and structured physical therapy for degenerative tears without mechanical locking at up to 5 years; ~25–35% of PT-randomized patients eventually cross over to surgery, usually within the first year. Framed as an NNT, roughly 3–4 patients would need to start with PT-first to spare one patient an arthroscopy that would not have added functional benefit over PT alone — useful framing for shared decision-making conversations.
- **Outerbridge/ICRS threshold for grafting**: ICRS grade 3B–4 (or Outerbridge III-IV) with a contained, focal, full-thickness lesion is the classic indication zone for OAT/ACI/OCA; diffuse/bipolar/kissing lesions and uncorrected malalignment are contraindications.
- **Discoid meniscus**: lateral >> medial; bilateral in 15–20%; Wrisberg-type (no posterior meniscotibial attachment) is unstable and requires repair, not saucerization alone.
- **OCD lesion size for poor prognosis**: >2 cm² and involvement of the weight-bearing lateral femoral condyle or unstable/detached fragments predict failure of conservative care.
- **SIFK progression to TKA**: roughly one-third of SIFK/SONK patients progress to arthroplasty; predictors are lesion size >5 cm² (or >50% condylar width on coronal MRI), baseline OA, and lack of early unloading.

### Red flags

- **Locked knee** (fixed flexion deformity, block to full extension) = bucket-handle tear until proven otherwise → urgent MRI/arthroscopy, do not delay for prolonged conservative trial.
- **Acute ACL tear + posteromedial joint-line tenderness/effusion out of proportion** → actively look for ramp lesion (often missed on standard MRI and standard anterior-portal arthroscopy).
- **Sudden-onset, severe, localized medial femoral condyle pain in a patient >55, worse at night, disproportionate to exam findings** → SIFK until proven otherwise; obtain MRI even with normal radiographs (early SIFK is radiographically occult).
- **Adolescent/young adult with vague activity-related knee pain and a normal exam** → consider OCD; get notch/tunnel view radiographs, do not dismiss as growing pains.
- **New varus thrust or rapid joint-space narrowing after meniscectomy or root tear** → accelerated OA trajectory, counsel and consider unloading (brace/HTO) early.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Meniscal structure and biomechanics

The menisci are C-shaped (lateral more circular and covering a greater proportion of the tibial plateau, medial more crescentic and firmly fixed peripherally to the deep MCL/capsule) fibrocartilaginous structures composed predominantly of circumferential type I collagen fibers that convert axial load into hoop stress, resisted at the anterior and posterior roots' bony insertions. Radial (tie) fibers on the surface resist longitudinal splitting and are the reason a radial tear is so mechanically destructive — it severs the circumferential fibers outright. Only the peripheral 10–30% (red-red zone) is vascularized via the perimeniscal capillary plexus (branches of the medial and lateral geniculate arteries); the middle red-white zone has limited peripheral vascularity; the inner two-thirds (white-white zone) is avascular and relies entirely on synovial fluid diffusion for nutrition. This vascular zonation is the single biggest determinant of intrinsic healing potential and therefore of repair candidacy — it should be assessed and documented (by location relative to the meniscosynovial junction) for every tear considered for repair.

Functionally the menisci provide: (1) load transmission — roughly 50–70% of compressive load through the medial compartment and up to 100% through the lateral compartment passes through the meniscus in extension, increasing further in flexion; (2) secondary restraint to anterior tibial translation (the medial meniscus, especially its posterior horn, becomes a critical secondary stabilizer in the ACL-deficient knee — this is why an unrepaired posterior horn tear or ramp lesion measurably increases residual laxity after ACL reconstruction); (3) shock absorption and congruity enhancement between the convex femoral condyles and relatively flat tibial plateau; (4) proprioception via mechanoreceptors concentrated in the anterior and posterior horns.

### Root anatomy

**Meniscal roots** anchor the anterior and posterior horns to the tibial eminence; the posterior medial root sits just anterior to the PCL tibial insertion, the posterior lateral root just posteromedial to the popliteus hiatus and closely related to the lateral meniscofemoral ligaments (Humphry/Wrisberg), which can partially substitute mechanically for a lateral posterior root tear and explain why isolated lateral root tears are sometimes better tolerated than medial ones. Root disruption abolishes hoop-stress conversion — the meniscus extrudes radially and becomes biomechanically non-functional even though the meniscal body itself is structurally intact, reproducing near-total-meniscectomy contact mechanics on dynamic loading studies.

### Ramp zone

**Ramp lesions** are tears/avulsions of the peripheral attachment of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus to the posteromedial capsule and meniscotibial (coronary) ligament — a distinct entity from a posterior horn substance tear, occurring at the meniscosynovial junction rather than within meniscal substance. Because this zone is well vascularized, ramp lesions have good repair-healing potential, but they are easily missed because the tear lies posterior to the PCL, out of the direct line of sight from a standard anterior arthroscopic portal — visualization requires a trans-notch view or a dedicated posteromedial portal.

### Discoid variant

**Discoid meniscus** is a congenital variant (failure of normal central resorption during fetal/early development) producing an abnormally thick, disc-shaped lateral meniscus that lacks the normal semilunar central deficiency; it is classified by shape (complete — covers the entire tibial plateau; incomplete — partial coverage) and, more clinically importantly, by stability of the posterior peripheral attachment (Wrisberg variant lacks the normal posterior meniscotibial/coronary attachment, relying only on the meniscofemoral ligament for posterior fixation, and is unstable — prone to a "snapping knee" as the unanchored posterior horn subluxates with flexion-extension).

### Articular cartilage biology

**Articular cartilage** is aneural, avascular, alymphatic hyaline cartilage with essentially no intrinsic healing capacity for full-thickness (chondral) defects — spontaneous repair, when it occurs at all, produces mechanically inferior fibrocartilage (type I collagen, lower proteoglycan content, inferior wear resistance) rather than native hyaline (type II collagen, organized zonal architecture). This is the central biological rationale for every cartilage restoration procedure: either recruit marrow-derived mesenchymal cells across a perforated subchondral plate (microfracture), transplant native hyaline cartilage en bloc with its subchondral bone (OAT/OCA), or harvest, expand, and re-implant the patient's own chondrocytes (ACI/MACI) to regenerate a more hyaline-like matrix.

### OCD pathophysiology

**OCD** (osteochondritis dissecans) is a focal, idiopathic subchondral bone lesion with variable involvement/stability of the overlying articular cartilage, classically located at the posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle (~70–80% of cases), with the lateral femoral condyle, patella, and trochlea accounting for most of the remainder. The leading pathophysiologic theory is repetitive microtrauma (the posterolateral medial condyle repeatedly abuts the tibial spine during activity) superimposed on a genetic/vascular predisposition (a relatively end-arterial, watershed blood supply to the involved epiphyseal region in some individuals). Must be distinguished from an incidental "ossification variant" in skeletally immature patients — done by lesion location, symmetry (variants are often bilateral/symmetric), and absence of MRI instability criteria.

### SIFK pathophysiology and nomenclature

**SIFK** (spontaneous insufficiency fracture of the knee, historically "SONK" — spontaneous osteonecrosis of the knee) is now understood as primarily a subchondral insufficiency fracture occurring in osteopenic/osteoporotic subchondral bone (typically women >55, sometimes precipitated by a rapid-onset meniscal root tear or extrusion that removes the hoop-stress protection normally shielding the adjacent condyle from peak contact stress), with osteonecrosis representing a secondary, end-stage histological finding on the collapsed fragment rather than the primary inciting event. This reclassification matters clinically because it reframes management around bone-health optimization and mechanical unloading (as for any insufficiency fracture) rather than around treating a primary vascular/necrotic process — and it explains the well-documented association between medial meniscus root tears and medial femoral condyle SIFK.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (twisting/pivoting vs. squatting vs. atraumatic insidious onset), mechanical symptoms (locking, catching, giving-way), effusion timing (immediate = more likely bony/ligamentous; delayed 24 h = more classic meniscal/synovial), and for older patients ask specifically about a discrete twisting event vs. gradual onset (distinguishes traumatic from degenerative tear pattern and reframes shared decision-making toward PT-first for the latter).

**Exam**: joint-line tenderness (sensitive, not specific), McMurray and Thessaly tests (moderate sensitivity/specificity, better combined than alone), Apley grind/compression-distraction, effusion, extension block/locked knee (bucket-handle), and always assess coronal alignment (standing exam, consider full-length films if considering root repair or cartilage restoration), gait (varus thrust), and ligamentous stability (ACL laxity dramatically changes meniscal repair risk calculus and mandates concurrent reconstruction).

**Root tear specific**: Godfrey/posterior sag and standard laxity exam are usually normal (root tear is not a ligament injury); MRI is the key diagnostic step — look for the "ghost sign" (absence of meniscal tissue on sagittal images through the normal root location), a vertical linear defect near the root on coronal images, and meniscal extrusion >3 mm on coronal MRI (measured from the tibial plateau margin) which correlates with root pathology and predicts poorer repair outcomes.

**Ramp lesion specific**: standard MRI sensitivity for ramp lesions is only moderate (~55–75%); posteromedial fluid/edema at the meniscocapsular junction on MRI raises suspicion. Definitive diagnosis is arthroscopic, requiring visualization through the intercondylar notch (trans-notch view) or a posteromedial portal — probing the posterior horn with the knee in a figure-4 position is essential in every ACL reconstruction case, especially with high-grade preoperative laxity (pivot shift 2+/3+).

**Discoid meniscus**: often presents in children/adolescents with a painless "snapping/clunking" knee (Wrisberg variant) or with symptoms only after superimposed tear; MRI shows a "bow-tie sign" on ≥3 consecutive sagittal slices (vs. normal 1–2) and increased meniscal width on coronal images.

**OCD**: AP, lateral, and notch/tunnel view radiographs (notch view best profiles the classic posterolateral medial condyle location); MRI is essential for staging stability — instability criteria include a high-signal (fluid) line ≥5 mm deep to the fragment on T2, a peripheral high-signal rim, multiple breaches/cysts beneath the fragment, and a focal osteochondral defect with fluid extending through the cartilage surface.

**SIFK**: radiographs are often normal in early disease (a key pitfall); MRI shows diffuse bone marrow edema in the subchondral femoral (or tibial) condyle with a subchondral fracture line — differentiate from AVN (which shows the classic serpentine geographic sclerotic-rim pattern, often bilateral and multifocal in steroid/alcohol-associated cases) and from a stress fracture in an athlete (different demographic and history). Always evaluate for an associated meniscal root tear/extrusion in medial-compartment SIFK — this is a recognized causal pathway.

**Weight-bearing/alignment imaging**: standing long-leg (hip-knee-ankle) films are mandatory before root repair, meniscal transplant, or any cartilage restoration procedure in a patient with clinical or radiographic malalignment — uncorrected varus >5° (or valgus for lateral-sided pathology) predicts failure of an isolated biologic procedure.

### Imaging pearls by diagnosis

| Diagnosis | Key imaging finding | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| Root tear | "Ghost sign" on sagittal MRI, coronal extrusion >3 mm, vertical linear defect at root | Extrusion can be mistaken for simple degenerative change; measure it explicitly on coronal images |
| Ramp lesion | Posteromedial fluid/edema at meniscocapsular junction; standard MRI sensitivity only ~55–75% | A "normal" MRI does not exclude a ramp lesion — probe arthroscopically in every ACL case |
| Discoid meniscus | "Bow-tie sign" on ≥3 consecutive sagittal slices; increased coronal meniscal width | Wrisberg instability is a clinical/arthroscopic diagnosis, not reliably seen on static MRI |
| OCD | Notch/tunnel radiograph view; MRI instability criteria (≥5 mm fluid line, peripheral rim, cysts, surface breach) | Do not rely on radiographs alone to stage stability — MRI is required for surgical decision-making |
| SIFK | Diffuse subchondral BME with fracture line; radiographs often normal early | Easily mistaken for early degenerative change or dismissed if radiographs are normal — MRI threshold should be low in the at-risk demographic |
| Focal chondral defect | Full-thickness cartilage signal loss ± subchondral edema; assess containment and bipolarity | MRI tends to underestimate true lesion size/depth versus arthroscopic findings — confirm intraoperatively before committing to a graft size |

### Differentiating the atraumatic knee-pain mimics

Because SIFK, OCD, degenerative meniscal tears, and early focal chondral disease all present with overlapping "atraumatic activity-related knee pain," a structured differential is worth stating explicitly:

- **Age/demographic pattern**: OCD skews adolescent/young adult; degenerative meniscal tear and SIFK skew middle-aged to older, with SIFK specifically over-represented in postmenopausal women.
- **Onset**: SIFK is classically sudden and severe; degenerative meniscal tears and chondral lesions are more often insidious/activity-related.
- **Localization**: SIFK pain is typically well-localized to the affected condyle and worse at night/rest, out of proportion to exam findings — this nocturnal, disproportionate quality is a useful discriminator from mechanical meniscal or chondral pain, which is more load-dependent.
- **Radiograph timing**: OCD is usually visible on notch-view radiographs at presentation; SIFK is frequently radiographically occult early and only becomes visible (subchondral flattening/collapse) at a later stage — a normal radiograph does not exclude either OCD (if the correct view wasn't obtained) or SIFK, and MRI should be pursued when clinical suspicion is significant.

## Classification

### Meniscus tear morphology

| Pattern | Description | Vascular zone typically involved | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vertical longitudinal | Tear parallel to meniscal circumference, full or partial thickness | Often peripheral (red-red/red-white) | Best repair candidate; if displaced and causing locking = bucket-handle |
| Bucket-handle | Displaced vertical longitudinal tear with the inner fragment flipped into the notch/gutter | Peripheral rim intact, fragment often white-white | Urgent reduction + repair if tissue viable and knee locked; discard fragment only if non-repairable |
| Radial | Perpendicular to circumferential fibers, disrupts hoop-stress transmission | Central, often white-white | Behaves biomechanically like a root tear if complete; repair (if reachable) vs. partial meniscectomy of the apex |
| Horizontal (cleavage) | Splits meniscus into superior/inferior leaves, parallel to tibial plateau | Often mid-body, degenerative | Usually degenerative etiology; partial meniscectomy of unstable flap, or observe if stable/asymptomatic |
| Root tear | Radial tear or avulsion within 1 cm of the bony root attachment | Root is well-vascularized bony insertion | Functionally = total meniscectomy if untreated; repair candidacy per extrusion/OA/alignment criteria below |
| Complex/degenerative | Combination of above, often with horizontal cleavage + flap, in osteoarthritic meniscus | Multiple zones, poor tissue quality | Usually not repairable; treat the person (PT-first), not the MRI — APM only for mechanical symptoms |
| Flap/parrot-beak | Oblique tear combining radial and horizontal components | Variable | Debride unstable flap only, preserve stable rim |

### LaPrade classification of meniscal root tears

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Type 1 | Partial stable root tear | Non-operative or observation; usually does not require repair |
| Type 2 | Complete radial tear within 9 mm of root attachment (2A: 0–3 mm, 2B: 3–6 mm, 2C: 6–9 mm) | Most common; primary indication for transtibial pull-out or suture-anchor repair |
| Type 3 | Bucket-handle tear with complete root detachment | Repair the bucket-handle component and the root simultaneously |
| Type 4 | Complex oblique/longitudinal tear extending into the root | Repair if tissue quality allows; often technically demanding |
| Type 5 | Avulsion fracture of the root attachment (bony root avulsion) | Suture or screw fixation of the bony fragment, generally excellent healing |

Repair candidacy modifiers layered on top of LaPrade type (per current evidence): Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≤2, meniscal extrusion ≤3 mm, mechanical axis within 5° of neutral, age generally <60–65 (relative), and absence of diffuse cartilage loss in the affected compartment. Outside these parameters, outcomes of repair approach those of partial meniscectomy/non-operative care and an arthroplasty-pathway discussion (or concurrent HTO) is more appropriate.

### ICRS (International Cartilage Repair Society) classification — chondral lesions

| Grade | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Normal cartilage | No treatment |
| 1 | Nearly normal — superficial fissures/soft indentation (1A) or superficial lacerations (1B) | Non-operative; not a restoration candidate |
| 2 | Abnormal — lesion extends <50% of cartilage depth | Debridement/chondroplasty if symptomatic; not a graft indication alone |
| 3 | Severely abnormal — defect extends >50% depth, to calcified layer, not through subchondral bone (3A/3B/3C by depth; 3D includes blistering) | Threshold zone for restorative procedures (microfracture, OAT, ACI/MACI) if focal, contained, symptomatic |
| 4 | Severely abnormal, full-thickness with subchondral bone penetration (4A) or osteochondral (4B) | OAT or OCA (osteochondral graft needed if bone loss present); ACI/MACI alone insufficient if subchondral bone deficient |

ICRS also provides a separate OCD staging system (I–IV: stable intact cartilage → cartilage breach with underlying fracture/fluid → partially detached fragment → fully detached/loose body), used arthroscopically alongside MRI instability criteria.

### Outerbridge classification — chondral lesions (arthroscopic/radiographic, historically applied to patella, now used knee-wide)

| Grade | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Normal cartilage | No treatment |
| I | Softening and swelling of cartilage | Non-operative, activity modification |
| II | Partial-thickness fissuring/fragmentation <1.5 cm diameter | Debridement/chondroplasty if mechanical symptoms; not a graft indication |
| III | Fissuring/fragmentation to subchondral bone, >1.5 cm diameter ("crabmeat" appearance) | Candidate for restorative procedure if focal and contained |
| IV | Full-thickness cartilage loss with exposed subchondral bone | Strongest indication for OAT/ACI/MACI/OCA depending on size and bone involvement; diffuse Grade IV trends toward arthroplasty pathway instead |

Outerbridge and ICRS are frequently used interchangeably in practice (both grade roughly 0–4 with similar depth criteria); ICRS is the more granular, currently preferred research standard, but Outerbridge remains ubiquitous in US operative notes.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Degenerative/atraumatic meniscus tears without mechanical block**: structured, supervised exercise therapy (progressive quadriceps/hip strengthening, neuromuscular training) is first-line and non-inferior to arthroscopic partial meniscectomy (APM) at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months, sustained to 5 years (ESCAPE trial); METEOR similarly showed no superiority of APM over PT at 12 months for degenerative tears with OA changes. A trial of ≥12 weeks structured PT is reasonable before considering APM in this population; APM remains appropriate for true mechanical locking/catching not resolving with PT.
- **Focal chondral defects, low-grade (ICRS 1–2) or asymptomatic-incidental**: activity modification, weight optimization, quadriceps/hip abductor strengthening, and correction of any underlying malalignment or instability driving abnormal loading.
- **Stable OCD (skeletally immature or young adult, MRI-stable, intact cartilage)**: activity restriction (avoid impact/pivoting sports) for 3–6 months, consider a period of protected/non-weight-bearing or unloader bracing, with serial radiographs/MRI; healing rates in skeletally immature stable OCD are favorable (majority heal with non-operative care) but decline sharply in skeletally mature patients — a much lower threshold for surgery is used in adults with OCD.
- **SIFK, early stage (BME without collapse)**: protected weight-bearing (partial to non-weight-bearing) for 6–8 weeks, unloader bracing, analgesia, and consideration of a bisphosphonate course (evidence mixed but commonly used); vitamin D/calcium optimization. Serial MRI to confirm resolution before progressive loading.
- **Discoid meniscus, asymptomatic (incidental finding)**: observation only — resection of an asymptomatic discoid meniscus is not indicated.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Intra-articular corticosteroid**: reasonable short-term symptomatic bridge in degenerative meniscal tears and low-grade chondral disease; does not alter tear morphology or repair biology.
- **Viscosupplementation (hyaluronic acid)**: modest, inconsistent symptomatic benefit in degenerative meniscopathy/early chondral disease; not disease-modifying. Best considered an adjunct while PT is optimized, not a substitute.
- **PRP**: increasingly used as an adjunct at the time of meniscal repair (root and complex tears) to augment the biologically limited vascular response, and as an adjunct to microfracture/minced-cartilage procedures; evidence supports a possible reduction in early failure/re-tear with meniscal repair augmentation, but is not yet strong enough to mandate use — reasonable shared-decision adjunct, not a stand-alone treatment for a structural tear or root tear.
- **Bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC)**: used as an augment at microfracture and at ACI/MACI sites in some protocols; evidence is early-stage/heterogeneous.
- **Minced cartilage / autologous chondral fragment techniques**: single-stage alternative to ACI, showing encouraging early clinical outcomes when combined with PRP or fibrin scaffold in recent case series (2024–2025 multicentric data), but longer-term comparative data versus MACI are still maturing.

### Operative

**Meniscus tear repair (traumatic, peripheral, vertical-longitudinal/bucket-handle)**
- Techniques: inside-out (gold standard for posterior horn access and fixation strength, requires posteromedial/posterolateral accessory incision and neurovascular protection — saphenous nerve medially, peroneal nerve laterally), all-inside (device-based, faster, avoids accessory incision, biomechanically comparable in modern implant generations), outside-in (favored for anterior horn/body tears, lowest neurovascular risk).
- Indications favoring repair over meniscectomy: peripheral (red-red or red-white zone) location, tear length >1 cm, patient age generally <40–50 (relative — biology matters more than chronologic age), acute/subacute tear, concurrent ACL reconstruction (biologically favorable environment — repair success rates are meaningfully higher when performed with concomitant ACLR versus in an ACL-stable/isolated knee).
- Pitfalls:
  - Incomplete reduction of a chronic bucket-handle fragment before fixation — an incompletely reduced fragment heals in a foreshortened, non-anatomic position and remains symptomatic.
  - Missing a concurrent ramp or root lesion — always probe the posterior horn of both menisci, not just the torn segment identified on MRI.
  - Repairing degenerative white-white tissue with poor healing potential — better served by limited debridement of the unstable flap than by a repair doomed to fail.
  - Vertical mattress/all-inside device placement too close to the popliteal neurovascular bundle on lateral posterior horn repairs — know the posterolateral safe zone.

**Root repair**
- Preferred technique for most Type 2 (LaPrade) tears: transtibial pull-out repair — two limbs of suture (simple or locking cinch stitch) through the root stump, passed through a tibial tunnel drilled at the anatomic footprint and fixed over a button/post on the anteromedial tibia; suture-anchor (all-inside) fixation directly into the footprint is an alternative, particularly for posterolateral root tears where tunnel trajectory is more difficult.
- Technical pearls: anatomic tunnel placement is critical (malpositioned tunnels under-restore hoop-stress mechanics); tension the repair with the knee near full extension; address concurrent extrusion — some surgeons add a centralization stitch, though evidence for extrusion correction with centralization remains mixed; a concurrent varus malalignment >5° should prompt staged or concurrent HTO, since isolated root repair in a malaligned knee has substantially worse survivorship.
- Outcomes: recent systematic reviews (2024–2025) confirm root repair yields superior radiographic (less joint-space narrowing) and clinical (IKDC, Lysholm) outcomes compared with partial meniscectomy or non-operative treatment for appropriately selected patients; healing on second-look/MRI is achieved in the majority but not all repairs, and extrusion often persists even after successful root healing.
- Counseling point: patients should understand that root repair reliably improves pain and function and slows (but does not fully arrest) progression of joint-space narrowing — it is a joint-preservation procedure, not a guaranteed cure for the degenerative trajectory already set in motion by chronic extrusion.

**Ramp lesion repair**
- Performed concurrently with ACL reconstruction whenever the ramp tear is unstable on probing (>2 mm mobility on hook-probe testing through the notch or a posteromedial portal); stable, non-displaceable lesions may be left alone or simply trephinated/abraded to stimulate healing.
- Technique: all-inside device repair via posteromedial portal or trans-notch approach is now most common (avoids a separate posteromedial incision); inside-out repair from a posteromedial incision remains an excellent option, particularly for more peripheral meniscocapsular/meniscotibial ligament avulsions ("reverse ramp"/hidden lesions) not well captured by all-inside devices.
- Rationale: unrepaired ramp lesions left in situ at the time of ACLR are associated with increased residual anteroposterior and rotational laxity, higher graft strain, and inferior second-look healing/stability compared with repaired lesions — repair is now considered standard of care when identified.

**Discoid meniscus surgery**
- Saucerization (partial central resection) to recreate a normal semilunar shape and width (typically leaving a peripheral rim of 6–8 mm), performed for symptomatic complete or incomplete discoid menisci.
- Repair of the posterior peripheral attachment is mandatory in Wrisberg-variant (unstable) discoid menisci and whenever an associated peripheral tear is found after saucerization — saucerization alone in an unstable meniscus leaves persistent instability/snapping and risks progressive tearing.
- Subtotal/total meniscectomy is now reserved for irreparable, severely degenerated discoid tissue; recent comparative data (2024) favor saucerization + repair over subtotal meniscectomy for complex discoid tears on 2-year outcomes, though degenerative changes can still progress with either approach.

**Focal chondral defect — the cartilage restoration ladder**
- **Microfracture (bone-marrow stimulation)**: awl/pick perforation of subchondral bone to recruit a mesenchymal clot that fills the defect with fibrocartilage. Historically first-line for small lesions (<2 cm²) due to simplicity/low cost, but has fallen out of favor as first-line therapy in current practice — durability declines after 2–3 years, results deteriorate with larger lesion size, and multiple comparative studies/registries show inferior structural repair tissue (fibrocartilage vs. hyaline) and higher conversion to secondary procedures versus OAT or ACI/MACI, particularly in athletes and larger defects. Retained role: very small (<2 cm²) low-demand lesions, or as an adjunct/marrow-access step within other techniques (e.g., "microfracture-plus" combined with a scaffold).
- **Osteochondral autograft transplantation (OAT/mosaicplasty)**: cylindrical osteochondral plugs harvested from a low-weight-bearing donor site (lateral trochlea/intercondylar notch periphery) and press-fit into the defect. Single-stage, transplants native hyaline cartilage with subchondral bone, good for lesions up to ~2–3 cm² (or a bit larger with mosaicplasty using multiple plugs). Pitfalls: donor-site morbidity (up to ~10% report donor-site symptoms), plug-fill gaps/incongruity between grafts, and limited graft availability for larger defects.
- **MACI (matrix-induced/matrix-applied autologous chondrocyte implantation)**: two-stage — arthroscopic cartilage biopsy, in-vitro chondrocyte expansion onto a collagen membrane, then open/mini-arthrotomy implantation of the cell-seeded scaffold fixed with fibrin glue (no periosteal patch, unlike first-generation ACI). Preferred for larger (>3–4 cm²), full-thickness contained chondral defects with intact subchondral bone; produces hyaline-like repair tissue with durability data now extending well beyond 10 years in appropriately selected patients. Two-stage nature and cost are the principal drawbacks; not appropriate if subchondral bone is deficient (needs bone grafting first, "sandwich" technique, or an osteochondral graft instead).
- **Osteochondral allograft (OCA)**: fresh (or fresh-frozen) size-matched cadaveric osteochondral plug/shell, single-stage, used for large (generally >4 cm²), deep, or osteochondral defects (including OCD with subchondral bone loss, post-traumatic defects, and salvage after failed prior cartilage procedures). Recent (2024–2025) systematic review data report roughly high-70s-to-high-80s percent survivorship at ~10 years and strong patient-reported outcome improvement, with best results in traumatic/OCD etiology, unipolar lesions, younger patients, and shorter symptom duration; graft availability, cost, and a theoretical (very low, but real) disease-transmission risk are the trade-offs.
- **Selection logic**: lesion size, containment (contained vs. shoulder/bipolar/kissing), depth (chondral-only vs. osteochondral with bone loss), patient age/activity demand, and — critically — whether alignment, meniscal integrity, and ligamentous stability have been or will be corrected. A cartilage restoration procedure performed without correcting a concurrent >5° malalignment, meniscal deficiency, or ligament instability is set up to fail and should be staged or combined with realignment osteotomy / meniscal repair or transplant.

**Alignment correction as prerequisite (applies across the entire cartilage/meniscus ladder)**: varus or valgus malalignment concentrates load on the affected compartment and is one of the most consistent predictors of failure across meniscal repair, root repair, and every cartilage restoration technique. Practical rule: obtain standing long-leg alignment films whenever a biologic procedure (repair, OAT, MACI, OCA) is being planned in a compartment showing any degenerative change or in a patient with clinical varus/valgus thrust; if the mechanical axis passes outside the central third of the affected compartment (or clinical/radiographic varus exceeds ~5°), plan concurrent or staged high tibial osteotomy (medial-sided pathology) or distal femoral osteotomy (lateral-sided pathology) rather than proceeding with an isolated biologic procedure. This principle applies identically to SIFK grafting, root repair, and chondral/osteochondral grafting — realignment is not an optional adjunct in a malaligned knee, it is a prerequisite for graft/repair survival.

**OCD (adult knee)**
- Stable lesions failing 3–6 months of non-operative care: arthroscopic drilling (transarticular or retroarticular/extra-articular) to stimulate revascularization and healing without disturbing an intact articular surface.
- Unstable/partially detached but viable fragment: fragment fixation (bioabsorbable pins/screws or compression screws) after debriding the fibrous interface and micro-drilling/bone-grafting the crater — preserves native hyaline cartilage and is preferred whenever fragment viability allows.
- Non-viable fragment or full-thickness defect after fragment excision: treat as a focal chondral/osteochondral defect per the ladder above — OAT for smaller lesions, OCA for larger or when subchondral bone stock is deficient, ACI/MACI reserved for chondral-only defects with adequate bone.

**SIFK (spontaneous insufficiency fracture of the knee)**
- Failed conservative management (persistent pain, MRI progression) without frank collapse: core decompression to relieve intraosseous pressure and stimulate healing.
- Subchondral collapse or focal osteochondral defect: osteochondral grafting (OAT for smaller lesions, OCA for larger defects) restores the joint surface; combine with high tibial osteotomy when varus malalignment is present, since unloading the affected (typically medial) compartment materially improves graft/repair survivorship — recent literature specifically supports combined mosaicplasty + HTO in this scenario.
- End-stage collapse with secondary osteoarthritis: unicompartmental or total knee arthroplasty.
- Always evaluate for and address a causal medial meniscus root tear — treating the root pathology (repair, if still feasible, or unloading osteotomy) removes the biomechanical driver of ongoing condylar overload.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

Rehabilitation should follow the 2024 formal EU-US Meniscus Rehabilitation Consensus (ESSKA-AOSSM-AASPT) framework where a specific protocol is not otherwise dictated by concurrent procedures (e.g., ACLR). The consensus emphasizes criteria-based (not purely time-based) progression through phases, individualized to tear pattern/location, repair construct, and concurrent procedures.

### Phase overview (generic framework, individualize per procedure below)

| Phase | Typical timing | Goals | Milestones to progress |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Protection | 0–2 wk | Effusion/pain control, quadriceps activation, protect repair/graft | Minimal effusion, active quad contraction, ROM per protocol |
| II — Early motion & loading | 2–6 wk | Restore ROM within limits, progressive weight-bearing | WB progression per procedure, ROM to protocol target |
| III — Strengthening | 6–12 wk | Closed-chain strengthening, proprioception, gait normalization | Full ROM, no extensor lag, symmetric gait |
| IV — Advanced strengthening & conditioning | 3–6 mo | Sport-specific strength, plyometric introduction (as cleared) | Strength symmetry ≥85–90%, no effusion with loading |
| V — Return to sport/work | 4–12+ mo (procedure-dependent) | Criteria-based clearance | Hop-test and strength symmetry ≥90%, sport-specific drills tolerated pain/effusion-free |

**Isolated meniscectomy (partial)**
- Weight-bearing: full weight-bearing as tolerated immediately.
- ROM: unrestricted, early active ROM encouraged.
- Milestones: quadriceps activation/effusion control days 0–14; return to low-impact activity 2–4 weeks; return to sport typically 3–6 weeks once full ROM, minimal effusion, and quadriceps strength ~90% of contralateral achieved.

**Meniscus repair (peripheral longitudinal/bucket-handle, isolated or with concurrent ACLR)**
- Weight-bearing: historically restricted (partial/non-weight-bearing 4–6 weeks); accelerated protocols (early WBAT with a hinged brace locked or limited to 0–90°) are increasingly supported by recent systematic-review data showing comparable failure rates with faster return of function — protocol should be individualized to tear complexity/location and surgeon preference, but the trend in 2024–2025 literature favors earlier controlled loading over prolonged protection.
- ROM: typically limited to 0–90° for the first 4–6 weeks (avoid deep flexion, which increases posterior horn shear/hoop stress), progressing to full ROM by 6–8 weeks.
- Avoid pivoting/deep squatting/kneeling until 3–4 months.
- Return to running: ~10–12 weeks; return to cutting/pivoting sport: 4–6 months with criteria-based clearance (quadriceps/hamstring symmetry ≥90%, hop-test symmetry ≥90%, no effusion, full painless ROM).

**Root repair (transtibial pull-out or suture anchor)**
- More conservative than peripheral repair given the biomechanical demand on the tibial fixation construct: non-weight-bearing or toe-touch weight-bearing for 4–6 weeks in a brace locked in extension or limited to 0–20/30°, given axial load through flexion drives failure of pull-out fixation.
- Progressive weight-bearing weeks 6–8, full weight-bearing by 8–10 weeks.
- ROM: gradual progression from 0–90° over the first 6 weeks to full ROM by ~10–12 weeks; avoid deep flexion/pivoting until 4–5 months.
- Return to sport: generally 6–9 months, later than peripheral repair, reflecting slower root-tunnel healing biology and higher consequence of failure.

**Focal cartilage restoration (OAT/OCA/MACI)**
- Weight-bearing: non-weight-bearing to toe-touch for 4–6 weeks for femoral condyle lesions (longer, 6–8 weeks, for larger OCA or tibial plateau/patellofemoral lesions), progressing gradually to full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks.
- CPM (continuous passive motion) is commonly prescribed for MACI/ACI (several hours daily for 4–6 weeks) to promote graft nutrition/maturation; less consistently used after OAT/OCA.
- Avoid impact loading (running, jumping) for 4–6 months; low-impact conditioning (stationary bike, pool) introduced progressively from ~6–8 weeks.
- Return to pivoting sport: typically 9–12 months for MACI (graft maturation is slow — hyaline-like tissue continues remodeling well past 12 months), 6–9 months for OAT, 9–12 months for OCA; criteria-based on absence of effusion, full strength symmetry, and (where available) imaging evidence of graft incorporation/fill.

**OCD fixation/drilling**
- Drilling (intact cartilage): weight-bearing as tolerated with brace protection, ROM progressed early; return to sport 3–4 months once healing confirmed on imaging.
- Fragment fixation: protected weight-bearing 6 weeks, ROM progression similar to cartilage restoration protocols, return to sport 6–9 months pending radiographic/MRI union of the fragment.

**SIFK**
- Protected weight-bearing 6–8 weeks minimum in early-stage disease; progression guided by symptom resolution and interval MRI showing BME regression, not by a fixed timeline alone.
- After core decompression or grafting, follow the cartilage-restoration protocol above.

**Return-to-work**: sedentary work typically resumes within 1–2 weeks post-meniscectomy or repair (with brace/crutches as needed); physical/manual labor is deferred to match the return-to-sport milestones above for the corresponding procedure (i.e., not before full weight-bearing and adequate strength/ROM recovery).

## Complications

### Approximate complication/failure rates by procedure

| Procedure | Reported complication/failure | Approximate rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isolated meniscal repair | Re-tear/failure | ~10–20% | Lower with concurrent ACLR; higher with white-white zone involvement or complex tear pattern |
| Meniscal repair with concurrent ACLR | Re-tear/failure | Generally lower than isolated repair | Favorable biologic environment (marrow venting, hemarthrosis) improves healing |
| Root repair (transtibial pull-out) | Incomplete healing on second-look MRI/arthroscopy | Meaningful minority (reported ranges vary widely by series) | Persistent extrusion common even with confirmed healing; not automatically treatment failure |
| OAT | Donor-site morbidity | Up to ~10% | Usually mild; graft-plug height mismatch/incongruity is the other main technical pitfall |
| OCA | 10-year graft survivorship | Roughly high-70s to high-80s % | Best in traumatic/OCD etiology, unipolar lesions, younger patients, shorter symptom duration |
| SIFK (overall) | Progression to arthroplasty | Roughly one-third | Predicted by lesion size, baseline OA, uncorrected malalignment, delayed diagnosis |
| OCD fixation | Nonunion / hardware-related reoperation | Variable, higher with larger/less vascular fragments | Hardware prominence/synovitis is the most common reason for reoperation even with union |

- **Meniscal repair failure/re-tear**: reported rates vary widely by tear pattern and concurrent ACLR status — roughly 10–20% for isolated repairs, generally lower when performed with concurrent ACLR (favorable biologic environment from marrow-venting/hemarthrosis).
- **Root repair**: incomplete healing on second-look arthroscopy/MRI in a meaningful minority even with technically sound fixation; persistent extrusion is common even after root healing and does not necessarily indicate failure; tunnel malposition and inadequate fixation tension are the main technical failure modes.
- **Neurovascular injury during repair**: saphenous nerve (infrapatellar branch) injury with inside-out medial repair or outside-in techniques; peroneal nerve injury with inside-out lateral repair — meticulous posteromedial/posterolateral portal placement and protective retraction are essential.
- **Post-meniscectomy osteoarthritis**: dose-dependent on resection volume; even limited partial meniscectomy accelerates radiographic OA progression versus repair or non-operative management over 5–10+ years.
- **Cartilage restoration graft failure**: delamination (more common with periosteal-patch first-generation ACI, less with MACI), graft hypertrophy, incomplete integration at graft-host margin (particularly OAT plug height mismatch), and — for OCA — graft resorption/collapse or, rarely, immunologic non-incorporation.
- **Donor-site morbidity (OAT)**: reported in up to ~10% of patients, generally mild but occasionally limiting.
- **OCD fixation**: hardware prominence/synovitis requiring removal, nonunion of the fragment, and progression to a full-thickness defect if fixation fails, converting the case to a salvage cartilage-restoration procedure.
- **SIFK progression**: subchondral collapse despite conservative treatment (roughly one-third of cases progress to arthroplasty), particularly with large lesion size, uncorrected malalignment, or delayed diagnosis due to early radiographic occult presentation.
- **Discoid meniscus surgery**: recurrent instability/snapping if an unstable posterior attachment is not repaired at the time of saucerization; progressive degenerative change over time even after well-executed saucerization + repair.

## Recent Evidence

- **2024 — [AAOS Osteochondritis Dissecans Clinical Practice Guideline Rapid Update](https://www.aaos.org/globalassets/quality-and-practice-resources/osteochondritis-dissecans/osteochondritis-dissecans-rapid-update-2023.pdf)**: reaffirms recommendations for MRI-based instability staging and supports drilling for stable lesions failing non-operative care, with fixation preferred over excision for unstable-but-viable fragments — reinforces preserving native cartilage whenever fragment viability permits.
- **2022 (long-term follow-up published, remains the practice-defining reference through 2024-2026)** — [Five-Year Follow-up of the ESCAPE Randomized Clinical Trial, JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2794027): confirms physical therapy remains non-inferior to arthroscopic partial meniscectomy for degenerative meniscal tears at 5 years, with improvements from the first 2 years sustained — solidifies PT-first as the durable, not just short-term, standard for this population.
- **2024 — [The Formal EU-US Meniscus Rehabilitation 2024 Consensus, Part I (rehabilitation after meniscus surgery), ESSKA-AOSSM-AASPT, published in Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy / IJSPT](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ksa.12674)**: first formal joint EU-US consensus (67 experts, 14 countries) on post-meniscectomy, post-repair, and post-reconstruction rehabilitation — moves practice toward individualized, criteria-based progression rather than rigid time-based restriction, and toward earlier controlled weight-bearing after repair where tear pattern allows.
- **2024/2025 — [The Formal EU-US Meniscus Rehabilitation 2024 Consensus, Part II (prevention, non-operative treatment, return to sport), IJSPT/JOSPT Open](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/140661-the-2024-formal-eu-us-meniscus-rehabilitation-consensus-an-esska-aossm-aaspt-initiative-part-ii-prevention-non-operative-treatment-and-return-to)**: formalizes non-operative pathway parameters and return-to-sport criteria (symmetry-based, not purely time-based) for both acute and degenerative meniscal lesions, complementing the surgical rehab guidance in Part I.
- **2024 — Lee et al., [Root Repair Has Superior Radiological and Clinical Outcomes Than Partial Meniscectomy and Nonoperative Treatment in the Management of Meniscus Root Tears, Arthroscopy](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2024.02.017)**: systematic review confirming root repair produces the greatest gains in IKDC/Lysholm and the least joint-space narrowing versus meniscectomy or non-operative care in appropriately selected patients — strengthens the repair-first recommendation for LaPrade Type 2/3/5 root tears meeting extrusion/alignment/OA-grade criteria.
- **2024 (Dec) — [Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Medial Meniscus Root Tears — Is Surgery the Key to Better Outcomes?, Cureus](https://www.cureus.com/articles/321675-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-medial-meniscus-root-tears-is-surgery-the-key-to-better-outcomes.pdf)**: pooled comparison of transtibial pull-out repair versus conservative management for MMPRT confirms significant subjective/functional improvement after repair and some (not complete) protection against Kellgren-Lawrence progression — tempers expectations that repair fully halts OA progression even when technically successful.
- **2025 (Jan) — [Effectiveness of Meniscus Root Tear Repair Versus Conservative Therapy and Adjunct Therapies: A Systematic Review, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39803041/)**: further consolidates the evidence base favoring surgical repair over conservative management for root tears in eligible patients, while flagging heterogeneity in adjunct-therapy (PRP/BMAC augmentation) reporting that limits firm conclusions on their added benefit.
- **2025 — Tollefson et al., [Medial Meniscal Ramp Tears in Patients With ACL Tears Undergoing Reconstruction: A Surgically Relevant Classification System Based on Tear Morphology, Arthroscopy](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2025.03.015)**: proposes a new 5-group morphology-based ramp classification (stable partial vs. femoral-sided unstable vs. tibial-sided/meniscotibial unstable, etc.) intended to standardize which ramp patterns require repair versus can be observed — relevant to refining current probe-based stability testing at time of ACLR.
- **2024 — [Reverse Ramp Lesion Repair in Patients With Meniscotibial Ligament Avulsion Injury: The Hidden AMRI, Arthroscopy Techniques](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.eats.2024.102930)**: describes a specific meniscotibial-ligament-avulsion ramp variant ("hidden" anteromedial rotatory instability contributor) easily missed even with a trans-notch view, reinforcing the need for posteromedial portal inspection in high-grade-laxity ACL cases.
- **2024 — Rublev et al., [Arthroscopic Saucerization of Discoid Lateral Meniscus, With Meniscus Repair as Indicated, Results in Excellent Outcomes in Pediatric Patients Younger Than 12, Arthroscopy, Sports Medicine, and Rehabilitation](https://www.arthroscopysportsmedicineandrehabilitation.org/article/S2666-061X(24)00033-6/fulltext)**: reports excellent/good outcomes (mean KOOS-Child 93) with saucerization ± repair even in children under 12, supporting repair-when-indicated over blanket resection even in the youngest symptomatic patients.
- **2024/2025 — [Combined Meniscal Saucerization and Repair Versus Subtotal Meniscectomy for Symptomatic Discoid Lateral Meniscal Tears in Children and Adolescents, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11808750/)**: 2-year comparative data favor saucerization-with-repair over subtotal meniscectomy for complex discoid tears on clinical outcome and OCD-development risk, though radiographic degenerative change can still progress in either group — reinforces repair-preserving strategy as the discoid-specific application of "save the meniscus."
- **2025 — [Autologous Chondrocyte Implantation, MACI, OAT, and Osteochondral Allograft Improve Knee Function and Pain With Considerations for Patient and Cartilage Defect Characteristics: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12310097/)**: head-to-head synthesis (search through mid-2024) confirming all four major restoration techniques improve function/pain, with defect size, containment, and patient characteristics — not a single "best" technique — driving selection; supports the size/containment-based decision ladder used in this monograph.
- **2025 — [The Biomechanical and Functional Outcomes of Fresh Osteochondral Allograft for the Knee: A Systematic Review, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11992398/)**: consolidates OCA survivorship and functional outcome data (best results in traumatic/OCD etiology, unipolar lesions, younger patients, shorter symptom duration), useful for patient-selection counseling when discussing allograft versus autograft or ACI/MACI.
- **2024 — Nuelle et al., [Osteochondral Allograft Transplantation in the Knee, Arthroscopy](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2024.01.006)**: current-concepts review reaffirming OCA as the workhorse for large/high-grade or bipolar chondral and osteochondral defects, with emphasis on graft-host biomechanical matching and concurrent correction of malalignment/meniscal deficiency to protect graft survivorship.
- **2024 — [Deepening the Subchondral Insufficiency Fracture and Osteonecrosis of the Knee Dilemma: Time for a New Classification, Current Concepts](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2059775425005395)**: proposes an updated SIFK/SONK classification integrating fracture-versus-necrosis pathophysiology, reinforcing the terminology shift from "spontaneous osteonecrosis" to "spontaneous insufficiency fracture" that should guide how these cases are worked up (bone-health/fracture-healing framework, not primarily an AVN framework) and documented going forward.
- **2022/2023 (frequently cited through the current period) — [Editorial Commentary: SIFK May Benefit From Combined Mosaicplasty and High Tibial Osteotomy, Arthroscopy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36604001/)**: supports combining osteochondral grafting with realignment osteotomy for SIFK with concurrent malalignment, echoing the general cartilage-restoration principle that alignment correction is a prerequisite, not an afterthought, for graft survivorship.
