## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Displaced midshaft clavicle fracture with shortening >2 cm, displacement ≥100% (bone-on-bone loss), comminution (Z-fragment, Robinson 2B2), open fracture, tenting/threatened skin, neurovascular injury, floating shoulder, or symptomatic nonunion.
- Distal (lateral) third: Neer type II (CC ligaments torn medial to fracture, ~30% nonunion risk untreated) and type V (comminuted, both main fragments detached from the CC ligaments, which remain attached to a free inferior fragment).
- Bilateral clavicle fractures or clavicle fracture in a polytrauma patient where early stable fixation facilitates mobilization/nursing care.

**Contraindications**:
- Non-displaced or minimally displaced midshaft fractures (Robinson 2A) — nonunion risk nonoperatively is low (~1–5%) and does not justify routine surgery.
- Active local or systemic infection.
- Medically unfit for elective anesthesia (unless the fracture is open or neurovascularly threatened, which changes the urgency calculus).
- Very thin, atrophic bone in a medial or lateral fragment too short to accept adequate screw purchase — consider an intramedullary device, a longer plate spanning more fragment, or a suspensory/hook construct instead of standard plating.
- Neer type I, III, and IV distal clavicle fractures are generally stable and managed nonoperatively (type IV in children/adolescents is a periosteal sleeve avulsion that remodels).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP + 20° cephalic tilt (Zanca-like) view of the clavicle; consider CT for comminuted or intra-articular (distal) patterns and for precise preoperative plate contouring/length planning in complex fractures. AP chest to screen for pneumothorax/rib fracture in high-energy mechanisms.
- **Implant selection and templating**: choose superior versus anteroinferior plate position before the day of surgery (see Step 1 below); template plate length against the contralateral clavicle or the injury film to ensure at least 3 bicortical screws (ideally more) on each side of the fracture; have both a standard precontoured plate and a hook plate or CC suspensory system available if the fracture is distal-third or if intraoperative findings differ from the plan.
- **Intramedullary option**: if a minimally invasive nail/pin technique is planned, confirm the fracture is a simple transverse/short-oblique pattern without comminution and that a nail of appropriate diameter and length is available (with removal instrumentation for later extraction).
- **Consent discussion**: counsel on the substantial (up to 20–30%) rate of symptomatic hardware requiring later removal, expected numbness inferior/distal to the incision from supraclavicular sensory nerve disruption, and the visible scar.
- **Concomitant injury check**: screen for an ipsilateral scapular neck/glenoid fracture (floating shoulder) — if present, plan combined displacement assessment, not isolated clavicle planning.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, frequently combined with an interscalene or superficial cervical plexus block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: modified beach-chair (semi-recumbent, 30–45° head-of-bed elevation) with a small bump or folded towel placed longitudinally between the scapulae to help extend/retract the shoulders and improve exposure; head secured in a padded headrest turned slightly away from the operative side.
- **Table setup**: arm free-draped so it can be manipulated for length/rotation reduction; a radiolucent table extension or standard OR table with fluoroscopy access from the head or foot of the bed.
- **Draping**: prep and drape from the base of the neck to well past the shoulder and proximal arm, exposing the entire clavicle, sternal notch, and acromion for orientation.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned to obtain AP and cephalic-tilt views of the clavicle without moving the drapes; confirm this trajectory before prepping.
- **Landmarks**: mark the sternal notch, full length of the clavicle, coracoid, and acromion; draw the planned incision (anterosuperior or anteroinferior, per chosen plate position) directly over the fracture with adequate proximal/distal extension for plate length.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Choose plate position.** Anteroinferior placement is lower-profile, less prominent, and keeps drilling directed away from the subclavian neurovascular bundle, but is technically less forgiving to contour and hold reduced during fixation. Superior placement gives better biomechanical purchase (greater bone stock, tension-band effect) but carries a higher symptomatic-hardware/removal rate (up to 20–30%) given its subcutaneous location.
2. **Incision and exposure.** Make a longitudinal incision directly over the clavicle centered on the fracture (anterosuperior or anteroinferior per plate choice). Incise skin and subcutaneous tissue in line, taking care to identify and preserve crossing supraclavicular sensory nerve branches where possible — warn the patient preoperatively that some numbness distal/inferior to the scar is expected regardless.
3. **Expose the fracture.** Elevate the platysma/periosteum minimally, just enough to visualize the fracture ends and any butterfly/comminuted fragments; avoid circumferential stripping, which devitalizes fragments and impairs healing.
4. **Reduce the fracture.** Use pointed reduction (bone) clamps, a small dental pick, or provisional K-wires to reduce simple fracture patterns anatomically; for comminuted (Z-fragment/Robinson 2B2) patterns, focus on restoring overall length, rotation, and alignment with a bridging plate technique rather than forcing anatomic reduction of every fragment, preserving their soft-tissue (periosteal) attachments.
5. **Contour and apply the plate.** Select a precontoured anatomic locking plate of appropriate length (aim for at least 3, ideally 4, screw holes/bicortical purchase on each main fragment). Provisionally fix with clamps or a temporary non-locking screw in a compression/eccentric position for simple fracture patterns to achieve interfragmentary compression before locking screws are placed.
6. **Definitive fixation.** Place bicortical locking (and/or compression) screws sequentially, confirming purchase and plate-to-bone apposition; for comminuted segments, use the plate in bridging mode with screws only in the well-fixed proximal/distal fragments, avoiding screws in comminuted intermediate fragments that would further devitalize them.
7. **Confirm reduction and hardware position fluoroscopically.** Obtain AP and cephalic-tilt fluoroscopic views to confirm restored length, rotation, and alignment, and to verify no screw is proud on the deep (posterior) cortex threatening the subclavian vessels/brachial plexus.
8. **Consider intramedullary alternative when appropriate.** For a simple transverse/short-oblique fracture without significant comminution, a titanium elastic nail or cannulated screw system can be passed retrograde from the fracture site (or, depending on system, antegrade from a small medial or lateral stab incision) under fluoroscopic guidance — smaller scars, less soft-tissue dissection, and easier subsequent removal, but not suited to comminuted fractures or very short medial/lateral fragments where rotational control is inadequate.
9. **Distal clavicle (Neer II) fixation.** If the fracture is distal-third with CC ligament disruption, choose: (a) a hook plate placed beneath the acromion for reliable reduction (plan and consent for a second surgery to remove the plate at ~3–4 months given subacromial impingement/rotator cuff irritation risk); (b) a coracoclavicular suture-button/suspensory device passed through a coracoid tunnel (technically demanding coracoid tunnel placement — see the AC Joint Reconstruction technique guide for coracoid tunnel pearls and suprascapular nerve precautions); or (c) a combined superior locking plate plus CC suture augmentation for comminuted or very small lateral fragments that cannot support standard distal screws alone.
10. **Layered closure.** Close the platysma/deep fascial layer over the plate where soft-tissue coverage allows, then subcutaneous tissue and skin (subcuticular closure gives the best cosmetic result along a visible, high-exposure location).
11. **Dressing and immobilization.** Apply a sling for comfort before the patient leaves the operating room.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Minimize periosteal stripping around comminuted fragments — preserving their soft-tissue attachments (biological fixation/bridging technique) is more important to union than forcing anatomic reduction of every piece.
- A folded towel between the scapulae, combined with gentle longitudinal traction on the arm, is often enough to disimpact a shortened, overriding fracture and dramatically eases reduction before clamps are applied.
- Pre-contour the plate on the back table against a clavicle model or the uninjured side's imaging when possible — the clavicle's S-shaped, non-uniform cross-section makes intraoperative contouring from scratch time-consuming and imprecise.
- Placing one non-locking screw in compression mode through an oval/dynamic plate hole first (for simple fracture patterns) achieves real interfragmentary compression before switching to locking screws for the remainder of the construct.
- Identify and gently retract (rather than sacrifice) crossing supraclavicular nerve branches whenever feasible; even so, counsel every patient preoperatively that a patch of numbness below the incision is a near-universal, usually well-tolerated finding.
- Keep drill bit depth and trajectory deliberately controlled on the deep (posterior) cortex — use a depth gauge and consider a soft-tissue protector, since the subclavian vessels and brachial plexus lie directly posterior/inferior, particularly with comminuted or posteriorly-displaced fragments.
- For distal clavicle fractures being treated with a suture-button/suspensory device, confirm coracoid tunnel trajectory fluoroscopically before final tensioning — a malpositioned tunnel is very difficult to salvage after the button has passed.
- If choosing an intramedullary device, confirm on preoperative imaging that the fracture pattern is simple (transverse/short oblique) — a comminuted fracture forced onto a nail alone will not control rotation and is a setup for malunion.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Subclavian vessels and brachial plexus lie directly posterior/inferior to the clavicle — particular caution with comminuted or posteriorly-displaced fragments during drilling and screw placement.
- Symptomatic hardware requiring removal occurs in a substantial minority (up to 20–30%), an important counseling point especially for superior plating of "borderline" displaced fractures.
- Nonunion is reduced but not eliminated by fixation (hardware failure, infection-associated nonunion still occur); nonoperative nonunion rates for displaced midshaft fractures are historically ~15% (COTS RCT control arm), higher (up to 20–30%) in comminuted (Robinson 2B2) patterns.
- Intramedullary devices are unsuitable for comminuted or very short fragments — do not use them outside simple transverse/short-oblique patterns; forcing a nail into a comminuted pattern risks malrotation and shortening.
- Hook plate fixation of distal clavicle fractures mandates a planned second surgery for removal (~3–4 months) due to subacromial impingement/rotator cuff irritation; counsel patients up front.
- CC suture-button coracoid tunnel placement is technically demanding and carries risk to the suprascapular nerve, which passes close to the posterosuperior/medial coracoid base.
- Refracture is reported both after nonoperative clavicle management and after premature/elective hardware removal — counsel patients that bone is relatively weak for several weeks after plate removal and to avoid contact/high-risk activity until full radiographic union.
- Malunion (residual shortening/angulation) can produce measurable scapular dyskinesis and reduced strength/endurance even without frank nonunion, though its real-world functional impact is debated in recent literature.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 wk**: sling for comfort; elbow, wrist, hand active ROM immediately; pendulum shoulder exercises as tolerated.
- **2–6 wk**: discontinue sling as comfort allows; progressive active-assisted then active ROM; avoid resisted/loaded activity; radiographic check for early callus.
- **6–12 wk**: progressive strengthening once radiographic union present (~6–10 wk, similar or slightly faster than nonoperative treatment with stable ORIF); return to light manual work typically 6–8 wk; contact/overhead sport typically 10–12 wk and only after radiographic union confirmed.
- Counsel patients to avoid contact/high-risk activity until full radiographic union, and expect a period of relative bone weakness for several weeks after elective plate removal (refracture risk).
