## Indications

Displaced midshaft clavicle fracture with shortening >2 cm, displacement ≥100% (bone-on-bone loss), comminution (Z-fragment, Robinson 2B2), open fracture, tenting skin, neurovascular injury, floating shoulder, or symptomatic nonunion. For distal (lateral) clavicle fractures: Neer type II (CC ligaments torn medial to fracture) and type V (comminuted, both fragments attached to a free inferior segment).

## Positioning & setup

Direct anterosuperior or anteroinferior longitudinal incision over the clavicle. Key structures at risk: supraclavicular sensory nerves (expect a patch of numbness distal/inferior to the scar — warn patients preoperatively), and the subclavian vessels/brachial plexus lying directly posterior/inferior, especially with comminuted, posteriorly-displaced fragments.

## Steps

1. **Choose plate position.** Anteroinferior placement is lower-profile, less prominent, and avoids the subclavian neurovascular structures during drilling, but is technically less forgiving. Superior placement gives better biomechanical purchase (greater bone stock) but carries a higher symptomatic-hardware/removal rate (up to 20–30%) given its subcutaneous location.
2. **Reduce and fix with a precontoured locking plate** — the workhorse for midshaft fractures.
3. **Consider an intramedullary device** (titanium elastic nail, cannulated screw system) as a minimally invasive alternative for simple transverse/short-oblique patterns without significant comminution — smaller scars, easier removal. Not suited to comminuted (Robinson 2B2) fractures or very short medial/lateral fragments.
4. **For distal clavicle (Neer II) fractures**, choose between: a hook plate (reliable reduction, but requires a planned second surgery for removal at ~3–4 months due to subacromial impingement/rotator cuff irritation risk); a coracoclavicular suture-button/suspensory device (avoids the second surgery, biomechanically sound, but technically demanding coracoid tunnel placement); or a combination (superior locking plate + CC augmentation) for comminuted/small lateral fragments.

## Pitfalls

- Subclavian vessels and brachial plexus lie directly posterior/inferior to the clavicle — particular caution with comminuted or posteriorly-displaced fragments during drilling.
- Symptomatic hardware requiring removal occurs in a substantial minority (up to 20–30%), an important counseling point especially for superior plating of "borderline" displaced fractures.
- Nonunion is reduced but not eliminated by fixation (hardware failure, infection-associated nonunion still occur).
- Intramedullary devices are unsuitable for comminuted or very short fragments — do not use them outside simple transverse/short-oblique patterns.
- Hook plate fixation of distal clavicle fractures mandates a planned second surgery; counsel patients up front.

## Post-op protocol

- **0–2 wk**: sling for comfort; elbow, wrist, hand active ROM immediately; pendulum shoulder exercises as tolerated.
- **2–6 wk**: discontinue sling as comfort allows; progressive active-assisted then active ROM; avoid resisted/loaded activity; radiographic check for early callus.
- **6–12 wk**: progressive strengthening once radiographic union present (~6–10 wk, similar or slightly faster than nonoperative treatment with stable ORIF); return to light manual work typically 6–8 wk; contact/overhead sport typically 10–12 wk and only after radiographic union confirmed.
- Counsel patients to avoid contact/high-risk activity until full radiographic union, and expect a period of relative bone weakness for several weeks after elective plate removal (refracture risk).
