## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- **Distal chevron osteotomy**: mild–moderate hallux valgus (HVA <30–40°, IMA <13–15°); workhorse first-line procedure. A **biplanar chevron** adds a rotational cut to correct an elevated DMAA (congruent-joint deformity).
- **Scarf (diaphyseal Z-osteotomy)**: moderate–severe deformity requiring more powerful IMA correction than a distal osteotomy can provide, while retaining a lower nonunion risk than proximal osteotomies.
- **Akin (proximal phalanx medial closing-wedge) osteotomy**: adjunct only, never a stand-alone procedure — for residual hallux valgus interphalangeus after the metatarsal deformity has been corrected.
- **MIS (percutaneous) bunion surgery, 3rd/4th generation** (e.g., percutaneous chevron/Akin — "PECA"): a technique choice, not a separate indication tier — comparative series and RCTs show broadly equivalent radiographic correction (HVA/IMA) and patient-reported outcomes to open chevron/scarf at 1–2 years in mild–moderate deformity, in the hands of a surgeon past the learning curve. Evidence for severe deformity and MIS-Lapidus equivalents is less mature.
- Add **lateral soft-tissue release/derotation of the sesamoid apparatus** (via the 1st webspace or as part of the distal osteotomy exposure) whenever sesamoid subluxation is significant (Hardy & Clapham ≥4–5), regardless of which osteotomy is chosen.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active or recent infection, inadequate vascular status of the limb, or uncontrolled peripheral neuropathy without careful risk-benefit counseling.
- Severe deformity (HVA >40°, IMA >16–18°) or a hypermobile/arthritic 1st TMT joint treated with an isolated distal or diaphyseal osteotomy — these deformities require a proximal osteotomy or Lapidus (1st TMT arthrodesis) instead, or the deformity will be undercorrected and prone to early recurrence.
- Significant 1st MTP arthritis (hallux rigidus) — correcting alignment alone in an arthritic joint does not address the primary pain generator; consider arthrodesis instead.
- MIS technique in a surgeon early in the learning curve for anything beyond mild deformity — thermal osteonecrosis and malunion risk are technique-dependent and steeply learning-curve-sensitive.
- Smokers and patients with poorly controlled diabetes carry meaningfully higher nonunion/wound-healing risk with any of these osteotomies and should be counseled and optimized preoperatively where possible.

## Preoperative Planning

- Weight-bearing AP, lateral, and sesamoid axial radiographs to measure HVA, IMA, DMAA, hallux valgus interphalangeus, and tibial sesamoid position (Hardy & Clapham grading) — these measurements, not a visual impression of the bunion, should drive osteotomy selection against the severity ladder.
- Assess 1st TMT joint congruency/obliquity and clinically stress the 1st ray for sagittal hypermobility; if hypermobility or TMT arthritis is present, reconsider whether Lapidus is more appropriate than the planned distal/diaphyseal osteotomy.
- For scarf osteotomy, template the planned Z-cut length and screw trajectories preoperatively — the diaphyseal geometry must allow two well-spaced screws without violating either cortex.
- For MIS technique, confirm access to dedicated percutaneous instrumentation (Shannon-type burrs, typically low-speed high-torque, in the 2.0–2.2 mm diameter range for the metatarsal cuts) with a continuous-irrigation attachment, and dedicated cannulated screw sets for percutaneous fixation.
- Instrumentation checklist: sagittal saw (open technique) or Shannon burr with irrigation (MIS technique), small-fragment or headless compression screws, K-wires for provisional fixation, and mini C-arm.
- Counsel and consent for the specific osteotomy planned, the possibility of intraoperative conversion (e.g., MIS to open if correction is inadequate percutaneously), and expected recurrence/revision rates for the chosen technique.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Ankle block or popliteal regional block with sedation is standard and well suited to same-day discharge; spinal or general anesthesia are alternatives per patient/surgeon preference.
- Supine position, foot free at the end of the table, ipsilateral hip bump to neutralize limb rotation.
- Thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination for open technique. For MIS technique, **tourniquet use is often deliberately minimized or avoided** — ambient blood flow is thought to help dissipate heat generated by the burr, so some MIS surgeons operate without a tourniquet or release it before the osteotomy cuts specifically to reduce thermal-necrosis risk.
- Mini C-arm positioned for AP and lateral views of the forefoot without breaking the sterile field; for MIS technique, fluoroscopy is used continuously/intermittently throughout the case to guide burr position, so confirm the C-arm angle and image quality before starting.
- Mark the planned incision(s): a dorsomedial longitudinal incision for open chevron/scarf, or the standard percutaneous portal positions (typically medial eminence, dorsomedial metaphyseal, and proximal phalanx base) for MIS technique. Mark the 1st webspace incision separately if a lateral release is planned as an open step.
- Have cold or room-temperature irrigation readily available and set up for continuous use if performing MIS osteotomy.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Medial eminence exposure (all open techniques)**: make a dorsomedial or medial longitudinal incision over the 1st MTP joint, protecting the dorsomedial cutaneous nerve; incise the capsule and expose the medial eminence.
2. **Distal chevron**: mark the apex of the V at the center of the metatarsal head; cut a V-shaped osteotomy through the metatarsal neck with the apex directed proximally, then laterally translate the capital fragment (typically up to ~40–50% of shaft width) to correct the IMA. Preserve the plantar/lateral soft-tissue hinge to protect the blood supply to the capital fragment. Fix with one or two screws or headless-compression screws after confirming translation and clinical alignment. For an elevated DMAA, add the biplanar rotational cut before fixation.
3. **Scarf**: make a long diaphyseal Z-cut — a long horizontal cut through the shaft with short dorsal-proximal and plantar-distal transverse limbs. Keep the horizontal limb cuts strictly **parallel** to each other; translate the capital fragment laterally (and plantarward/dorsally or with rotation as needed for the specific deformity), then fix with typically two screws directed to avoid the opposing cortex. Non-parallel cuts are the classic technical error causing "troughing" (vertical displacement of one fragment relative to the other during translation).
4. **Akin (when indicated)**: after the metatarsal-level correction is complete and fixed, mark and perform a medial closing-wedge osteotomy of the proximal phalanx base, hinging on the lateral cortex; close the wedge and fix with a small staple, screw, or crossed K-wires. Never perform Akin as a stand-alone correction for a true metatarsal-driven deformity.
5. **Lateral soft-tissue release/derotation**: when sesamoid subluxation is significant, release the adductor hallucis tendon and lateral MTP capsule/suspensory ligament via the 1st webspace (open or percutaneous) or through the distal osteotomy exposure, allowing the sesamoids to derotate back beneath the metatarsal head.
6. **MIS/percutaneous technique — distal metatarsal osteotomy**: through a small medial portal, resect the medial eminence with the burr, staying in line with the metatarsal shaft to avoid a residual prominence or over-resection. Through a dorsomedial portal, perform the transverse/chevron-type metatarsal neck osteotomy with the Shannon burr under continuous irrigation and intermittent fluoroscopic guidance, working the osteotomy **in stages with pauses** rather than a single continuous cut — this measurably reduces peak bone temperature and lowers thermal-osteonecrosis/nonunion risk compared with a single prolonged pass.
7. **MIS — translation and fixation**: use a percutaneous instrument (elevator or K-wire) to laterally translate and derotate the capital fragment under fluoroscopic control, correcting IMA and DMAA as needed, then place one or two cannulated screws percutaneously to secure the correction — formal internal fixation is what distinguishes contemporary (3rd/4th-generation) MIS technique from earlier percutaneous methods that relied on cast immobilization alone.
8. **MIS — Akin equivalent**: perform the percutaneous phalangeal osteotomy through a separate small portal in analogous fashion, with burr cuts under irrigation and fluoroscopic control, fixed percutaneously.
9. Confirm final alignment (HVA, IMA, sesamoid position) with fluoroscopy in AP and, if needed, a simulated weight-bearing view before final fixation is locked in any technique.
10. Irrigate copiously (critical after burr use in MIS technique, to clear bone paste/debris and further dissipate residual heat), repair the capsule in open technique (percutaneous portals are typically closed with simple sutures or Steri-Strips), and apply a light compressive dressing with the toe held in a corrected, slightly overcorrected position (bunion splint/strapping) for the initial healing period.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- On a scarf osteotomy, cut the two horizontal limbs with the saw blade referenced against a consistent external landmark (or use a cutting guide) to guarantee parallelism — troughing is far easier to prevent than to fix once translation has begun.
- Preserve the lateral/plantar soft-tissue hinge on a distal chevron capital fragment; combining an aggressive concurrent lateral release with a distal osteotomy in the same stage is a recognized risk factor for capital fragment avascular necrosis, so if a substantial release is needed, consider whether a scarf or proximal osteotomy (with an intact distal blood supply) is more appropriate.
- For MIS osteotomies, perform the bone cut in **stages with pauses for irrigation and fluoroscopic check** rather than one continuous burr pass — published series describe this staged technique as considerably reducing burr/bone temperature.
- Use a low-speed, high-torque burr with continuous irrigation for any MIS bone cut; irrigation (room-temperature is generally adequate, though some surgeons use chilled saline) is the single most effective way to prevent thermal osteonecrosis.
- Consider minimizing or foregoing tourniquet use for MIS osteotomy specifically — perfusing blood flow through the bone is thought to help dissipate cutting heat, and some published protocols deliberately avoid tourniquet inflation during the osteotomy cuts.
- When correcting an elevated DMAA, do not rely on shaft translation alone — add the biplanar rotational cut (open) or the percutaneous equivalent, or the congruent-joint deformity will be undercorrected and prone to recurrence.
- Confirm sesamoid reduction, not just HVA/IMA correction, before final fixation — residual sesamoid subluxation predicts recurrence even when the bony angles look acceptable on a single fluoroscopic view.
- In scarf and chevron cases, place fixation screws to maximize purchase in both fragments while avoiding the opposing cortex and the joint surface — check screw position on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy, not AP alone.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not combine an **aggressive lateral release with a distal osteotomy in a single stage** when perfusion is a concern — this combination is a recognized risk factor for capital fragment/1st metatarsal head avascular necrosis.
- Scarf osteotomy is technically demanding; non-parallel cuts risk **troughing** (vertical displacement) and require meticulous two-screw fixation.
- MIS technique carries a **steep learning curve** and a recognized risk of **thermal osteonecrosis** if burring technique is poor (continuous single-pass cutting without adequate irrigation is the classic cause); recurrence and revision rates are similar to open technique only in experienced hands. Thermal injury is also implicated as a contributing factor in MIS osteotomy nonunion.
- Undercorrected IMA, unaddressed elevated DMAA, or unaddressed 1st-ray hypermobility are the principal drivers of **recurrence** (reported ~10–16% across series).
- **Iatrogenic hallux varus** — from overcorrection of IMA, excessive lateral release/fibular sesamoidectomy, or medial capsule overplication — is often more disabling than the original deformity.
- Excessive 1st-ray shortening or elevation redistributes load laterally and causes **transfer metatarsalgia** under the lesser metatarsal heads.
- Match osteotomy level to deformity: an isolated distal procedure under-corrects a diaphyseal or angle-driven deformity, and proximal/Lapidus should be favored instead when HVA >40°, IMA >16–18°, or the 1st TMT joint is hypermobile/arthritic.
- Overheating during percutaneous burring can occur even with adequate irrigation if the cut is rushed as a single pass — staged cutting with pauses is a specific, described mitigation, not merely a generic caution.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: postoperative shoe or boot; weight-bear as tolerated on the heel/lateral border in most rigid-fixation constructs (surgeon- and construct-dependent); elevation and ice for swelling; wound check at 10–14 days.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing in a stiff-soled postoperative/surgical shoe; begin gentle active/active-assisted 1st MTP ROM once the wound is healed (~2–3 weeks) to limit stiffness; avoid running/impact.
- **Weeks 6–8**: radiographic union check; transition to a wide, supportive regular shoe as swelling/comfort allow; progress ROM and strengthening (towel scrunches, intrinsic strengthening, gait retraining).
- **Weeks 8–12**: return to low-impact exercise (cycling, swimming) as comfort allows; return to running/impact sport typically **10–12+ weeks**, guided by radiographic union and symptom resolution.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often as early as 1–2 weeks in a postoperative shoe; standing/manual labor 6–10 weeks, dependent on osteotomy type and fixation stability.
- MIS technique shows the same general protocol but with **less early soft-tissue swelling/pain and a faster early return to shoes** than open technique per comparative series.
- General return-to-sport gating (all forefoot osteotomy procedures): radiographic union confirmed, near-full pain-free active ROM versus the contralateral side, pain-free single-leg heel-rise and single-leg hop, swelling resolved enough for normal athletic footwear, and a graduated walk → brisk walk → jog → run → sport-specific drills progression, with contact/pivoting sport typically not cleared before 12–16 weeks.
