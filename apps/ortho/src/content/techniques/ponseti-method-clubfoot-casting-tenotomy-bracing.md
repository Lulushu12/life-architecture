## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- First-line treatment for essentially all idiopathic clubfeet (congenital talipes equinovarus), ideally started in the first weeks of life.
- Also used, with lower predictability, in positional and syndromic/teratologic clubfoot (arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele, distal arthrogryposis), which respond less reliably to standard Ponseti casting and require closer monitoring for atypical relapse patterns.
- Used in a modified form for complex/atypical clubfoot (short, plump foot with a transverse plantar crease, deep posterior crease, cavus, and a hyperextensible first ray) — the modified technique alters hand position (thumb placed on the cuboid rather than the talar head) to avoid overcorrection.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- A fully passively correctable, positional clubfoot (no true bony/structural deformity) does not require the full Ponseti casting protocol — gentle stretching or minimal casting suffices; distinguishing positional from true structural clubfoot on the first newborn exam is important to avoid over-treating.
- Skin breakdown, open wounds, or other local skin compromise over the foot/leg is a temporary contraindication to casting until resolved.
- Severe teratologic/syndromic feet with markedly diminished passive correctability from the outset should be counseled honestly that full Ponseti correction may not be achievable and that surgical intervention is more likely to be needed than in idiopathic cases.
- Extreme prematurity or medical instability may require deferring the start of casting until the infant is stable enough for repeated clinic visits and (later) tenotomy.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Clinical staging:** score each foot with the **Pirani** (0–6, weekly) and/or **Dimeglio** (0–20, at presentation) systems at the first visit and at intervals — a higher initial Dimeglio grade (III–IV, 10–20) or Pirani score (>4–5) predicts a longer casting course and a higher likelihood of needing percutaneous Achilles tenotomy; use this to set expectations with the family from the outset.
- **Imaging:** radiographs are rarely needed for idiopathic clubfoot diagnosis or casting decisions — the diagnosis and correction are followed clinically. Reserve imaging for atypical presentations or to screen for associated anomalies (spine, hips) in syndromic/teratologic feet.
- **Casting supplies:** plaster of Paris (preferred by many for its superior moldability during the critical shaping of each cast, though fiberglass is used by some) in pediatric widths, adequate cast padding, and a stockinette; have a cast saw and cast-removal supplies available for each weekly visit.
- **Tenotomy equipment (when the time comes):** a No. 11 blade or beaver blade (or 16-gauge needle for a needle-tenotomy technique), local anesthetic (typically buffered lidocaine, dosed by weight), sterile prep supplies, and materials for the final long-leg cast; some surgeons use a mini-open technique with direct tendon visualization instead of a fully percutaneous approach, particularly for larger/older infants or when there is any uncertainty about tendon position.
- **Bracing prescription:** measure the infant's foot and order the Foot Abduction Brace (FAB)/Denis-Browne bar sizing (shoe size and bar length) well before the anticipated end of casting so the brace is ready to fit immediately after tenotomy-cast removal, avoiding a gap in bracing that risks early relapse.
- **Family counseling/planning:** discuss the expected number of casts (average 5–6), the near-certainty of needing tenotomy (~85–90% of idiopathic feet), and — most importantly — the brace-wear schedule and its outsized importance for relapse prevention, before the first cast is even applied.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Casting/manipulation visits:** no anesthesia — performed awake in clinic with the infant calm (feeding or pacifier can help), typically with the surgeon seated and the infant supine or held by a parent/assistant across the lap.
- **Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy:** performed under **local anesthesia in clinic** for most infants (typically under ~9–12 months), avoiding a general anesthetic; older or larger children, or centers preferring a controlled setting, may perform tenotomy under brief general/sedation in a procedure room or OR.
- **Positioning for tenotomy:** infant supine, the leg to be tenotomized held with the knee extended and ankle in maximum dorsiflexion by an assistant, exposing the tendon under tension.
- **Local landmarks:** identify and mark the Achilles tendon and the palpable gap/soft spot just anterior to it (where the neurovascular bundle runs) before infiltrating local anesthetic — the entry point for the blade or needle is planned relative to these landmarks, roughly 10–20 mm proximal to the tendon's calcaneal insertion, which balances adequate tendon width for a complete release against a safe working distance from the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle.
- **Prep:** standard antiseptic skin prep of the posterior ankle/heel; no formal draping is required for the brief clinic procedure beyond a sterile field around the injection/incision site.
- **Fluoroscopy:** not routinely used for tenotomy — the procedure relies on direct palpation and the classic tactile/audible "pop" or give as the tendon releases, plus confirmation of markedly increased passive dorsiflexion.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### A. Weekly manipulation and casting

1. At each visit, gently manipulate the foot in the correct sequence — cavus first, then simultaneous hindfoot/midfoot correction — before applying the new cast.
2. **Correct cavus first:** elevate the first ray to align the forefoot with the hindfoot (supinate/elevate the first metatarsal, restoring a normal longitudinal arch relationship). Never pronate the forefoot to correct cavus — this is a classic technical error that worsens the deformity by driving the forefoot further into pronation relative to the hindfoot.
3. **Abduct the foot beneath the talar head:** with the thumb of one hand applying lateral counter-pressure directly over the head of the talus (palpable anterolateral to the ankle), and the other hand supporting the foot, gently and progressively abduct the foot around the fixed talar head. Never push on the calcaneus/heel to try to directly correct hindfoot varus — the heel corrects passively as a consequence of foot abduction around the talus, not from direct heel manipulation.
4. Hold the corrected position and apply a long-leg cast with the knee flexed to approximately 90° — the knee flexion is essential to control rotation of the cast and prevent it from slipping off, not merely for comfort.
5. Mold the cast carefully around the foot in the corrected position, with particular attention to maintaining counter-pressure over the talar head while the plaster/fiberglass sets, and to avoiding any pressure directly over the heel that could cause skin breakdown or an inadequately corrected hindfoot.
6. Repeat weekly, reassessing abduction (target ~60–70° of foot abduction relative to the tibial axis) and equinus/hindfoot varus (which correct passively as abduction progresses) at each visit — average 5–6 casts to reach maximal correction.
7. At each visit, re-score Pirani if being used serially to document objective progress and flag any plateau or worsening, which should prompt reassessment of technique or suspicion of an atypical/complex foot rather than simply continuing the same approach.

### B. Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy

1. Confirm the indication: foot abduction of ~60–70° has been achieved, but ankle dorsiflexion remains <10–15° due to persistent equinus from the contracted Achilles tendon — this occurs in roughly 85–90% of idiopathic feet.
2. Position the infant supine with the leg held in maximum dorsiflexion by an assistant to place the tendon under tension, making it easier to palpate and release cleanly.
3. Identify the tendon and the entry point roughly 10–20 mm proximal to its calcaneal insertion, staying anterior/medial to the palpable soft-tissue gap that marks the plane of the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle, which lies immediately posterior to the medial malleolus and contracted flexor tendons — awareness of this bundle's proximity is the single most important safety point of the procedure.
4. Infiltrate local anesthetic at the entry site.
5. Insert the blade (or needle, if using a needle-tenotomy technique) percutaneously, oriented to cut from medial to lateral (or per surgeon's preferred orientation) directly through the tendon, staying immediately adjacent to the tendon itself and away from the posterior neurovascular plane.
6. Complete the release with a controlled cutting motion or gentle rocking of the blade, feeling for the characteristic give/pop as the tendon fibers separate; confirm by gently but firmly dorsiflexing the ankle and feeling for a marked, sudden increase in passive dorsiflexion (typically to 15–20° or more) compared to before the tenotomy.
7. Apply firm digital pressure over the tenotomy site for several minutes to achieve hemostasis (bleeding, if it occurs, is usually from small local vessels and controlled with pressure; true arterial bleeding — e.g., from an inadvertently injured peroneal or posterior tibial artery — requires prompt recognition and, if it does not resolve with pressure, surgical exploration).
8. Once hemostasis is confirmed and maximum dorsiflexion/abduction is held, apply the final long-leg cast in maximum correction (foot abducted and dorsiflexed).

### C. Final cast and brace transition

1. Leave the final post-tenotomy cast in place for approximately 3 weeks to allow the tenotomized tendon to heal in the lengthened position.
2. At cast removal, confirm maintained correction (abduction, dorsiflexion, resolved hindfoot varus) before proceeding to bracing.
3. **Fit the Foot Abduction Brace (FAB)/Denis-Browne bar** immediately at cast removal — set at approximately 70° external rotation on the corrected (previously affected) side, and approximately 40–45° on the unaffected side if the deformity is unilateral.
4. Instruct the family in full-time wear (~23 hours/day) starting immediately, and demonstrate proper shoe/boot fit and bar positioning before discharge from the visit.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Correct components in the correct order every time (cavus, then simultaneous abduction/derotation around the talar head, with equinus and varus resolving passively) — deviating from this sequence, especially attempting to force hindfoot correction before the foot has abducted adequately, is the most common source of a poorly corrected or rocker-bottom foot.
- Keep your counter-pressure thumb consistently on the lateral head of the talus throughout each manipulation, not on the cuboid or calcaneus — for the complex/atypical foot variant, some surgeons intentionally shift the counter-pressure point to the cuboid to avoid overcorrecting an already hypermobile first ray, a useful modification to recognize when the foot doesn't fit the typical idiopathic pattern.
- Mold each cast with your hand maintaining the corrective forces while an assistant applies the plaster — do not let go of the correction to reach for material, since the plaster can set in a position that has already begun to lose correction.
- Re-score Pirani at every visit even once you feel confident correction is progressing well — a plateauing score despite technically sound casting is an early, objective signal to reconsider technique or reassess for an atypical/complex foot before more casts are wasted on the wrong approach.
- For the tenotomy, have the assistant hold true maximum dorsiflexion (not just moderate stretch) — tension on the tendon makes both palpation and a clean, complete release easier and reduces the number of passes needed.
- A complete tenotomy should produce an unmistakable, sudden increase in passive dorsiflexion; if the improvement feels partial or equivocal, reassess for incomplete release before casting in maximum correction — an incompletely released tendon undermines the entire correction achieved by casting.
- Have the FAB brace sized and in hand before the final cast comes off — any delay between cast removal and brace fitting is lost time in which the freshly corrected, still-plastic infant foot can begin to lose correction.
- At every follow-up visit after bracing begins, spend time on adherence counseling, not just physical exam — asking specifically about hours of daily wear, skin issues, and barriers to compliance uncovers the single most powerful modifiable lever against relapse.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Never pronate the forefoot** to correct cavus — this is a classic technical error that worsens the deformity.
- **Never push on the calcaneus/heel** during abduction — counter-pressure belongs on the lateral talar head.
- **Do not force mid-tarsal manipulation before hindfoot equinus is corrected** — forceful mid-tarsal correction ahead of equinus correction causes residual or rocker-bottom deformity, a classic technical error to avoid.
- **Tenotomy neurovascular risk:** reported complication rate around 2% (mainly neurovascular), with accidental injury to the peroneal artery being the most commonly cited vascular complication in the literature, alongside risk to the posterior tibial artery, lesser saphenous vein, and the tibial or sural nerves. Vascular anomalies are notably prevalent in idiopathic clubfoot — the anterior tibial artery is reported absent or severely hypoplastic in up to 85% of affected limbs — reinforcing the importance of staying immediately adjacent to the tendon and using firm pressure (not blind exploration) to manage any bleeding encountered.
- **Incomplete tenotomy release** leaves residual equinus undercorrected; confirm a clear, marked increase in passive dorsiflexion before declaring the tenotomy complete, and consider a mini-open technique with direct visualization if percutaneous release feels equivocal or if anatomy is atypical.
- **Brace non-adherence is the single strongest predictor of relapse** — published relapse rates run roughly 80%+ with poor adherence versus <10–20% with consistent bracing; contemporary cohorts continue to reinforce this as the highest-yield relapse-prevention intervention.
- Other recognized complications: overcorrection (rare, valgus/pes planus from over-abduction or excessive tenotomy), dynamic supination during swing phase from an overactive/unopposed tibialis anterior (may require later tibialis anterior tendon transfer), and dorsal bunion from an overactive/imbalanced first ray after correction.
- Calf atrophy is typical in clubfoot and does not by itself indicate a worse prognosis.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–1 (post-tenotomy):** final cast in maximum correction; non-weight-bearing not required in infants; cast removed at ~3 weeks.
- **Weeks 3+ (post-cast removal):** begin FAB full-time wear (23h/day). Counsel parents extensively on adherence, skin checks, and brace tolerance strategies, as adherence is the dominant modifiable relapse risk factor.
- **Months 3 through age 4–5y:** transition to naps + nighttime brace wear (~12–14h/day); ongoing clinic follow-up every 2–4 months in year 1, then every 4–6 months, watching for early relapse signs (recurrent adductus, rising Pirani-equivalent findings, loss of dorsiflexion).
- **Beyond brace discontinuation:** annual follow-up recommended through skeletal maturity, since late relapse and dynamic supination can present into the school-age years.
