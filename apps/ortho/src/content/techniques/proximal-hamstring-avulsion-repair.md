## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Complete 3-tendon avulsion with ≥2 cm retraction — acute repair (within 4-6 weeks) gives the best strength recovery and return to play.
- Two-tendon avulsion with significant retraction and functional deficit in an active patient.
- Delayed presentation (>6 months) with complete avulsion and persistent functional deficit/sciatic symptoms — still improves outcomes versus non-operative treatment, though with a higher complication rate and generally inferior ultimate strength recovery than acute repair.
- Failed non-operative management of a partial tear with persistent pain, weakness, or sciatic-type symptoms attributable to scar tethering (hamstring syndrome).

**Contraindications**
- Partial tears (<2 tendons involved) with minimal retraction, especially in lower-demand/elderly patients — non-operative management remains appropriate here.
- Active infection or significant soft-tissue compromise over the posterior thigh/buttock.
- Medical unfitness for prone positioning and a multi-hour procedure, or inability to comply with a prolonged protected weight-bearing and hip-flexion-limiting brace protocol.
- Very low-demand patients for whom the strength deficit from non-operative management is functionally acceptable — surgery adds risk (sciatic nerve irritation, wound complications) without a commensurate functional benefit in this group.
- Relative: distal vascular injury or other high-energy associated trauma that requires higher surgical priority or staged management — address life/limb-threatening injury first.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI is mandatory for surgical planning — quantifies the number of tendons involved, measures retraction distance from the ischial tuberosity (a key driver of repair difficulty and expected strength recovery), and assesses tendon quality/degree of retraction-associated scarring, particularly important in delayed presentations.
- **Timing decision:** confirm and document time since injury precisely — acute repair (within 4-6 weeks) is associated with the best strength recovery and return to play; delayed repair (>6 months) still improves outcomes versus non-operative treatment in complete avulsions but should be consented for with the understanding of higher complication rates and inferior ultimate strength recovery due to fibrosis/retraction.
- **Equipment:** 2-4 suture anchors sized for the ischial tuberosity (commonly 4.5-5.5 mm, PEEK or bioabsorbable per surgeon preference), heavy non-absorbable whipstitch suture for the retracted tendon ends, a nerve stimulator/vessel loops for sciatic nerve identification and protection, and a large self-retaining retractor system given the depth and length of this exposure — especially in delayed/retracted cases where extensive scar release is anticipated.
- **Revision planning:** for revision repair (failed primary repair), anticipate a distinct — generally inferior — complication and outcome profile compared with primary repair; plan for likely additional scar tissue, a technically more difficult sciatic nerve dissection, and possibly compromised residual tendon quality, and counsel the patient accordingly before surgery.
- **Vascular screening:** in high-energy mechanisms with severe/complete avulsion, confirm distal pulses are documented and, if there is any concern, obtain vascular imaging pre-operatively given the (rare) risk of associated vascular injury.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia is standard given prone positioning and procedure length; a regional block (e.g., sciatic/posterior thigh block placed after confirming no pre-operative nerve deficit that would be masked, or placed post-operatively once intraoperative nerve status is documented) can supplement post-operative analgesia — discuss timing with anesthesia to avoid masking a nerve exam.
- **Positioning:** prone is most commonly used, giving direct posterior access to the ischial tuberosity and conjoint tendon; modified lithotomy (with the hip flexed) is an alternative favored by some surgeons for improved access to the more medial/anterior semimembranosus origin and for easier intraoperative assessment of repair tension through a range of hip/knee motion. Whichever position is chosen, pad all bony prominences carefully given the prone, often extended-duration nature of this case.
- **Tourniquet:** not routinely used given the proximal thigh/buttock location of this exposure; meticulous hemostasis throughout is essential instead.
- **Landmarks to mark:** the ischial tuberosity, the gluteal fold, and the expected course of the sciatic nerve as it exits beneath the piriformis and runs along the posterior/lateral aspect of the conjoint tendon — mark a generous safety margin given that the nerve is frequently displaced or tethered by scar/hematoma in both acute and especially chronic cases.
- **Imaging:** fluoroscopy is not routinely required for standard suture-anchor fixation but can be helpful in revision cases with distorted anatomy to confirm anchor trajectory relative to the sciatic notch.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Position the patient prone (or modified lithotomy per surgeon preference) and prep/drape the buttock and posterior thigh widely enough to allow intraoperative hip/knee motion for tensioning assessment.
2. Make a transverse or longitudinal incision centered over the ischial tuberosity (transverse incisions along the gluteal fold offer a cosmetic advantage and are favored by many surgeons for isolated proximal exposure; a longitudinal or curved incision may be preferred when more distal exposure is needed for a very retracted tendon).
3. Develop the subcutaneous plane and identify the inferior border of the gluteus maximus; retract it superiorly/laterally to expose the underlying posterior femoral cutaneous nerve and the ischial tuberosity region.
4. Identify the sciatic nerve early, before deep dissection around the tuberosity — it lies immediately deep/lateral to the conjoint tendon (biceps femoris long head + semitendinosus) and is frequently adherent to scar tissue in delayed or chronic cases (sciatic nerve entrapment syndrome). Circumferentially free the nerve from surrounding scar and protect it with a vessel loop for the remainder of the case, retracting it gently and intermittently (not under fixed continuous tension) throughout.
5. Identify the retracted tendon stump(s) — the conjoint tendon (biceps femoris long head + semitendinosus) typically retracts as a unit, while the semimembranosus origin, when involved, may be found separately and more anteriorly/medially. In chronic cases, carefully dissect the tendon free from surrounding scar without devascularizing or excessively thinning it.
6. Debride the ischial tuberosity footprint of residual scar/fibrous tissue down to a bleeding cancellous bone bed, preserving as much of the native footprint contour as possible.
7. Whipstitch each retracted tendon end (or the conjoint tendon as a unit, with the semimembranosus separately if distinct) with heavy non-absorbable suture, achieving secure purchase over an adequate length to resist pull-out under tensioning.
8. Place 2-4 suture anchors into the prepared ischial tuberosity footprint, positioned to recreate the native anatomic footprint (conjoint tendon typically more lateral/posterior, semimembranosus more medial/anterior) — stagger anchor position and trajectory to distribute load and avoid convergence/fracture risk in a small bony footprint.
9. Progressively advance and tension the tendon(s) down to the anchors with the hip in extension and the knee flexed (a position that relaxes the hamstring origin and allows maximal tendon excursion for advancement) — this is often the most technically demanding step in delayed/retracted cases, where extensive scar release and sometimes local tendon mobilization techniques are needed to achieve tension-free apposition without over-shortening the muscle-tendon unit.
10. Tie down the sutures securely, then take the hip/knee through a gentle range of motion to confirm the repair holds without gapping and that the sciatic nerve glides freely without tethering by the completed repair or by retained suture/anchor material.
11. For revision repair, anticipate more extensive scar release, a technically harder nerve dissection through prior scar, and potentially inferior tendon quality/length requiring adaptation of the anchor number/position — proceed more conservatively with tensioning given generally inferior tissue in this setting.
12. Achieve meticulous hemostasis (no tourniquet is used in this exposure, so this step matters more here than in tourniquet-controlled limb surgery) before closure.
13. Close the gluteal fascia over the repair, then subcutaneous tissue and skin in layers; consider a drain in cases with extensive dissection/scar release given the risk of post-operative hematoma in this deep, vascular field.
14. Apply the post-operative brace (hip-extension/knee-flexion-limiting) per surgeon protocol before the patient leaves the operating room, positioned to protect the repair at the tension achieved intraoperatively.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify and circumferentially free the sciatic nerve before committing to deep dissection around the tuberosity, even in straightforward acute cases — it is far easier to protect a nerve that has already been isolated than to find it unexpectedly during aggressive scar release.
- Retract the sciatic nerve gently and intermittently rather than under continuous fixed tension throughout the case — sustained traction, not just direct injury, is a recognized mechanism of post-operative neurapraxia.
- In chronic/delayed cases, expect the sciatic nerve to be tethered by scar (hamstring syndrome) — plan extra time for careful neurolysis as a distinct step before tendon mobilization, rather than trying to mobilize the tendon and free the nerve simultaneously.
- Tension the repair with the hip extended and the knee flexed — this position relaxes the hamstring origin and gives the greatest tendon excursion for advancement, making tension-free apposition achievable in cases that would otherwise seem to have an irreducible gap.
- Stagger suture anchor position and trajectory across the tuberosity footprint rather than clustering them — this distributes load and reduces the risk of anchor-to-anchor bony fracture in a relatively small footprint.
- Recreate the native footprint architecture (conjoint tendon lateral/posterior, semimembranosus medial/anterior) rather than simply bunching all retracted tendon into a single central repair point — anatomic reduction better restores the normal length-tension relationships of the individual muscle bellies.
- In delayed/chronic repairs, do not force full anatomic-length restoration at the cost of nerve tension or repair security — a slightly shortened but secure, nerve-safe repair outperforms an anatomically perfect repair that over-tensions the sciatic nerve or fails at the bone-tendon interface.
- Consider a drain for cases involving extensive scar release/dissection given the absence of tourniquet control in this field and the resulting higher risk of post-operative hematoma.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The sciatic nerve is at direct risk during dissection and repair, particularly in chronic/delayed cases where it may be tethered by scar (sciatic nerve entrapment syndrome) — meticulous identification and protection is essential throughout.
- Delayed repair (>6 months) carries a higher complication rate (nerve irritation, wound issues) and generally inferior ultimate strength recovery due to fibrosis/retraction, even though it still outperforms non-operative treatment for complete avulsions.
- Revision repair has a documented complication profile and outcomes distinct from — and generally inferior to — primary repair.
- Vascular injury is a rare but recognized risk with severe/complete avulsion in high-energy mechanisms — check distal pulses pre-operatively.
- Post-operative bracing (hip extension/knee flexion limiting brace) is common practice, but evidence for its necessity is mixed per recent systematic review.
- Excessive fixed retraction of the sciatic nerve during exposure/tensioning is a modifiable technical cause of post-operative neurapraxia — retract intermittently and gently rather than continuously.
- Over-tensioning the repair to achieve anatomic length in a significantly retracted, chronic case can place excessive traction on the sciatic nerve or risk suture pull-out — accept a degree of controlled shortening when necessary rather than forcing full length restoration.
- Hematoma formation is a recognized risk given the lack of tourniquet control in this exposure — meticulous hemostasis and selective drain use mitigate this.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0-2:** brace limiting the hip flexion/knee extension combination (protects the repair), protected weight-bearing (usually partial to full with brace/crutches per surgeon protocol), no active hamstring contraction against resistance.
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive hip/knee ROM, gentle isometrics introduced ~4-6 weeks.
- **Weeks 6-12:** progressive resistance training, closed-chain strengthening, gradual sitting tolerance normalization.
- **Months 3-6:** running progression, eccentric hamstring loading (Nordic curls), sport-specific drills.
- **Return to sport:** typically 6-9 months, criterion-based on strength symmetry (≥85-90%) and functional testing; earlier RTP correlates with earlier surgical timing (repair within 4-6 weeks of injury).

Complications: sciatic nerve irritation/neurapraxia (most common — often transient, due to traction/retraction near the nerve), infection, heterotopic ossification, re-rupture, persistent posterior thigh/sitting discomfort; overall complication rate reported around 15% (major complications ~4-5%) in recent systematic review.
