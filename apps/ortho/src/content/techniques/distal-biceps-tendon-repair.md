## Indications

Complete or high-grade partial distal biceps tendon rupture in patients who want to restore supination/flexion strength — repair ideally within 2-3 weeks of injury (before retraction and scarring compromise primary anatomic repair). Non-operative treatment (accepting a functional deficit) is reserved for elderly, low-demand, sedentary patients.

## Positioning & setup

Supine positioning with the arm on a hand table; document pre-operative posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) function (thumb/finger extension) before surgery, since pre-existing deficits from radial tunnel compression (mass effect from the ruptured tendon/hematoma) are common.

## Steps

1. Confirm diagnosis and timing: repair ideally within 2-3 weeks of injury; beyond 4-6 weeks, primary repair may not be feasible and graft reconstruction (semitendinosus autograft, Achilles allograft, or dermal allograft) may be needed to bridge the gap.
2. **Single-incision anterior approach with cortical button ± interference screw:** favored in many contemporary series for better cosmesis, faster early recovery, and comparable-or-superior functional/satisfaction outcomes to double-incision in modern technique series. Use meticulous retraction technique and supination during exposure to reduce PIN traction risk.
3. **Two-incision (Boyd-Anderson modified) approach:** lower nerve injury risk (avoids working directly in the PIN-adjacent zone) but higher risk of heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis if posterior dissection violates the interosseous membrane broadly. Use meticulous subperiosteal technique and avoid a single continuous posterior/anterior soft-tissue bridge disruption to reduce this risk.
4. Fix the tendon to the radial tuberosity footprint; fixation choice (cortical button vs interference screw vs suture anchor vs combined) is driven by surgeon preference and bone quality. Button constructs generally provide the highest biomechanical pull-out strength, supporting earlier active rehabilitation in reliable patients.
5. Account for the bicipital tuberosity "cam" effect — the footprint externally rotates as the forearm pronates/supinates — since repair fixation angle/position affects supination strength recovery.

## Pitfalls

- The lacertus fibrosus, if intact, can mask a full rupture on exam (partial retraction only) and can itself be a source of persistent pain if not addressed at the time of surgery.
- PIN neuropraxia is the principal risk of the single-incision approach — usually transient, resolving over weeks to months in the large majority, but document pre-operative PIN function so a post-operative deficit can be correctly attributed.
- Heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis is the principal risk of the two-incision approach if posterior dissection is not meticulous.
- Beyond 3-4 weeks, tendon retraction/quality deteriorates rapidly — do not delay definitive management once the diagnosis is confirmed.
- Lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury/numbness is a recognized complication independent of approach.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0-2:** splint/brace at ~90° flexion, protect repair; some protocols allow early protected motion within a hinged brace (extension-block) rather than full immobilization, depending on fixation strength (button constructs support earlier motion).
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive active-assisted then active ROM, avoid resisted supination/flexion.
- **Weeks 6-12:** progressive resistance strengthening beginning with light isotonic work, advancing gradually.
- **Months 3-4+:** return to heavy lifting/manual labor and sport, guided by strength symmetry testing (~85-90% of contralateral); light desk work return often 2-4 weeks, heavy labor typically 4-6 months.
- Complications: PIN neuropraxia (single-incision, usually transient), lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury/numbness, heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis (higher with two-incision technique if posterior dissection is not meticulous), re-rupture (uncommon with modern fixation, low single-digit %), persistent strength deficit.
