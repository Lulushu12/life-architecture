## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Complete distal biceps tendon rupture in a patient who wants to restore supination/flexion strength — repair ideally within 2-3 weeks of injury, before retraction and scarring compromise primary anatomic repair.
- High-grade partial tears with significant functional deficit and MRI (FABS view) confirmation, refractory to a trial of activity modification/therapy.
- Chronic rupture (>4-6 weeks) in an active/high-demand patient willing to accept the added morbidity of graft reconstruction to restore strength.

**Contraindications**
- Elderly, low-demand, or sedentary patients willing to accept a functional deficit (~30-40% supination strength, ~15-40% flexion strength) — non-operative treatment is a legitimate first-line choice in this group, not merely a fallback.
- Active local infection or skin compromise over the antecubital fossa.
- Medically unfit for surgery/anesthesia, or significant uncontrolled comorbidity that would not tolerate a several-week course of protected rehabilitation.
- Relative: very chronic, retracted ruptures (many months out) with poor-quality proximal muscle belly on imaging where even graft reconstruction is unlikely to restore meaningful function — counsel accordingly and consider non-operative management.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI (FABS view — Flexion, Abduction, Supination) confirms complete versus partial tear, quantifies retraction, and helps predict whether primary repair is feasible or graft reconstruction should be planned for. Document the interval since injury precisely — feasibility of primary repair drops sharply beyond 3-4 weeks.
- **Implant selection:** decide in advance between cortical button, interference screw, suture anchors, or a combined/hybrid construct based on surgeon preference, bone quality, and cost/availability — button constructs generally provide the highest biomechanical pull-out strength and are favored when early active rehabilitation is planned in a reliable patient.
- **Graft planning for delayed presentation:** if the interval since injury is beyond 4-6 weeks or MRI shows significant retraction/scarring, consent and plan for possible graft reconstruction (semitendinosus autograft, Achilles allograft, or dermal allograft) to bridge the gap — this should not be a surprise intraoperative pivot.
- **Nerve documentation:** formally document pre-operative posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) function (thumb/finger MCP extension) in the chart before surgery — pre-existing deficits from radial tunnel compression (mass effect from the retracted tendon/hematoma) are common and must be distinguished from a post-operative surgical complication.
- **Equipment:** single- vs double-incision instrument sets differ (button-passing guide/drill system for single-incision cortical button technique; retractor set and osteotome/burr access for the two-incision posterior exposure); confirm the chosen system is opened and available.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or a supraclavicular/infraclavicular brachial plexus block (± general) both work well; regional block provides valuable post-operative analgesia during the first 12-24 hours.
- **Positioning:** supine with the arm extended on a radiolucent hand table; a sterile tourniquet on the proximal arm allows a bloodless field for the critical nerve-adjacent dissection.
- **Landmarks to mark:** the antecubital flexion crease, the palpable tendon defect (if present), the course of the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve (emerges lateral to the biceps tendon, subcutaneous, at risk with any anterior incision), and — for a two-incision technique — the posterior interval between the extensor carpi ulnaris and anconeus.
- **Tourniquet:** inflate after exsanguination; plan to release and achieve hemostasis before final closure, particularly for the posterior interosseous membrane dissection in the two-incision technique, where post-tourniquet bleeding can obscure the field if not controlled.
- **Imaging:** fluoroscopy is not routinely required but can be used to confirm button deployment/flip on the far cortex if there is any doubt about implant position.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Prep and drape the arm free, from above the tourniquet to the hand, allowing intraoperative forearm pronation/supination to assess the radial tuberosity position and confirm tendon excursion.
2. Confirm diagnosis and timing: repair ideally within 2-3 weeks of injury; beyond 4-6 weeks, primary repair may not be feasible and graft reconstruction (semitendinosus autograft, Achilles allograft, or dermal allograft) may be needed to bridge the gap.
3. **Single-incision anterior approach:** make a transverse (or short longitudinal) incision approximately 3-4 cm distal to the antecubital flexion crease, centered over the radial tuberosity. Identify and protect the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve immediately upon superficial dissection — it lies just deep to the subcutaneous fat in this field.
4. Retrieve the retracted tendon stump (often requiring a finger sweep or a small proximal counter-incision/blunt instrument to deliver it distally) and whipstitch the tendon end with heavy non-absorbable suture.
5. Develop the interval to the radial tuberosity with the forearm held in full supination — this rotates the tuberosity anteriorly, moving the PIN posteriorly and away from the working field, which is the single most important maneuver for nerve protection in the single-incision approach.
6. Expose the tuberosity footprint subperiosteally, decorticate a bleeding bony bed, and drill the fixation tunnel/socket with the forearm held in supination and the drill/guide oriented to aim 0-20° proximally toward the radial-capitellar joint (rather than perpendicular or distally angled) — this measurably increases the working distance from the PIN. Use fluoroscopy or direct palpation to confirm trajectory if there is any uncertainty.
7. Pass the whipstitched tendon into the socket/tunnel and secure fixation per the chosen construct: seat and flip a cortical button on the far (dorsal) cortex and tension the tendon into the near socket, or place an interference screw alongside the tendon within a unicortical socket, or supplement with suture anchors — a combined button + interference screw construct is used by some surgeons for maximal fixation strength in robust tendon/bone.
8. **Two-incision (Boyd-Anderson modified) approach (alternative to single-incision, or when a wider exposure/graft reconstruction is planned):** make the anterior incision to retrieve and prepare the tendon as above, then make a separate posterior incision in the interval between the extensor carpi ulnaris and anconeus with the forearm pronated (this pronated position rotates the PIN away from the posterior approach's working zone).
9. Develop the posterior interval subperiosteally, exposing the posterior aspect of the tuberosity while strictly avoiding wide dissection along the interosseous membrane — limit soft-tissue stripping to what is necessary to create the fixation trough, since broad interosseous membrane disruption predisposes to heterotopic ossification/synostosis.
10. Create a bony trough/socket in the posterior tuberosity, pass the tendon (delivered from the anterior wound through the interosseous space) into the trough, and fix with suture anchors or a transosseous suture technique, tensioning with the forearm in the position used for socket preparation.
11. Once fixation is complete (either approach), take the elbow/forearm through a full passive range of motion (flexion-extension and pronation-supination) to confirm the repair tracks smoothly without impingement and that fixation is secure under motion.
12. Release the tourniquet and achieve meticulous hemostasis before closure — this is particularly important in the posterior interval, where residual bleeding into a broadly dissected space can contribute to heterotopic bone formation.
13. Close the fascia/subcutaneous layers and skin in standard fashion; apply a well-padded posterior splint or hinged elbow brace at approximately 90° flexion and neutral-to-slight-supination.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Hold the forearm in maximal supination throughout the anterior tuberosity exposure and drilling in the single-incision technique — this single positioning maneuver does more to protect the PIN than any retractor placement.
- Aim the drill/guide pin 0-20° proximally toward the radiocapitellar joint rather than perpendicular to the radial shaft; anatomic studies show this meaningfully increases the distance between the fixation implant and the PIN.
- Identify and protect the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve early in the superficial dissection of the anterior incision — it is the most commonly injured nerve overall in distal biceps repair, independent of approach.
- Retrieve a retracted tendon gently with blunt technique (finger sweep, blunt Kelly, or a tendon retriever) rather than aggressive traction, which can further fray or shorten the usable tendon length.
- If the lacertus fibrosus is intact, release it deliberately during exposure — beyond unmasking the true extent of tendon retraction, an unreleased lacertus can itself remain a source of persistent anterior forearm pain/tightness if left untouched.
- For the two-incision technique, keep the posterior dissection strictly subperiosteal and limited to the trough site — avoid connecting the anterior and posterior soft-tissue planes with a continuous stripped corridor, which is the mechanism thought to predispose to synostosis.
- Pronate the forearm for the posterior exposure in the two-incision technique (opposite of the supination used anteriorly) — this rotates the PIN out of the posterior working zone.
- Achieve rigorous tourniquet-down hemostasis before closing, especially posteriorly — residual hematoma in a widely dissected interosseous corridor is a recognized contributor to heterotopic ossification.
- Consider an intramedullary/unicortical button technique when available — anchoring the button within the medullary canal (rather than flipping it on the far cortex) has been described to further increase the working distance from the PIN compared with standard bicortical button placement.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The lacertus fibrosus, if intact, can mask a full rupture on exam (partial retraction only) and can itself be a source of persistent pain if not addressed at the time of surgery.
- PIN neuropraxia is the principal risk of the single-incision approach — reported as high as approximately 5% in some single-incision cortical button series — usually transient, resolving over weeks to months in the large majority, but document pre-operative PIN function so a post-operative deficit can be correctly attributed rather than assumed to be new.
- Drill/guide-pin trajectory that is perpendicular or angled distally (rather than 0-20° proximal toward the radiocapitellar joint) brings fixation hardware measurably closer to the PIN — a modifiable technical risk factor.
- Heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis is the principal risk of the two-incision approach if posterior dissection is not meticulous and subperiosteal, or if the anterior and posterior soft-tissue planes are inadvertently connected by broad stripping.
- Beyond 3-4 weeks, tendon retraction/quality deteriorates rapidly — do not delay definitive management once the diagnosis is confirmed.
- Lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury/numbness is a recognized complication independent of approach — most common overall nerve injury in this procedure.
- Over-aggressive tendon retrieval traction can shorten or fray the tendon stump, compromising the length available for tension-free fixation.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0-2:** splint/brace at ~90° flexion, protect repair; some protocols allow early protected motion within a hinged brace (extension-block) rather than full immobilization, depending on fixation strength (button constructs support earlier motion).
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive active-assisted then active ROM, avoid resisted supination/flexion.
- **Weeks 6-12:** progressive resistance strengthening beginning with light isotonic work, advancing gradually.
- **Months 3-4+:** return to heavy lifting/manual labor and sport, guided by strength symmetry testing (~85-90% of contralateral); light desk work return often 2-4 weeks, heavy labor typically 4-6 months.

Complications: PIN neuropraxia (single-incision, usually transient), lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve injury/numbness, heterotopic ossification/proximal radioulnar synostosis (higher with two-incision technique if posterior dissection is not meticulous), re-rupture (uncommon with modern fixation, low single-digit %), persistent strength deficit.
