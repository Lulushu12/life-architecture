## Indications

Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and balloon kyphoplasty (PKP) are indicated for persistent, function-limiting pain from an acute (typically <6–8 weeks, MRI-edema-confirmed) osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) refractory to 2–4 weeks of conservative therapy, or in patients who cannot tolerate bed rest/bracing (e.g., cognitive impairment, high fall risk). Kyphoplasty is typically preferred over vertebroplasty when height restoration/kyphosis correction is a specific clinical goal.

## Positioning & setup

Performed under biplanar fluoroscopy or CT guidance, using a bipedicular or unilateral (extrapedicular/transpedicular) approach.

## Steps

1. **Confirm the target level and acuity**: MRI (STIR sequence) confirms acute (marrow edema) versus chronic fracture and identifies the symptomatic level when multiple deformities coexist — essential before proceeding, since treating the wrong level in a patient with multiple deformities will not relieve the presenting pain.
2. **Select technique**: vertebroplasty injects cement directly without cavity creation; kyphoplasty first creates a cavity with a balloon tamp before cement injection, aiming to restore vertebral height/reduce kyphosis and lower the extravasation risk versus vertebroplasty.
3. **Choose puncture approach**: bipedicular or unilateral (extrapedicular/transpedicular), under continuous biplanar fluoroscopy or CT guidance.
4. **Control cement viscosity and injection rate**: slow injection under continuous lateral fluoroscopy is the key maneuver to minimize leakage into the canal, disc space, or venous plexus; avoid injection if the cement is too liquid or if venous filling is seen.
5. **For kyphoplasty, inflate the balloon tamp** to create the cavity and attempt height restoration before cement delivery.

## Pitfalls

- Avoid cement injection if viscosity is too low or venous filling is seen on fluoroscopy — these are the direct predictors of leakage into the canal, disc space, or venous plexus.
- Cement leakage is common radiographically (up to ~20–30% in some series) but usually asymptomatic; rare symptomatic leaks can cause radiculopathy, pulmonary cement embolism, or (rarely) cord compression.
- Randomized trial evidence for benefit has been genuinely mixed historically: the 2009 sham-controlled trials (Buchbinder, Kallmes/INVEST) showed no benefit over sham, while later, better-selected trials with acute, MRI-confirmed fractures (VAPOUR 2016) showed clinically meaningful early pain relief versus sham in a subgroup with acute severe pain — patient selection (acute, MRI-confirmed, severe pain) materially affects expected benefit.
- A 2025 propensity-matched comparative analysis found a higher one-year risk of subsequent (often adjacent-level) vertebral fracture after kyphoplasty compared with vertebroplasty — an important counseling point when choosing between the two techniques.
- Newer unilateral dual-plane puncture techniques (2025 literature) report reduced re-collapse rates by improving cement fill pattern.
- Facet/medial branch blocks and epidural steroid injections have no established role in acute spine trauma management and should not be substituted for augmentation or conservative care in this setting.

## Post-op protocol

Vertebral augmentation is an adjunct to, not a replacement for, the broader OVCF conservative-care pathway: analgesia, early mobilization, extension bracing for comfort, and initiation/optimization of osteoporosis therapy (calcium/vitamin D, bisphosphonate or anabolic agent per bone density and fracture risk) since the fracture is a sentinel event for future fracture risk (roughly 5-fold increased risk of a subsequent vertebral fracture). Interval reassessment includes DEXA follow-up, medication adherence review, fall-risk assessment/modification, and balance/posture (extensor-strengthening) programs shown to reduce subsequent fracture risk. Contemporary 2025 network meta-analyses (46 studies, n=5,660) favor augmentation (vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty) over nonsurgical management for pain (VAS), disability (ODI), and quality of life (EQ-5D) in appropriately selected acute OVCF, while flagging adjacent-level fracture risk as a real trade-off.
