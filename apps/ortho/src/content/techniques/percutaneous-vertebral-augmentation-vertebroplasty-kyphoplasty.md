## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Persistent, function-limiting pain from an acute (typically <6–8 weeks, MRI-edema-confirmed) osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) refractory to 2–4 weeks of conservative therapy.
- Patients who cannot tolerate bed rest/bracing (cognitive impairment, high fall risk, severe comorbid deconditioning) where prolonged immobility carries its own material risk.
- Painful vertebral pathologic fractures (myeloma, metastatic disease) as a palliative/stabilizing procedure, distinct from the osteoporotic indication but using the same technique.
- Progressive vertebral collapse with escalating pain despite bracing.
- Kyphoplasty specifically favored over vertebroplasty when height restoration/kyphosis correction is a clinical goal, or in fractures with a mobile/dynamic component on flexion-extension imaging.

**Contraindications**
- **Asymptomatic OVCF** or a fracture that is not clearly the source of the patient's pain (multiple deformities present — confirm the symptomatic level with MRI/exam before treating).
- **Active local or systemic infection** (osteomyelitis, discitis, untreated sepsis).
- **Uncorrectable coagulopathy** or inability to hold anticoagulation safely.
- **Retropulsed fragment causing canal compromise with neurologic deficit** — this is a surgical decompression/stabilization problem, not an augmentation problem; augmentation alone does not address canal compromise.
- **Fracture with posterior wall/cortex disruption** is a relative contraindication (higher leakage risk into the canal) requiring more cautious technique, not necessarily an absolute contraindication.
- **Allergy to bone cement components** (rare).
- **Vertebra plana** (severe collapse) can make cannula access and cement delivery technically difficult, though not always impossible with modified technique.
- **Chronic, long-standing fracture without MRI edema** — unlikely to respond to augmentation since the pain generator (an actively mobile, unhealed fracture) may no longer be present.

## Preoperative Planning

- **MRI (STIR sequence)** to confirm acute (marrow edema) versus chronic fracture and to identify the symptomatic level when multiple deformities coexist — this is the single most important preoperative study, since treating the wrong level in a patient with multiple deformities will not relieve the presenting pain.
- **CT** of the target level to assess posterior wall integrity, pedicle dimensions (for transpedicular approach planning), and the degree of collapse/vertebra plana that might complicate cannula access.
- **Coagulation studies** and medication reconciliation (hold anticoagulants/antiplatelets per institutional protocol before the procedure).
- **Confirm bone density/osteoporosis workup** is underway or planned, since this procedure treats the fracture, not the underlying disease — augmentation without initiating anti-osteoporotic therapy leaves the patient at ongoing fracture risk.
- **Cement selection**: polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) is standard; higher-viscosity cement formulations are increasingly preferred because increased viscosity is strongly associated with reduced leakage rates compared with low-viscosity cement — confirm the specific product's working time (many high-viscosity cements have a working window starting roughly 90 seconds after mixing and lasting at least ~15 minutes at room temperature) and plan injection timing accordingly.
- **Equipment**: biplanar fluoroscopy (or CT-fluoroscopy hybrid) suite, bone biopsy/access needles (11–13 gauge typically), vertebroplasty cement delivery system (manual syringes or hydraulic/screw-delivery systems for tactile-feedback, controlled injection), and — for kyphoplasty — an inflatable balloon tamp system sized to the vertebral body.
- **Level and approach planning**: decide bipedicular versus unilateral (extrapedicular/transpedicular) approach based on pedicle anatomy and the ability to achieve adequate cement fill across the midline from a single approach — a well-placed unilateral approach reaching past midline can achieve comparable fill to bipedicular access with a shorter procedure and less bilateral instrumentation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Conscious sedation with local anesthesia** at the access site is standard for most patients (monitored anesthesia care); general anesthesia is reserved for patients unable to tolerate prone positioning/sedation, multi-level procedures, or per anesthesia/patient factors.
- **Prone positioning** on a radiolucent table with chest and pelvic bolsters to allow free abdominal excursion and to extend the thoracolumbar spine slightly, which can help open the posterior disc/vertebral body space and improve fluoroscopic visualization.
- Arms positioned forward/cephalad (surgeon/anesthesia-dependent) to keep them out of the fluoroscopic field.
- **Fluoroscopy setup is central to this procedure**: position the C-arm to obtain a true AP view (spinous process centered between the pedicles, endplates squared) and a true lateral view of the target level without needing to reposition the patient — biplanar fluoroscopy or CT-fluoroscopy allows simultaneous or rapidly alternating AP/lateral imaging, which materially speeds the procedure and improves safety during cement injection.
- Mark and confirm the target level under fluoroscopy with a radiopaque marker before prepping, counting from a reliable anatomic landmark (sacrum, or a prior marked level) to avoid wrong-level treatment — level localization errors are a recognized, avoidable pitfall in a percutaneous procedure without direct visualization.
- Prep and drape a wide sterile field over the target level(s), extending far enough laterally to allow bilateral pedicle access even if a unilateral approach is planned, in case conversion to bipedicular access is needed intraoperatively.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the target level and re-confirm acuity/symptom correlation** one final time before needle placement, cross-referencing preoperative MRI with the fluoroscopic level count.
2. **Select technique**: vertebroplasty injects cement directly into the vertebral body without prior cavity creation; kyphoplasty first creates a cavity with a balloon tamp before cement injection, aiming to restore vertebral height/reduce kyphosis and generally allowing injection of a more viscous cement at lower pressure (lowering, though not eliminating, extravasation risk compared with vertebroplasty).
3. **Choose puncture approach and confirm pedicle entry point** on AP fluoroscopy — for a transpedicular approach, the ideal starting point is typically the superolateral aspect of the pedicle ("owl's eye" or "bull's eye" technique), angling medially and caudally to reach the anterior third of the vertebral body while staying within the pedicle cortex on both AP and lateral views throughout advancement.
4. **Advance the access needle/trocar under continuous or frequently-checked biplanar fluoroscopy**, confirming on AP view that the needle tip does not cross medial to the pedicle's medial wall before it has passed the posterior vertebral body line on the lateral view (crossing medially too early risks a medial pedicle wall breach into the canal).
5. **Advance to final position**, typically the anterior third to anterior half of the vertebral body on the lateral view, at or approaching midline on the AP view for a unilateral approach intended to achieve bilateral fill.
6. **For kyphoplasty**: introduce the balloon tamp through the working cannula and inflate incrementally under lateral fluoroscopic visualization, monitoring balloon pressure and vertebral body height restoration, and stopping inflation if the balloon approaches the cortical margins (anterior, lateral, or endplate) to avoid cortical breach; deflate and remove the balloon once an adequate cavity is created and/or height restoration goals are met, leaving a low-pressure cavity for subsequent cement fill.
7. **Prepare cement per manufacturer instructions**, tracking mixing time closely — injection into the vertebral body should generally begin early in the cement's working window once it reaches an appropriately viscous (toothpaste-like, not liquid) consistency, since injecting cement that is still too liquid is a primary driver of leakage, while waiting too long risks the cement hardening in the delivery system before adequate fill is achieved.
8. **Inject cement slowly under continuous lateral fluoroscopy**, watching the leading edge of the cement column with every increment — stop immediately if cement approaches the posterior vertebral wall, tracks toward the pedicle/canal, or fills a paravertebral vein (seen as a linear, vascular-pattern filling distinct from the trabecular "cotton-ball" pattern of appropriate intraosseous fill).
9. **Switch to the contralateral pedicle (if bipedicular) or reposition within the same cannula system** to complete fill across the midline if a single approach has not achieved adequate bilateral cement distribution.
10. **Stop injection once the cement has filled the anterior two-thirds to three-quarters of the vertebral body** with a satisfactory cross-midline pattern — overfilling in pursuit of complete fill materially increases leakage risk without proportionate clinical benefit.
11. **Withdraw the cannula while rotating gently** (many systems recommend a slow rotational withdrawal) to reduce the cement "tail" left along the needle track, which is a recognized source of trackback leakage into the paraspinal soft tissues or pedicle.
12. **Hold pressure at the access site**, obtain final AP and lateral fluoroscopic images documenting cement distribution, and apply a small dressing — this is typically a same-day or short-stay procedure.
13. **Monitor the patient in recovery** for neurologic status change (would suggest canal leakage/compression) and for signs of pulmonary cement embolism (rare but can present as acute dyspnea/hypoxia) before discharge.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the "owl's eye"/"bull's eye" pedicle-entry technique on AP fluoroscopy and confirm the needle stays lateral to the medial pedicle wall until it has passed the posterior vertebral body line on the lateral view — this single habit prevents the most common form of medial breach into the canal.
- Favor higher-viscosity cement where available — viscosity is one of the most consistent, technique-modifiable predictors of reduced leakage rate across the literature, and higher-viscosity cement can typically be injected at an earlier, more working-time-forgiving point after mixing.
- Time the injection window deliberately: begin injecting once the cement is toothpaste-consistency (not before, when it is too liquid and prone to vascular/leak tracking) and be aware of your product's specific working time so you are not rushing the last increments as cement begins to set.
- Watch for a linear, vein-pattern filling distinct from the normal trabecular "cotton-ball" cement pattern — this is the earliest fluoroscopic sign of venous filling and should prompt an immediate pause or stop, not just a slower injection rate.
- A well-placed unilateral (extrapedicular or transpedicular) approach that crosses past midline can achieve fill comparable to bipedicular access, shortening the procedure and reducing total needle passes — reserve bipedicular access for cases where a single approach clearly cannot achieve adequate cross-midline fill.
- In kyphoplasty, stop balloon inflation as soon as it approaches any cortical margin (endplate, anterior, or lateral wall) on fluoroscopy, even if target height restoration has not been fully achieved — cortical breach during balloon inflation risks fragment displacement or dural injury.
- Withdraw the cannula with a slow rotational motion rather than a straight pull to minimize the cement "tail" that can otherwise leak along the needle track.
- Consider a unilateral dual-plane (angled) puncture technique when available — recent series report improved cement fill pattern and reduced re-collapse rates compared with a conventional single-plane unilateral puncture.
- Do not chase complete, symmetric cement fill for its own sake — stopping once an adequate, well-distributed fill is achieved (rather than maximal fill) reduces leakage risk without a proportionate loss of clinical benefit.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Avoid cement injection if viscosity is too low or venous filling is seen on fluoroscopy** — these are the direct predictors of leakage into the canal, disc space, or venous plexus; increased cement viscosity is specifically associated with reduced leakage rates in comparative series.
- **Cement leakage is common radiographically (up to ~20–30% in some series) but usually asymptomatic**; rare symptomatic leaks can cause radiculopathy, pulmonary cement embolism, or (rarely) cord compression.
- **Wrong-level treatment** is an avoidable pitfall unique to percutaneous, non-direct-visualization procedures — always cross-reference the fluoroscopic level count with preoperative MRI and a reliable anatomic landmark before the first needle pass.
- **Medial pedicle wall breach** during needle advancement risks canal violation and neural injury — confirmed by strict adherence to the AP/lateral fluoroscopic sequencing described in the technique steps.
- **Randomized trial evidence for benefit has been genuinely mixed historically**: the 2009 sham-controlled trials (Buchbinder, Kallmes/INVEST) showed no benefit over sham, while later, better-selected trials with acute, MRI-confirmed fractures (VAPOUR 2016) showed clinically meaningful early pain relief versus sham in a subgroup with acute severe pain — patient selection (acute, MRI-confirmed, severe pain) materially affects expected benefit.
- **A 2025 propensity-matched comparative analysis found a higher one-year risk of subsequent (often adjacent-level) vertebral fracture after kyphoplasty compared with vertebroplasty** — an important counseling point when choosing between the two techniques.
- **Newer unilateral dual-plane puncture techniques (2025 literature) report reduced re-collapse rates by improving cement fill pattern.**
- **Facet/medial branch blocks and epidural steroid injections have no established role in acute spine trauma management** and should not be substituted for augmentation or conservative care in this setting.
- **Balloon over-inflation during kyphoplasty** risks cortical breach, fragment displacement, or (rarely) dural injury — stop inflation at the first sign of approaching a cortical margin on fluoroscopy.
- **Pulmonary cement embolism**, while rare, is a recognized complication from venous cement extravasation — monitor for acute dyspnea/hypoxia postoperatively and have a low threshold for chest imaging if suspected.

## Postoperative Protocol

Vertebral augmentation is an adjunct to, not a replacement for, the broader OVCF conservative-care pathway: analgesia, early mobilization, extension bracing for comfort, and initiation/optimization of osteoporosis therapy (calcium/vitamin D, bisphosphonate or anabolic agent per bone density and fracture risk) since the fracture is a sentinel event for future fracture risk (roughly 5-fold increased risk of a subsequent vertebral fracture). Interval reassessment includes DEXA follow-up, medication adherence review, fall-risk assessment/modification, and balance/posture (extensor-strengthening) programs shown to reduce subsequent fracture risk. Contemporary 2025 network meta-analyses (46 studies, n=5,660) favor augmentation (vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty) over nonsurgical management for pain (VAS), disability (ODI), and quality of life (EQ-5D) in appropriately selected acute OVCF, while flagging adjacent-level fracture risk as a real trade-off.
