## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Symptomatic acetabular dysplasia in skeletally mature patients (closed triradiate cartilage) with a congruent joint and preserved cartilage — Tönnis grade 0–1, occasionally selected Tönnis grade 2, LCEA <20–25°.
- Borderline dysplasia (LCEA 18–25°) with instability features on exam (positive anterior/posterior apprehension) or on imaging (acetabular version abnormality), particularly when these dominate over pure impingement symptoms.
- Symptomatic hip instability from acetabular retroversion with a deficient posterior wall, where reorientation restores posterior coverage.

**Contraindications**
- Open triradiate cartilage (skeletal immaturity) — the fragment mobility and blood supply preservation techniques assume a closed triradiate; a different pediatric osteotomy (e.g., triple innominate) is indicated instead.
- Tönnis grade ≥2–3 with advanced joint-space narrowing, incongruent joint, or significant loss of femoral head sphericity — reorientation of an already-arthritic or incongruent joint does not reliably relieve symptoms and total hip arthroplasty is the procedure of choice.
- Advanced osteonecrosis of the femoral head.
- Active infection.
- Relative: severe obesity, which increases technical difficulty of the approach and osteotomy visualization, and raises wound complication risk.
- Relative: significant femoral-sided deformity (severe cam morphology, abnormal femoral version) not addressed concurrently — isolated acetabular reorientation without addressing a coexisting severe femoral deformity can leave residual or create new impingement.
- Relative: poor bone quality/osteoporosis, which compromises fixation and increases nonunion risk.

## Preoperative Planning

- **AP pelvis radiograph** (well-centered, minimal pelvic tilt/rotation) to measure LCEA, Tönnis angle, crossover sign (acetabular retroversion), and Shenton's line.
- **False-profile view** to measure the anterior center-edge angle and assess anterior coverage, which the AP view alone does not capture.
- **CT with 3D reconstruction** (increasingly standard) to assess femoral and acetabular version, precisely quantify 3D coverage, and plan the osteotomy trajectory and the degree/direction of fragment reorientation needed — particularly valuable in complex or revision cases and in retroverted acetabuli.
- **MRI** to confirm cartilage status, labral pathology (frequently hypertrophic and torn in dysplastic hips), and to screen for femoral head osteonecrosis or other cartilage-threatening pathology that would change candidacy.
- **Dynamic/CT-based impingement simulation** in centers with access to this technology, to anticipate whether correction to target coverage will create new femoral-acetabular impingement, informing whether concomitant femoral osteochondroplasty ("FAO") should be planned.
- **Template the correction**: define target LCEA (25–35°), acetabular version correction needed, and acceptable medialization (≤10 mm) preoperatively so intraoperative fluoroscopic targets are explicit rather than judged purely by eye.
- **Blood management**: PAO carries meaningful blood loss (osteotomies through cancellous, well-vascularized pelvic bone); plan for type and screen/crossmatch, consider tranexamic acid protocol, and discuss autologous/cell-saver blood options per institutional protocol.
- **Equipment**: dedicated PAO osteotome and chisel set (curved and straight osteotomes matched to the ischial, pubic, iliac, and posterior column cuts), a Schanz pin/joystick for fragment manipulation, fluoroscopy (C-arm) for intraoperative correction confirmation, and cannulated or standard cortical screws for fixation. If combined arthroscopy or arthrotomy for labral/femoral work is planned, have that instrumentation available in the same setup.
- **Neuromonitoring**: some centers use intraoperative neuromonitoring (particularly for sciatic nerve function) during the ischial and posterior column cuts, given the nerve's proximity; discuss availability and surgeon preference in advance.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, frequently combined with a regional technique (epidural or regional block) for postoperative analgesia given the significant early postoperative pain of a pelvic osteotomy; controlled hypotensive anesthesia is often used to reduce intraoperative blood loss.
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent table, positioned to allow full fluoroscopic visualization of the pelvis (AP, obturator oblique, and false-profile-type views) and to allow the operative limb to be draped free for intraoperative manipulation (flexion, rotation) during fragment reorientation and impingement testing.
- **Approach exposure setup**: the modified Smith-Petersen (bikini or longitudinal) anterior approach is standard, interval between sartorius/tensor fasciae latae and, deeper, between rectus femoris and gluteus medius; ensure the prep and drape extend to allow this full interval to be developed, from the iliac crest to below the hip joint.
- **Draping**: the operative leg is draped free to the toes to permit intraoperative flexion, rotation, and abduction/adduction for dynamic assessment of correction and impingement.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm positioned to obtain a true AP pelvis view intraoperatively (critical for judging correction accurately) plus obturator-oblique and iliac-oblique views as needed to assess the ischial and posterior column cuts; confirm the C-arm can access these views without repositioning the patient before starting.
- **Tourniquet**: not used — the osteotomy sites span cancellous pelvic bone across a wide surgical field not amenable to tourniquet control; hemostasis relies on surgical technique, hypotensive anesthesia, and tranexamic acid.
- **Surface landmarks marked**: anterior superior iliac spine, iliac crest, and the planned incision (bikini incision two fingerbreadths distal to the iliac crest, or a longitudinal incision along the anterior iliac crest extending distally) marked before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Develop the modified Smith-Petersen interval**: incise through the superficial interval between sartorius and tensor fasciae latae, then the deep interval between rectus femoris and gluteus medius, protecting the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve throughout (identify and retract it early, as it crosses near the sartorius origin and is one of the most commonly injured structures in this approach).
2. **Expose the pelvic brim and pubic ramus**: elevate iliacus subperiosteally from the inner table of the ilium, expose the pectineal eminence and superior pubic ramus, and identify the psoas tendon and iliopsoas, protecting the femoral neurovascular bundle medially.
3. **Perform the ischial osteotomy first** (or per surgeon's preferred sequence): using a curved osteotome directed from an anteromedial starting point, cut the ischium below the acetabulum under fluoroscopic (obturator-oblique) guidance. Keep the chisel insertion point no more than approximately one fingerbreadth anterior to the greater sciatic notch and direct the ischial cut at roughly one fingerbreadth below the joint edge — the sciatic nerve has been reported as close as 8–11 mm from the distal portion of this cut, and it is also at risk if the osteotome migrates laterally during the cut, so meticulous fluoroscopic control and conservative depth are essential.
4. **Perform the superior pubic ramus osteotomy**: after protecting the obturator neurovascular bundle with a Crego (or similar curved) retractor placed around the ramus, complete the cut through the pubic ramus medial to the iliopectineal eminence, angled to avoid the hip joint and obturator canal.
5. **Perform the iliac (supra-acetabular) osteotomy**: cut from the anterior inferior iliac spine region obliquely across the ilium above the acetabulum toward the posterior column, staying extra-articular and above the joint capsule.
6. **Perform the posterior column osteotomy**, connecting the iliac cut to the ischial cut while preserving the posterior column's structural and vascular continuity: use fluoroscopically-guided, width-specific osteotomes advanced incrementally, taking measurements from just above the greater sciatic notch down to the inferior acetabulum to plan cut depth and confirm the posterior column is not being violated. Complete this cut carefully and fully — an incomplete posterior column cut is one of the most common technical pitfalls, since reliance on tactile feedback through largely indirect visualization makes it easy to leave the cut short, which then limits fragment mobilization or causes uncontrolled fracture propagation into the joint or posterior column when the fragment is forced.
7. **Mobilize the acetabular fragment**: once all four cuts (ischial, pubic, iliac, posterior column) are complete and confirmed, place a Schanz pin into the acetabular fragment (typically from the iliac crest/AIIS region) to use as a joystick for controlled fragment mobilization — gently rock and rotate the fragment to confirm it is fully free before attempting definitive reorientation.
8. **Reorient the fragment**: rotate the fragment anteriorly, laterally, and/or in version as needed to correct lateral coverage (LCEA), anterior coverage (anterior center-edge angle), and acetabular version simultaneously, guided by the preoperative template and intraoperative fluoroscopy.
9. **Confirm correction fluoroscopically** against target parameters: LCEA 25–35°, normalized acetabular version (crossover sign corrected without creating posterior wall deficiency/retroversion), and medialization of the hip center ≤10 mm. Take care to avoid overcorrection — LCEA >40° risks iatrogenic pincer impingement and is a recognized cause of PAO failure — and avoid undercorrection, which leaves persistent instability and defeats the purpose of the procedure.
10. **Provisionally fix the fragment** with Kirschner wires in the corrected position, then re-confirm coverage and version fluoroscopically before committing to definitive fixation.
11. **Definitively fix the fragment** with multiple (typically 2–4) cortical or cannulated screws directed from the ilium across the osteotomy into the acetabular fragment, achieving rigid fixation sufficient for early protected mobilization.
12. **Dynamically assess for impingement** by taking the hip through flexion and internal rotation intraoperatively; if new femoroacetabular impingement is created by the correction (common when significant anterior/lateral rotation is needed), perform a concomitant femoral osteochondroplasty through the same or an adjunctive approach, or proceed to arthroscopic/open labral and cam treatment as planned preoperatively ("FAO," femoral-acetabular osteotomy).
13. **Address labral/chondral pathology**, if planned, via concomitant hip arthroscopy or a limited arthrotomy — labral tears are common in dysplastic hips due to the labrum's secondary weight-bearing role, and many surgeons treat significant labral pathology at the same setting rather than staging it.
14. **Obtain final fluoroscopic images** in multiple planes to document final coverage, version, and fixation position before closure.
15. **Close in layers**, reapproximating the deep (rectus femoris/gluteus medius) and superficial (sartorius/TFL) intervals, with attention to hemostasis given the vascularity of the exposed cancellous bone surfaces; a drain is used per surgeon preference given expected postoperative blood loss/hematoma risk.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Sequence the four osteotomies in a consistent, deliberate order (many surgeons favor ischial, then pubic, then iliac, then posterior column) so that fluoroscopic landmarks and retractor positions are reproducible from case to case, reducing the chance of a missed step.
- Use a curved (rather than straight) osteotome for the ischial cut directed from an anteromedial start point — this better follows the natural curve of the ischium and keeps the working tip further from the sciatic nerve than a straight trajectory would.
- Keep the ischial chisel insertion point within about one fingerbreadth anterior to the greater sciatic notch, and the ischial cut level at about one fingerbreadth below the joint edge — these are reproducible intraoperative tactile/visual checkpoints that keep the cut a safe distance from the sciatic nerve without needing to directly visualize it.
- Take deliberate, incremental measurements of posterior column width before committing to osteotome width and depth for that cut — using a specific-width osteotome matched to the measured column width reduces the risk of both an incomplete cut (osteotome too narrow, cut stops short) and inadvertent penetration into the sciatic notch or joint (osteotome too wide or advanced too far).
- Confirm each cut is fully complete before moving to fragment mobilization — an incomplete cut discovered only when the fragment will not rotate freely risks an uncontrolled fracture propagation into the joint or posterior column when force is applied to a fragment that is not actually free.
- Use the Schanz pin as a true joystick — small, controlled rotational adjustments with fluoroscopic feedback between each adjustment, rather than a single large correction attempt, make it easier to land precisely on target coverage and version values.
- Re-check fluoroscopic coverage parameters after provisional K-wire fixation and before final screw fixation — this is the last easy opportunity to adjust position, since screws are far more difficult to revise than K-wires.
- Actively test for new impingement intraoperatively (dynamic flexion/internal rotation) after fixation, since correcting dysplastic undercoverage not infrequently creates or unmasks cam-type impingement that was previously subclinical in the undercovered hip — addressing this in the same setting avoids a second surgery.
- Consider a minimally invasive two-incision variant (separate anterior and lateral/posterior windows) in appropriately selected cases and experienced hands — comparative series suggest this can reduce soft-tissue morbidity while maintaining correction accuracy, though it adds technical complexity and is not a substitute for mastering the standard single-approach technique first.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Undercorrection leaves persistent instability; overcorrection (>40° LCEA) risks iatrogenic pincer impingement and is a recognized cause of PAO failure — correction targets (LCEA 25–35°, medialization ≤10 mm) must be respected and confirmed fluoroscopically before final fixation.
- Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury is common (usually transient) from retraction during the Smith-Petersen approach; identify and protect the nerve early in the dissection.
- Sciatic nerve injury is rare but serious and is specifically associated with the ischial and posterior column cuts — reported as close as 8–11 mm from the distal ischial cut and at risk if osteotomes migrate laterally during the ischial or posterior iliac cuts; risk rises with concurrent correction of severe deformity. Meticulous fluoroscopic control, conservative chisel insertion depth, and (where available) intraoperative neuromonitoring mitigate this risk.
- Incomplete osteotomy cuts, particularly of the posterior column, are a well-recognized pitfall attributable to the largely indirect visualization and tactile-feedback-dependent nature of this procedure; an incomplete cut can limit fragment mobilization and cause fracture propagation into the posterior column or joint when the fragment is forced during reorientation. Careful preoperative measurement of column width and deliberate, confirmed completion of each cut before mobilization is the primary mitigation.
- Nonunion of osteotomy sites is low (<5%) in experienced hands but rises with inexperience, poor bone quality, or inadequate fixation.
- Intra-articular penetration of osteotomy cuts and heterotopic ossification are recognized complications; extra-articular cut trajectories and, in some practices, prophylactic NSAID/radiation protocols for high-risk patients mitigate heterotopic ossification.
- Outcomes are volume-dependent with a long learning curve — PAO is best performed at high-volume hip-preservation centers, and cadaveric/mentored case experience is strongly recommended before independent practice.
- Conversion to THA in a minority of patients is technically more complex after PAO due to altered version/anatomy and retained hardware — hardware removal planning and awareness of altered acetabular bone stock should factor into any future arthroplasty discussion with the patient.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Touch-down/partial weight-bearing (foot-flat, ~20 lb) on crutches; hip precautions to protect osteotomy fixation | Pain control, gentle passive/active-assisted ROM, isometric activation, DVT prophylaxis |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated once radiographic union of osteotomy sites confirmed (typically by 6–8 weeks) | Stationary bike, pool therapy, progressive closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining off crutches |
| III | 3–6 months | Full weight-bearing | Progressive strengthening (abductors, core), balance/proprioception, low-impact conditioning |
| IV — Return to sport/high demand activity | 6–12 months | — | Full radiographic healing, symmetric strength, sport-specific progression; impact/pivoting sport typically not cleared before 6–9 months; heavy manual labor return around 4–6 months, sedentary work 6–8 weeks |

Reported 10–20 year THA-free survivorship in appropriately selected patients (Tönnis 0 preoperatively) exceeds 60–90% in mature cohort series.
