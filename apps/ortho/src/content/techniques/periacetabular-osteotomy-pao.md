## Indications

The procedure of choice for symptomatic acetabular dysplasia in skeletally mature patients (closed triradiate cartilage) with a congruent joint and preserved cartilage — Tönnis grade 0–1, occasionally selected Tönnis grade 2, LCEA <20–25°.

## Positioning & setup

Peri-acetabular cuts are made through the ischium, superior pubic ramus, ilium, and posterior column, preserving the posterior column and its blood supply to allow free rotation of the acetabular fragment.

## Steps

1. Perform peri-acetabular cuts: ischial, superior pubic ramus, iliac, and posterior column osteotomies, keeping the posterior column and its blood supply intact.
2. Freely rotate and reorient the acetabular fragment, correcting lateral (LCEA), anterior (anterior center-edge angle on false-profile), and version deformities simultaneously.
3. Target correction: LCEA 25–35°, acetabular version normalized, medialization of the hip center ≤10 mm (undercorrection risks persistent instability; overcorrection >40° risks iatrogenic pincer impingement and is a recognized cause of PAO failure).
4. Fix the fragment internally with screws.
5. When cam morphology or labral pathology coexists (common in dysplastic hips), perform concomitant hip arthroscopy or arthrotomy for labral/chondral treatment and femoral osteochondroplasty — combined "FAO" (femoral-acetabular osteotomy).

## Pitfalls

- Undercorrection leaves persistent instability; overcorrection (>40° LCEA) risks iatrogenic pincer impingement and is a recognized cause of PAO failure — correction targets (LCEA 25–35°, medialization ≤10 mm) must be respected.
- Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury is common (usually transient); sciatic/femoral nerve injury is rare but serious, more common with concurrent correction of severe deformity.
- Nonunion of osteotomy sites is low (<5%) in experienced hands but rises with inexperience.
- Intra-articular penetration of osteotomy cuts and heterotopic ossification are recognized complications.
- Outcomes are volume-dependent with a long learning curve — PAO is best performed at high-volume hip-preservation centers.
- Conversion to THA in a minority of patients is technically more complex after PAO due to altered version/anatomy and retained hardware.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Touch-down/partial weight-bearing (foot-flat, ~20 lb) on crutches; hip precautions to protect osteotomy fixation | Pain control, gentle passive/active-assisted ROM, isometric activation, DVT prophylaxis |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated once radiographic union of osteotomy sites confirmed (typically by 6–8 weeks) | Stationary bike, pool therapy, progressive closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining off crutches |
| III | 3–6 months | Full weight-bearing | Progressive strengthening (abductors, core), balance/proprioception, low-impact conditioning |
| IV — Return to sport/high demand activity | 6–12 months | — | Full radiographic healing, symmetric strength, sport-specific progression; impact/pivoting sport typically not cleared before 6–9 months; heavy manual labor return around 4–6 months, sedentary work 6–8 weeks |

Reported 10–20 year THA-free survivorship in appropriately selected patients (Tönnis 0 preoperatively) exceeds 60–90% in mature cohort series.
