## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Massive irreparable rotator cuff tears (Goutallier ≥3, Patte 3, or positive tangent sign) with a preserved glenohumeral joint and pseudoparalysis absent or "correctable."
- Best candidates are younger, active patients with preserved teres minor/infraspinatus function and a less severe external rotation lag — i.e., predominantly posterosuperior loss without the marked external-rotation weakness that favors lower trapezius transfer instead.
- Patients who wish to avoid or delay reverse total shoulder arthroplasty and are not candidates for it by age/activity level.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Glenohumeral arthritis or joint incongruity — the joint must be preserved for SCR to have a mechanical foundation to stabilize.
- True pseudoparalysis that is not correctable under intra-articular local anesthetic, or advanced fatty infiltration of the deltoid — the construct depends on a functioning deltoid and dynamic muscles for its effect.
- A positive tangent sign combined with severely compromised teres minor/infraspinatus function — this combination undermines the dynamic support the graft depends on and predicts a poor chance of success; consider lower trapezius transfer or rTSA instead.
- Irreparable subscapularis tear — anterior cuff insufficiency is not addressed by SCR and compromises the overall force-couple balance the reconstruction relies on.
- Active infection, smoking without cessation counseling, or inability to comply with the extended immobilization/rehabilitation protocol required for graft incorporation.
- Older, lower-demand patients for whom rTSA or a simpler partial repair/debridement carries a more favorable risk-benefit profile than a technically demanding reconstruction with a meaningful graft failure rate.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to confirm tear pattern and irreparability (Goutallier, Patte, tangent sign), assess subscapularis and teres minor/infraspinatus integrity specifically (both drive candidacy), and rule out glenohumeral arthritis; plain radiographs (true AP/Grashey, axillary) to confirm joint space preservation and acromiohumeral distance.
- **Graft selection**: fascia lata autograft (harvested thicker, typically 6–8 mm) has shown more durable outcomes than thin dermal allograft in several comparative series — thickness and stiffness of the graft are directly tied to its ability to restore superior glenohumeral stability and decrease subacromial peak contact pressure; dermal allograft avoids autograft harvest morbidity but carries higher reported graft failure rates in some series. Discuss the trade-off explicitly with the patient and plan harvest site/graft source accordingly.
- **Sizing**: measure the glenohumeral gap (glenoid to greater tuberosity) on preoperative imaging to estimate graft dimensions needed; undersizing is a recognized, avoidable cause of early failure, so plan to harvest or order a graft generously sized relative to the measured defect.
- **Equipment**: standard shoulder arthroscopy tower, knotless suture anchors for medial glenoid fixation (commonly 2–3, e.g., positioned around the 10-, 12-, and 2-o'clock glenoid positions), a suture-bridge anchor set for lateral (greater tuberosity) fixation, cannulas sized to pass a bulky graft (e.g., 12 mm working cannula), graft-passing/traction sutures, and — if harvesting fascia lata autograft — a separate open harvest set for the lateral thigh.
- **Concomitant procedures**: plan for partial repair of any mobile residual cuff tissue to augment the construct, and address biceps pathology (tenotomy is commonly performed as part of the exposure/interval work) at the same setting.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with an interscalene regional block, as for other extensive arthroscopic shoulder reconstructions; discuss anticipated longer operative time with anesthesia given the complexity of graft preparation and multi-portal fixation.
- **Position**: beach-chair or lateral decubitus per surgeon preference, as for arthroscopic cuff repair; beach-chair is commonly favored when a fascia lata autograft harvest is planned, since it allows simultaneous or sequential access to the ipsilateral lateral thigh without repositioning.
- **Table setup**: standard shoulder positioner; if harvesting fascia lata, prep and drape the ipsilateral lateral thigh into the same sterile field so harvest can be performed without breaking sterility or repositioning the patient.
- **Draping**: wide shoulder exposure as for standard arthroscopy, plus the lateral thigh field if autograft harvest is planned; keep both fields accessible simultaneously to allow graft preparation on a back table while portal work proceeds.
- **No tourniquet needed for the shoulder portion**; a thigh tourniquet may be used during fascia lata harvest per surgeon preference.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: standard shoulder portal landmarks (posterior soft spot, anterior, anterolateral, lateral, and posterolateral portals — SCR typically requires more portals than a routine cuff repair to manage graft passage and multi-point fixation) plus, if harvesting fascia lata, the lateral thigh incision site along the iliotibial band.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Perform diagnostic arthroscopy** and confirm true irreparability of the posterosuperior cuff, subscapularis integrity, and teres minor/infraspinatus function before committing to SCR — if the tear proves more reparable than expected, revert to primary repair.
2. **Establish the standard posterior viewing portal**, then a **mid-lateral portal** off the lateral edge of the acromion (through a working cannula sized to pass the graft), and **anterior portal(s)**: a low/medial anterior interval portal for biceps tenotomy and interval work, and often a second, more superior/lateral anterior portal to facilitate graft traction and suture management.
3. **Address the biceps** (tenotomy is commonly performed at this stage as part of the interval work) and perform any needed subacromial bursectomy for visualization.
4. **Prepare the glenoid**: lightly decorticate the superior glenoid neck to a bleeding bed and place knotless suture anchors at the planned medial fixation points (commonly around the 10-, 12-, and 2-o'clock positions) for graft attachment.
5. **Prepare the greater tuberosity footprint** as for a standard cuff repair — debride to a bleeding bed and place lateral-row anchors for the suture-bridge fixation of the graft's lateral edge.
6. **Prepare the graft on the back table** while portal work proceeds: size the fascia lata autograft or dermal allograft to bridge the measured glenoid-to-tuberosity gap with adequate margin, and pre-tension/pre-shuttle sutures through the graft's medial and lateral edges for later retrieval.
7. **Pass the graft into the joint** through the lateral working cannula, using traction sutures placed through the anterior portals to control graft orientation and prevent it from folding or twisting during passage — a lasso-loop or similar traction technique through dedicated anterior portals materially eases this step.
8. **Secure medial fixation** first: tie or load the graft's medial sutures onto the previously placed glenoid anchors, seating the graft against the glenoid neck.
9. **Tension and secure lateral fixation**: bring the graft laterally over the greater tuberosity footprint under appropriate tension and fix it using a suture-bridge construct into the lateral-row anchors — adequate lateral footprint fixation is essential, not just medial fixation, to restore the superior glenohumeral restraint the construct is intended to provide.
10. **Augment with side-to-side sutures** between the graft's posterior edge and any mobile residual infraspinatus tissue, and between the graft's anterior edge and any residual subscapularis/rotator interval tissue, where feasible — this integrates the graft into the surrounding dynamic cuff rather than leaving it as an isolated static sheet.
11. **Confirm construct integrity**: probe the graft circumferentially at both medial and lateral fixation points, and gently cycle the shoulder through a limited range of motion to confirm no gapping or suture pull-through before withdrawing instruments.
12. **Close portals**, apply a sterile dressing, and place the arm in an abduction brace to protect the graft in a position of reduced tension before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Measure the glenohumeral gap carefully on preoperative imaging and harvest/order the graft generously relative to that measurement — undersizing is one of the most common, entirely avoidable causes of early failure.
- Favor a fascia lata autograft of 6–8 mm thickness where feasible — graft thickness and stiffness are directly tied to the construct's ability to restore superior stability and reduce subacromial contact pressure, and thicker autograft has outperformed thin dermal allograft in comparative series.
- Use a traction-suture (lasso-loop or equivalent) technique through the anterior portals when passing the graft into the joint — this prevents the graft from folding or twisting during passage through the lateral cannula, which is one of the more technically frustrating steps in the case.
- Secure medial fixation before lateral fixation, and do not consider the case complete until both are solid — the fixation error most associated with graft failure is adequate medial fixation paired with inadequate lateral footprint fixation.
- Augment the graft with side-to-side sutures to any mobile residual cuff tissue (posterior to infraspinatus, anterior to subscapularis/interval) — this integrates the graft mechanically rather than leaving it to function as an isolated static sheet.
- Use a working cannula sized adequately for the graft's bulk (commonly ~12 mm) — an undersized cannula makes graft passage unnecessarily difficult and risks graft trauma during forced passage.
- Case selection is as important as technique: a positive tangent sign or advanced fatty infiltration in the muscles the graft depends on for dynamic support undermines the construct's chance of success regardless of how well the graft is fixed.
- Prep the ipsilateral thigh into the same sterile field from the outset if autograft harvest is planned, so graft preparation can proceed in parallel with portal work rather than sequentially, shortening overall operative time.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Undersized or thin graft, or medial-only fixation without adequate lateral footprint fixation, is a common cause of early graft failure — both graft sizing and complete (medial + lateral) fixation matter.
- Reported graft failure rates run up to 20–40% depending on graft type/series, and are higher with dermal allograft than fascia lata autograft in some comparative studies.
- SCR is technically demanding; case selection matters as much as technique — a positive tangent sign or advanced fatty infiltration in the muscles the graft depends on for dynamic support undermines the construct's chance of success.
- Recent (2025) comparative literature suggests lower trapezius tendon transfer restores deltoid function and dynamic shoulder stability more effectively than SCR in this patient population, particularly when external rotation weakness (Hornblower sign) is prominent — factor this into candidate selection rather than defaulting to SCR for all massive irreparable tears with preserved joint.
- Deltoid dysfunction can follow aggressive deltoid splitting/retraction during the open exposure portions of the procedure — favor muscle-sparing exposure where feasible.
- Graft folding or twisting during passage through an undersized cannula can compromise graft integrity before fixation even begins — use a traction-suture technique and an adequately sized cannula to avoid forcing the graft through.
- Fascia lata autograft harvest carries its own donor-site morbidity (lateral thigh pain, rare seroma) that should be disclosed during consent, distinct from the shoulder-side risks of the reconstruction itself.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Typically 6 weeks of strict immobilization (abduction brace commonly used to protect the graft in a position of reduced tension).
- Passive ROM introduced gradually from week 4–6.
- Active ROM from ~8–12 weeks.
- Strengthening from 3–4 months.
- Full recovery/plateau often 9–12 months, given graft incorporation timelines.
