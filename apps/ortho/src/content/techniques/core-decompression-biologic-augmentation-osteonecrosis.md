## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Precollapse osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ARCO 0–IIC / Ficat-Arlet I–II) that remains symptomatic despite a trial of protected weight-bearing and risk-factor modification.
- Best candidates: small-to-medium lesions (Steinberg <30% head involvement), medial or central location on coronal MRI, combined necrotic (Kerboul) angle <200°.
- Biologic augmentation (bone marrow aspirate concentrate/BMAC, concentrated bone marrow mononuclear or mesenchymal cells, ± demineralized bone matrix) is increasingly the default rather than an add-on for ARCO I–II disease, given a consistent 2023–2025 meta-analytic signal of improved pain scores and reduced collapse/conversion-to-THA rates versus decompression alone.
- Bilateral, asymptomatic-but-MRI-positive precollapse disease in a high-risk patient (chronic steroids, sickle cell) is a reasonable indication for prophylactic decompression in some practices, though evidence for this specific scenario is weaker than for symptomatic disease.

**Contraindications**
- Postcollapse disease with >2 mm subchondral depression (ARCO IIIB or worse) — core decompression alone has a high failure rate here and is not the primary indication; consider vascularized grafting, rotational osteotomy, or THA instead.
- Large lateral/weight-bearing-zone lesions (Steinberg C, Kerboul angle >200°) — poor track record with decompression alone even precollapse; counsel toward vascularized grafting or earlier arthroplasty discussion.
- Active or recent septic arthritis/osteomyelitis of the hip — treat/clear infection first.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or inability to tolerate even brief anesthesia in medically fragile patients.
- Relative: ARCO IV (established secondary osteoarthritis) — decompression will not address the arthritic joint; THA is indicated instead.
- Relative: ongoing high-dose corticosteroid requirement with no ability to reduce dose — decompression can still be offered, but recurrence/progression risk is higher and should be part of counseling.

## Preoperative Planning

- **MRI (non-contrast, dedicated hip protocol)** within the preceding weeks to confirm ARCO/Ficat-Arlet stage, measure lesion size (Steinberg %, Kerboul/combined necrotic angle on mid-coronal and mid-sagittal cuts), and rule out subchondral fracture/collapse that would change the plan intraoperatively.
- **AP pelvis and frog-leg/Dunn lateral radiographs** to confirm no radiographic collapse and to have a baseline for postoperative comparison; a CT can help delineate a subtle crescent sign if the plain films are equivocal.
- **Contralateral hip MRI** if not already obtained — bilateral involvement is present in 50–80% of atraumatic ONFH, and a positive contralateral hip changes the conversation about simultaneous versus staged treatment.
- **Decide core technique** in advance: multiple small-diameter drilling (typically 3–4 mm Kirschner wires or drill bits passed in a fan pattern) versus a single larger-diameter (8–10 mm) core tract. Neither has been shown unequivocally superior in comparative meta-analyses; multiple drilling is a lower-morbidity option that preserves more structural bone and is favored by many surgeons for smaller/medial lesions, while a single larger core provides a wider channel for structural graft or a cannulated delivery system when higher graft/cell volumes are planned.
- **Plan biologic source and processing**: iliac crest bone marrow aspiration site (posterior superior iliac spine is the highest-yield site; anterior iliac crest is an alternative if the patient is supine only) and confirm availability of a point-of-care centrifuge/concentration system if BMAC is planned same-day. If demineralized bone matrix or a structural allograft/synthetic bone-void filler is planned as an adjunct, have it opened and ready before decompression to minimize open-tract time.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy (C-arm) with a radiolucent table, cannulated core decompression instrumentation set (guide pin, cannulated reamer/trephine matched to chosen tract diameter), bone marrow aspiration needle/trocar (e.g., Jamshidi-type) if harvesting autologous marrow, and a bone marrow concentration centrifuge system if used.
- **Anticoagulation/DVT risk assessment** — most patients are ambulatory quickly, but core decompression does involve reaming through cancellous bone with fat/marrow embolization potential; review standard perioperative VTE prophylaxis protocol.
- **Discuss with the patient** the realistic collapse-prevention rate given lesion size, and set expectations that surveillance MRI at 3 and 6 months (and periodically thereafter) is part of the plan regardless of symptomatic improvement.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia is acceptable; this is typically a short (30–60 minute) procedure and either technique works. Regional anesthesia with light sedation is often preferred for same-day discharge pathways.
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent (fracture) table or a standard radiolucent OR table that allows unobstructed AP and lateral fluoroscopic imaging of the proximal femur. A bump under the ipsilateral buttock can help internally rotate the limb for a true AP femoral neck view.
- **C-arm setup**: position the C-arm on the operative side (or have it swing freely) to obtain orthogonal AP and cross-table/frog-lateral views of the femoral head and neck without repositioning the limb. Confirm both views are obtainable and unobstructed before prepping — this is the single most common source of intraoperative delay.
- **Draping**: standard hip/proximal thigh prep and drape exposing the lateral thigh from the greater trochanter to mid-thigh, wide enough to accommodate the entry point below the trochanter and any needed adjustment of trajectory.
- **Bone marrow harvest site**: if BMAC is planned from the ipsilateral posterior iliac crest, this requires either repositioning (lateral or prone) or a separate prep of the posterior iliac crest with the patient supine and slightly rolled; many surgeons instead harvest from the anterior iliac crest with the patient supine to avoid repositioning, accepting a somewhat lower marrow cellularity yield than the posterior crest.
- **Tourniquet**: not used — this is a percutaneous/small-incision procedure through cancellous bone, not an open exposure with a controlled surgical field requiring tourniquet hemostasis.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the greater trochanter and the planned entry point on the lateral femoral cortex, roughly 1–2 cm distal to the vastus ridge/below the trochanter, before draping so the entry trajectory can be visualized relative to skin landmarks.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm lesion location on fluoroscopy.** Obtain baseline AP and frog-lateral (or cross-table lateral) images of the hip to correlate the fluoroscopic anatomy with the preoperative MRI lesion location (typically anterosuperior/superolateral quadrant).
2. **Mark the skin entry point.** Choose a lateral thigh entry point below the greater trochanter, at or just distal to the vastus ridge — low enough that the resulting cortical defect does not sit at the same level as the high-stress subtrochanteric region. A high entry point combined with a large-diameter core is the classic combination that produces an iatrogenic stress riser and subtrochanteric fracture.
3. **Make a small stab incision** (approximately 1 cm) at the marked entry point and bluntly spread to bone with a hemostat to avoid injury to superficial vessels/nerves.
4. **Insert the guide pin** under fluoroscopic control, aiming from the lateral cortex toward the necrotic lesion. Confirm trajectory in the AP plane first, then rotate the C-arm (or reposition the limb) to the lateral/frog-lateral view and re-confirm — the guide pin must be checked in **two orthogonal fluoroscopic planes** before any reaming proceeds, since a trajectory that looks correct in one plane alone can still be off-axis and either miss the lesion or breach the articular surface.
5. **Advance the guide pin to just short of the subchondral plate.** Aim to stop 3–5 mm from the subchondral bone in the AP and lateral views — advancing further risks penetrating the articular cartilage, which both fails to add benefit and creates a portal for joint fluid/infection tracking and a focal area of subchondral weakness.
6. **Ream or drill over the guide pin** to the chosen tract diameter:
 - *Multiple small-diameter drilling*: pass several (typically 3–4) 3–4 mm drill bits or Kirschner wires in a fan/cluster pattern through the same skin entry, each individually confirmed on fluoroscopy, decompressing a broader volume of the lesion with less structural bone removed at any single point.
 - *Single large-diameter core*: ream a single 8–10 mm cannulated trephine tract over the guide pin, which provides a larger decompression volume and a wider channel for delivering graft/cells but removes more structural bone at the entry site and carries the higher fracture-risk profile if placed too proximally.
7. **Retrieve a core biopsy** if the trephine technique is used — the cylindrical bone core removed can be sent for histology if the diagnosis is still in question, or used as autograft material reinserted after biologic augmentation.
8. **Curette or gently debride** any obviously necrotic, soft, or granulation-type tissue encountered within the tract under fluoroscopic control, taking care not to advance instruments beyond the confirmed safe depth.
9. **Prepare and deliver the biologic augment**, if planned:
 - Aspirate bone marrow from the iliac crest (posterior or anterior) using multiple smaller-volume draws (typically 2–4 mL per pass, repositioning the needle between draws) rather than one large-volume draw, which improves mesenchymal stem cell concentration and yield.
 - Process the aspirate through a point-of-care centrifuge system to produce BMAC.
 - Deliver the concentrated marrow (± demineralized bone matrix or a bone-void filler carrier) through the decompression tract via a cannula or syringe under gentle pressure, confirming fluoroscopically that the material fills the decompressed cavity without extravasation beyond the cortical entry.
10. **Confirm final tract position and fill** on orthogonal fluoroscopic views.
11. **Close the stab incision** with a single subcuticular or simple suture and a local anesthetic injection at the entry site for postoperative analgesia.
12. **Apply a simple dressing.** No formal bracing is required; crutches are provided for protected weight-bearing per the postoperative protocol.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Obtain both the AP and lateral fluoroscopic views *before* committing to the guide pin trajectory rather than after — adjusting a pin that has already engaged cancellous bone is harder and creates a second tract.
- Keep the entry point on the lateral cortex just distal to the vastus ridge; this keeps the cortical defect out of the high bending-moment subtrochanteric zone and reduces fracture risk without sacrificing access to an anterosuperior lesion.
- Use a bump or slight internal rotation of the limb to obtain a true AP of the femoral neck (neck profile without foreshortening) — this makes trajectory judgment far more reliable than a rotated, foreshortened view.
- When using multiple small-diameter drilling, fan the passes from a single skin entry rather than making multiple skin incisions — this reduces soft-tissue trauma while still decompressing a broad lesion volume.
- Harvest marrow in small-volume aliquots (2–4 mL) and reposition the aspiration needle between draws; large single-volume aspirates dilute the marrow with peripheral blood and lower the concentration of viable progenitor cells.
- If a structural graft or demineralized bone matrix is being used as a carrier, have it opened and ready on the back table before the tract is completed — minimizing time between reaming and filling reduces the chance of tract collapse or clot formation impeding delivery.
- Stop reaming/advancing 3–5 mm short of the subchondral plate rather than trying to get as close as possible; the margin protects against inadvertent articular breach from a slight misjudgment of depth on imaging.
- Send the extracted bone core for histology in any case where the diagnosis is not already secure (e.g., atypical MRI findings, unclear etiology) — this is a low-cost opportunity that is easy to skip once decompression has become routine.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that pain relief and imaging stabilization are the two separate endpoints being tracked, and that symptomatic improvement does not guarantee radiographic non-progression — this avoids a difficult postoperative conversation if a surveillance MRI shows interval change despite the patient feeling better.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Subtrochanteric stress fracture**: the most feared technical complication, particularly with large-diameter cores or entry points placed too proximally/medially. Incidence is low (<5%) but entirely avoidable with attention to entry-point selection and core diameter; counsel patients on protected weight-bearing and avoid early return to impact loading.
- **Failure to confirm needle-tip/guide-pin position in two fluoroscopic planes** risks tract malposition, missing the necrotic lesion (decompressing viable bone instead), or breaching the articular surface.
- **Articular surface penetration** — even a small breach can seed a portal for infection tracking into the joint and creates a focal area of subchondral weakness; always leave a 3–5 mm safety margin.
- **Lesion size/location remains the dominant determinant of outcome, not technique**: the single strongest predictor of core decompression failure is precollapse lesion size and, once present, degree of collapse. Large lateral (weight-bearing zone) lesions have up to 30–50% failure at 5 years even with technically flawless decompression — patient selection matters as much as surgical execution, and this should be part of informed consent.
- **Intra-articular corticosteroid is not used adjunctively** for ONFH pain — pain in early ONFH is typically intraosseous rather than synovial, and there is a theoretical concern that steroid could accelerate collapse; do not substitute an injection for definitive treatment of a precollapse lesion.
- **Heterotopic bone formation** can occur within the decompression tract, occasionally producing a palpable/imageable density without clinical significance.
- **Inadequate marrow aspirate cellularity** from a single large-volume draw can undermine the rationale for biologic augmentation without the surgeon realizing it intraoperatively — if using a point-of-care system with a cell-count readout, confirm an adequate concentration factor before delivery.
- **Postoperative fracture through the tract** from premature return to high-impact activity — reinforce weight-bearing restrictions, particularly in patients whose pain resolves quickly and who feel capable of more activity than the bone can yet tolerate.
- **Missing progression on imaging** because follow-up MRI was not scheduled — symptomatic improvement is not a substitute for surveillance imaging at 3 and 6 months; build this into the postoperative plan at the time of surgery, not reactively.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Toe-touch to partial (protected) weight-bearing on crutches | Pain control, gentle hip ROM, avoid high-impact/pivoting activity |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to full weight-bearing as tolerated if imaging/symptoms allow | Stationary bike, pool therapy, progressive strengthening |
| III | 3–6 months | Full weight-bearing | Return to low-impact recreational activity; repeat MRI at 3 and 6 months to confirm no interval collapse before clearing higher-impact activity |
| IV | 6–12 months | Full | Return to unrestricted activity if imaging stable and asymptomatic; ongoing surveillance imaging at 6–12 month intervals given risk of late collapse/contralateral disease |

Bilateral involvement is present in 50–80% of atraumatic ONFH, so contralateral hip surveillance imaging is part of the same follow-up program.
