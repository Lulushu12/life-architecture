## Indications

Precollapse osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ARCO 0–IIC / Ficat-Arlet I–II) that is symptomatic, particularly small-to-medium lesions (Steinberg <30% head involvement, medial/central location). Biologic augmentation (bone marrow aspirate concentrate/BMAC, concentrated bone marrow mononuclear or mesenchymal cells, ± demineralized bone matrix) is increasingly the default technique at hip-preservation centers for ARCO I–II disease given a consistent 2023–2024 meta-analytic signal of improved pain scores and reduced collapse rates versus decompression alone.

## Positioning & setup

Performed under fluoroscopic guidance. Choice of a multiple small-diameter "multiple drilling" technique versus a single larger-diameter (8–10 mm) core tract is a technique decision informed by comparative meta-analyses, without a single technique shown to be unequivocally superior.

## Steps

1. Select technique: multiple small-diameter drill tracts, or a single larger-diameter (8–10 mm) core tract.
2. Enter the lateral cortex below the greater trochanter, choosing an entry point low enough to avoid a stress riser at the subtrochanteric region — a high entry point or large-diameter core raises fracture risk.
3. Direct the tract into the necrotic lesion under fluoroscopic guidance, confirming needle-tip position in **two fluoroscopic planes** before proceeding.
4. Advance the tract to just short of the subchondral plate, avoiding breach of the articular surface.
5. If augmenting: inject bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC), concentrated bone marrow mononuclear/mesenchymal cells, ± demineralized bone matrix or structural graft through the decompression tract.

## Pitfalls

- Low or high entry point / large-diameter core increases subtrochanteric stress fracture risk (incidence low, <5%, but avoidable with attention to entry-point technique).
- Failing to confirm needle-tip position in two fluoroscopic planes risks tract malposition or articular breach.
- The single strongest predictor of core decompression failure is precollapse lesion size and, once present, degree of collapse — large lateral lesions have up to 30–50% failure at 5 years, so patient selection (small/medial-central lesions) matters as much as technique.
- Intra-articular corticosteroid is not used adjunctively for ONFH pain — pain in early ONFH is typically intraosseous rather than synovial, and there is a theoretical concern that steroid could accelerate collapse.
- Heterotopic bone formation can occur in the decompression tract.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Toe-touch to partial (protected) weight-bearing on crutches | Pain control, gentle hip ROM, avoid high-impact/pivoting activity |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to full weight-bearing as tolerated if imaging/symptoms allow | Stationary bike, pool therapy, progressive strengthening |
| III | 3–6 months | Full weight-bearing | Return to low-impact recreational activity; repeat MRI at 3 and 6 months to confirm no interval collapse before clearing higher-impact activity |
| IV | 6–12 months | Full | Return to unrestricted activity if imaging stable and asymptomatic; ongoing surveillance imaging at 6–12 month intervals given risk of late collapse/contralateral disease |

Bilateral involvement is present in 50–80% of atraumatic ONFH, so contralateral hip surveillance imaging is part of the same follow-up program.
