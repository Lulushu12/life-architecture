## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications for surgical (rather than aspiration-only) management**
- Hip or shoulder involvement — deep joints where arthrocentesis alone rarely achieves complete decompression and where the hip in particular carries a real risk of femoral head vascular compromise from a tense effusion.
- Failure to improve clinically after 1–2 aspirations.
- Loculated fluid on imaging (ultrasound or cross-sectional).
- A prosthetic-adjacent native joint (raises the stakes of source control given proximity to implanted material).
- An immunocompromised host.
- Presentation delayed beyond 3–5 days from symptom onset.
- Any confirmed or strongly suspected native septic arthritis in a large or deep joint (knee, hip, shoulder) generally warrants emergent decompression rather than a trial of aspiration alone.

**Relative contraindications / cautions**
- A small, superficial joint (wrist, ankle) with rapid, sustained clinical response to a single or limited series of aspirations may be reasonably managed non-operatively — surgery is not mandatory for every confirmed case, but the threshold to convert to operative management should remain low.
- Severe medical instability may require damage-control aspiration/drainage with deferred definitive washout, but source control should not be delayed longer than necessary to achieve basic physiologic stability — septic arthritis itself is a driver of that instability in many cases.
- Coexisting crystal arthropathy does not contraindicate surgical washout when infection is confirmed or remains a realistic possibility — a positive crystal result does not exclude septic arthritis, and washout should proceed on the infection indication regardless.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Diagnostic aspiration first**: whenever feasible, perform (or confirm has been performed) diagnostic arthrocentesis before starting antibiotics — this is a planning as much as a technique point, since starting empiric antibiotics pre-aspiration is a common and consequential sequencing error that both reduces diagnostic yield and delays true source control.
- **Imaging**: plain radiographs at minimum to assess baseline joint space and exclude an obvious osseous source (adjacent osteomyelitis); ultrasound is valuable pre-operatively to confirm effusion, guide diagnostic aspiration of deep joints (hip, shoulder), and characterize loculation, which influences the arthroscopic-versus-open decision.
- **Approach selection**: decide the arthroscopic-versus-open approach ahead of the case where possible, based on joint (knee/shoulder generally favor arthroscopic in centers with expertise; hip more often favors open arthrotomy), imaging findings of loculation (favors open for thick, multi-loculated purulence), and surgeon/center arthroscopic capability for the specific joint — do not default to open for every case out of habit if arthroscopic expertise and equipment are available and loculation is not extensive.
- **Equipment**: standard arthroscopy tower/pump/instrumentation set for the joint if arthroscopic washout is planned (large-bore inflow/outflow cannulae preferred over standard arthroscopic irrigation to handle thick purulent fluid without repeated clogging); open arthrotomy tray with a large-bore drain if open washout is planned.
- **Culture kit**: ensure synovial fluid collection tubes/bottles for cell count with differential, Gram stain, aerobic and anaerobic culture, and crystal analysis are ready before the case, plus additional deep tissue sample containers for intra-operative synovial biopsy.
- **Anticipate a second-look procedure**: plan (and consent the patient for) the possibility of repeat washout at 48–72h, since this is required in a meaningful minority of cases — this is a planning point, not an intra-operative surprise to introduce later.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is typical for arthroscopic or open washout of a major joint; regional block may supplement post-operative analgesia per joint and anesthesiology input. A hip or shoulder washout in a systemically unwell, potentially septic patient warrants close anesthetic coordination and often invasive monitoring depending on physiologic status.
- **Position — knee**: supine with a leg holder or lateral post for arthroscopic access; standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals.
- **Position — shoulder**: beach-chair or lateral decubitus per surgeon preference for arthroscopic access; standard posterior viewing portal with anterior working portal(s).
- **Position — hip**: supine on a radiolucent or fracture table if a traction-assisted arthroscopic approach is used; supine or lateral decubitus for open anterior (Smith-Petersen-type) arthrotomy. The anterior approach — using the superficial interval between sartorius and tensor fasciae latae and the deep interval between rectus femoris and gluteus medius — is favored for open hip arthrotomy in this setting because it affords good capsular exposure while avoiding disruption of the abductor mechanism, an important consideration since these patients may need revision or further surgery later and abductor function should be preserved wherever possible.
- **Position — wrist/ankle/elbow**: supine with the limb supported on a hand/arm table or per standard small-joint arthroscopy positioning if surgical management is chosen for a smaller joint.
- **Tourniquet**: a pneumatic tourniquet may be used for arthroscopic or open access to improve visualization, but plan to release it prior to final assessment of synovial tissue viability/bleeding if extensive synovectomy is performed alongside the washout.
- **Fluoroscopy**: primarily relevant for hip arthroscopy/arthrotomy (confirming portal or capsular access trajectory) and should be set up and tested before draping.
- **Draping**: standard sterile arthroscopic or open draping for the joint; ensure the drape allows conversion from arthroscopic to open access without a full re-prep if thick, unmanageable purulence is encountered during an arthroscopic attempt.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark standard portal sites (arthroscopic cases) or the planned open incision (arthrotomy) before incision, referencing the anterior superior iliac spine and greater trochanter for hip approaches, the coracoid and acromion for shoulder, and standard tibial/femoral landmarks for the knee.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Aspirate the joint before starting antibiotics** whenever feasible — delaying aspiration to "see the effect of antibiotics first" is a common and consequential error that both worsens diagnostic yield and delays source control. Use ultrasound guidance for deep joints (hip, shoulder) or when the effusion is not readily palpable.
2. **Send synovial fluid** for cell count with differential (>50,000/µL with >90% PMNs strongly suggestive in a native joint; lower thresholds of >25,000–30,000 apply in immunosuppressed/early presentations), Gram stain (~50–75% sensitive), culture (~70–90% sensitive with adequate volume and rapid transport), and crystal analysis — remember a positive crystal result does not exclude septic arthritis, since crystal arthropathy can coexist with infection.
3. **Position and prep** per the chosen approach (arthroscopic or open) as above.
4. **Establish portals or make the arthrotomy incision.** For arthroscopic washout: establish standard viewing and working portals for the joint; for the hip, use fluoroscopic guidance to confirm capsular puncture given the deep, less palpable anatomy. For open arthrotomy: carry the approach down through the interval appropriate to the joint (e.g., the anterior Smith-Petersen interval for the hip) to expose the capsule.
5. **Perform capsulotomy** (open) or introduce the arthroscope through the capsule (arthroscopic) and obtain a direct view/access to the joint. Note the character of the fluid (frank pus, cloudy, fibrinous debris) and the appearance of the synovium (hyperemic, hypertrophic, or frankly necrotic in advanced cases).
6. **Evacuate purulent fluid and debride fibrinous/necrotic synovium.** For arthroscopic cases, use a shaver to debride hypertrophic and necrotic synovial tissue and break up loculations under direct vision; if purulence is too thick to manage through standard arthroscopic cannulae despite adequate inflow/outflow, convert to open without hesitation rather than persisting with an inadequate washout.
7. **Perform thorough, high-volume lavage** of the joint (several liters of normal saline, pulsed or gravity lavage per surgeon preference) until the effluent runs clear, systematically irrigating all recesses and compartments of the joint (e.g., suprapatellar pouch and posterior compartments in the knee; axillary recess and subscapularis recess in the shoulder; the entire femoral head-neck junction and acetabular fossa in the hip).
8. **Obtain synovial tissue biopsy** for additional culture and histopathology alongside the fluid already sent.
9. **Assess and address any concomitant osseous source** (adjacent osteomyelitis, a loose body, or damaged cartilage) if identified during the washout — this may extend the procedure but is important for durable source control.
10. **Decide on drain placement.** A closed-suction or Penrose-type drain is commonly used after open arthrotomy, particularly for the hip, to prevent fluid reaccumulation; arthroscopic cases may or may not require a drain depending on the degree of synovial inflammation and fluid volume encountered — decide based on the intra-operative findings rather than a fixed rule.
11. **Close in layers** (open cases) or close portal sites (arthroscopic cases); avoid a fully watertight closure if ongoing drainage is anticipated and a drain has not been placed.
12. **Document the washout explicitly** in the operative note — approach used, fluid character, extent of synovectomy, drain status — since this directly informs the 48–72h reassessment decision.
13. **Reassess clinically and biochemically at 48–72h** with a low threshold for repeat washout if improvement stalls; repeat washout is required in roughly 10–30% of cases, higher for *S. aureus* — plan this reassessment as a scheduled checkpoint, not an ad hoc decision only if things look obviously wrong.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Do not let the desire to "just aspirate and start antibiotics" delay definitive source control in a joint that meets operative criteria — aspiration and washout are complementary, sequential steps, not alternative strategies, once operative criteria are met.
- For arthroscopic washout, use large-bore inflow/outflow cannulae from the outset rather than standard-diameter arthroscopic tubing — thick purulent fluid clogs standard tubing repeatedly and wastes operative time; anticipate this rather than troubleshooting it mid-case.
- Irrigate systematically through every recess of the joint, not just the visually obvious central compartment — residual loculated purulence in an unaddressed recess (suprapatellar pouch, axillary recess, acetabular fossa) is a common cause of the 48–72h reassessment showing inadequate improvement.
- Have a low threshold to convert an arthroscopic washout to open if purulence is too thick or loculation too extensive to clear arthroscopically — this is not a failure, it is appropriate case management, and persisting with an inadequate arthroscopic washout to avoid conversion is a false economy.
- For hip arthrotomy, use the anterior (Smith-Petersen-type) interval preferentially — it preserves the abductor mechanism, which matters disproportionately in this population since further surgery (including arthroplasty, should secondary arthritis or avascular necrosis develop) is a realistic future possibility.
- Send a synovial tissue biopsy in addition to fluid at every washout — tissue culture and histopathology can be positive when fluid culture is negative, and provide a second, independent line of diagnostic evidence.
- Build the 48–72h clinical/biochemical reassessment into the post-operative plan explicitly at the time of the index procedure, rather than waiting for a lack of improvement to prompt it reactively.
- In a hip with a tense effusion and any concern for vascular compromise of the femoral head, treat time-to-decompression with the same urgency as a compartment syndrome — this is not a routine-timing add-on case.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Delaying aspiration for a trial of antibiotics worsens diagnostic yield and delays source control.
- A positive crystal analysis does not exclude septic arthritis — crystal arthropathy is the principal mimic and can coexist with infection.
- Cartilage degradation begins within as little as 8 hours of untreated infection (matrix metalloproteinase- and cytokine-driven chondrocyte catabolism) — the physiologic basis for treating septic arthritis as a surgical emergency.
- Hip septic arthritis: intra-articular pressure rise from a tense effusion can compromise the femoral head blood supply — a time-sensitive complication demanding truly urgent decompression.
- Serum CRP/ESR/WBC are adjuncts, not substitutes, for joint fluid analysis.
- Incomplete lavage of a loculated recess is a common cause of apparent treatment failure at 48–72h reassessment — if improvement stalls, assume incomplete source control until proven otherwise and have a low threshold for repeat washout.
- Persisting with an inadequate arthroscopic washout in the face of thick, unmanageable purulence rather than converting to open access risks incomplete source control — know when to convert.
- Iatrogenic articular cartilage injury from aggressive shaver use during arthroscopic synovectomy is avoidable with careful, directed debridement rather than indiscriminate shaving near articular surfaces.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Weeks 0–2:** Protected/non-weight-bearing per stability and soft-tissue status; early passive/active-assisted ROM of adjacent joints unless a re-look procedure is imminent.

**Weeks 2–6:** Progressive weight-bearing as infection control allows; continue systemic antibiotics per culture-directed duration; physiotherapy to restore joint ROM and prevent contracture, particularly important after septic arthritis given the rapid cartilage injury described above.

**Beyond 6 weeks:** Strengthening progresses once infection is controlled. Return to work is individualized to job physical demand and joint status. Return to sport only after infection-marker normalization sustained off antibiotics.
