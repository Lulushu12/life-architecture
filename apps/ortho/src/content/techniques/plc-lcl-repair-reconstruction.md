## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Grade III / any combined PLC injury (Fanelli Type B or C). Acute repair is preferred within roughly 3 weeks of injury; chronic or delayed presentation requires reconstruction.

**Contraindications**:
- Fanelli Type A (isolated popliteofibular ligament/popliteus laxity without varus opening) — reasonable for a non-operative trial with bracing and activity modification if truly isolated and low-grade.
- Active infection or compromised lateral soft-tissue envelope.
- A pre-existing complete peroneal nerve palsy from the initial trauma does not, by itself, contraindicate reconstruction, but should be thoroughly documented and discussed with the patient preoperatively, since surgery will not reliably improve a nerve injury that occurred at the time of dislocation and expectations must be set accordingly.
- Uncorrected varus mechanical malalignment (varus thrust gait) in chronic PLC insufficiency — an isolated ligament reconstruction into a varus-malaligned limb is at high risk of recurrent stretch/failure; proximal tibial or distal femoral osteotomy should be considered first or staged with the ligament reconstruction.
- A knee with combined, uncorrected cruciate instability where the PLC is being addressed in isolation outside a coordinated combined-injury plan — treating the PLC alone while leaving a cruciate deficiency unaddressed sets the corner reconstruction up for early overload and failure.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to characterize which PLC structures are involved (LCL, popliteus tendon/popliteofibular ligament, posterolateral capsule) and to what degree, and to screen for an arcuate sign (fibular styloid avulsion) on plain radiographs, which is a reliable marker of PLC injury. Assess for combined cruciate injury, since isolated PLC injury is uncommon and combined patterns dominate clinically. A long-leg alignment radiograph is valuable in chronic presentations to rule out varus malalignment as a contributing/recurrence factor.
- **Grade and classify** using the Fanelli system (Type A: isolated popliteofibular/popliteus laxity; Type B: popliteofibular + LCL laxity with mild-moderate varus opening; Type C: full disruption with gross varus opening) — this determines whether isolated popliteofibular reconstruction/repair or comprehensive multi-strand reconstruction is required.
- **Graft selection for reconstruction**: autograft (semitendinosus ± gracilis) or allograft (Achilles or tibialis tendon allograft, commonly used given the need for adequate length to reconstruct multiple structures — LCL, popliteofibular ligament, and popliteus tendon function — from one or two graft strands); allograft is frequently preferred in the combined MLKI setting to limit cumulative donor-site morbidity when multiple grafts are harvested in the same sitting.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: standard arthroscopy tower (for concurrent intra-articular assessment/procedures); a dedicated peroneal nerve neurolysis/retraction set; guide pins and cannulated reamers for femoral (LCL and popliteus footprints) and fibular head tunnels; interference screws sized to tunnel diameter for fibular fixation; suspensory/adjustable-loop fixation devices as an alternative for tibial or femoral fixation in single-graft techniques; a small fragment set on standby if a fibular head or lateral tibial plateau avulsion requires fixation instead of/in addition to soft-tissue reconstruction.
- **Case-specific planning**: if combined with cruciate reconstruction, plan tunnel trajectories carefully — the PCL tibial tunnel, ACL femoral tunnel, and PLC femoral tunnels (LCL and popliteus footprints) can converge if not deliberately separated, and this should be mapped out before the case rather than discovered intraoperatively.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia; regional block for postoperative analgesia is used selectively and discussed explicitly with the patient, since a dense peroneal-distribution block can mask a postoperative peroneal nerve injury during the critical early monitoring window — many surgeons prefer to delay a nerve block (or use a block that spares distal motor/sensory peroneal function) until nerve status has been confirmed intact postoperatively.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard or radiolucent table with a leg-holder or lateral post; the knee is held at approximately 90° of flexion for the initial lateral exposure, with the ability to extend and apply varus stress intraoperatively for assessment.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh pneumatic tourniquet, padded, limb exsanguinated; many surgeons deflate the tourniquet before final closure specifically to confirm hemostasis around the peroneal nerve dissection and to visually confirm nerve perfusion/absence of compressive hematoma.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm available for confirming fibular and femoral tunnel guide-pin position and depth.
- **Exam under anesthesia**: varus stress at 0° and 30°, and dial test (external rotation asymmetry) at 30° and 90° — asymmetry at 30° only indicates isolated PLC injury, while asymmetry at both 30° and 90° indicates combined PLC + PCL injury; document findings and side-to-side comparison explicitly before incision, since this comparison is the single most important preoperative confirmation step for a diagnosis that is frequently under-recognized clinically.
- **Draping**: circumferential prep from mid-thigh to toes, leg free to allow varus stress testing and knee flexion/extension throughout the case.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: fibular head and the palpable course of the common peroneal nerve as it wraps around the fibular neck, Gerdy's tubercle, lateral epicondyle, and the planned curvilinear incision along the iliotibial band extending distally between the fibular head and Gerdy's tubercle.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy first**, if combined intra-articular pathology (cruciate, meniscal) is present, to confirm the injury pattern and address concurrent findings before extending fluid into the lateral soft tissues via the open PLC exposure.
2. **Make a curvilinear lateral incision** along the iliotibial band, extending distally in the interval between the fibular head and Gerdy's tubercle, with the knee held at 90° of flexion.
3. **Identify and protect the common peroneal nerve early**, before any deep dissection — isolate the nerve at the posterior border of the biceps femoris as it courses around the fibular neck, and perform a gentle neurolysis to decompress and mobilize it for protection throughout the remainder of the case; retract it gently and continuously visualize its position during subsequent tunnel drilling and graft passage steps, since it is the structure most at risk throughout this procedure.
4. **Expose the fibular head subperiosteally**, identifying the "champagne glass drop-off" transition at its lateral aspect — the landmark used to orient the fibular tunnel.
5. **For acute repair** (within ~2–3 weeks of injury, tissue quality permitting): identify the specific avulsed/torn structures (LCL, popliteus tendon, popliteofibular ligament, posterolateral capsule) at their femoral, fibular, or tibial attachment sites, and repair with suture anchors or transosseous suture fixation at the anatomic footprints, augmenting with suture-tape internal bracing when tissue quality is borderline. PLC structures retract and scar down poorly once beyond this early window, which is why acute repair is time-sensitive in a way the MCL is not.
6. **For chronic/delayed presentation** (the majority in practice, given how frequently PLC injury is initially missed), proceed to anatomic reconstruction:
   - Create a fibular head tunnel directed anterolateral to posteromedial, at the anatomic LCL fibular attachment (located approximately 8 mm posterior to the anterior margin of the fibular head and roughly 28 mm distal to the fibular styloid tip).
   - Drill a femoral guide pin at the LCL femoral footprint, directed with a proximal-anterior angulation (roughly 35°) to avoid tunnel convergence with a concurrently placed ACL or PCL femoral tunnel, and over-ream to the planned tunnel diameter and depth.
   - Drill a second femoral guide pin, parallel to the first, at the popliteus tendon (popliteofibular ligament complex) femoral insertion site, and over-ream similarly.
   - Prepare the graft strand(s): in a two-strand technique, one graft (or limb) reconstructs the LCL and the other reconstructs the popliteus tendon/popliteofibular ligament; single-graft techniques with adjustable-loop suspensory fixation have also been described for select cases.
7. **Pass the popliteus/popliteofibular limb** through the popliteal hiatus, exiting posterolaterally at the popliteal sulcus, recreating the popliteofibular ligament's course and its rotational restraint function.
8. **Pass the LCL limb** deep to the iliotibial band and biceps femoris tissue, exiting into the biceps bursa, then through the fibular tunnel from anterior to posterior (or per the specific technique's described trajectory).
9. **Fix the femoral side(s) first** (interference screw or suspensory fixation, technique-dependent), leaving the fibular/tibial ends free for final tensioning.
10. **Tension and fix the fibular-tunnel LCL limb with the knee at approximately 30° of flexion** and a neutral-to-slightly-corrective (valgus-reducing) force applied, confirming restoration of a firm varus-stress endpoint comparable to the contralateral limb before final fixation.
11. **Tension and fix the popliteofibular/popliteus limb**, checking that external rotation at 30° is restored to a side-to-side symmetric range without overconstraining rotation.
12. **If combined with cruciate reconstruction**, sequence the PLC reconstruction relative to the cruciate work per the multiligament reconstruction framework — commonly the peripheral PLC tunnels are drilled and grafts prepared first, with final PLC tensioning performed after the cruciate graft(s) are tensioned and the knee is centered, so the corner is fixed against a reduced, stable knee rather than fighting residual cruciate laxity.
13. **Final checks.** Deflate the tourniquet to confirm hemostasis and visually inspect the peroneal nerve for any compressive hematoma or kinking along its course; repeat varus stress and dial test under anesthesia to confirm restored symmetric stability before closure.
14. **Closure.** Close the iliotibial band and fascial layers over the reconstruction, close subcutaneous tissue and skin, and apply an extension brace with weight-bearing restrictions per protocol to protect the reconstruction from early varus and rotational stress.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Isolate and protect the common peroneal nerve as the very first step of the deep dissection, before any tunnel work begins — a brief neurolysis at the outset makes every subsequent step of the case safer and is far preferable to identifying the nerve reactively after it has already been placed at risk.
- Use the "champagne glass drop-off" transition at the lateral fibular head as a reliable, reproducible landmark for orienting the fibular tunnel — anatomic studies place the LCL fibular attachment approximately 8 mm posterior to the anterior fibular head margin and roughly 28 mm distal to the fibular styloid tip, which is a useful mental checkpoint even before instrumentation.
- Direct the LCL femoral tunnel with proximal-anterior angulation and keep the popliteus femoral tunnel parallel to it — deliberate divergent angulation relative to a concurrently placed ACL or PCL femoral tunnel is the specific technical maneuver that avoids tunnel convergence in combined cases.
- Perform the dial test bilaterally and at both 30° and 90° of flexion before finalizing the operative plan, not just at 30° — this is what separates isolated PLC injury from combined PLC+PCL injury and directly changes which structures need to be addressed in the same sitting.
- Tension the LCL limb at approximately 30° of flexion, not full extension, to avoid overconstraining terminal extension and to match the flexion angle at which the native LCL is functionally most engaged.
- Deflate the tourniquet before final closure specifically to visually confirm peroneal nerve perfusion and rule out a compressive hematoma along its course — this is a simple step that catches a preventable cause of postoperative nerve palsy before the patient leaves the OR.
- When reconstructing in the combined MLKI setting, sequence PLC tunnel drilling before final cruciate graft tensioning, but finish PLC tensioning after the cruciate grafts are seated — this avoids the corner reconstruction fighting a knee that is not yet centered.
- Favor allograft (Achilles or tibialis tendon) for the PLC reconstruction when multiple simultaneous grafts are needed in a combined case — it avoids adding a third or fourth autograft donor site to an already lengthy combined procedure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Under-diagnosis is common because varus laxity can be masked by an intact ACL/PCL tensioning the knee in the exam room — always perform the dial test with side-to-side comparison and document findings at both 30° and 90° of flexion before finalizing the surgical plan.
- Peroneal nerve injury/palsy — pre-existing from the initial trauma or iatrogenic during reconstruction — is a recognized risk, and is more frequent with KD-IIIL-pattern injuries.
- Recurrent laxity/graft stretch is a risk given the corner's demanding biomechanical environment.
- Failing to address the PLC when it is injured alongside a cruciate ligament is the single most common cause of early failure of the cruciate reconstruction — the PLC must be treated in the same coordinated plan, not deferred.
- Tunnel convergence between the LCL/popliteus femoral tunnels and a concurrently placed ACL or PCL femoral tunnel is a specific technical risk in combined reconstructions; deliberate divergent tunnel angulation, planned preoperatively, is the primary mitigation.
- Overtensioning the LCL or popliteofibular limb can overconstrain external rotation or produce lateral compartment overload; always compare final tensioned motion and stability to the contralateral limb rather than tensioning to a fixed target in isolation.
- Missing an underlying varus malalignment in a chronic presentation predisposes to recurrent graft stretch/failure regardless of how technically sound the reconstruction itself is — this should be screened for and addressed (via staged or combined osteotomy) rather than discovered only after a failed reconstruction.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: strict non-weight-bearing or touch-down weight-bearing (protocol-dependent) in an extension brace to protect the repair/reconstruction from varus and rotational stress; gentle passive ROM avoiding terminal extension stress on the repair.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive weight-bearing, ROM advancement, gentle closed-chain strengthening avoiding varus loading.
- **Months 3–9**: progressive strengthening, agility, and sport-specific training.
- Return to cutting/pivoting sport typically not before 9–12 months, given the biomechanical demands on the reconstructed corner.
