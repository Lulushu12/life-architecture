## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- **ORIF:** Mason II fractures with a mechanical block to motion or displacement threatening stability; Mason III fractures with ≤3 major reconstructable fragments and no gross instability.
- **Radial head arthroplasty (RHA):** Mason III/IV fractures that are comminuted or non-reconstructable, especially with associated elbow instability (terrible triad, Essex-Lopresti, Monteggia-variant) — the radial head must function as a stabilizing strut in these patterns.
- **Radial head excision without replacement:** relatively contraindicated whenever there is associated LCL injury, coronoid fracture, or interosseous membrane/DRUJ injury (converts a stable injury into a chronically unstable one); reserved only for isolated, low-demand fractures with a confirmed intact LCL, coronoid, and IOM.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Mason I fractures (non/minimally displaced, no mechanical block) — treated non-operatively with sling and early AROM, not surgery.
- Radial head excision alone is contraindicated in any setting where associated instability (LCL, coronoid, or IOM/DRUJ injury) has not been actively excluded — do not resect "just to see" before this assessment.
- Active infection at the operative site — treat/eradicate before implant placement.
- RHA is relatively contraindicated when the fracture is genuinely reconstructable with ≤3 fragments and the elbow is otherwise stable — favor ORIF to preserve native anatomy when fixation is biomechanically reasonable.
- Severe pre-existing radiocapitellar or PRUJ arthrosis unrelated to the acute fracture may alter implant selection/expectations — discuss with the patient that outcomes may differ from an otherwise "clean" acute reconstruction.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP/lateral elbow radiographs; CT with 2D/3D reconstruction for comminuted radial head fractures to accurately count fragments and assess reconstructability before committing to ORIF vs. RHA — fragment count on plain film alone is frequently inaccurate compared to CT.
- **Associated-injury screen:** obtain a true AP wrist film with contralateral comparison (ulnar variance) whenever a radial head fracture is accompanied by any wrist symptoms, to exclude Essex-Lopresti before considering excision; assess elbow stability (varus/valgus stress, PLRI/pivot-shift apprehension) as part of preoperative planning, recognizing this is best formally tested under anesthesia intraoperatively rather than in the awake patient.
- **Implant selection (RHA):** have both monopolar and bipolar modular metal (titanium or cobalt-chrome) head options and a range of trial sizes available; plan to size against the resected native head fragments (reassembled on the back table if possible) rather than guessing from preoperative imaging alone.
- **Equipment checklist:** small-fragment and mini-fragment sets, headless compression screws (2.0–2.7mm) for marginal ORIF fragments, low-profile plating options for larger fragments requiring buttressing, radial head arthroplasty trial and implant system with multiple stem/head size combinations, pointed reduction clamps, mini C-arm, tourniquet.
- **Case-specific planning:** if part of a terrible triad or Monteggia-variant presentation, plan the radial head step within the broader sequence (coronoid before radial head in terrible triad); consent the patient for possible intraoperative conversion from planned ORIF to RHA if fragments prove less reconstructable than anticipated on imaging.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or regional (supraclavicular/axillary brachial plexus) block; general anesthesia is favored when combined instability testing (varus/valgus stress, pivot-shift) is planned as part of a broader elbow fracture-dislocation workup, since full muscular relaxation gives a more reliable exam.
- **Position:** supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, shoulder abducted, elbow flexed to 90° and forearm pronated to protect the PIN during the lateral approach; the arm should be freely mobile to allow intraoperative pronation/supination during exposure and final rotational testing.
- **Tourniquet:** pneumatic tourniquet on the upper arm, exsanguinate, inflate to standard limb pressure; document tourniquet time.
- **Fluoroscopy:** mini C-arm draped into the field, used to confirm fracture reduction/screw position (outside the "safe zone" arc), implant seating and sizing for RHA, and final concentric radiocapitellar and ulnohumeral reduction with a full passive flexion-extension arc.
- **Draping:** standard extremity prep from above the tourniquet to the fingertips, circumferential drape allowing free forearm pronation/supination for both the approach and the final stability check.
- **Landmarks marked:** palpate and mark the lateral epicondyle, olecranon, and the interval between anconeus and ECU (Kocher) before incision; mark the radial styloid-to-Lister's-tubercle line on the skin as a rough external reference for the "safe zone" arc if helpful.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a lateral incision centered over the **Kocher interval** (between anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris), extending from just distal to the lateral epicondyle toward the radial neck.
2. **Pronate the forearm** before deepening the dissection anterior to the radiocapitellar joint — this is the single most important maneuver protecting the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN), which lies closer to the joint in supination; the PIN is at particular risk if dissection strays anterior/proximal to the radiocapitellar joint.
3. Develop the interval, incise the annular ligament/lateral capsule longitudinally (preserving as much of the LCL origin as possible if it is intact), and expose the radial head and neck; if an EDC-splitting approach is used instead (less capsular disruption but slightly less extensile for combined coronoid work), develop the interval through the extensor digitorum communis muscle belly itself.
4. Deliver the fractured radial head fragments; assess fragment number and reconstructability directly (CT-based preoperative planning informs but does not replace direct intraoperative assessment).

**If proceeding with ORIF (≤3 major fragments, reconstructable):**

5. Reduce fragments anatomically using fine pointed clamps and provisional K-wire fixation.
6. Place fixation in the **"safe zone"** — the roughly 110° non-articulating arc of the radial head, identified with the forearm in neutral rotation as the arc extending from the radial styloid to the Lister's tubercle line — to avoid impingement on the proximal radioulnar joint (PRUJ) during pronosupination; use headless compression screws buried beneath the articular cartilage for marginal/rim fragments, or a low-profile mini-plate placed within the safe zone for larger buttressing needs.
7. Take the forearm through a full passive pronation-supination arc under direct vision to confirm no hardware impingement on the PRUJ.

**If proceeding with RHA (comminuted/non-reconstructable, or instability-associated):**

5. Excise the comminuted fragments, preserving the radial neck for implant seating; measure neck diameter and resection level carefully.
6. Reassemble the excised native head fragments on the back table (if feasible) to use as a sizing template.
7. Ream/broach the radial canal per the implant system; select a trial stem and head, **erring toward the smaller trial when uncertain — trial down, not up**. Oversizing is a well-documented cause of capitellar erosion, stiffness, and pain from overstuffing the radiocapitellar and PRUJ joints.
8. With the trial in place, reduce the elbow and assess radiocapitellar and ulnohumeral congruity, joint space symmetry on fluoroscopy, and passive range of motion — a properly sized implant should not "jam" the joint or create palpable/visible overstuffing; adjust trial size/height accordingly before committing to the final implant.
9. Impact or cement the final implant per the system's design (press-fit vs. cemented stems both in common use; follow the manufacturer's specific technique), then reduce and re-confirm congruent tracking through a full arc.

**Radial head excision without replacement (rarely indicated):**

5. Confirm intact LCL, coronoid, and IOM before proceeding — actively test elbow and forearm stability intraoperatively; do not excise without having excluded Essex-Lopresti and coronoid/LCL injury.
6. Excise the fragments, contour the remaining neck, and reassess stability through a full arc before closing.

**Closure (all techniques):**

10. If the LCL/annular ligament was taken down for exposure, repair it back to its anatomic origin (suture anchor or transosseous tunnel) before closing — an unrepaired lateral-sided capsuloligamentous injury undermines the stability the radial head reconstruction is meant to preserve.
11. Irrigate, close the fascia/capsule, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers, and apply a well-padded posterior splint or hinged brace with the elbow at 90° flexion and forearm in neutral rotation.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Pronate the forearm before any dissection anterior to the radiocapitellar joint — this single positioning maneuver is the most effective way to protect the PIN throughout the Kocher approach.
- Reassemble excised comminuted fragments on the back table before choosing an RHA implant size — this gives a far more accurate sizing reference than eyeballing the resected specimen or relying on preoperative imaging alone.
- When in doubt about RHA sizing, trial down rather than up — an undersized implant is clinically far more forgiving than an overstuffed joint, which reliably causes capitellar erosion and stiffness.
- Confirm the "safe zone" for screw/plate placement using the forearm in neutral rotation and visualizing the arc from the radial styloid to the Lister's tubercle line — placing hardware outside this arc, even by a small margin, risks PRUJ impingement that only becomes apparent with forearm rotation.
- Always repair the lateral capsule/annular ligament (and LCL origin if disrupted) before closing, regardless of whether the radial head was fixed, replaced, or (rarely) excised — this is easy to omit once the bony work is complete but is essential to overall lateral-sided stability.
- Take the forearm through a genuinely full pronation-supination arc intraoperatively (not just a partial check) before closing — impingement can be subtle at mid-arc and easy to miss with a cursory check.
- If a Mason III fracture proves less reconstructable than anticipated once directly visualized, do not persist with a marginal ORIF — converting intraoperatively to RHA is preferable to a construct with inadequate fixation strength, particularly in an unstable elbow.
- In combined terrible-triad or Monteggia-variant cases, treat the radial head step within the broader sequence rather than in isolation — confirm coronoid fixation (if applicable) is secure before finalizing radial head reconstruction, since the two structures are assessed together for overall stability.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Screws/plates placed outside the "safe zone" impinge on the PRUJ during forearm rotation.
- Oversized RHA implants cause capitellar erosion, stiffness, and pain — trial down, not up, when sizing is uncertain.
- Do not resect the radial head without excluding associated LCL, coronoid, or interosseous membrane/DRUJ injury (Essex-Lopresti) — resection in the presence of these converts a stable injury into a chronically unstable one.
- PIN injury risk during the Kocher approach if dissection strays anterior/proximal to the radiocapitellar joint — pronating the forearm protects the nerve.
- Meta-analyses in Mason III fractures show ORIF trends toward more reoperations/hardware removal and worse rotation in some series, while RHA trends toward more reliable early stability in comminuted/instability-associated patterns — factor this into shared decision-making, not just fragment count.
- Long-term (5–14 year) follow-up data show no significant difference in most functional scores between RHA and ORIF overall, but better pronation with RHA and worse functional outcomes when ORIF later requires secondary resection/removal — supports a low threshold to choose RHA when reconstruction is marginal rather than persisting with borderline fixation.
- Failure to repair the lateral capsule/annular ligament after exposure undermines lateral-sided stability even if the bony radial head work is technically excellent.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–1 week:** sling for comfort, begin AROM within the first few days.
- **1–6 weeks:** progressive AROM including forearm rotation; avoid resisted supination against the healing/implant construct in the first 6 weeks.
- **6–12 weeks:** strengthening once healed/implant stable; return to full activity by 3 months in most patients.
