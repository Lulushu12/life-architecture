## Indications

Massive irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears in younger, active patients with preserved anterior cuff (subscapularis), particularly when external rotation weakness (positive Hornblower's/external rotation lag sign) is prominent. Latissimus dorsi transfer is added for combined posterosuperior loss when broader coverage is needed. Growing preference for LTT over superior capsular reconstruction in centers with transfer expertise, given comparative evidence that LTT restores deltoid function and dynamic shoulder stability more effectively than SCR in this population.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed in the source beyond the graft-bridging technique below and the general open-exposure principles it shares with other reconstructive cuff procedures.

## Steps

1. **Harvest/mobilize the lower trapezius tendon**, taking care during exposure to protect the spinal accessory nerve and dorsal scapular neurovascular bundle — meticulous exposure is required, as this is the procedure's principal structure-at-risk.
2. **Use a tendon graft (Achilles allograft or similar) to bridge** from the lower trapezius to the infraspinatus footprint.
3. **Respect the natural line of pull** of the lower trapezius when routing and tensioning the graft, so the transfer functions as a dynamic external rotation force couple rather than a static restraint.
4. **Add latissimus dorsi transfer** when the posterosuperior loss is combined/more extensive and a single transfer is judged insufficient to restore the force couple.

## Pitfalls

- Injury to the spinal accessory nerve or dorsal scapular neurovascular bundle during harvest is the key technical risk — meticulous exposure is required.
- LTT provides a dynamic external rotation force couple, which is mechanistically different from SCR's static superior restraint — patient selection (prominent external rotation weakness/Hornblower sign) should reflect which mechanism is actually needed.
- A 2025 systematic review comparing arthroscopic-assisted lower trapezius transfer with SCR (including its use to augment partial repairs) found both improve outcomes but with variability by technique and graft choice — individualize selection rather than applying a one-size-fits-all salvage algorithm.
- Deltoid dysfunction (temporary or, rarely, permanent weakness) can follow aggressive deltoid splitting/retraction during the open approach — favor muscle-sparing exposure where feasible.

## Post-op protocol

- Grouped with other massive-tear reconstructions: typically 6 weeks of strict immobilization, with an abduction brace commonly used specifically for tendon transfers to protect the repair in a position of reduced tension.
- Passive ROM introduced gradually from week 4–6.
- Active ROM from ~8–12 weeks.
- Strengthening from 3–4 months.
- Full recovery/plateau often 9–12 months, given transfer incorporation timelines.
