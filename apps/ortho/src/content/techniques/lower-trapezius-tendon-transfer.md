## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Massive irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tears in younger, active patients with preserved anterior cuff (subscapularis).
- Particularly indicated when external rotation weakness (positive Hornblower's sign/external rotation lag sign) is prominent — LTT provides a dynamic external rotation force couple, addressing this mechanism more directly than a static restraint procedure.
- Growing preference for LTT over superior capsular reconstruction in centers with transfer expertise, given comparative evidence that LTT restores deltoid function and dynamic shoulder stability more effectively than SCR in this population.
- Latissimus dorsi transfer added for combined posterosuperior loss when broader coverage is needed and a single transfer is judged insufficient to restore the force couple.
- Failed prior massive cuff tear surgery (failed repair, failed SCR) with preserved deltoid and glenohumeral joint, in a patient not yet a reverse arthroplasty candidate.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Glenohumeral arthritis or cuff tear arthropathy with joint incongruity — favor reverse total shoulder arthroplasty instead.
- True pseudoparalysis with advanced fatty infiltration of the deltoid or a non-functioning deltoid — the transfer depends on deltoid function for its lever arm and will not succeed if the deltoid itself is compromised.
- Subscapularis deficiency (irreparable anterior cuff loss) — anterior instability from subscapularis insufficiency is not addressed by a posterosuperior transfer and undermines the construct.
- Axillary or spinal accessory nerve palsy — both are functionally relevant to this procedure (deltoid innervation and trapezius innervation respectively) and must be intact preoperatively.
- Active infection, or a patient unable to tolerate the extended immobilization and rehabilitation timeline (this is a slow-incorporating tendon transfer, not a quick recovery).
- Older, lower-demand patients for whom a partial repair/debridement or rTSA is a more appropriate risk-benefit choice.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to confirm tear pattern (posterosuperior, irreparable per Goutallier/Patte/tangent sign), assess subscapularis integrity (must be intact or repairable), and assess deltoid and trapezius muscle quality; plain radiographs to confirm absence of significant glenohumeral arthritis or joint incongruity.
- **Neurologic confirmation**: clinically confirm intact spinal accessory nerve function (trapezius shrug strength) and axillary nerve function (deltoid contraction) before committing to the transfer.
- **Graft selection**: Achilles tendon allograft is commonly used to bridge from the lower trapezius to the infraspinatus footprint; alternatives described in the literature include hamstring (semitendinosus) autograft or a quadricipital tendon allograft with a double-row construct — select based on surgeon experience and graft availability, sizing to bridge the gap without excessive tension.
- **Approach planning**: decide between a fully open two-incision technique versus an arthroscopic-assisted technique (arthroscopic preparation of the greater tuberosity/infraspinatus footprint and graft fixation, combined with an open posterior incision for trapezius harvest) — arthroscopic-assisted approaches reduce deltoid morbidity and are increasingly favored where surgeon expertise allows.
- **Equipment**: standard arthroscopy tower if performing the arthroscopic-assisted variant, open harvest instrumentation (retractors, nerve stimulator/monitoring if desired near the spinal accessory nerve), double-row anchor set for tuberosity fixation, and Pulver-Taft suture technique instruments for tendon-to-tendon fixation of the graft to the trapezius.
- **Concomitant procedures**: confirm whether latissimus dorsi transfer will be added, and whether any residual repairable cuff tissue will be incorporated into the construct (partial repair augmented by transfer).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with an interscalene regional block for postoperative analgesia; discuss with anesthesia whether intraoperative neuromonitoring is desired given the proximity of dissection to the spinal accessory nerve.
- **Position**: lateral decubitus position is generally preferred, as it provides simultaneous access to the posterior scapular border/medial incision for trapezius harvest and to the lateral shoulder for arthroscopic or open work at the greater tuberosity; beach-chair is an alternative for surgeons who prefer it for the arthroscopic portion, with the patient repositioned or the table adjusted for posterior exposure.
- **Table setup**: axillary roll under the down side, arm in a mechanical holder or balanced traction for the arthroscopic portion, with the ability to reposition/flex the table to improve access to the medial scapular border for the trapezius harvest incision.
- **Draping**: wide exposure encompassing the entire posterior scapula (medial border to lateral border), the shoulder girdle, and the proximal-to-mid humerus, to allow the two-incision approach (medial harvest incision, lateral transacromial/deltoid-splitting or arthroscopic-portal incision) and the deep tunnel connecting them.
- **No routine tourniquet**; fluoroscopy is not routinely required but may be available if bony landmarks need confirmation during tuberosity preparation.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: medial scapular border, scapular spine, acromion, and the planned posterior (medial) harvest incision and lateral transacromial or arthroscopic portal sites.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm irreparability and subscapularis integrity** on diagnostic arthroscopy before proceeding to transfer — if the posterosuperior cuff proves unexpectedly repairable, revert to primary repair.
2. **Prepare the greater tuberosity/infraspinatus footprint** arthroscopically or through the lateral incision: debride to a bleeding bed as for a standard cuff repair, and place double-row anchors in the standard transosseous-equivalent configuration to receive the graft.
3. **Make the posterior (medial) harvest incision** along the medial border of the scapula to expose the trapezius; identify the interval between the middle and lower trapezius fibers.
4. **Harvest the lower trapezius tendon**: superficial dissection can safely separate the muscle fibers of the middle and lower trapezius, but deep dissection must proceed cautiously — identify and protect the spinal accessory nerve, which runs on the deep surface of the trapezius, throughout the harvest.
5. **Mobilize the lower trapezius muscle-tendon unit** sufficiently to allow it to reach the lateral incision/tunnel without excessive tension, preserving its vascular pedicle and the dorsal scapular neurovascular bundle running near the medial scapular border.
6. **Create a deep tunnel connecting the medial and lateral incisions** (deep to the deltoid, superficial to the scapular spine/infraspinatus), through which the graft will be passed — this is the two-incision technique's defining step and must be developed bluntly and carefully to avoid injury to structures along its course.
7. **Attach the tendon graft (Achilles allograft or equivalent) to the harvested lower trapezius tendon** using a Pulver-Taft weave or equivalent tendon-to-tendon suture technique, creating a single composite unit long enough to bridge to the greater tuberosity/infraspinatus footprint.
8. **Pass the composite graft through the deep tunnel** from the medial incision to the lateral incision/arthroscopic field.
9. **Fix the distal end of the graft to the greater tuberosity/infraspinatus footprint** using the previously placed double-row anchors in a knotless transosseous-equivalent construct, compressing the graft onto the prepared bone.
10. **Respect the natural line of pull** of the lower trapezius when routing and tensioning the graft — the transfer should be tensioned with the arm in a position that allows it to function as a dynamic external rotation force couple (typically with the arm at the side or in slight abduction and external rotation) rather than as a static restraint; over- or under-tensioning relative to this line of pull undermines the intended biomechanics.
11. **Add latissimus dorsi transfer** through the same or an adjacent approach when the posterosuperior loss is combined/more extensive and a single transfer is judged insufficient to restore the force couple.
12. **Confirm construct integrity**: cycle the shoulder through gentle internal/external rotation to confirm the transfer glides appropriately through the tunnel without impingement and that fixation at both the trapezius-graft junction and the tuberosity is secure.
13. **Close in layers**: close the medial harvest incision over the trapezius defect, close the lateral incision/portals, and apply a sterile dressing followed by an abduction brace to protect the transfer in a position of reduced tension.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify the spinal accessory nerve early in the harvest, before deep dissection proceeds — it is far easier to protect a structure you have already visualized than to avoid one you are hoping is not in the way.
- Favor an arthroscopic-assisted approach for the tuberosity preparation and graft fixation where expertise allows — it reduces deltoid splitting/retraction morbidity compared with a fully open lateral exposure.
- Use a nerve stimulator or intraoperative neuromonitoring near the spinal accessory nerve if available, particularly early in a surgeon's experience with this transfer.
- Size the composite graft (trapezius tendon plus allograft extension) generously before final fixation — a graft that is too short creates excessive tension across the transfer and risks early attritional failure or loss of the intended force-couple mechanics.
- Tension the graft with the arm positioned to reflect its intended dynamic function (a position of slight external rotation) rather than tensioning it in a neutral or internally rotated position, which would set the transfer too loose to generate meaningful external rotation torque.
- Reported outcomes with meticulous technique are favorable — more than 90% of patients achieve lasting pain relief and restored external rotation, with range of motion commonly restored to 80–100% — set expectations accordingly while still counseling on the technical demands and recovery timeline.
- Develop the deep tunnel bluntly and progressively, confirming a clear path before passing the graft, rather than forcing the graft through an inadequately developed tunnel.
- Protect the dorsal scapular neurovascular bundle along the medial scapular border during harvest — it runs close to the surgical field and is a second, less commonly discussed structure at risk alongside the spinal accessory nerve.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Injury to the spinal accessory nerve or dorsal scapular neurovascular bundle during harvest is the key technical risk — meticulous exposure is required.
- LTT provides a dynamic external rotation force couple, which is mechanistically different from SCR's static superior restraint — patient selection (prominent external rotation weakness/Hornblower sign) should reflect which mechanism is actually needed.
- A 2025 systematic review comparing arthroscopic-assisted lower trapezius transfer with SCR (including its use to augment partial repairs) found both improve outcomes but with variability by technique and graft choice — individualize selection rather than applying a one-size-fits-all salvage algorithm.
- Deltoid dysfunction (temporary or, rarely, permanent weakness) can follow aggressive deltoid splitting/retraction during the open approach — favor muscle-sparing/arthroscopic-assisted exposure where feasible.
- Under-tensioning the composite graft, or tensioning it in a position that does not reflect its intended line of pull, undermines the dynamic force-couple mechanism the transfer is meant to restore — this is a distinct failure mode from simple fixation failure.
- Inadequate tunnel development before graft passage risks graft abrasion or an incompletely delivered transfer — develop the tunnel deliberately rather than forcing passage.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Grouped with other massive-tear reconstructions: typically 6 weeks of strict immobilization, with an abduction brace commonly used specifically for tendon transfers to protect the repair in a position of reduced tension.
- Passive ROM introduced gradually from week 4–6.
- Active ROM from ~8–12 weeks.
- Strengthening from 3–4 months.
- Full recovery/plateau often 9–12 months, given transfer incorporation timelines.
