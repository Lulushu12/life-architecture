## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Acute or chronic displaced anteroinferior glenoid rim fracture (bony Bankart) that is large enough to be biomechanically relevant — generally more than ~10–15% of the glenoid articular arc — and reducible to an anatomic position.
- A bony Bankart converts a purely labral (soft-tissue) problem into a bone-loss problem: the fracture fragment carries the anteroinferior capsulolabral attachment with it, so fixation restores both the glenoid arc and the soft-tissue tension simultaneously if the fragment is viable.
- Acute fractures (within a few weeks of injury) generally fix more reliably than chronic, resorbed fragments — earlier surgery is favored once the fragment is confirmed reducible.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Comminuted fragments that cannot be reduced to a stable, congruent construct — attempting fixation of non-reconstructable bone risks early failure and wastes the index surgical opportunity.
- Chronic, resorbed, or medialized fragments with insufficient bone stock to achieve fixation purchase — these behave as glenoid bone loss and should be treated as such (Latarjet/bone graft) rather than pursued for fixation.
- Fragment involving less than ~10–15% of the glenoid arc in an otherwise stable joint may be treated as a soft-tissue Bankart (excise the non-viable sliver and repair the capsulolabral tissue directly to the glenoid rim) rather than committing to formal bony fixation.
- Active infection or poor soft-tissue envelope at the planned surgical site (rare, mainly relevant in high-energy fracture-dislocations with associated soft-tissue injury).

## Preoperative Planning

- **CT with 3D reconstruction (with the humeral head digitally subtracted)** is mandatory, not optional — it defines fragment size as a percentage of the glenoid arc, comminution, degree of displacement/medialization, and any associated Hill-Sachs lesion. This single study determines whether the case proceeds as a fixation or is converted preoperatively to a bone-block plan.
- Assess fragment **viability and reducibility** on CT: a single large fragment with an identifiable fracture plane favors fixation; multiple small fragments or a "eroded/rounded" appearance favors treating the case as chronic glenoid bone loss.
- MR arthrogram if the capsulolabral extent of injury and any concomitant lesions (SLAP, posterior extension, HAGL) need better soft-tissue definition, though CT remains the primary planning study for the bony decision.
- **Implant planning**: have both a suture-anchor set (for smaller/simple fragments where the fragment is fixed indirectly through capsulolabral suture-anchor repair) and a screw or suture-button system (for larger fragments requiring direct bony compression) available — the intraoperative fragment appearance, not just the preoperative CT estimate, often determines the final construct choice.
- Consent should cover conversion to Latarjet or other bone-graft reconstruction if the fragment proves non-viable or excessively comminuted on direct arthroscopic inspection.
- Plan for fluoroscopy availability if screw fixation is anticipated, to confirm screw length/trajectory and avoid articular penetration.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically with an interscalene regional block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning**: beach chair or lateral decubitus with traction, per surgeon preference — beach chair is generally favored when there is a realistic possibility of intraoperative conversion to an open procedure (e.g., unexpected comminution requiring open reduction or bone grafting).
- Pad all bony prominences; secure the head and maintain neutral cervical alignment.
- Prep and drape to allow both arthroscopic portal access and conversion to a deltopectoral approach without re-prepping.
- Mark standard shoulder arthroscopy portal sites (posterior, anterosuperolateral, anteroinferior) and, if screw fixation is planned, mark a percutaneous or mini-open anterior working portal trajectory that allows a perpendicular approach to the fragment.
- Have **fluoroscopy draped into the field** if screw fixation is planned, positioned to obtain an axillary-type view intraoperatively.
- Perform an exam under anesthesia to confirm the direction and degree of instability before starting.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the plan with fresh review of CT** (fragment size, comminution, healing potential) immediately before incision — do not proceed on memory of the imaging read days earlier.
2. **Diagnostic arthroscopy.** Establish standard posterior, anterosuperolateral, and anteroinferior portals. Perform a circumferential survey including the axillary pouch (exclude HAGL) and assess the rotator cuff and biceps/SLAP complex.
3. **Directly inspect the fragment.** Identify the bony Bankart fragment, its size relative to the glenoid arc, degree of displacement, and soft-tissue attachment (the capsulolabral sleeve should remain attached to the fragment — this is what allows combined bony and soft-tissue restoration). Confirm comminution status directly, since intraoperative appearance sometimes differs from CT estimation.
4. **Mobilize the fragment and its soft-tissue sleeve** off the glenoid neck and scapula, exactly as for a soft-tissue Bankart, preserving the capsulolabral attachment to the bone fragment.
5. **Prepare the glenoid neck bed.** Lightly decorticate the native glenoid neck bed to bleeding bone where the fragment will be reduced, to promote union.
6. **Reduce the fragment anatomically** to the glenoid rim defect using a probe or dedicated reduction instrument, restoring the native glenoid arc and articular contour.
7. **Fix the fragment.**
   - *Small-to-moderate, non-comminuted fragment*: place suture anchors along the glenoid face incorporating both the fragment and the surrounding capsulolabral tissue in the same passes used for a standard Bankart repair, effectively "suturing the bone home" with the soft tissue.
   - *Larger or more displaced fragment*: use direct fragment fixation — a suture-button construct passed through drill holes in the fragment and glenoid neck, or percutaneous/mini-open cannulated screw fixation directed perpendicular to the fracture plane, under fluoroscopic guidance to confirm appropriate length and to avoid articular penetration.
8. **Provisional fixation check.** After initial fixation, probe the fragment for stability and confirm restoration of a smooth, congruent articular arc under direct arthroscopic visualization.
9. **Abandon fixation if the fragment proves non-viable.** If, despite preoperative planning, the fragment is found to be too comminuted or resorbed to achieve stable fixation, excise the non-viable fragment(s), repair the soft-tissue sleeve directly to the glenoid rim as a standard Bankart repair, and counsel that the case has effectively converted to a bone-loss problem — plan a staged or same-setting bone-block procedure as appropriate.
10. **Address concomitant pathology** (SLAP, posterior labral extension) identified on diagnostic arthroscopy in the same setting.
11. **Final inspection.** Confirm fixation stability, congruent reduction, and full passive ROM without impingement of hardware or excessive soft-tissue tension.
12. **Closure.** Evacuate the joint, remove instruments, close portals (and any open incision if converted), and place the arm in a sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Bring the actual CT images (not just the report) into the OR for reference — fragment morphology and the fracture plane orientation directly guide screw/anchor trajectory.
- Preserve the capsulolabral soft-tissue attachment to the bone fragment during mobilization whenever possible — this "soft-tissue hinge" both aids reduction and adds a biological healing surface alongside the bony one.
- Lightly decorticate the glenoid neck bed before reduction; a fibrous, non-bleeding interface is a common reason for eventual fibrous non-union even with adequate mechanical fixation.
- Have both a suture-anchor and a screw/suture-button system opened and available before starting — fragment size and comminution are sometimes only fully appreciated once the fragment is directly visualized arthroscopically, and switching strategies mid-case should not be limited by instrument availability.
- When using screws, confirm trajectory and length with intraoperative fluoroscopy before final tightening — articular penetration is both a technical failure and a source of chondral damage to the humeral head.
- Reassess the glenoid arc after fixation by direct arthroscopic palpation/visualization, not just by the "feel" of a reduced fragment — a step-off or persistent gap predicts poor healing even if the fragment feels stable to a probe.
- Set a low threshold to convert to excision-and-soft-tissue-repair (or staged bone block) if fixation quality is marginal — a poorly fixed bony Bankart is worse than a well-executed soft-tissue repair of an excised fragment in a borderline-size defect.
- As with any instability arthroscopy, do a full circumferential survey (including the axillary pouch) before committing to the bony fixation plan — a missed HAGL or posterior extension will undermine an otherwise technically sound bony repair.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Attempting fixation of a comminuted or resorbed fragment that cannot achieve stable biologic healing; recognize this early intraoperatively and convert to a bone-block strategy instead rather than persisting with an unstable construct.
- Underestimating fragment size or missing comminution without CT — CT is mandatory, not optional, before committing to a fixation strategy.
- Inadequate decortication of the glenoid neck bed predisposes to fibrous non-union even when mechanical fixation appears adequate.
- Screw malposition or excessive length risks articular penetration and humeral head chondral injury; confirm with fluoroscopy before final seating.
- As with soft-tissue Bankart repair, failing to address concomitant lesions (SLAP, posterior labral extension, HAGL) found on diagnostic arthroscopy risks persistent instability even with a technically successful bony fixation.
- Recurrent instability after fixation failure or fragment resorption effectively recreates a glenoid bone-loss scenario — recognize this on follow-up imaging promptly rather than attributing recurrent symptoms solely to rehab non-compliance.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not detail a rehabilitation protocol specific to bony Bankart fixation. General return-to-sport criteria described for instability surgery apply across procedures and are not purely time-based: full painless active and passive ROM within 5–10° of the contralateral side, rotator cuff/periscapular strength ≥90% of the contralateral limb, negative apprehension/relocation testing, and successful sport-specific functional progression before clearance to contact/collision sport. Given the bony-healing component, obtain interval radiographs (or CT if union is uncertain clinically) to confirm fragment healing before progressing to unrestricted loading and contact clearance, analogous to the union-gated timeline used after Latarjet.
