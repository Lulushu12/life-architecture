## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Herring lateral pillar B (or the B/C border) in a child ≥8 years at onset — the strongest, most consistent indication; multiple natural-history cohorts show containment surgery improves the odds of a spherical/congruent (Stulberg I–II) outcome versus non-operative management in this group.
- Selected younger lateral pillar C patients, on surgeon judgment, despite genuinely debated benefit in this subgroup.
- "Head-at-risk" features on exam/imaging (Catterall signs: Gage sign, lateral calcification, lateral subluxation, horizontal physis, metaphyseal cysts) in a hip otherwise borderline for surgery.

**Contraindications / not indicated**
- Lateral pillar A hips — do well regardless of treatment and are not offered surgery.
- Late reossification-stage or already-healed disease with a fixed, incongruent head shape — true containment surgery has diminishing structural benefit here; consider salvage options instead (see below).
- Established lateral head extrusion/hinge abduction beyond the window for true containment — these patients are better served by salvage procedures (shelf acetabuloplasty, Chiari osteotomy) than by standard containment.
- Active synovitis/acute irritability should generally be settled before elective containment surgery where timing allows, though this must be balanced against the narrowing window during fragmentation.

## Preoperative Planning

- Stage the disease with AP and frog-leg lateral pelvis radiographs (Waldenström stage) and grade prognosis with the Herring lateral pillar classification on films taken during early fragmentation.
- Assess femoral head sphericity and dynamic containment with fluoroscopic arthrography (or MR arthrography) preoperatively where available — this is the key study that differentiates a coverage problem correctable by femoral redirection from one requiring acetabular redirection/reshaping.
- Plan and template the femoral varus derotation osteotomy angle if selected (target neck-shaft angle no lower than ~110–120°, avoiding over-correction), and select fixation (pediatric blade plate or locking proximal femoral plate).
- If a pelvic osteotomy is planned, decide between Salter (single-plane redirection, requires open triradiate cartilage and a spherical, congruent head) and Pemberton (incomplete, triradiate-hinged reshaping, tolerates more head deformity) based on preoperative arthrographic assessment.
- For salvage cases, plan shelf acetabuloplasty graft source (iliac crest autograft) or Chiari osteotomy displacement based on the degree of lateral extrusion.
- Have fluoroscopy, arthrogram contrast, and the full range of pediatric pelvic/proximal femoral osteotomy instrumentation available, since the final choice of procedure is often confirmed only after intraoperative dynamic arthrography.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia; consider regional/caudal block adjunct.
- Supine on a radiolucent table, with the operative hemipelvis elevated slightly on a bump if a combined femoral/pelvic procedure is anticipated; entire limb and hemipelvis (including iliac crest if pelvic osteotomy or graft harvest is planned) prepped free.
- Position the C-arm for AP pelvis views with the ability to obtain arthrographic images dynamically (abduction/adduction stress views) before committing to the final procedure.
- Mark the ASIS, iliac crest, greater trochanter, and planned incision(s) — lateral for femoral osteotomy, iliac crest/bikini-line for pelvic osteotomy — before prepping.
- Have the arthrography needle and contrast set up and ready before incision, since intraoperative dynamic arthrography under fluoroscopy is typically performed first to finalize the surgical plan.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Perform dynamic fluoroscopic arthrography** first, with the hip taken through abduction/internal rotation, to directly assess femoral head sphericity, coverage, and hinge abduction — this determines whether femoral, acetabular, or combined correction will best contain the head, and should override a purely radiograph-based preoperative plan if the two disagree.
2. **If femoral varus derotation osteotomy is selected**:
   a. Expose the subtrochanteric femur through a lateral approach, splitting/elevating vastus lateralis subperiosteally.
   b. Place rotational reference pins (e.g. one at the tip of the greater trochanter, one distal) to control the derotation component.
   c. Prepare and seat a proximal femoral blade or locking plate along the femoral neck axis under fluoroscopic guidance (confirm on both AP and lateral views before full seating).
   d. Perform the subtrochanteric closing-wedge osteotomy, sized to redirect the anterolateral epiphysis into the acetabular socket while targeting a neck-shaft angle no lower than approximately 110–120°.
   e. Reduce and fix the distal fragment to the plate, confirming corrected neck-shaft angle and rotation on fluoroscopy before final fixation.
3. **If Salter or Pemberton acetabular osteotomy is selected**:
   a. Expose the ilium through an anterior (Smith-Petersen-type) approach, elevating the abductors subperiosteally from the outer table (and inner table for Salter).
   b. For **Salter**: perform a complete osteotomy above the acetabulum, hinge the distal (acetabular) fragment anteriorly/inferiorly at the pubic symphysis, and hold the correction open with an iliac crest bone graft wedge, fixed with pins/wires as needed.
   c. For **Pemberton**: perform an incomplete osteotomy through the outer table, curving down to but not through the triradiate cartilage, and hinge the acetabular roof down and forward over the femoral head, holding the correction with the interposed bone graft (inherently stable at the triradiate hinge, generally requiring no additional fixation).
4. **For combined procedures**, perform both the femoral and acetabular components as above through separate incisions/exposures within the same anesthetic, confirming that each addresses a genuine component of the coverage deficit (avoid over-treating with both if arthrography shows one alone would achieve adequate containment).
5. **For salvage procedures** (reserved for older children/adolescents with established lateral extrusion/hinge abduction beyond the window for true containment):
   - **Shelf acetabuloplasty**: augment lateral coverage with an extra-articular bone graft buttress placed above the deficient lateral acetabular rim.
   - **Chiari osteotomy**: a medializing displacement osteotomy of the ilium above the acetabulum, using the capsule as an interposed pseudo-articular surface to increase coverage in a hip too deformed for redirection alone.
6. **Confirm final containment dynamically on fluoroscopy** (with arthrography if used) through a range of abduction/rotation before closure.
7. **Close in layers** — reattach the abductor origin/iliac apophysis as relevant, fascia, subcutaneous tissue, skin.
8. **Apply postoperative immobilization** appropriate to the construct — spica cast in younger children (especially after combined osteotomy) or brace/crutch protection in older children after an isolated, rigidly fixed femoral osteotomy.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Let intraoperative dynamic arthrography, not the preoperative radiograph alone, make the final call between femoral, acetabular, or combined containment — head shape and coverage often look different under live fluoroscopic stress than on static films.
- Use twin rotational reference pins for the femoral osteotomy component just as in an isolated derotation osteotomy — this avoids under- or over-rotating the head into the socket.
- Undercorrect varus slightly rather than aggressively chasing maximal coverage — a neck-shaft angle target of no lower than ~110–120° balances containment against creating an iatrogenic abductor lever-arm deficiency.
- When choosing between Salter and Pemberton, remember Salter requires a reasonably spherical, congruent head (it redirects an already-round socket), while Pemberton tolerates more head irregularity because it reshapes the roof directly over whatever head shape is present.
- Size the Pemberton or Salter graft wedge generously and impact it securely — an undersized or loosely seated graft risks loss of correction as the osteotomy settles, particularly in Pemberton where the graft alone provides stability.
- Time surgery to precede or fall early within the fragmentation stage whenever the diagnosis and referral timeline allow — this is the single biggest modifiable factor in whether containment surgery can still influence final head shape.
- In combined procedures, resist doing "more surgery" than the arthrogram demonstrates is needed — a well-executed single-component procedure that fully addresses the demonstrated deficit is preferable to routinely combining femoral and acetabular osteotomies.
- For salvage cases, counsel families explicitly that shelf/Chiari procedures aim to improve coverage and symptoms rather than restore true containment-stage remodeling potential — expectations should be set accordingly.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Over-correcting into excessive varus creates an iatrogenic abductor lever-arm deficiency and Trendelenburg gait that can be as functionally limiting as the disease itself; greater trochanteric overgrowth/apophysiodesis may later be needed to rebalance the abductor mechanism.
- Choose femoral versus pelvic versus combined containment based on dynamic fluoroscopic arthrography intraoperatively, not radiograph alone.
- Timing matters: a 2026 cohort examining containment surgery performed during the reossification stage found inferior radiographic remodeling compared with earlier (initial/fragmentation-stage) surgery.
- For late-onset Perthes (historically the worst-prognosis subgroup), femoral varus derotation osteotomy remains a mainstay option, with recent skeletal-maturity outcome data continuing to support meaningful radiographic benefit in appropriately selected cases.
- Lateral shelf acetabuloplasty has been specifically evaluated as a useful salvage option in older children when standard containment is no longer feasible.
- Undersized or poorly seated bone graft in Salter/Pemberton osteotomy risks loss of correction as the osteotomy settles postoperatively.
- Nonunion/delayed union at the femoral osteotomy site and hardware prominence requiring later removal are recognized, if uncommon, complications, as with any proximal femoral osteotomy.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: protected weight-bearing (touch-down or partial, per surgeon protocol and fixation construct) in a hip spica (younger children, especially after combined osteotomy) or brace/crutches (older children after isolated osteotomy); passive/active-assisted ROM emphasizing abduction and internal rotation once soft-tissue healing allows.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive weight-bearing as radiographic union of the osteotomy is confirmed; physiotherapy focused on hip abductor strengthening (critical given the abductor lever-arm changes after varus osteotomy) and restoring symmetric ROM.
- **Months 3–6**: full weight-bearing, return to low-impact activity; contact/pivoting sports typically deferred until osteotomy union is solid and abductor strength has normalized (often 4–6 months), with continued radiographic surveillance through the remaining fragmentation/reossification stages, since containment surgery changes head position but does not shorten the underlying disease course.
- **Long-term**: surveillance radiographs through skeletal maturity to determine final Stulberg class; patients with Stulberg III–V benefit from patient education regarding earlier-onset hip osteoarthritis risk and activity counseling, with periodic adult hip surveillance thereafter.
