## Indications

The guiding principle is to keep the softened, fragmenting femoral head well-covered by the acetabulum (acting as a mold) during the vulnerable fragmentation stage. The strongest, most consistent indication is Herring lateral pillar B (or the B/C border) in a child ≥8 years at onset, where multiple natural-history cohorts show containment surgery improves the odds of a spherical/congruent (Stulberg I–II) outcome versus non-operative management. Lateral pillar A hips do well regardless of treatment and are not offered surgery. Lateral pillar C hips have the worst prognosis and the benefit of surgical containment is genuinely debated, though many surgeons still offer it in younger lateral pillar C patients.

## Positioning & setup

Choice between femoral, acetabular, or combined containment is assessed dynamically under fluoroscopic arthrography intraoperatively (or preoperatively), which evaluates femoral head sphericity and containment/hinge-abduction — this directly informs the adequacy and type of containment chosen, rather than relying on static radiographs alone.

## Steps

1. **Assess containment dynamically under fluoroscopic arthrography** to decide between femoral, acetabular, or combined correction.
2. **Femoral varus derotation osteotomy** — redirects the anterolateral epiphysis into the acetabular socket; preferred when the deformity is primarily one of coverage correctable by redirecting the femoral head.
3. **Salter or Pemberton acetabular osteotomy** — redirects/reshapes the socket over the femoral head; favored when the acetabulum itself is dysplastic or shows lateral subluxation on arthrogram.
4. **Combined femoral + acetabular procedure** for more severe deformity/subluxation.
5. **Target neck-shaft angle no lower than ~110–120°** when performing the femoral osteotomy — avoid over-correcting into excessive varus.
6. **Time surgery before or early in the fragmentation stage**, before the head becomes fixed in a deformed, incongruent shape — late (reossification-stage) surgery shows diminishing structural benefit.
7. **Reserve salvage procedures** (shelf acetabuloplasty, Chiari osteotomy, or a combination) for older children/adolescents with established lateral head extrusion/hinge abduction beyond the window for true containment.

## Pitfalls

- Over-correcting into excessive varus creates an iatrogenic abductor lever-arm deficiency and Trendelenburg gait that can be as functionally limiting as the disease itself; greater trochanteric overgrowth/apophysiodesis may later be needed to rebalance the abductor mechanism.
- Choose femoral versus pelvic versus combined containment based on dynamic fluoroscopic arthrography intraoperatively, not radiograph alone.
- Timing matters: a 2026 cohort examining containment surgery performed during the reossification stage found inferior radiographic remodeling compared with earlier (initial/fragmentation-stage) surgery.
- For late-onset Perthes (historically the worst-prognosis subgroup), femoral varus derotation osteotomy remains a mainstay option, with recent skeletal-maturity outcome data continuing to support meaningful radiographic benefit in appropriately selected cases.
- Lateral shelf acetabuloplasty has been specifically evaluated as a useful salvage option in older children when standard containment is no longer feasible.

## Post-op protocol

- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing (touch-down or partial, per surgeon protocol and fixation construct) in a hip spica (younger children, especially after combined osteotomy) or brace/crutches (older children after isolated osteotomy); passive/active-assisted ROM emphasizing abduction and internal rotation once soft-tissue healing allows.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing as radiographic union of the osteotomy is confirmed; physiotherapy focused on hip abductor strengthening (critical given the abductor lever-arm changes after varus osteotomy) and restoring symmetric ROM.
- Months 3–6: full weight-bearing, return to low-impact activity; contact/pivoting sports typically deferred until osteotomy union is solid and abductor strength has normalized (often 4–6 months), with continued radiographic surveillance through the remaining fragmentation/reossification stages, since containment surgery changes head position but does not shorten the underlying disease course.
- Long-term: surveillance radiographs through skeletal maturity to determine final Stulberg class; patients with Stulberg III–V benefit from patient education regarding earlier-onset hip osteoarthritis risk and activity counseling, with periodic adult hip surveillance thereafter.
