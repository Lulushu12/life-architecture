## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Palmer 1B peripheral tears with DRUJ instability, or failed 3–6 months of conservative treatment; 1C/1D tears causing instability; intraoperative finding of TFCC disruption with persistent DRUJ instability after anatomic distal radius fracture (DRF)/ulnar styloid fixation. First-line management of acute peripheral tears is immobilization (wrist splint/cast) 4–6 weeks + NSAIDs, followed by structured hand therapy; surgical referral is reserved for those failing conservative management or with objective DRUJ instability.

**Contraindications**:
- Palmer 1A (central) tears — the central two-thirds to 80% of the disc is avascular and repair attempts here have historically poor healing rates; debride rather than repair.
- Degenerative (Palmer Class 2, ulnocarpal impaction spectrum) tears — a chronic overload pathway, not amenable to primary repair; managed with activity modification, ulnar shortening osteotomy, or debridement depending on stage, not covered by this acute-repair technique.
- Established DRUJ or radiocarpal osteoarthritis — repair will not address an arthritic joint and may be contraindicated in favor of salvage procedures.
- Active wrist infection — arthroscopy/open repair should wait until infection is cleared.
- Poor patient compliance potential for the postoperative rotational-restriction protocol — a repair that is not protected as prescribed is prone to failure, so this should factor into the shared decision to operate.
- Chronic, longstanding tears with substantially degenerated tissue quality — repair strength and healing potential decline with tear chronicity; assess tissue quality intraoperatively and be prepared to convert to debridement or a different fixation strategy if tissue will not hold sutures.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI arthrography is preferred over standard MRI when repair is being considered, given superior sensitivity for peripheral tears (approaching arthroscopy); confirm tear location (peripheral vs central vs radial) and foveal involvement pre-operatively where possible, since this determines arthroscopic versus open strategy.
- **Equipment/instrumentation for arthroscopic repair**: standard hand table with traction tower, 2.7 mm 30° arthroscope (a smaller-diameter scope than a knee arthroscope, appropriate for the radiocarpal joint size), wrist arthroscopy shaver/probe set, outside-in or inside-out suture-passing device (e.g., a spinal needle/suture-relay technique or a dedicated suture lasso device), non-absorbable suture (typically 2-0), and a mini fluoroscopy unit for concurrent DRF assessment if applicable.
- **Equipment for open repair**: standard hand instrument set, small suture anchors or a drill for transosseous tunnel creation at the fovea, non-absorbable suture.
- **Case-specific planning**: if this repair is being performed in conjunction with DRF ORIF, sequence the plan so the radius is anatomically fixed first, DRUJ stability is assessed, and TFCC repair (arthroscopic or open) is added only if instability persists — do not repair the TFCC before confirming the radius fixation has not already resolved the instability. Plan for possible concurrent chondral or ligamentous pathology to be addressed at the same arthroscopic sitting, since diagnostic arthroscopy commonly reveals findings beyond the TFCC alone.
- **Approach selection**: arthroscopic outside-in or inside-out repair for isolated Palmer 1B (peripheral) tears; open transosseous foveal reattachment for deep-fiber (foveal) disruption not adequately addressed by peripheral capsular repair, or for combined bony (ulnar styloid base) + ligamentous injury requiring direct fixation. Base the choice on tear pattern/foveal involvement and surgeon experience — 2024–2025 systematic reviews show broadly equivalent outcomes between open and arthroscopic repair, so neither should be assumed categorically superior.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or regional (axillary/supraclavicular block) anesthesia; regional block is well suited to wrist arthroscopy and provides good postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: supine on a standard hand table, arm abducted on an arm board, with the hand and wrist suspended in finger traps under longitudinal traction (typically 10–15 lb) via an overhead traction tower — this distracts the radiocarpal joint enough to permit safe instrument passage.
- **Tourniquet**: pneumatic upper-arm tourniquet, inflated for the arthroscopic/open portions of the case.
- **Portal/approach setup**: prep and drape the entire hand and forearm free, allowing full wrist and forearm rotation intraoperatively (essential for the pronation/supination DRUJ stress test and for positioning the forearm in supination during suture tying). Mark the 3-4 portal (just distal to Lister's tubercle, between the third and fourth extensor compartments) as the primary viewing portal, and the 6R portal (just radial to the ECU tendon) as the primary working/outflow portal — palpate and mark the ECU subsheath before establishing the 6R portal to avoid injury.
- **Fluoroscopy**: have mini fluoroscopy available intraoperatively, particularly when TFCC assessment/repair is combined with DRF fixation or DRUJ stress testing.
- **Landmarks to mark before incision**: Lister's tubercle, the 3-4 and 6R portal sites, the ECU tendon course, and (for open repair) the dorsal ulnar approach interval between the fifth and sixth extensor compartments.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Establish traction via finger traps on the traction tower with the wrist suspended (typically 10–15 lb longitudinal traction) to distract the radiocarpal joint.
2. Establish the 3-4 portal under direct palpation/landmark guidance and introduce the 2.7 mm arthroscope; perform diagnostic arthroscopy of the radiocarpal joint to characterize the TFCC tear (location, size, foveal involvement) and to identify any concomitant chondral, scapholunate, or lunotriquetral ligament pathology.
3. Establish the 6R portal under direct visualization, just radial to the ECU tendon, initially used as an outflow portal; introduce a probe or small shaver through it to palpate the tear and debride focal synovitis or redundant/frayed tissue at the tear edge to create a healthy bleeding bed for repair.
4. Perform the trampoline test (probe the TFCC disc and assess resistance/tension) and hook test (probe the peripheral rim and assess for translation) to confirm the tear is peripheral and repairable rather than centrally deficient.
5. For a Palmer 1B peripheral tear: pass a suture-relay device or spinal-needle-based suture through the 6R (or a dedicated dorsal) portal, directing it through the TFCC tissue adjacent to the tear and out through the capsule, using either an outside-in or inside-out technique per surgeon preference and available instrumentation.
6. Place a mattress-type stitch capturing healthy TFCC tissue on both sides of the tear, tensioning it to re-approximate the peripheral disc edge to the dorsal/volar capsule.
7. Release wrist traction before final knot tightening, and hold the forearm in supination while tying — sutures are tied sequentially (typically via a sliding knot technique) through the working portal under arthroscopic visualization, since releasing traction and positioning the forearm correctly affects final tension and DRUJ congruity.
8. Repeat steps 5–7 to place additional mattress sutures as needed (commonly 2–3) to achieve a secure, tension-appropriate repair along the length of the peripheral tear.
9. For deep-fiber (foveal) disruption not addressed by the peripheral repair, or combined bony/ligamentous injury: convert to (or proceed directly with) an open approach — a dorsal-ulnar incision between the fifth and sixth extensor compartments, protecting the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve; expose the fovea at the base of the ulnar head.
10. Create a transosseous tunnel (or use a suture anchor) at the fovea and pass suture through the TFCC's deep ligamentous fibers, then through the bone tunnel, tying over the ulnar cortex (or onto the anchor) to re-establish the foveal ligament insertion directly.
11. For 1A central tears identified during diagnostic arthroscopy: debride the frayed central tissue back to a stable rim using a shaver rather than attempting repair, given the avascular central zone.
12. After any TFCC repair, actively stress-test DRUJ stability under fluoroscopy in pronation, neutral, and supination to confirm the repair has restored stability before closing.
13. Close portal sites with simple sutures or Steri-Strips; close the open incision in layers if a formal open approach was used, with attention to protecting the dorsal sensory ulnar nerve branch and ECU subsheath.
14. Apply a well-padded splint or cast with the forearm positioned in the rotational arc found stable on final testing (commonly neutral-to-supination), consistent with any concurrent DRUJ fixation.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Perform the trampoline and hook tests before committing to a repair strategy — they distinguish a repairable peripheral tear from a centrally deficient one and guide whether debridement alone will suffice.
- Debride the tear edges and adjacent synovitis before passing sutures — a bleeding, healthy tissue bed improves healing potential and makes suture passage through the tissue more predictable.
- Release traction and hold the forearm in supination before final knot tying — tying sutures under traction with the forearm in an arbitrary rotational position can over- or under-tension the repair relative to the joint's natural resting congruity.
- Use a sliding knot technique through the working portal so knots can be adjusted and re-tensioned before final locking, rather than a fixed knot that cannot be revised once tied.
- Mark and palpate the ECU subsheath before establishing the 6R portal — the portal should sit just radial to the tendon, and straying too far ulnarly risks tendon injury or subsheath disruption.
- For combined bony and ligamentous injury (basilar ulnar styloid fracture plus TFCC foveal disruption), consider addressing the bony fixation first — restoring bony anatomy can simplify subsequent soft-tissue repair and may itself partially restore foveal tension.
- When performing open transosseous foveal repair, protect the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve throughout the dorsal-ulnar approach — it crosses the field and is easily injured with aggressive retraction.
- Always finish with an intraoperative DRUJ stress test after the repair is complete and before leaving the OR — a technically sound repair that does not actually restore DRUJ stability on testing should prompt reassessment (additional sutures, foveal repair if only peripheral repair was done, or transfixion) rather than being accepted on faith.
- Concurrent findings (chondral lesions, LT/SL ligament injury) identified during diagnostic arthroscopy should be addressed in the same setting where feasible, since re-exploring the joint later is far more difficult once the TFCC repair has matured.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- 1A central tear repair attempts have historically poor healing rates given the avascular central zone — debride, do not repair.
- TFCC repair failure is more likely with a missed foveal (deep) component or inadequate immobilization compliance postoperatively.
- Peripheral (1B) repairs generally heal well given the vascularity of the outer 10–20% zone.
- Injury to the ECU tendon/subsheath or dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve is a recognized risk with portal placement (6R) and the dorsal-ulnar open approach respectively — careful landmark identification and blunt dissection to capsule reduce this risk.
- Over-tensioning the repair while sutures are tied under traction (rather than with traction released) can create an overly tight, non-physiologic DRUJ that limits rotation postoperatively — always tie with traction released and forearm supinated.
- A repair that appears technically secure but fails intraoperative DRUJ stress testing indicates an incomplete repair (commonly an unaddressed foveal component) — do not close on the assumption that peripheral repair alone has resolved instability without confirming it.
- Postoperative stiffness in forearm rotation is the dominant recovery-limiting complication, given the mandatory period of restricted rotation — reinforcing the importance of maintaining digit/elbow/shoulder motion throughout and progressing rotational ROM promptly once permitted.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: immobilized in the stable rotational position; digit AROM.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: forearm rotation blocked until wire/immobilization milestones per associated fixation; protected rotation continues.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: forearm rotation is typically restricted to protected/limited arcs until 6 weeks post-repair, then progressed; progressive rotational ROM thereafter.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: full strengthening once union/repair maturity is confirmed.
- **Return to sport**: individualized for TFCC repairs, where forearm rotation loading is the rate-limiting recovery domain; generally not before 3 months for high-demand/impact activity.
