## Indications

Palmer 1B peripheral tears with DRUJ instability, or failed 3–6 months of conservative treatment; 1C/1D tears causing instability; intraoperative finding of TFCC disruption with persistent DRUJ instability after anatomic distal radius fracture (DRF)/ulnar styloid fixation. First-line management of acute peripheral tears is immobilization (wrist splint/cast) 4–6 weeks + NSAIDs, followed by structured hand therapy; surgical referral is reserved for those failing conservative management or with objective DRUJ instability.

## Positioning & setup

Wrist arthroscopy setup for arthroscopic repair; open exposure for transosseous foveal reattachment when foveal (deep-fiber) disruption or combined bony injury is present. Wrist arthroscopy is also the diagnostic/therapeutic gold standard for assessing TFCC tears and DRUJ instability, and is increasingly used intraoperatively during DRF ORIF ("fluoroscopically assisted arthroscopic reduction") to directly visualize articular reduction and TFCC/ligamentous status.

## Steps

1. Confirm tear pattern and foveal involvement (arthroscopically or on pre-operative MRI arthrography, which approaches arthroscopy in sensitivity).
2. For isolated 1B peripheral tears: perform arthroscopic outside-in or inside-out peripheral repair — favored because the zone is well vascularized, the approach is minimally invasive, and it allows concurrent assessment/treatment of associated chondral or ligamentous injury.
3. For foveal (deep-fiber) disruption not adequately addressed by peripheral capsular repair, or for combined bony (styloid base) + ligamentous injury requiring direct fixation: perform open repair via transosseous foveal reattachment.
4. For 1A central tears: debride rather than repair, given the avascular central zone.
5. Select approach (open vs arthroscopic) based on tear pattern/foveal involvement and surgeon experience — 2024–2025 systematic reviews show broadly equivalent outcomes between the two techniques, so no presumed superiority of one over the other should drive the decision.

## Pitfalls

- 1A central tear repair attempts have historically poor healing rates given the avascular central zone — debride, do not repair.
- TFCC repair failure is more likely with a missed foveal (deep) component or inadequate immobilization compliance postoperatively.
- Peripheral (1B) repairs generally heal well given the vascularity of the outer 10–20% zone.

## Post-op protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: immobilized in the stable rotational position; digit AROM.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: forearm rotation blocked until wire/immobilization milestones per associated fixation; protected rotation continues.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: forearm rotation is typically restricted to protected/limited arcs until 6 weeks post-repair, then progressed; progressive rotational ROM thereafter.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: full strengthening once union/repair maturity is confirmed.
- **Return to sport**: individualized for TFCC repairs, where forearm rotation loading is the rate-limiting recovery domain; generally not before 3 months for high-demand/impact activity.
