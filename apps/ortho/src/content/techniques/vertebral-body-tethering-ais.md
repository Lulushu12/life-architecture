## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Vertebral body tethering (VBT) received FDA **Humanitarian Device Exemption** approval for skeletally immature patients with progressive idiopathic scoliosis of 30–65°. Ideal candidates: Sanders stage 2–4/Risser 0–2 with substantial growth remaining, flexible curves correcting to <30° on bending/traction films, thoracic curves (the best-studied pattern). It is best framed as a **narrow, well-selected alternative** to fusion, not a general substitute — long-term (10-year+) durability data are still maturing.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:
- Skeletal maturity or near-maturity (Risser ≥4, Sanders ≥6) — little remaining growth means little modulation potential, and durability concerns are magnified in a patient who won't continue growing to "use up" the correction mechanism.
- Curve magnitude outside the studied range — generally <30° (insufficient benefit to justify surgical risk) or >65° (correction potential and evidence base thin out).
- A rigid curve that does not correct to <30° on bending/traction films — VBT relies on residual flexibility that the tether and continued growth can act on.
- Significant thoracic hypokyphosis — a relative concern, since anterior tethering can further flatten kyphosis and has been associated with sagittal-plane issues in some series.
- Significant cardiopulmonary disease precluding single-lung ventilation, which the thoracoscopic approach requires.
- Patient/family unable to accept the uncertain long-term durability, meaningful tether-breakage rate, and real possibility of eventual conversion to fusion.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: standing PA/lateral radiographs (EOS preferred to limit cumulative radiation in a population needing serial follow-up); supine bending/traction films to confirm flexibility; Sanders stage or Risser grade to quantify remaining growth.
- **CT for implant planning**: vertebral body width, pedicle/vertebral body dimensions for screw sizing, and — critically — the position of the aorta relative to each vertebral body at the planned instrumented levels, since aortic proximity is a specific technical hazard of anterior thoracic screw placement.
- **Pulmonary evaluation**: pulmonary function testing and anesthesia/cardiology consultation to confirm the patient can tolerate single-lung ventilation via a double-lumen endotracheal tube.
- **Implant selection**: number of levels to span, screw length/diameter per level, and tether/cord length.
- **Segmental vessel anatomy**: review preoperative imaging to anticipate which segmental vessels will need to be identified and, if necessary, ligated during exposure.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia with a double-lumen endotracheal tube** for single-lung ventilation — the ipsilateral lung is deflated to allow thoracoscopic access to the anterior/lateral thoracic spine.
- **Lateral decubitus position** with the convex side of the curve facing up; the table is flexed/jackknifed to open the intercostal spaces; an axillary roll is placed, and the arms are positioned to avoid brachial plexus stretch; all bony prominences are padded.
- Fluoroscopy (AP) positioned for real-time imaging during thoracoscopic portal placement and screw insertion, with the C-arm draped into the sterile field.
- Planned thoracoscopic portal sites are marked over the convexity based on preoperative imaging and rib level.
- **Confirm accurate single-lung isolation with fiberoptic bronchoscopy before incision** — inadequate deflation of the ipsilateral lung is the most common reason for a difficult thoracoscopic exposure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Reassess curve flexibility on the table** to confirm intraoperative candidacy.
2. **Position lateral decubitus**, isolate the ipsilateral lung, prep and drape.
3. **Place thoracoscopic portals** (typically 3–4) along the convexity, spanning the levels of the structural curve.
4. **Deflate the ipsilateral lung** and enter the thoracic cavity; mobilize/retract the lung as needed for visualization.
5. **Incise the parietal pleura** over the vertebral bodies at each planned screw level.
6. **Identify and protect (or ligate as needed) the segmental vessels** crossing the vertebral body waist at each level before proceeding to screw placement.
7. **Place a guide pin/awl at the midpoint of the vertebral body** under fluoroscopic AP guidance, aiming for a trajectory that avoids the spinal canal medially and the aorta anteriorly.
8. **Place bicortical (or, per level/curve segment, planned unicortical) vertebral body screws** with a washer or staple plate at each level along the convexity, confirming position on AP (and, when needed, lateral) fluoroscopy.
9. **Sequentially load the polymer tether/cord** through each screw head across all instrumented levels.
10. **Tension the tether** under fluoroscopic and direct visualization, achieving intraoperative curve correction while avoiding overcorrection — reassess coronal correction on fluoroscopy before final locking.
11. **Lock/crimp the tether** at each screw and trim the excess.
12. **Confirm hemostasis**, re-expand the ipsilateral lung under direct thoracoscopic visualization, and place a chest tube.
13. **Close the portal incisions in layers.**
14. **Obtain a postoperative chest radiograph** to confirm lung re-expansion and implant position, and a standing scoliosis film once the patient is mobilized to assess correction.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm double-lung isolation with bronchoscopy before incision — inadequate deflation of the ipsilateral lung is the most common reason for a difficult thoracoscopic exposure.
- Identify and control segmental vessels at each level before screw placement — inadvertent injury during exposure is a preventable source of bleeding that can force conversion to an open approach.
- Place each screw at the true midpoint of the vertebral body under AP fluoroscopy — screws placed too posteriorly risk canal penetration, and too anteriorly risk poor purchase or aortic proximity. Published accuracy series report that only a minority of screws land in the ideal middle-third trajectory without canal penetration or aortic contact, which underscores that meticulous fluoroscopic technique at every level matters more than it might seem.
- Achieving adequate intraoperative curve correction is the single strongest predictor of durable success — under-correction on the table predicts later treatment failure, so do not rush the tensioning step.
- Avoid overcorrection — the growth-modulation mechanism continues to act after surgery, and a curve that is overcorrected at time zero can progress in the opposite direction as the patient continues to grow.
- Preferentially select thoracic curves — the best-studied pattern with the most predictable results; extrapolate cautiously to lumbar or thoracolumbar segments where the evidence base is thinner.
- Counsel the family explicitly and document that tether breakage is common (roughly half of patients by 36 months in FDA IDE cohort follow-up) and does not automatically mean treatment failure — many broken tethers still leave an acceptable curve if adequate correction occurred early and growth has since slowed.
- Plan implant length and screw sizing from preoperative CT, not plain radiographs alone — vertebral body width and segmental vessel position vary meaningfully by level and are best assessed cross-sectionally.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Tether breakage**: reported in a meaningful minority of cases in early-to-mid-term series; FDA IDE cohort follow-up found roughly 50% cumulative tether breakage by 36 months.
- **Overcorrection** is a recognized risk of the growth-modulation mechanism.
- **Conversion to fusion** occurs in a non-trivial proportion of patients at longer follow-up.
- **Durability declines over time** in reported cohorts: ~85% success at 1 year, 75% at 2 years, 62% at a mean 3.8 years in FDA IDE cohort follow-up — counsel patients that success is not guaranteed to be durable past skeletal maturity.
- An umbrella review (2024) found the underlying VBT evidence base still heterogeneous and low-to-moderate quality overall, supporting continued restriction to well-selected, flexible thoracic curves rather than broad adoption as a fusion alternative.
- **Screw malposition/canal or great-vessel proximity**: technically demanding thoracoscopic screw placement carries a real risk of canal penetration or aortic proximity even in experienced hands, per published accuracy series — meticulous midline/mid-body fluoroscopic technique at every level is the primary mitigation.
- **Pulmonary complications**: pneumothorax, atelectasis, or prolonged air leak related to single-lung ventilation and thoracoscopic access; monitored with a postoperative chest tube and chest radiograph.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks**: inpatient stay typically 2–4 days; chest tube removed once drainage is low (generally <100–150mL/day) and there is no air leak; multimodal pain control (intercostal blocks or thoracic epidural analgesia are commonly used given thoracotomy-adjacent pain); incentive spirometry and pulmonary toilet given the lung deflation required intraoperatively; mobilize from postoperative day 1; no formal bracing needed.
- **2–6 weeks**: gradual return to school and light activity; avoid high-impact/contact sports and heavy lifting; standing radiograph around 6 weeks to assess early correction and screw/tether position.
- **3–6 months**: gradual, surgeon-directed return to sport; continued radiographic surveillance for curve correction and tether integrity.
- **6–12 months and beyond**: serial standing radiographs (commonly every 4–6 months through the remaining growth period) to monitor curve correction, screw/tether integrity, and to detect overcorrection or breakage early; full return to sport typically allowed once early healing is confirmed, but final activity clearance is individualized; continued surveillance is recommended through and beyond skeletal maturity given the still-maturing long-term durability data.
