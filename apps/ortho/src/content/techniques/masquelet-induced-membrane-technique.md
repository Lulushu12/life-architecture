## Indications

Segmental diaphyseal or metaphyseal bone loss at or above critical size (commonly cited as ≥ 4–5 cm), particularly shorter defects (reliably up to roughly 4–6 cm), patients who cannot tolerate prolonged external fixation, or cases where soft-tissue coverage is already achieved. Favored over bone transport when defect size and patient factors permit, given its simpler technique and shorter time to full weight-bearing.

## Positioning & setup

A staged, two-surgery protocol. Stage 1 requires stable skeletal fixation (plate or nail) established at the time of spacer placement; Stage 2 is performed roughly 6–8 weeks later once the induced membrane has formed.

## Steps

1. **Stage 1**: radical debridement to bleeding, viable bone; achieve stable fixation (plate or nail).
2. Insert an antibiotic-loaded PMMA cement spacer into the defect; this induces a vascularized biological membrane (pseudo-periosteum) around it over 6–8 weeks.
3. **Stage 2** (after 6–8 weeks): remove the spacer while carefully preserving the induced membrane.
4. Fill the defect with autologous cancellous bone graft (± allograft-extended volume) — iliac crest or Reamer-Irrigator-Aspirator (RIA) harvest — ± BMP for larger graft volumes where autograft alone is inadequate.
5. For infected or previously osteomyelitic defects, always exclude/eradicate infection first via culture-directed debridement, implant removal or exchange, and an antibiotic cement spacer, delaying grafting until infection is controlled.
6. For ultra-long defects following osteomyelitis debridement, a modified technique combining Masquelet with vascularized fibular transplantation has achieved union in very long infected defects that exceed standard Masquelet critical-size limits.

## Pitfalls

- Reliable up to roughly 4–6 cm defects reproducibly; success drops for defects > 6 cm, with ~62 mm cited as an approximate critical threshold in some series.
- Donor-site morbidity from iliac crest graft harvest (chronic pain, sensory nerve injury, hematoma) is a real trade-off, especially for larger graft volumes.
- Grafting an infected defect before eradicating infection reliably fails — culture, debride, and use a staged antibiotic-spacer protocol first; this is a common and costly avoidable error.
- Choosing Masquelet vs. bone transport by habit rather than by defect size, soft-tissue status, and patient tolerance for prolonged external fixation is a pitfall — both are valid, complementary tools. Masquelet trades a size ceiling for a technically simpler, shorter overall protocol; bone transport is not limited by defect size but requires much longer external fixation.
- Comparative meta-analysis data (6 studies, 364 patients) found no significant overall outcome difference between Masquelet and bone transport except in subgroup measures; Masquelet showed lower cost, shorter time-to-full-load after final surgery, and better quality-of-life scores, while bone transport showed a higher union rate (~95% vs ~86% for Masquelet).

## Post-op protocol

**Nonunion/segmental defect reconstruction (Masquelet)**
- Post-stage 1 (spacer placement): non- to touch-weight-bearing, ROM of adjacent joints maintained, pin-site care education if external fixation was used for stabilization.
- Post-graft (stage 2): protected weight-bearing progressing over 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic incorporation; full weight-bearing typically not before 3–4 months and often later for large-defect reconstructions.
- Return to work/sport is defined case-by-case and typically delayed 9–18 months for large segmental reconstructions given the prolonged biological remodeling timeline.
