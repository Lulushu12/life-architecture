## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Segmental diaphyseal or metaphyseal bone loss at or above critical size (commonly cited as ≥ 4–5 cm), reliably reconstructable up to roughly 4–6 cm with a single-stage graft volume.
- Patients who cannot tolerate prolonged external fixation, or in whom soft-tissue coverage is already achieved (flap healed, wound closed) and only the skeletal defect remains to be reconstructed.
- Infected nonunion/osteomyelitis with segmental bone loss after eradication debridement — the antibiotic spacer stage doubles as dead-space management while infection is cleared.
- Combined bone-and-soft-tissue defects where a free flap is planned concurrently or has already been placed — Masquelet integrates well with a "primary free-flap plus induced-membrane" single-admission strategy in appropriately resourced centres.
- For ultra-long defects following osteomyelitis debridement that exceed standard Masquelet critical-size limits, a modified technique combining Masquelet with vascularized fibular transplantation has achieved union even in very long infected defects — an option to plan for jointly with microsurgical colleagues rather than pushing bulk autograft volume beyond what it can reliably support.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active, uncontrolled infection at the time of planned stage 2 grafting — grafting into a still-infected bed reliably fails; infection must be eradicated (clinically, biochemically, and on repeat culture where feasible) before committing to bone graft.
- Defect length beyond what an achievable single-stage autograft volume (± allograft extension, ± BMP) can reasonably fill — very large defects (> 6 cm, with ~62 mm cited as an approximate threshold where success drops in some series) are a relative contraindication in favor of bone transport or a hybrid vascularized-fibula approach.
- Unstable or inadequate skeletal fixation that cannot be corrected at stage 1 — the induced membrane and graft cannot succeed over a mechanically unstable construct; fix the mechanics before committing to the biologic strategy.
- Poor host bed vascularity (e.g., extensive prior irradiation, severe peripheral vascular disease) that will not support membrane formation or graft incorporation regardless of technique — consider a vascularized bone option instead.
- Patient unable to tolerate two planned surgeries roughly 6–8 weeks apart, or with medical instability precluding a second general anesthetic in that window.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length orthogonal radiographs with a radiopaque scale marker to measure true defect length; CT to assess residual bone quality, sequestrum, and canal patency at both fragment ends, particularly important if intramedullary nailing is planned as the stage-1 fixation method.
- **Fixation method selection**: decide between intramedullary nail and plate fixation for stage 1 before the case, not intraoperatively — comparative series in the femur and tibia report faster time to union, fewer secondary grafting procedures, fewer reoperations for any cause, and earlier weight-bearing (roughly 2.4 vs 4.6 months to full load in one series) with IM nailing compared with plate fixation, attributed to circumferential graft loading and earlier mechanical stimulation of the graft under a nail versus relative stress-shielding under a plate. Plate fixation remains necessary when canal access is unavailable, the defect is juxta-articular, or deformity/rotational control requires a plate-and-screw construct; a dual-plate construct is a reasonable option for challenging distal-tibial defects where nailing is not feasible.
- **Graft volume and source planning**: estimate the defect volume and plan graft source accordingly — iliac crest for smaller volumes, Reamer-Irrigator-Aspirator (RIA) harvest from the contralateral or ipsilateral femur for larger cancellous volumes with lower donor-site morbidity; plan allograft extension or BMP augmentation in advance for defects near the upper size limit of reliable single-stage grafting.
- **Cement spacer planning**: decide on plain versus antibiotic-loaded PMMA. Masquelet's original description favored plain cement (concern that antibiotic elution may alter membrane histology and that culture-negative recurrence with plain cement is itself a favorable sign), while most contemporary trauma practice uses antibiotic-loaded cement (typically vancomycin and/or an aminoglycoside) for its local antimicrobial effect in the early post-debridement period, accepting that elution is clinically meaningful only for the first several days. Choose per case infection risk and local protocol, and document the choice, since it affects interpretation of "spacer in place without recurrence" as a marker of infection control.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: implant system for the chosen stage-1 fixation method, PMMA cement (plain or antibiotic-loaded) with a mixing bowl and mould (a split large-syringe barrel produces a uniformly cylindrical, low-spillage spacer and is a useful low-cost mould option), pulse lavage or bulb irrigation for cooling during cement setting, RIA set or iliac crest harvest tray for stage 2, and a full culture-collection setup for both stages.
- **Infection workup**: for any prior open fracture, prior surgery, or clinical suspicion of osteomyelitis, obtain deep tissue cultures and inflammatory markers preoperatively and plan a culture-directed antibiotic course bridging the two stages.
- **Interval planning**: block stage 2 at 6–8 weeks from stage 1 as the default; delay if there is any clinical, biochemical, or culture concern for persistent infection, since the membrane remains a viable, usable biologic structure well beyond 8 weeks if stage 2 needs to be deferred.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia standard for both stages; regional block for postoperative analgesia is reasonable once any concurrent flap or neurovascular monitoring concerns have resolved.
- **Positioning**: supine for most femoral/tibial defects, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for tibial cases to neutralize rotation; position and drape to allow simultaneous access to the graft harvest site (iliac crest or contralateral femur for RIA) at stage 2 without recontaminating the primary field.
- **Table/imaging setup**: radiolucent table with image intensifier access for both stages — confirming fixation, spacer position, and (at stage 2) graft fill and final alignment fluoroscopically.
- **Draping**: wide prep incorporating the full length of the fixation construct and the planned graft harvest site as a single or clearly separated sterile field; at stage 2, prep generously enough to extend the approach if more extensive membrane exposure is needed than initially planned.
- **Landmarks**: mark the existing incision (reuse at stage 2 whenever feasible to avoid a new zone of soft-tissue disruption), the defect margins as measured on preoperative imaging, and the graft harvest site landmarks before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Stage 1 — debridement, fixation, and spacer placement**

1. **Radically debride the defect** to bleeding, viable bone and healthy soft tissue on all sides — resect back to the "paprika sign" (punctate cortical/cancellous bleeding) on both fragment ends; send deep tissue and (if hardware present) implant sonication cultures before starting antibiotics for the case.
2. **Achieve stable skeletal fixation** across the defect using the preoperatively selected method (IM nail or plate) — this construct must independently provide definitive mechanical stability, since the spacer itself provides no structural support and the membrane it induces is a biologic, not mechanical, structure.
3. **Measure and prepare the PMMA cement** (plain or antibiotic-loaded per the preoperative plan); mix and mould it — free-hand or using a split-syringe-barrel or purpose-made mould — to a size and contour matching the native bone's cross-section.
4. **Avoid overstuffing the defect**: size the spacer to fill the bony gap without so much bulk that it tents the soft-tissue envelope or precludes tension-free closure — overstuffing is a recognized cause of wound-closure problems and, later, a spacer that is difficult to remove without damaging the membrane.
5. **Irrigate continuously with cool saline while the cement cures** in situ — PMMA polymerization is exothermic and can thermally injure adjacent soft tissue and the very membrane you are trying to induce if allowed to cure dry against tissue.
6. **Mould the spacer to interdigitate snugly with both bone ends** and to sit against (but not encase circumferentially in a way that traps) any plate used for fixation — where a plate is used, the membrane will form around both the plate and the spacer together; plan the eventual stage-2 membrane incision line along the plate's border so it can be reopened and reclosed without sacrificing membrane integrity.
7. **Close soft tissue over the spacer without tension**, using local tissue rearrangement or coordinating with a flap procedure at the same or a closely staged sitting if the soft-tissue envelope is inadequate for primary closure.
8. **Continue culture-directed systemic antibiotics** per the infection workup and case-specific contamination burden through the interval between stages.

**Interval (6–8 weeks)**

9. **Allow the induced membrane to mature** over 6–8 weeks — this vascularized pseudo-periosteal membrane secretes growth factors and provides a biologically active, relatively avascular-graft-protective envelope; do not proceed to stage 2 prematurely, but also recognize the membrane remains usable well beyond 8 weeks if stage 2 must be delayed for infection surveillance or medical reasons.

**Stage 2 — spacer removal and grafting**

10. **Re-expose through the prior incision**, opening the membrane longitudinally along the pre-planned line (adjacent to the plate border if a plate was used), preserving as much membrane surface area and vascularity as possible.
11. **Remove the cement spacer carefully**, en bloc if possible or in fragments using a rongeur or drill if it has interdigitated tightly with the bone ends — exceptional care is taken at this step to avoid tearing or devascularizing the surrounding membrane, since a violated membrane behaves more like ordinary scar and loses much of its biologic benefit; confirm every fragment of cement is retrieved, since retained cement fragments will block bony bridging.
12. **Freshen both bone ends** with a burr or curette to bleeding bone if they have become sclerotic or fibrous during the interval, without aggressively debriding so much that the defect length increases meaningfully.
13. **Harvest bone graft** — iliac crest (protecting the lateral femoral cutaneous and ilioinguinal nerves) or RIA harvest (through a validated femoral entry point with continuous irrigation/aspiration) — sized to the measured defect volume; extend with allograft chips or add BMP if autograft volume alone is judged inadequate for the defect size.
14. **Pack graft material densely within the membrane cavity**, ensuring circumferential contact with both bleeding bone ends and complete filling of the cavity without voids — under-filling leaves dead space that fibroses rather than ossifies.
15. **Close the membrane over the graft without tension**, using it as a biologic barrier that contains the graft and contributes growth factors and vascularity to the fusion mass — this closure is a distinct, deliberate step, not an afterthought before skin closure.
16. **Confirm final alignment and fixation stability on fluoroscopy** before layered soft-tissue and skin closure; leave a drain if graft-site or surgical-site hematoma risk is significant.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a split large-syringe barrel (or a similar low-cost cylindrical mould) to shape the stage-1 spacer — this produces a uniform, well-contoured spacer with minimal cement spillage into surrounding soft tissue compared with free-hand shaping.
- Irrigate continuously during cement polymerization, not just after — the exothermic curing reaction, not just the finished implant, is what risks thermal injury to the nascent membrane bed.
- Deliberately under-fill rather than overstuff the defect with cement at stage 1 — a snug but not oversized spacer is both easier to remove intact at stage 2 and less likely to compromise soft-tissue closure at stage 1.
- Plan and mark the stage-2 membrane incision line at stage 1 (typically running along the plate's border when a plate is used) so the membrane can be opened and later re-closed along a single, predictable, low-morbidity line.
- Favor intramedullary nail fixation over plating for stage 1 in diaphyseal femoral and tibial defects when canal access allows — comparative series report meaningfully faster union, fewer secondary grafting procedures, and fewer reoperations with nailing, likely reflecting earlier circumferential mechanical loading of the graft compared with the relative stress-shielding of a plate construct.
- At stage 2, remove every visible cement fragment before grafting — a missed shard is a mechanical block to bridging and a nidus that is easy to overlook once graft material is packed around it.
- Pack graft material to genuine density, checking for voids by direct palpation/visualization circumferentially around the defect before closing the membrane — a seemingly "full" cavity can still have unfilled pockets that become fibrous rather than osseous.
- For defects approaching the upper limit of reliable single-stage grafting, plan allograft extension or BMP augmentation before the case, rather than discovering an inadequate autograft volume intraoperatively.
- In infected or previously osteomyelitic defects, treat stage 1 spacer placement as part of the infection-eradication protocol, not merely as a mechanical placeholder — culture-directed antibiotics and a genuinely radical debridement at stage 1 determine whether stage 2 grafting will succeed.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Reliable up to roughly 4–6 cm defects reproducibly**; success drops for defects > 6 cm, with ~62 mm cited as an approximate critical threshold in some series — do not extend Masquelet past its reliable range without a hybrid strategy (vascularized fibula, staged approach) in mind.
- **Grafting an infected defect before eradicating infection reliably fails** — culture, debride, and use a staged antibiotic-spacer protocol first; this is a common and costly avoidable error.
- **Overstuffing the defect with cement at stage 1** compromises soft-tissue closure and makes intact spacer removal at stage 2 more difficult, risking membrane injury during extraction.
- **Membrane injury during spacer removal** — tearing or devascularizing the induced membrane while removing the cement (particularly with an oversized or tightly interdigitated spacer) converts a biologically active envelope into ordinary scar and measurably worsens the odds of successful grafting; defective membrane properties (reduced mesenchymal stem cell content and fibroblast density) have been directly associated with clinical grafting failures in comparative histologic series.
- **Retained cement fragments** at stage 2 mechanically block bony bridging if not completely retrieved — inspect thoroughly before grafting.
- **Donor-site morbidity from iliac crest graft harvest** (chronic pain, sensory nerve injury, hematoma) is a real trade-off, especially for larger graft volumes; RIA harvest is generally better tolerated for large-volume needs.
- **Choosing plate fixation over nailing by habit** rather than by anatomic feasibility — where canal access allows, nailing carries a documented advantage in union speed, reoperation rate, and time to weight-bearing in comparative series.
- **Choosing Masquelet vs. bone transport by habit** rather than by defect size, soft-tissue status, and patient tolerance for prolonged external fixation is a pitfall — both are valid, complementary tools. Masquelet trades a size ceiling for a technically simpler, shorter overall protocol; bone transport is not limited by defect size but requires much longer external fixation.
- **Comparative meta-analysis data** (6 studies, 364 patients) found no significant overall outcome difference between Masquelet and bone transport except in subgroup measures; Masquelet showed lower cost, shorter time-to-full-load after final surgery, and better quality-of-life scores, while bone transport showed a higher union rate (~95% vs ~86% for Masquelet).

## Postoperative Protocol

**Nonunion/segmental defect reconstruction (Masquelet)**
- Post-stage 1 (spacer placement): non- to touch-weight-bearing, ROM of adjacent joints maintained, pin-site care education if external fixation was used for stabilization.
- Post-graft (stage 2): protected weight-bearing progressing over 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic incorporation; full weight-bearing typically not before 3–4 months and often later for large-defect reconstructions — nailed constructs generally progress to full load faster than plated constructs (roughly 2.4 vs 4.6 months to full weight-bearing in comparative series), consistent with earlier circumferential graft loading under a nail.
- Return to work/sport is defined case-by-case and typically delayed 9–18 months for large segmental reconstructions given the prolonged biological remodeling timeline.
