## Indications

- Wide surgical resection of primary bone sarcoma (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, high-grade or dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma) after appropriate neoadjuvant therapy, when required by histology.
- Limb salvage (endoprosthesis, allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, or rotationplasty in select pediatric cases) is oncologically equivalent to amputation for local control, provided adequate margins are achievable with neurovascular preservation.
- Amputation is reserved for cases where a wide margin/functional limb cannot otherwise be achieved, extensive neurovascular involvement, or patient preference. Skip lesions and pathologic fracture at presentation are relative contraindications to limb salvage.
- Expandable/growing endoprosthesis is indicated in skeletally immature patients undergoing resection near an open physis (distal femoral osteosarcoma is the classic scenario), when reconstruction cannot be physis-sparing.

## Positioning & setup

Positioning follows the resection site. Preoperative planning requires MRI of the entire compartment/bone including the joint above and below to define soft tissue extension, marrow involvement, neurovascular relationship, and skip lesions before any biopsy or surgical planning — the biopsy tract itself must be planned as part of the eventual resection incision.

## Steps

1. Complete neoadjuvant therapy as indicated by histology (MAP chemotherapy for osteosarcoma over ~10 weeks; VDC/IE chemotherapy for Ewing sarcoma) before proceeding to resection.
2. Plan the resection to achieve a wide margin (through normal tissue, with a cuff of uninvolved tissue circumferentially), incorporating the prior biopsy tract en bloc with the specimen.
3. In skeletally immature patients with an open physis nearby, evaluate whether tumor margins allow a physis-sparing intercalary resection; if not, plan for an expandable/growing endoprosthesis, non-invasively lengthened (typically via an external magnetic driver in modern designs) to accommodate remaining growth.
4. Discuss the long-term surgical plan (staged lengthenings, eventual conversion to a definitive adult-sized implant near skeletal maturity) with the family at the outset when a growing prosthesis is planned.
5. Reconstruct with endoprosthesis, allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, or (in select pediatric cases) rotationplasty, based on defect size, location, and patient factors.
6. Perform meticulous soft-tissue reconstruction around the implant — extensor mechanism reattachment at the proximal tibia, abductor reattachment at the proximal femur — as this is as important to functional outcome as the implant itself.
7. For osteosarcoma, grade histologic necrosis response (Huvos grading) on the resection specimen to guide adjuvant chemotherapy continuation/adjustment (≥90% necrosis = good responder, associated with better survival).
8. For Ewing sarcoma, prefer surgical resection over radiation alone for local control when feasible, given better local control rates and avoidance of radiation-associated secondary sarcoma risk in a typically young population; reserve radiation for unresectable disease, close/positive margins, or poor surgical candidates.
9. Consider pulmonary metastasectomy for resectable lung metastases (isolated or oligometastatic) in osteosarcoma, as it meaningfully improves survival in this subset.
10. Consider carbon-fiber-reinforced PEEK (CFR-PEEK) implants where radiolucency for artifact-free postoperative tumor surveillance and radiotherapy planning/delivery is valuable, and where an elastic modulus closer to bone than titanium is desired; comparative long-term outcome data vs titanium remain limited.

## Pitfalls

- Under-tensioning the extensor mechanism repair (proximal tibia) or abductor repair (proximal femur) is a common, avoidable cause of extensor lag or Trendelenburg gait — soft-tissue reconstruction quality is as important as implant selection.
- Infection is the leading complication of endoprosthetic limb-salvage reconstruction, with reported rates roughly 10–40% depending on anatomic site (higher for proximal tibia, given thin soft tissue coverage, than distal femur), reconstruction extent, and prior/adjuvant chemoradiation — often requiring staged revision and a major driver of secondary amputation.
- Structural allograft reconstructions carry a persistent risk of nonunion at the host-graft junction, fracture, and infection, motivating a shift toward endoprosthetic or allograft-prosthetic composite options at many centers for large-segment defects.
- Skip lesions and pathologic fracture at presentation are relative contraindications to limb salvage — evaluate carefully before committing to a limb-salvage plan in these scenarios.
- Doxorubicin cardiotoxicity (cumulative dose-dependent), cisplatin oto-/nephrotoxicity, ifosfamide nephrotoxicity/encephalopathy, and secondary treatment-related malignancy are late chemotherapy effects requiring lifelong survivorship surveillance handed off from pediatric/medical oncology.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Protection | 0–2 weeks | Non-WB to touch-down WB (protect extensor/abductor repair) | Passive ROM of adjacent joints as construct allows | Wound care, drain management, extended DVT prophylaxis, pain control, chemo-cycle coordination |
| 2 — Early mobilization | 2–6 weeks | Progress toward partial WB by 6 weeks | Active-assisted → active ROM; gentle isometrics | Neutropenia checks before advancing high-fall-risk activity in chemo patients; early gait training |
| 3 — Progressive loading | 6–12 weeks | Partial → full WB (proximal femoral replacements progress slower given abductor vulnerability) | Full active ROM goal | Closed-chain strengthening initiated, gait normalization, home exercise program |
| 4 — Strengthening & return to function | 3–6 months | Full WB established | Full ROM maintenance | Proprioceptive/balance training, progressive resistance, functional/vocational retraining |

Endoprosthetic reconstructions (proximal tibial replacement especially) require protection of the extensor mechanism repair with bracing in extension for the first 6 weeks before progressive flexion is allowed. Chemotherapy-receiving patients need rehabilitation explicitly coordinated around chemotherapy cycles, expecting slower, non-linear progression and dedicated conditioning work alongside limb-specific therapy.

Return to work: sedentary work typically 6–12 weeks after limb-salvage reconstruction once wound-healed and pain-controlled; physical labor delayed until adequate strength/endurance is demonstrated, often 4–6 months or longer, and may never be fully regained after proximal femoral/pelvic endoprosthetic reconstruction. Return to sport/high-impact activity is generally discouraged or significantly restricted after endoprosthetic reconstruction given implant longevity concerns; low-impact activity (swimming, cycling) is typically permitted once healed (~4–6 months).

Post-treatment surveillance: local imaging every 3 months years 1–2 then spacing out, with CT chest every 3 months years 1–2, then every 4–6 months years 3–5, then annually, for a minimum of 5–10 years given late-relapse potential (osteosarcoma/Ewing sarcoma).
