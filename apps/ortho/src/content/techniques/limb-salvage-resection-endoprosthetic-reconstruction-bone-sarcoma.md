## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Wide surgical resection of primary bone sarcoma (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, high-grade or dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma) after appropriate neoadjuvant therapy, when required by histology.
- Limb salvage (endoprosthesis, allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, or rotationplasty in select pediatric cases) is oncologically equivalent to amputation for local control, provided adequate margins are achievable with neurovascular preservation.
- Expandable/growing endoprosthesis is indicated in skeletally immature patients undergoing resection near an open physis (distal femoral osteosarcoma is the classic scenario), when reconstruction cannot be physis-sparing.

**Contraindications**

- Amputation is reserved for cases where a wide margin/functional limb cannot otherwise be achieved, extensive neurovascular involvement, or patient preference.
- Skip lesions and pathologic fracture at presentation are relative contraindications to limb salvage — the resection specimen must extend beyond the skip lesion or the fracture hematoma, which can substantially compromise the remaining limb segment or force conversion to amputation.
- Uncontrolled active infection, or a patient too medically unstable to tolerate a long reconstructive procedure and its rehabilitation demands, are relative contraindications.
- Progression of disease on neoadjuvant therapy to a point where an adequate margin is no longer achievable should prompt re-staging and reconsideration of the limb-salvage plan before proceeding.

## Preoperative Planning

- MRI of the entire compartment/bone including the joint above and below to define soft tissue extension, marrow involvement, neurovascular relationship, and skip lesions before any biopsy or surgical planning — the biopsy tract itself must be planned as part of the eventual resection incision.
- CT chest for systemic staging, and bone scan or whole-body/PET-CT for skeletal metastases/multifocal disease as indicated by histology (osteosarcoma, Ewing).
- Complete neoadjuvant therapy as indicated by histology before proceeding to resection (see Step-by-Step Technique), with repeat local MRI shortly before surgery to confirm the resection plan remains valid (tumor response, margin adequacy) and to detect any interval progression.
- Template resection length precisely off the most recent MRI, and template/order the endoprosthesis (fixed or expandable/growing), allograft, or allograft-prosthetic composite components sized to the anticipated defect, with a range of implant sizes and stem options available in the room given that intraoperative findings can shift the planned resection length.
- For skeletally immature patients, confirm growth remaining (bone age, growth plate status) to decide between a physis-sparing intercalary resection (if tumor margins allow) and an expandable/growing endoprosthesis; discuss the long-term surgical plan (staged non-invasive lengthenings, typically via an external magnetic driver, and eventual conversion to a definitive adult-sized implant near skeletal maturity) with the family at the outset.
- Plan soft-tissue reconstruction in advance, not as an afterthought — for proximal tibial reconstructions specifically, plan for a medial gastrocnemius rotational flap and extensor mechanism reconstruction (patellar tendon reattachment to the prosthesis, often reinforced with mesh/tape and autologous bone graft) as a routine part of the procedure, not a rescue maneuver.
- Consider carbon-fiber-reinforced PEEK (CFR-PEEK) implants where radiolucency for artifact-free postoperative tumor surveillance and radiotherapy planning/delivery is valuable, and where an elastic modulus closer to bone than titanium is desired; comparative long-term outcome data vs titanium remain limited, so weigh this against center experience and implant availability.
- Cross-match blood; large resections with medullary canal instrumentation can bleed substantially.
- Coordinate the operating room date with the chemotherapy schedule (adequate count recovery, cardiac/renal function checks after anthracycline/platinum-based regimens) and confirm clearance from medical oncology.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia, often combined with regional/neuraxial technique for postoperative analgesia, given the magnitude of dissection.
- Positioning follows the resection site: supine for femoral/tibial resections (with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for lateral femoral exposure as needed), lateral decubitus for proximal humeral resections, and site-specific positioning for pelvic/axial resections.
- Use a radiolucent table with fluoroscopy available to confirm resection level and final implant position.
- Prep and drape the entire limb circumferentially from well above to well below the resection site, incorporating the biopsy tract into the prepped field so it can be excised en bloc.
- Apply a sterile tourniquet if the resection level allows exsanguination proximal to the tumor without violating tumor margins; for proximal lesions where a tourniquet cannot be safely applied without risking the margin, prepare for direct control of major vessels instead.
- Mark surface landmarks (joint line, neurovascular bundle course, planned osteotomy level measured off preoperative MRI, biopsy scar) before incision.
- Have blood products immediately available, and confirm large-bore IV/central access appropriate for a case with potential for significant blood loss.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Complete neoadjuvant therapy as indicated by histology (MAP chemotherapy for osteosarcoma over ~10 weeks; VDC/IE chemotherapy for Ewing sarcoma) before proceeding to resection.
2. Re-confirm the resection plan against updated imaging; measure the planned osteotomy level(s) off the most recent MRI with an adequate margin beyond the tumor's marrow and soft-tissue extent.
3. Incise incorporating the prior biopsy tract en bloc with the specimen, and develop the approach through the plane(s) dictated by the tumor's compartmental extent.
4. Identify and protect (or, if directly involved, plan formal resection/reconstruction of) the major neurovascular bundle early in the dissection, working from normal tissue proximal and distal to the tumor toward the involved segment.
5. Perform the bony osteotomies at the planned levels, using an oscillating saw under direct vision or, for intramedullary levels, confirmed with intraoperative fluoroscopy, to achieve a wide margin (through normal tissue, with a cuff of uninvolved tissue circumferentially) — send the specimen for margin assessment (frozen section where the plan may need real-time adjustment).
6. Remove the specimen en bloc, maintaining the soft-tissue envelope around the tumor intact and without violating the pseudocapsule/reactive zone.
7. In skeletally immature patients with an open physis nearby, having already determined whether tumor margins allow a physis-sparing intercalary resection, either complete that resection or proceed with the expandable/growing endoprosthesis construct as planned preoperatively.
8. Prepare the host bone ends (broach/ream the medullary canal per the implant system's technique guide) and trial the endoprosthesis, allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, or rotationplasty construct chosen based on defect size, location, and patient factors.
9. Cement or press-fit the definitive implant per its design (most modern modular oncologic systems use cemented stems for immediate stability), restoring limb length, offset, and rotation — check limb length against the contralateral side and confirm rotational alignment before final implant seating.
10. Perform meticulous soft-tissue reconstruction around the implant, treating this as equal in importance to implant selection:
    - **Proximal femur**: reattach the abductors (and other short external rotators as feasible) directly to the prosthesis through dedicated suture/mesh attachment points, restoring as much of the native abductor lever arm as possible.
    - **Proximal tibia**: reattach the patellar tendon to the prosthesis (commonly with heavy nonabsorbable tape/mesh, reinforced with autologous bone graft at the attachment site), then rotate a medial gastrocnemius muscle flap over the proximal implant and knee joint and suture it to the patellar tendon and quadriceps mechanism, both to reinforce the extensor repair and to provide vascularized soft-tissue coverage over the implant. When mobilizing the gastrocnemius flap, use the medial sural cutaneous nerve as a landmark to find the midline, identify the median raphe by the differing fascial fiber orientation, release it longitudinally with electrocautery, and release the flap distally from the underlying soleus fascia near the confluence with the Achilles tendon; keep the flap wrapped in saline-soaked sponges whenever not being actively worked on to prevent desiccation.
    - **Distal femur**: reconstruct the capsule and collateral ligament attachments as anatomically as possible to restore knee stability.
11. Close the surrounding musculature over the implant in layers to maximize soft-tissue coverage, minimizing dead space; place drain(s) given the large dissection and cavity created.
12. For osteosarcoma, send the resection specimen for histologic necrosis response grading (Huvos grading) to guide adjuvant chemotherapy continuation/adjustment (≥90% necrosis = good responder, associated with better survival).
13. For Ewing sarcoma, this surgical resection is generally preferred over radiation alone for local control when feasible, given better local control rates and avoidance of radiation-associated secondary sarcoma risk in a typically young population; reserve radiation for unresectable disease, close/positive margins, or poor surgical candidates.
14. Discuss pulmonary metastasectomy with thoracic surgery for resectable lung metastases (isolated or oligometastatic) in osteosarcoma as a staged procedure, given it meaningfully improves survival in this subset.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Re-image shortly before surgery and re-measure the osteotomy level off the freshest MRI — tumor response to neoadjuvant therapy (or, rarely, progression) can shift the safe resection margin from what was planned weeks earlier.
- Work from normal, unviolated tissue toward the tumor when developing the neurovascular dissection — identifying the vessels/nerve in a clean plane proximal and distal to the lesion first, then following them past the tumor, is safer than starting directly over the mass.
- For proximal tibial reconstruction, plan the medial gastrocnemius flap as a routine part of the case, not a bailout — routine flap coverage plus a reinforced patellar tendon repair (tape/mesh plus autologous bone graft at the attachment) improves extensor mechanism function and reduces wound complications over skin closure alone.
- Reinforce the patellar tendon-to-prosthesis repair even when tension appears adequate intraoperatively; a meaningful proportion of patients need secondary reinforcement, and a robust primary repair reduces that likelihood.
- Restore abductor tension carefully in proximal femoral reconstructions — checking hip stability and abductor lever arm intraoperatively (trial reduction, assessing tension through a range of motion) before final implant seating prevents a postoperative Trendelenburg gait that is difficult to correct later.
- Keep resected specimens and margins oriented for the pathologist (ink/suture-mark key margins) so that any close or positive margin can be localized back to a specific anatomic zone for possible re-resection or radiation planning.
- Match implant modularity to the reconstruction needs — modular oncologic systems allow intraoperative adjustment of segment length and stem size, which is valuable given that final resection length is sometimes only confirmed after the specimen is out.
- For expandable prostheses in growing children, confirm the lengthening mechanism and expected lengthening increments with the family and pediatric orthopedic team before surgery, and document baseline limb lengths for future comparison at lengthening visits.
- Maintain meticulous hemostasis before final closure — large dead space around a modular implant is a nidus for hematoma, which is itself a major risk factor for the periprosthetic infection that dominates this procedure's complication profile.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Under-tensioning the extensor mechanism repair (proximal tibia) or abductor repair (proximal femur) is a common, avoidable cause of extensor lag or Trendelenburg gait — soft-tissue reconstruction quality is as important as implant selection. Reconstruction of the extensor mechanism is widely regarded as the single most technically challenging element of proximal tibial endoprosthetic reconstruction; even with a well-executed repair, a meaningful minority of patients require secondary reinforcement of the patellar tendon.
- Infection is the leading complication of endoprosthetic limb-salvage reconstruction, with reported rates roughly 10–40% depending on anatomic site (higher for proximal tibia, given thin soft tissue coverage, than distal femur), reconstruction extent, and prior/adjuvant chemoradiation — often requiring staged revision and a major driver of secondary amputation.
- Structural allograft reconstructions carry a persistent risk of nonunion at the host-graft junction, fracture, and infection, motivating a shift toward endoprosthetic or allograft-prosthetic composite options at many centers for large-segment defects.
- Skip lesions and pathologic fracture at presentation are relative contraindications to limb salvage — evaluate carefully before committing to a limb-salvage plan in these scenarios.
- Injury to the neurovascular bundle during dissection, or inadequate margin around a vessel/nerve closely apposed to the tumor, can force intraoperative conversion from a planned limb salvage to amputation — this possibility should be discussed with the patient during consent.
- Leg-length discrepancy is a recognized long-term issue in growing children even with an expandable prosthesis, if lengthening visits are not kept on schedule as the contralateral limb continues to grow.
- Doxorubicin cardiotoxicity (cumulative dose-dependent), cisplatin oto-/nephrotoxicity, ifosfamide nephrotoxicity/encephalopathy, and secondary treatment-related malignancy are late chemotherapy effects requiring lifelong survivorship surveillance handed off from pediatric/medical oncology.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Protection | 0–2 weeks | Non-WB to touch-down WB (protect extensor/abductor repair) | Passive ROM of adjacent joints as construct allows | Wound care, drain management, extended DVT prophylaxis, pain control, chemo-cycle coordination |
| 2 — Early mobilization | 2–6 weeks | Progress toward partial WB by 6 weeks | Active-assisted → active ROM; gentle isometrics | Neutropenia checks before advancing high-fall-risk activity in chemo patients; early gait training |
| 3 — Progressive loading | 6–12 weeks | Partial → full WB (proximal femoral replacements progress slower given abductor vulnerability) | Full active ROM goal | Closed-chain strengthening initiated, gait normalization, home exercise program |
| 4 — Strengthening & return to function | 3–6 months | Full WB established | Full ROM maintenance | Proprioceptive/balance training, progressive resistance, functional/vocational retraining |

Endoprosthetic reconstructions (proximal tibial replacement especially) require protection of the extensor mechanism repair with bracing in extension for the first 6 weeks before progressive flexion is allowed. Chemotherapy-receiving patients need rehabilitation explicitly coordinated around chemotherapy cycles, expecting slower, non-linear progression and dedicated conditioning work alongside limb-specific therapy.

Return to work: sedentary work typically 6–12 weeks after limb-salvage reconstruction once wound-healed and pain-controlled; physical labor delayed until adequate strength/endurance is demonstrated, often 4–6 months or longer, and may never be fully regained after proximal femoral/pelvic endoprosthetic reconstruction. Return to sport/high-impact activity is generally discouraged or significantly restricted after endoprosthetic reconstruction given implant longevity concerns; low-impact activity (swimming, cycling) is typically permitted once healed (~4–6 months).

Post-treatment surveillance: local imaging every 3 months years 1–2 then spacing out, with CT chest every 3 months years 1–2, then every 4–6 months years 3–5, then annually, for a minimum of 5–10 years given late-relapse potential (osteosarcoma/Ewing sarcoma).
