## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Flexor tendon repair is indicated for FDP and/or FDS lacerations across all Verdan zones, including FDP avulsion injuries ("jersey finger," typically Zone I). Zone II ("no man's land") carries the highest technical difficulty and adhesion/re-rupture risk given the tight fibro-osseous sheath housing both tendons together. Ideally repair is performed within days of injury (primary repair); delayed presentation up to roughly 2-3 weeks can generally still be repaired primarily in most zones.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Delayed presentation beyond approximately 3-4 weeks, particularly for FDP avulsion (jersey finger) with proximal tendon retraction into the palm or forearm, may preclude primary repair because of tendon retraction, myostatic contracture, and pulley/sheath collapse — staged reconstruction (silicone rod placement followed by tendon grafting) or DIP fusion as salvage should be planned instead.
- Grossly contaminated wounds, associated active infection, or a mangled/segmentally destroyed tendon with inadequate remaining length for a tension-free repair are relative contraindications to immediate primary repair; thorough debridement with delayed primary or staged reconstruction may be preferable.
- Segmental tendon loss precluding end-to-end approximation without excessive tension requires grafting rather than direct repair.
- A patient/social situation that cannot support a structured, supervised early active motion therapy program is a relative contraindication to an EAM-based rehabilitation strategy specifically (not to repair itself) — a more protected/passive motion protocol, or accepting a higher stiffness risk, should be planned instead.
- Concomitant unreconstructable neurovascular injury or severe associated fracture/bone loss may change repair sequencing and timing (bony stabilization typically precedes tendon repair).

## Preoperative Planning

- Document the zone of injury (Verdan I-V), which tendons are involved (FDP alone vs FDP+FDS), and whether the injury is a laceration versus an avulsion (jersey finger) — this determines both technique and urgency.
- For jersey finger, classify by Leddy-Packer type when possible (retraction to the palm with vinculum disruption is more urgent than retraction to the level of the A4/PIP with intact long vinculum) since retraction level governs repair method (suture anchor vs pull-out button) and time-sensitivity.
- Plan suture choice and core technique in advance: a 3-0 or 4-0 core suture (braided polyester or similar) in a ≥4-strand configuration (e.g., cross-locked cruciate/"Adelaide," Strickland, or a locking variant) plus a 6-0 circumferential epitendinous suture is the modern standard for early active motion protocols.
- Confirm hand therapy availability and a structured EAM program before committing to an early active motion protocol — the repair strength on the table is only half of the equation; supervised, protocol-driven therapy is required to safely mobilize the repair.
- Equipment: tenotomy/hand tendon set, fine-tipped forceps, tendon retrievers (pediatric feeding tube or similar to retrieve retracted proximal stumps), loupe magnification (or microscope for very small-caliber repairs), suture anchors or a pull-out button system on hand for avulsion injuries.
- Plan anesthesia around the need for intraoperative active motion testing (see below) — WALANT is strongly preferred for this reason in elective and many acute cases.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** WALANT (lidocaine + epinephrine, no tourniquet, no sedation) is increasingly the preferred anesthetic approach for elective and many acute flexor repairs, because it allows intraoperative active motion testing to confirm gliding and identify triggering/gapping before skin closure. Regional block or general anesthesia with tourniquet remains appropriate for multi-structure "spaghetti wrist" injuries, uncooperative/pediatric patients, or when concurrent bony/neurovascular repair is anticipated to take longer than local anesthesia comfortably allows.
- **Positioning:** supine, arm extended on a hand table.
- **Tourniquet:** if not using WALANT, exsanguinate and inflate a standard arm tourniquet; plan the case sequence to complete the tendon repair efficiently, since tourniquet time limits (~90-120 min typical) can become a factor in complex multi-tendon injuries.
- **Landmarks/incision planning:** extend traumatic wounds using standard zig-zag (Bruner) or mid-lateral incisions to gain adequate proximal and distal exposure of the sheath and to retrieve retracted tendon ends without creating flap-tip ischemia at incision apices.
- **Draping:** standard hand drape; if a pull-out button technique is planned, ensure the dorsal nail/fingertip is accessible in the field for button placement.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Expose the injury** through a Bruner or mid-lateral extension of the traumatic wound, opening the flexor sheath through a window between preserved pulleys (avoid incising directly through A2 or A4) to access the tendon ends.
2. **Retrieve the proximal tendon stump**, which typically retracts due to muscle tension; use a small feeding-tube/tendon retriever passed distally through the sheath and sutured or looped to the tendon end to deliver it into the wound without a separate proximal counter-incision when possible; if the stump has retracted into the palm or forearm, a separate proximal incision may be needed, and the wrist/MCP/IP joints can be flexed to reduce the retrieval distance.
3. **Debride frayed tendon ends minimally**, preserving as much length as possible; confirm both FDP and FDS (if both are injured) are identified and not mistaken for one another.
4. **Place the core suture** using a ≥4-strand technique (cross-locked cruciate/Adelaide, Strickland, or equivalent locking multi-strand configuration): pass the core suture longitudinally through each tendon end with grasping/locking loops placed roughly 7-10 mm from the cut edge, working from a dorsal position within the tendon substance where possible (the dorsal two-thirds of the tendon carries more of the tensile load and dorsal suture placement is biomechanically favorable and less disruptive to the palmar vincular blood supply).
5. **Approximate and tension the core suture**, tying knots buried within the repair site (not superficially, to minimize gliding resistance and gap formation) and confirming end-to-end apposition without gapping or excessive bunching.
6. **Place a circumferential epitendinous suture** (typically 6-0 nylon or polypropylene) in a running or running-locking fashion around the entire repair circumference; this adds meaningful additional strength (often cited as increasing repair strength substantially beyond the core suture alone), smooths the repair surface to reduce gliding resistance and catching on the pulley edges, and helps close any residual gap.
7. **Preserve the A2 and A4 pulleys.** If repair bulk causes visible triggering/catching as the tendon passes beneath a pulley, vent a portion of the pulley (partial release of one edge) rather than sacrificing it completely — venting more than 25-50% of a critical pulley risks clinically significant bowstringing.
8. **Repair a concomitantly injured FDS** if present (typically before or after FDP depending on exposure) using the same core-plus-epitendinous principle scaled to the thinner FDS slips; do not sacrifice an intact FDS slip to make room for the FDP repair.
9. **Test gliding intraoperatively.** Passively flex and extend the wrist (tenodesis effect) to observe smooth excursion of the repair through the pulley system; under WALANT, have the patient actively flex and extend the digit through a full comfortable range to directly confirm gliding, absence of gapping, and absence of triggering before closure — this is one of the most valuable advantages of awake local anesthesia in flexor tendon surgery.
10. **For FDP avulsion (jersey finger):** reattach the tendon to its distal phalanx footprint using a suture anchor (preferred when adequate distal bone stock and minimal retraction) or a pull-out button technique passed through a drill hole in the distal phalanx and tied over a button on the fingernail (useful for very distal/small bony fragments or when anchor purchase is inadequate); match technique to retraction level and remaining vincular supply, and proceed promptly since delayed presentation beyond roughly 3-4 weeks may preclude primary repair.
11. **Close the sheath window loosely** if possible without constricting the repair, then close skin; apply a dorsal blocking splint before the patient leaves the OR so the first therapy visit starts from a protected position.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Place core suture purchase points dorsally within the tendon substance when feasible — this loads the biomechanically stronger dorsal portion of the tendon and better preserves the palmar vincular blood supply.
- Bury core suture knots within the repair bulk, not on the tendon surface, to minimize friction against the pulley and reduce gap-prone weak points.
- Always complete the case with a circumferential epitendinous suture — it is not optional for an early active motion protocol; it meaningfully increases repair strength and smooths the repair for gliding.
- Use a pediatric feeding tube (or equivalent soft retriever) passed through the sheath to retrieve a retracted proximal stump atraumatically rather than grasping the tendon directly with forceps, which can fray the end you are about to suture.
- Flex the wrist and adjacent joints to reduce tension when retrieving a retracted proximal tendon end — this often eliminates the need for a separate proximal incision.
- Test gliding with passive tenodesis motion before closing sheath/skin, and — under WALANT — with true active motion; catching or gapping identified on the table can still be revised, but the same problem discovered in the therapy gym cannot.
- When venting a pulley for repair bulk, vent only as much as needed to eliminate catching (often a partial, not complete, release of one pulley edge) — over-venting is the direct cause of bowstringing.
- In multi-digit injuries, repair each tendon methodically and verify each finger independently through full active/passive motion — a repair that looks fine in isolation can still be functionally compromised by adjacent quadriga effects if an FDP repair is over-advanced.
- For jersey finger, act promptly once retraction to the palm is identified — proximal retraction disrupts the long vinculum's blood supply and shortens the window for primary repair; this is a more time-sensitive injury than an isolated FDS laceration.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Tendon rupture risk is highest in the first 3-6 weeks, especially days 7-21, when suture strength is lowest relative to healing tissue.
- Adhesion formation/stiffness is the major cause of poor outcome, especially in Zone II.
- Pulley bowstringing occurs if A2/A4 are over-vented rather than preserved or judiciously vented.
- Quadriga (over-advancement of FDP, avoid >1 cm advancement) causes incomplete flexion of adjacent, uninjured fingers.
- Lumbrical-plus deformity and infection are additional recognized complications.
- Re-rupture rates with modern EAM protocols in Zone II are commonly reported in the 4-10% range in specialized hand therapy settings — lower than older passive-motion-only protocols' adhesion/stiffness burden, but EAM requires close therapy supervision and patient compliance and should not be used without a structured, monitored therapy program.
- Grasping the raw tendon end with toothed forceps during retrieval can fray tissue that then compromises core suture purchase — use atraumatic retrieval methods instead.
- Superficial (non-buried) knot placement increases gliding resistance and catching against pulley edges, contributing to both triggering and gap formation under load.
- Missing a concomitant FDS injury (mistaking it for an uninjured slip, or sacrificing it unnecessarily) worsens functional outcome even when the FDP repair itself is technically sound.

## Postoperative Protocol

Early active motion (EAM) protocol, contingent on ≥4-strand core repair:

- **Days 0-3:** dorsal blocking splint (wrist ~20-30° flexion, MCP ~40-60° flexion, IP free/near full extension); begin place-and-hold or true active flexion (per surgeon protocol — e.g., modified Duran, Indiana/Strickland-based active protocols, or relative motion flexion for Zone I-III) within the first 3-5 days under hand therapy supervision.
- **Weeks 3-4:** progress from protected active motion to increased active ROM within the splint; initiate gentle blocking exercises as healing allows.
- **Weeks 4-6:** discontinue splint for controlled activities, progress AROM, begin light functional use.
- **Weeks 6-8:** begin passive stretching for residual stiffness, light resistance/putty exercises.
- **Weeks 8-12:** progressive strengthening; return to full unrestricted activity/heavy labor and contact sport generally not before 10-12 weeks, guided by tendon gliding and strength milestones rather than calendar alone.
