## Indications

Full-thickness rotator cuff tears in symptomatic patients who fail an adequate nonoperative trial, or who present acutely (traumatic tear, significant weakness) — earlier surgery is favored in the traumatic setting, ideally within ~6 weeks before retraction and atrophy progress. Also indicated for progressive tears on serial imaging, and for most tears in young/high-demand patients regardless of size once symptomatic. Trial length before surgery is individualized: 3–6 months of structured physiotherapy in low-demand/older patients with atraumatic degenerative tears, versus a shorter 4–6 week trial in active/younger patients with high-grade partial or full tears.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed beyond the general arthroscopic shoulder setup implied by the technique (standard beach-chair or lateral decubitus arthroscopic positioning, subacromial and glenohumeral portal access for cuff visualization, mobilization, and fixation).

## Steps

1. **Assess reparability** using Cofield (size), Patte (retraction), and Goutallier (fatty infiltration) grades, plus the tangent sign — confirm the tear is a repair candidate before committing to a fixation construct.
2. **Select repair construct by tear size**:
   - **Single-row repair**: simpler, faster, lower cost; adequate for small–medium tears (<3 cm), where meta-analyses show no significant functional or retear-rate difference versus double-row.
   - **Double-row / transosseous-equivalent (suture-bridge) repair**: restores footprint contact area and pressure more anatomically; consistently shows lower retear rates and better functional scores for large/massive tears (≥3 cm) versus single-row, with no clear benefit for small tears. Knotted vs. knotless double-row constructs show no significant retear-rate difference, so construct choice can follow surgeon preference/cost.
3. **Mobilize a retracted tendon before declaring it irreparable**: release the rotator interval and perform an anterior or posterior interval slide as needed. Keep release purposeful — excessive subscapularis or capsular release can destabilize the joint.
4. **If the tear cannot be fully reduced to the footprint**, perform margin convalescence (partial repair) — repair what is mobile and accept a smaller residual defect; this still improves function even without complete footprint restoration.
5. **Tension the construct correctly**: for double-row/suture-bridge repair, tension the medial row first, then lay the lateral row to restore native footprint pressure without over-medializing the musculotendinous junction. Avoid over-tensioning, which causes "gap-strangulation" of vascularity at the repair site and worsens healing independent of tear size.
6. **Decide on concomitant acromioplasty selectively, not routinely** — the AAOS 2025 CPG does not support routine subacromial decompression/acromioplasty as adding therapeutic benefit over repair alone for small-medium tears; reserve it for a clearly hooked/impinging acromion morphology or bursal pathology that needs debridement for visualization.
7. **Address the long head of biceps concomitantly if pathologic** (fraying, subluxation, partial tear, or SLAP-associated instability) — tenodesis or tenotomy is commonly performed at the same setting as cuff repair.

## Pitfalls

- Over-tensioning the repair causes gap-strangulation of vascularity at the repair site — this is a distinct failure mechanism from an inadequately sized construct and is easy to overlook.
- Excessive subscapularis or capsular release during mobilization can destabilize the joint; release should be purposeful and only as extensive as needed to achieve tension-free apposition.
- Retear/failure of healing is the dominant complication and scales with tear size and technique: small tears <10–20%, large/massive tears up to 40–90% depending on definition and imaging modality used for detection. Risk factors include tear size, chronicity, Goutallier ≥2, smoking, diabetes, poor tendon quality, and revision setting.
- Avoid subacromial corticosteroid injection shortly (<3 months, ideally longer) before a planned repair given concerns about tendon quality/healing.
- Stiffness/adhesive capsulitis is more common after aggressive early immobilization or in predisposed patients (diabetics) — usually responds to PT, occasionally needs manipulation or arthroscopic capsular release.
- Infection risk is low but real (<1–2%) with arthroscopic repair.
- Anchor pullout/hardware failure is a particular concern in osteoporotic bone — consider medializing anchors or augmenting fixation in elderly patients.
- PRP/biologic augmentation is an area of active investigation (a focus of the AAOS 2025 CPG's "optimizing the healing environment" emphasis) but evidence remains mixed/heterogeneous — treat as an adjunct discussion, not a substitute for sound mechanical repair.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: sling immobilization (abduction pillow if tension is high); passive ROM only (pendulum, passive forward elevation and external rotation within surgeon-specified limits); no active ROM or lifting. The AAOS 2025 CPG notes outcomes are similar for early vs. delayed (up to 8 weeks) mobilization for small-medium tears, so protocol timing can be individualized to repair tension/quality rather than treated as fixed dogma.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling; progress to active-assisted then active ROM; initiate scapular stabilization and light isometrics; avoid resisted strengthening before ~8–12 weeks.
- **Weeks 12–16**: progressive resistive strengthening (theraband, light weights); restore full active ROM.
- **Months 4–6**: sport/heavy-labor-specific strengthening, plyometrics for overhead athletes.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often by 2–6 weeks (non-operative arm tasks); manual labor typically 4–6 months depending on repair size and job demand; graded/light-duty return often possible earlier (6–10 weeks) with formal work-hardening before full unrestricted duty.
- **Return to sport**: non-contact/non-overhead sport ~4–6 months; overhead/throwing and contact sport 6–9 months, contingent on full painless ROM, ≥90% (85–90%) strength symmetry, negative lag signs, and (where available) imaging confirmation of healing for high-risk repairs (massive tears, revision surgery, elite athletes).
