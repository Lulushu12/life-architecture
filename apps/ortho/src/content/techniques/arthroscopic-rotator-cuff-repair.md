## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Full-thickness rotator cuff tears in symptomatic patients who fail an adequate nonoperative trial.
- Acute traumatic tears with significant weakness (pseudoparalysis, positive drop-arm/lag signs) — earlier surgery is favored, ideally within ~6 weeks before retraction and fatty infiltration progress.
- Progressive tear enlargement on serial imaging.
- Most tears in young/high-demand patients regardless of size once symptomatic.
- Trial length before surgery is individualized: 3–6 months of structured physiotherapy in low-demand/older patients with atraumatic degenerative tears, versus a shorter 4–6 week trial in active/younger patients with high-grade partial or full tears.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active glenohumeral or subacromial infection — treat/eradicate before proceeding.
- Advanced cuff tear arthropathy with glenohumeral arthritis and pseudoparalysis — favor reverse total shoulder arthroplasty over repair.
- Irreparable tear pattern on imaging/exam (Goutallier ≥3–4 in two or more muscles, Patte 3 retraction with fixed contracture, positive tangent sign) in an older, low-demand patient — consider salvage (partial repair, SCR, tendon transfer) or nonoperative management instead.
- Axillary or suprascapular nerve palsy as the primary cause of weakness — investigate and address the neurologic lesion; repairing a structurally intact-but-weak cuff will not correct nerve-driven pseudoparalysis.
- Uncontrolled medical comorbidities precluding elective surgery or postoperative sling compliance (e.g., significant cognitive impairment, poorly controlled seizure disorder).
- Active, unaddressed smoking or uncontrolled diabetes are not absolute contraindications but should be discussed explicitly — both materially raise retear risk and should factor into shared decision-making, especially for large/massive tears.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI (or MR arthrogram if labral pathology suspected) to grade tear size (Cofield), retraction (Patte), and fatty infiltration (Goutallier) on sagittal oblique sequences; true AP/Grashey and axillary radiographs for acromiohumeral distance, critical shoulder angle, glenohumeral arthritis, and calcific deposits. Confirm tear pattern (crescent, U-shaped, L-shaped) preoperatively where possible — this changes the mobilization/fixation strategy.
- **Reparability assessment**: correlate Cofield/Patte/Goutallier/tangent-sign findings with exam (active vs passive ROM, lag signs) before consenting the patient — discuss the possibility of intraoperative conversion from repair to partial repair/margin convalescence or debridement if the tissue proves unreparable.
- **Implant selection**: single-row anchor set for small-medium tears vs a double-row/suture-bridge (transosseous-equivalent) construct for large-massive tears; stock both anchor types and adequate numbers (typically 2 medial-row anchors minimum, 2 lateral-row anchors for a standard suture-bridge) plus a range of suture-anchor diameters for variable bone quality. Knotless vs knotted lateral row is surgeon preference — no proven retear-rate difference.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: standard 30° arthroscope, radiofrequency ablation and mechanical shaver, suture passers/shuttling devices (spinal needle, suture-passing grasper, or dedicated cuff-repair passer), knot pusher (if knotted), anchor driver/tap, and — for retracted tears — releases instruments (elevators, arthroscopic scissors/graspers for interval slide).
- **Biceps plan**: decide preoperatively (and confirm intraoperatively) whether concomitant LHB tenodesis or tenotomy will be performed if pathology is found — have tenodesis implants (interference screw, suture anchor, or cortical button) available.
- **Anesthesia coordination**: discuss interscalene block with anesthesia in advance; confirm no contraindication (contralateral phrenic nerve palsy, severe COPD).
- **Osteoporotic bone**: for elderly patients, plan medialized anchor position or an augmented/all-suture anchor construct to reduce pull-out risk.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia combined with an interscalene regional block is standard — the block provides postoperative analgesia and allows lighter general anesthesia intraoperatively; controlled hypotensive anesthesia (mean arterial pressure ~60–65 mmHg, coordinated with anesthesia) improves arthroscopic visualization by reducing bleeding.
- **Position**: either **beach-chair** (semi-sitting, ~60–70° trunk elevation, head secured in a headrest, operative arm free-draped or held in a mechanical arm-holder) or **lateral decubitus** (operative side up, axillary roll under the down side, arm in balanced skin/skeletal traction, typically 5–10 lb, arm abducted ~30–45° and forward flexed ~15–20°) — surgeon preference; beach-chair allows easier conversion to open procedures and more intuitive orientation, lateral decubitus offers superior distraction/visualization of the glenohumeral joint and is favored by some for SLAP/labral work performed concomitantly.
- **Table setup**: beach-chair requires a dedicated shoulder positioner/table attachment; secure the torso with straps, pad the contralateral arm and all bony prominences, and keep the head neutral and well-supported to avoid cervical strain, particularly with an interscalene block in place (patient cannot report positional discomfort intraoperatively).
- **Draping**: wide sterile exposure from the base of the neck to well past the mid-humerus, including the entire scapula posteriorly, to allow portal flexibility and possible open extension.
- **Fluid management**: arthroscopic pump with pressure typically set 40–60 mmHg, titrated to visualization and patient hemodynamics; monitor for fluid extravasation into the deltoid/chest wall.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: acromion border (anterior, lateral, posterior), coracoid process, AC joint, and planned portal sites (posterior "soft spot," anterolateral, anterior, and lateral subacromial portals) — mark with a sterile pen after prep to keep orientation once swelling begins.
- **No routine fluoroscopy** is required for standard arthroscopic cuff repair; intraoperative imaging is reserved for cases with associated hardware or bony pathology.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Establish the posterior viewing portal** ~2 cm inferior and 1–2 cm medial to the posterolateral acromion corner ("soft spot"), enter the glenohumeral joint with the trocar directed toward the coracoid process, and perform a diagnostic glenohumeral arthroscopy — assess the labrum, biceps anchor/LHB tendon, subscapularis, and articular-sided cuff surface.
2. **Establish the anterior portal** using an outside-in spinal-needle localization technique through the rotator interval, just lateral to the coracoid tip, under direct visualization; place a cannula to allow instrument passage and, if indicated, address concomitant biceps or labral pathology at this stage.
3. **Redirect into the subacromial space**: withdraw the scope from the glenohumeral joint, redirect posteriorly and superiorly into the subacromial bursa, and perform a bursectomy with the shaver/radiofrequency device to obtain a clear working view of the bursal-sided cuff, acromion undersurface, and coracoacromial ligament.
4. **Establish the lateral (working) portal** ~3 cm lateral to the acromion edge, in line with the tear, again using spinal-needle localization to confirm optimal angle of approach to the footprint.
5. **Assess reparability** using Cofield (size), Patte (retraction), and Goutallier (fatty infiltration, from preoperative MRI) grades, plus intraoperative tear pattern and mobility with a grasper — confirm the tear is a genuine repair candidate before committing to a fixation construct; if not reparable, pivot to partial repair/margin convalescence or a salvage plan discussed preoperatively.
6. **Prepare the footprint**: debride frayed, non-viable tendon edge back to healthy tissue, and lightly decorticate the greater tuberosity footprint with a shaver or bur to a bleeding cancellous bed (avoid aggressive decortication, which weakens bone stock for anchor purchase).
7. **Mobilize a retracted tendon before declaring it irreparable**: release the rotator interval (coracohumeral ligament) and perform an anterior and/or posterior interval slide as needed to restore excursion. Keep releases purposeful — excessive subscapularis or capsular release can destabilize the joint; release only as much as needed to achieve tension-free apposition to the footprint.
8. **Select and place the repair construct by tear size**:
   - **Single-row repair** (small–medium tears, <3 cm): place 2–3 suture anchors along the footprint at the articular margin, pass sutures through the tendon (mattress or simple configuration) using a suture-passing device, and tie sequentially, working medial-to-lateral or in the sequence that best restores the native tear shape (crescent vs U/L-shaped tears benefit from margin-convergence sutures placed first in the latter).
   - **Double-row / transosseous-equivalent (suture-bridge) repair** (large–massive tears, ≥3 cm): place medial-row anchors as far medial as possible on the articular margin/near-cartilage junction, pass and tie (or use knotless technology to load) the medial sutures through the tendon, then tension the limbs laterally over the footprint and fix into lateral-row anchors placed at or just distal to the greater tuberosity's lateral edge, compressing the tendon onto the prepared bone.
9. **Tension the construct correctly**: for double-row/suture-bridge repair, tension the medial row first, then lay the lateral row to restore native footprint pressure without over-medializing the musculotendinous junction. Avoid over-tensioning, which causes "gap-strangulation" of vascularity at the repair site and worsens healing independent of construct or tear size.
10. **If the tear cannot be fully reduced to the footprint**, perform margin convalescence (partial repair) — repair what is mobile and accept a smaller residual defect, converging the tear margins with side-to-side sutures before anchor fixation if applicable; this still improves function even without complete footprint restoration.
11. **Confirm provisional fixation**: probe the repair with a grasper to check tendon-to-bone apposition circumferentially, cycle the shoulder through a gentle range of motion arthroscopically to confirm no gapping or anchor pull-through, and inspect suture limbs for fraying or impingement against anchor eyelets.
12. **Address the long head of biceps concomitantly if pathologic** (fraying, subluxation, partial tear, or SLAP-associated instability) — perform tenodesis or tenotomy at the same setting rather than staging it separately.
13. **Decide on concomitant acromioplasty selectively, not routinely** — reserve it for a clearly hooked/impinging acromion morphology or bursal pathology that needs debridement for visualization; do not perform it reflexively as an adjunct to repair for small-medium tears.
14. **Final inspection and closure**: irrigate the subacromial space and joint thoroughly to clear debris, remove instruments and cannulas under direct visualization to avoid suture entanglement, close portal skin incisions with simple sutures or adhesive strips, and apply a sterile dressing followed by the sling/immobilizer.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use an outside-in spinal-needle check for every new portal before committing to the incision — it confirms trajectory to the target and avoids a portal that is too vertical or too horizontal relative to the footprint.
- Place medial-row anchors as far medial as safely possible (near the articular cartilage margin) to maximize footprint coverage and restore contact area/pressure most anatomically.
- When passing sutures through thin or friable tendon, use a mattress (horizontal or vertical) configuration rather than simple sutures — it distributes load over a larger tendon area and resists cut-through.
- For U- or L-shaped tears, place margin-convergence (side-to-side) sutures first to convert the tear into a more favorable crescent shape before anchor fixation — this reduces tension at the final bone-tendon interface.
- Controlled hypotensive anesthesia (coordinated with anesthesia colleagues) meaningfully improves visualization during bursectomy and footprint preparation — request it explicitly rather than relying on pump pressure alone.
- Cycle the arm through a gentle range of motion after final fixation, arthroscopically, to confirm the repair does not gap with physiologic motion before leaving the OR.
- In osteoporotic bone, consider a medialized anchor row, larger-diameter or all-suture anchors, or supplementing with an additional row/rip-stop stitch to reduce pull-out risk.
- Keep bursectomy and acromial decompression conservative — over-aggressive bursectomy can devascularize the bursal-sided cuff and worsen healing potential, and unnecessary acromioplasty adds surgical time without proven benefit for small-medium tears.
- When mobilizing a retracted tear, release the interval and perform interval slides incrementally, reassessing tendon excursion after each step, rather than performing a maximal release up front — this avoids unnecessary destabilization of the joint.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Over-tensioning the repair causes gap-strangulation of vascularity at the repair site — this is a distinct failure mechanism from an inadequately sized construct and is easy to overlook.
- Excessive subscapularis or capsular release during mobilization can destabilize the joint; release should be purposeful and only as extensive as needed to achieve tension-free apposition.
- Retear/failure of healing is the dominant complication and scales with tear size and technique: small tears <10–20%, large/massive tears up to 40–90% in some series depending on definition and imaging modality used for detection. Risk factors include tear size, chronicity, Goutallier ≥2, smoking, diabetes, poor tendon quality, and revision setting.
- Avoid subacromial corticosteroid injection shortly (<3 months, ideally longer) before a planned repair given concerns about tendon quality/healing.
- Stiffness/adhesive capsulitis is more common after aggressive early immobilization or in predisposed patients (diabetics) — usually responds to PT, occasionally needs manipulation or arthroscopic capsular release.
- Infection risk is low but real (<1–2%) with arthroscopic repair.
- Anchor pullout/hardware failure is a particular concern in osteoporotic bone — consider medializing anchors or augmenting fixation in elderly patients.
- Aggressive footprint decortication can paradoxically weaken bone stock and reduce anchor purchase — decorticate to a bleeding bed, not to raw exposed cancellous trough.
- Suture abrasion/breakage at the anchor eyelet is an under-recognized cause of early construct failure — inspect suture limbs for fraying during passage and avoid sharp turns over anchor edges.
- PRP/biologic augmentation is an area of active investigation (a focus of the AAOS 2025 CPG's "optimizing the healing environment" emphasis) but evidence remains mixed/heterogeneous — treat as an adjunct discussion, not a substitute for sound mechanical repair.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: sling immobilization (abduction pillow if tension is high); passive ROM only (pendulum, passive forward elevation and external rotation within surgeon-specified limits); no active ROM or lifting. The AAOS 2025 CPG notes outcomes are similar for early vs. delayed (up to 8 weeks) mobilization for small-medium tears, so protocol timing can be individualized to repair tension/quality rather than treated as fixed dogma.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling; progress to active-assisted then active ROM; initiate scapular stabilization and light isometrics; avoid resisted strengthening before ~8–12 weeks.
- **Weeks 12–16**: progressive resistive strengthening (theraband, light weights); restore full active ROM.
- **Months 4–6**: sport/heavy-labor-specific strengthening, plyometrics for overhead athletes.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often by 2–6 weeks (non-operative arm tasks); manual labor typically 4–6 months depending on repair size and job demand; graded/light-duty return often possible earlier (6–10 weeks) with formal work-hardening before full unrestricted duty.
- **Return to sport**: non-contact/non-overhead sport ~4–6 months; overhead/throwing and contact sport 6–9 months, contingent on full painless ROM, ≥90% (85–90%) strength symmetry, negative lag signs, and (where available) imaging confirmation of healing for high-risk repairs (massive tears, revision surgery, elite athletes).
