## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Multilevel degenerative cervical myelopathy/OPLL with pre-existing kyphosis requiring correction — fusion allows correction/stabilization not achievable with laminoplasty.
- Significant segmental instability, or a prior failed/contraindicated laminoplasty.
- K-line-negative, high canal-occupying-ratio OPLL requiring lordosis restoration, where posterior indirect decompression alone (laminoplasty) is predicted to be inadequate.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Preserved cervical lordosis with low complication tolerance — laminoplasty is typically preferred instead given its lower morbidity, shorter OR time, and lower blood loss.
- Severe osteoporosis without a plan for screw augmentation or extended construct — lateral mass screw pullout is a real risk in poor bone.
- Active infection at the planned surgical site.
- Patient factors that make prolonged prone positioning or extended anesthesia unsafe (severe cardiopulmonary disease) without appropriate optimization.

## Preoperative Planning

- MRI to define compression pattern and cord signal change; CT for bony anatomy, lateral mass/pedicle morphology at each planned instrumented level, and (in OPLL) canal-occupying ratio and morphologic type.
- Flexion-extension radiographs to quantify baseline kyphosis and instability, and to set the intraoperative lordosis-correction goal (C2–C7 Cobb angle target, sagittal vertical axis).
- **Construct planning**: decide fusion levels and screw type per level — lateral mass screws (Magerl, Anderson, or An trajectory) for C3–C6, and pars or pedicle screws at C7/C2 as needed for a longer construct; template screw length and trajectory on preoperative CT, particularly at levels with anatomic variance (short pedicles, high-riding vertebral artery, prior laminectomy).
- Have rod contouring templates/benders available intraoperatively to match the planned lordosis correction; consider a reduction/derotation technique plan if correcting fixed kyphotic deformity.
- Autograft (local laminectomy bone, iliac crest if needed) vs. allograft/DBM decision for the fusion bed.
- Neuromonitoring (SSEP/MEP) booking for severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery, or any case with a deformity-correction component; establish an intraoperative signal-change response protocol (MAP augmentation, halt correction, wake-up test) with anesthesia in advance.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General endotracheal anesthesia; avoid excessive neck extension during intubation and positioning in severe stenosis or myelopathy — awake fiberoptic intubation is reasonable in severe myelopathy with unstable or high-grade stenosis to avoid a positioning-related cord injury.
- Prone position on a radiolucent frame (e.g., Jackson table) or horseshoe headrest, or rigid cranial fixation (Mayfield tongs) for cases anticipating deformity correction or where maximal head stability is desired; obtain baseline neuromonitoring signals before final positioning and again after, to detect any positioning-related change before incision.
- Neck flexed only to the degree the pathology and canal reserve tolerate — avoid excessive flexion, which can paradoxically narrow the canal in a stenotic segment; position in as close to neutral/slight flexion as achievable for exposure while being mindful of baseline alignment.
- Shoulders taped caudally for lower cervical/upper thoracic fluoroscopic access if instrumentation extends to C7/T1.
- Arms tucked, padded pressure points, eyes protected and periodically checked (risk of postoperative vision loss with prolonged prone positioning), abdomen free-hanging to reduce venous pressure and intraoperative bleeding.
- Prep and drape from occiput to mid-thoracic level depending on planned construct length; obtain a lateral fluoroscopic image to confirm level counting before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the case favors fusion over laminoplasty**: pre-existing kyphosis requiring correction, significant instability, prior failed/contraindicated laminoplasty, or K-line-negative high-canal-occupying-ratio OPLL.
2. **Midline posterior incision and subperiosteal exposure** of the spinous processes, laminae, and lateral masses bilaterally at all levels to be decompressed and instrumented, preserving the facet capsules at levels not being fused where possible.
3. **Confirm levels with fluoroscopy** before proceeding with bony work.
4. **Laminectomy.** Remove the spinous processes and laminae at the involved levels with a high-speed burr and Kerrison rongeurs, working from a previously decompressed or more normal level toward the most stenotic level to avoid working "blind" against a maximally compressed cord; thin the lamina with a burr before final removal with Kerrisons to avoid cord contact from an angled rongeur tip.
5. **Complete the decompression** by removing ligamentum flavum and any residual compressive elements (facet-joint medial hypertrophy, foraminal osteophyte) at symptomatic levels, and undercut the facet joints just enough to relieve foraminal compression without destabilizing them at non-fused adjacent levels.
6. **Lateral mass screw placement.** For each instrumented level (typically C3–C6), select an entry point and trajectory — the Magerl technique starts 1 mm medial and 2 mm superior to the center of the lateral mass, angled roughly 30° cephalad and 25° lateral; the Anderson and An techniques use more medial entry points with more caudally-directed trajectories and have been shown in comparative anatomic studies to carry a lower rate of nerve root violation (An technique lowest) at some cost to screw length/pullout strength. Choose a consistent technique per level and confirm trajectory against a Penfield elevator placed in the facet joint to be included in fusion, keeping the drill parallel to it to avoid facet violation.
7. **Drill and tap under controlled technique.** Use the drill at high speed, advancing only a short distance at a time and backing out repeatedly rather than plunging, feeling for the second (ventral) cortex; avoid excessive bicortical penetration, which risks the exiting nerve root or vertebral artery (the artery lies anterior to the lateral mass and is protected primarily by adequate lateral angulation, not by depth control alone).
8. **Place screws**, confirming purchase and trajectory with intraoperative fluoroscopy or navigation if available; at C7 or in a long construct extending to the cervicothoracic junction, consider pars or pedicle screws for stronger fixation given C7's smaller lateral mass.
9. **Rod contouring and lordosis correction.** Contour dual rods to the target cervical lordosis; sequentially seat and provisionally tighten screw-rod connections, using controlled reduction maneuvers (segmental compression/distraction, cantilever technique) to correct kyphotic segments toward the templated goal — perform any correction maneuver gradually, checking neuromonitoring signals throughout if in use.
10. **Final tightening and construct check.** Torque all set screws to specification, confirm no hardware impingement on the decompressed neural elements, and obtain AP/lateral fluoroscopy to document final screw position and achieved lordosis.
11. **Decorticate the lateral masses and facet joints** (at fused levels) and lay autograft/allograft along the posterolateral gutters and over the decorticated bone to promote fusion.
12. **Hemostasis and closure.** Achieve meticulous hemostasis, particularly from the epidural venous plexus and muscle bed; place a subfascial drain given the higher blood loss of this procedure relative to laminoplasty; close fascia, subcutaneous layer, and skin in layers.
13. **Postoperative imaging.** Obtain upright or supine radiographs (and CT if concern for screw malposition) before mobilization to confirm construct integrity and alignment.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Decompress from a less-stenotic level toward the most severely stenotic level when performing the laminectomy — this avoids instrumenting Kerrison rongeurs "blind" against a maximally compressed, non-compliant cord segment where there is no room for even a small angled tool tip.
- Use a Penfield elevator seated in the target facet joint as a live trajectory guide during lateral mass screw drilling — keeping the drill parallel to the elevator is a simple, reliable way to avoid facet violation without relying on angle estimation alone.
- Choose lateral mass screw technique deliberately rather than by habit: Magerl's steeper cephalad trajectory gives a longer, biomechanically stronger screw but carries a higher reported rate of nerve root violation in comparative anatomic studies; the An technique's more caudal trajectory reduces nerve violation risk at some cost to screw purchase — match the technique to the bone quality and biomechanical demand of the specific case.
- Advance the lateral mass drill in short increments with frequent withdrawal ("peck drilling") rather than a continuous plunge, to feel the second cortex reliably and avoid over-penetration that can compromise the exiting nerve root.
- Correct kyphosis gradually and in stages across multiple levels rather than attempting maximal correction at a single segment — distributing the correction reduces focal stress on any one screw-rod interface and reduces the risk of an acute, uncompensated cord stretch during reduction.
- Preserve facet capsules at levels immediately adjacent to the planned fusion when they are not themselves being instrumented, to limit iatrogenic extension of instability beyond the intended construct.
- Have a low threshold to check neuromonitoring signals specifically during the lordosis-correction/reduction maneuver, not just during decompression — correction is a distinct risk window for cord traction injury.
- Use local autograft generated from the laminectomy itself as the primary fusion substrate at the lateral mass/facet gutters before considering iliac crest harvest, reserving the latter for cases with inadequate local bone stock.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Higher blood loss and longer OR time** than laminoplasty — plan blood availability and anesthesia time accordingly, particularly for long multilevel constructs.
- **C5 palsy**: occurs at rates similar to or higher than laminoplasty in some series; typically deltoid/biceps weakness, mechanism debated (nerve root tethering during decompression, or direct injury during lateral mass screw placement/foraminal undercutting).
- **Lateral mass/pedicle screw malposition**: risks the exiting nerve root (more common with steeper Magerl-type trajectories per comparative anatomic data) or, less commonly, the vertebral artery with excessive medial angulation or bicortical over-penetration — use controlled, incremental drilling and confirm trajectory against a facet-joint reference.
- **Loss of motion with attendant adjacent segment risk** — a trade-off against laminoplasty's motion preservation; extending the construct unnecessarily beyond the pathologic levels compounds this.
- **Loss of cervical lordosis/progressive kyphosis is a risk of laminectomy performed without fusion** — this is precisely why current practice favors laminectomy + fusion over laminectomy alone.
- **Wound infection, epidural hematoma**: standard posterior spine surgery risks; hematoma is particularly concerning given baseline cord compromise — low threshold for urgent imaging with any post-op neurological decline.
- **Screw pullout/loosening** in osteoporotic bone — consider bicortical purchase where safe, screw augmentation, or an extended construct in poor bone quality.
- **Comparative evidence** (2024–2025 meta-analyses): laminectomy-fusion shows better lordosis maintenance/correction and, specifically in OPLL, better control of OPLL progression than laminoplasty, but at the cost of longer OR time, more blood loss, and comparable-or-higher complication/C5 palsy rates.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: collar use more consistent in multilevel laminectomy-fusion constructs than in laminoplasty; inpatient mobilization with PT/OT from day 1 given baseline myelopathic gait/balance impairment and elevated fall risk.
- **Weeks 2–6**: gait training progression, gentle ROM, monitor for delayed C5 palsy (can present days to ~2 weeks post-op).
- **Weeks 6–12**: formal outpatient PT with proprioceptive/balance training, strengthening, hand dexterity/fine motor rehabilitation (occupational therapy referral valuable for hand-intensive deficits).
- **Months 3–12+**: neurological recovery is gradual and can continue for 12–24 months post-decompression; mJOA recovery ratio typically assessed at 1 year but continued gains documented beyond that in a subset.
- **Return to work**: sedentary 2–6 weeks; manual labor individualized, often delayed 3+ months given fusion/bone healing. Return to contact sport is generally discouraged — both construct integrity and underlying cord vulnerability argue against it.
