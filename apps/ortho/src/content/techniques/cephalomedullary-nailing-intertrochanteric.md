## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Cephalomedullary nailing (CMN) is mandatory for unstable intertrochanteric patterns: reverse obliquity (Evans V), transverse fractures, subtrochanteric extension, thin/fractured lateral wall (<20.5 mm, i.e. AO 31-A2 with an incompetent lateral wall, or 31-A3), or gross comminution. The sliding hip screw's mechanism depends on an intact lateral wall to resist medialization of the shaft, and these patterns fixed with SHS have unacceptably high cutout/fixation-failure rates. For stable patterns (31-A1, most reducible A2, Evans I–II), CMN and a sliding hip screw (SHS) have statistically equivalent union and functional outcomes, so either is reasonable; SHS is a lower-cost, evidence-supported alternative for these patterns.

**Contraindications/relative contraindications:**

- Active local or systemic infection precluding implantation of hardware (treat septic focus first; consider external fixation as a bridge in the physiologically unstable patient).
- Non-ambulatory, severely cognitively impaired, or comfort-care patients where surgery would not change functional trajectory — non-operative management is a reasonable, if uncommon, alternative.
- Deformity or pre-existing implant (long-stem arthroplasty, retained hardware) that precludes an antegrade canal — plan for an alternative implant or nail-around-implant strategy pre-operatively rather than discovering this intraoperatively.
- Skeletally immature patients — different entry-point and physeal-preserving considerations apply and are outside standard adult trauma nailing technique.
- Medical instability precluding anesthesia — optimize per the 24–48 h fragility-fracture pathway rather than delaying indefinitely; true contraindications to timely surgery are rare and should be specific and correctable (reversible anticoagulation, active cardiac ischemia, uncontrolled electrolyte/glucose derangement).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP pelvis, AP and cross-table lateral hip. CT is used selectively to characterize lateral wall integrity, posteromedial comminution, and coronal-plane (Hoffa-type) fragments when plain films are ambiguous or a reverse-obliquity/A3 pattern is suspected.
- **Templating:** template the contralateral hip (or use intraoperative fluoroscopy of the uninjured side) for neck-shaft angle, femoral bow, and canal diameter. Confirm nail length (short vs. long) and diameter against the fracture pattern — any subtrochanteric extension, reverse obliquity, or coronal-plane fragment mandates a long nail that bypasses the entire zone of comminution.
- **Implant selection:** choose lag screw vs. helical blade, and single vs. dual (integrated screw-and-blade or dual-screw) lag device, based on bone quality and rotational stability of the pattern — dual-lag constructs add rotational control in osteoporotic, unstable patterns.
- **Equipment:** fracture table (or radiolucent flat-top table for freehand technique) with a well-leg holder, C-arm capable of unobstructed orthogonal (true AP and true lateral) imaging of the hip, full CMN instrument set including the entry reamer/awl, ball-tipped and olive-tipped guidewires, blocking (Poller) screw set, and percutaneous reduction clamps available on standby.
- **Blood/anesthesia planning:** type and screen (crossmatch if significant comminution/expected blood loss); confirm tranexamic acid dosing plan and VTE prophylaxis with anesthesia in advance.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** spinal or general anesthesia per patient factors and anesthesia team preference; a fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block placed pre- or intra-operatively meaningfully reduces opioid requirement and delirium risk and should be standard.
- **Positioning:** supine on a fracture table with the affected limb secured in a foot holder under longitudinal traction, and the contralateral leg either abducted and flexed in a well-leg holder (to allow C-arm access for the true lateral) or positioned flat with the C-arm swept beneath. A freehand (non-traction, radiolucent table) technique is a valid alternative, particularly for reverse-obliquity or subtrochanteric-extension patterns where table traction can distract the fracture into an unfavorable position or make manual reduction maneuvers difficult.
- **Reduction check before prepping:** obtain provisional AP and lateral fluoroscopic images with gentle traction, slight internal rotation (15–20°) of the limb, and adduction of the operative leg (drop the leg off the table edge or lower the table-side rail) to allow unobstructed access to the greater trochanter tip.
- **C-arm setup:** position the C-arm on the contralateral side of the table, entering from the unaffected side, so it can rotate between true AP and true lateral without repositioning the base — confirm this arc is unobstructed before draping.
- **Draping and landmarks:** prep and drape widely from the iliac crest to mid-thigh to allow extension to an open reduction if needed. Mark the tip of the greater trochanter and the anticipated proximal incision (typically 3–5 cm proximal to the trochanteric tip, in line with the femoral shaft axis) before incision.
- **Tourniquet:** not used (proximal femoral procedure, precludes safe tourniquet placement).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm closed reduction under fluoroscopy** before making the incision — traction, slight internal rotation, and adduction typically restore length and rotation for most stable and many unstable patterns. Obtain and inspect true AP and true lateral views; do not proceed to entry-point instrumentation on an unreduced fracture.
2. **Make the proximal (entry) incision**, typically 3–5 cm, starting just proximal to the tip of the greater trochanter and extending proximally in line with the femoral shaft axis; split the gluteus medius fibers bluntly in line with the incision to avoid abductor injury.
3. **Establish the entry point at the tip of the greater trochanter** (or minimally medial to the tip, per the specific nail design's proximal geometry) using a guidewire under both AP and lateral fluoroscopy. A start point that is too lateral drives the proximal fragment into varus as the nail is subsequently seated (the nail forces the shaft to align with an off-axis proximal entry); a start point too medial risks calcar comminution/blowout. Confirm the guidewire is co-axial with the canal on both views before opening.
4. **Open the entry point** with the cannulated entry reamer/awl over the guidewire, then insert the ball-tipped guidewire down the canal to the level of the fracture under fluoroscopic control.
5. **Correct the proximal fragment's deformity before ream-and-pass.** The proximal fragment characteristically sits flexed and externally rotated (unopposed iliopsoas/short external rotators) — use a percutaneous joystick (Schanz pin in the proximal fragment), a bone hook, or manual manipulation to bring it to neutral before the entry reamer/nail is passed, rather than relying on the nail to force reduction, which drives varus malreduction. If the guidewire tends to skive medially into the fracture line rather than down the canal, apply brief additional longitudinal distraction to wedge the wire against the lateral cortex of the proximal fragment and allow the reamer to cut an accurate path — this is the single most effective maneuver to prevent varus malreduction at this step.
6. **Ream sequentially** (hand or power reaming per nail system) to 1–1.5 mm over the selected nail diameter, monitoring reduction on fluoroscopy throughout — reduction can be lost during reaming and must be re-confirmed, not assumed to persist from step 1.
7. **Select and insert the nail**, seating it to the planned depth (confirm on fluoroscopy — too proximal risks lag-screw malposition/impingement, too distal risks inadequate proximal fixation and trochanteric prominence). For subtrochanteric-extension or reverse-obliquity patterns, use a long nail spanning to the distal femoral metaphysis.
8. **Direct the proximal (lag screw/blade) guidewire** through the targeting jig into the femoral head under simultaneous AP and lateral fluoroscopy. Aim for a central-central position on both views, or slightly inferior on the AP view (never superior — a superior position increases cutout risk even at an acceptable TAD). Advance the wire to within 5–10 mm of subchondral bone.
9. **Confirm tip-apex distance (TAD) before drilling/reaming for the lag screw** — measure on both AP and lateral views and sum, correcting for magnification per the nail system's calibration tool. Target TAD <25 mm (Baumgaertner); this is associated with cutout risk <2% versus >20% above threshold, and is the single most modifiable technical variable in this procedure.
10. **Place the lag screw or helical blade** over the guidewire to the templated depth, and consider a second (dual) lag device or an integrated screw-and-blade construct in osteoporotic bone or rotationally unstable patterns for added anti-rotation control.
11. **Re-confirm reduction on both views** after lag-screw placement — screw insertion can itself displace a marginal reduction; correct any loss before proceeding.
12. **Lock distally** using the jig-guided or freehand (perfect-circle) technique once length, alignment, and rotation are confirmed satisfactory; for long nails, use a minimum of two distal locking screws, and for reverse-obliquity or subtrochanteric-extension patterns favor static (not dynamic-slot-only) distal locking to control rotation until union.
13. **Assess rotational alignment clinically** before closure — compare intraoperative passive hip rotation and/or use the lesser trochanter profile technique (comparing lesser trochanter size/shape to the contralateral fluoroscopic image) to detect rotational malalignment, which is not reliably seen on AP/lateral fluoroscopy alone.
14. **Irrigate and close in layers**, repairing the split fascia/gluteus medius fibers, followed by standard subcutaneous and skin closure.
15. **Obtain final fluoroscopic images** (or portable radiographs) in the operating room confirming implant position, TAD, and reduction before the patient leaves the table.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use brief over-distraction on the table to wedge the entry guidewire against the lateral cortex of the proximal fragment when it tends to skive medially into the fracture line — this single maneuver prevents most varus malreductions at the entry-point step.
- Neutralize the proximal fragment's flexion/external rotation deformity with a joystick or bone hook before reaming — do not expect the nail itself to correct this as it passes.
- Check the entry point on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy before opening the canal; a start point that looks acceptable on AP alone can still be anteriorly or posteriorly malpositioned on lateral, which translates into a flexion/extension malreduction as the nail seats.
- Aim the lag screw/blade central-central or slightly inferior, never superior — superior positioning is a stronger cutout predictor than TAD alone in several series.
- Recheck TAD after final seating of the lag device, not only on the planning guidewire image — screw/blade advancement can subtly change position.
- In reverse-obliquity or subtrochanteric-extension patterns, use a long nail and consider a percutaneous reduction clamp or cerclage cable placed with minimally invasive technique to control the medial spike before nail passage, rather than accepting a malreduction the nail will lock in place.
- Use the lesser trochanter profile comparison against the contralateral fluoroscopic image intraoperatively to catch rotational malalignment before closure — clinical exam under anesthesia alone is unreliable in this position.
- If the guidewire for the proximal locking screw tends to walk superiorly toward the femoral head, stop and reassess for occult varus malreduction of the proximal fragment before continuing — a superior-tending trajectory is often a downstream sign of a malreduced entry point, not a targeting-jig problem.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not rely on the nail alone to reduce the fracture — restore length/alignment/rotation first; the nail should traverse an already-reduced fracture. A lateral start point drives the fracture into varus as the nail seats and is a leading cause of malreduction.
- TAD ≥25 mm sharply increases cutout risk (>20% vs. <2% below threshold) — this is the single most modifiable technical variable in CMN fixation.
- Loss of fixation/cutout is minimized by TAD <25 mm and central-inferior screw placement; varus collapse and medialization of the shaft is more of an SHS-in-unstable-pattern problem but can still occur with suboptimal CMN technique or an unrecognized varus malreduction.
- Z-effect/reverse Z-effect is a recognized failure mode specific to dual-screw nail systems — one screw backs out laterally (Z-effect) or migrates medially into the pelvis (reverse Z-effect) when differential loading occurs between the two lag devices; minimize by ensuring both screws are well-seated and appropriately spaced within the femoral head.
- Peri-implant femur fracture at the nail tip is more common with older, shorter, stiffer nail designs — mitigated with modern nail geometry and by avoiding a stress riser at the tip in osteoporotic bone (consider a longer nail in markedly osteoporotic patients even for otherwise stable patterns).
- A 2023 systematic review found CMN carries a higher reoperation and refracture rate than SHS for intertrochanteric fractures overall — reserve CMN for patterns that mechanically require it (unstable per Evans-Jensen/AO) rather than defaulting to nailing for every pattern; a 2025 meta-analysis found CMN offers shorter operative time and less blood loss versus DHS/SHS, an efficiency argument distinct from the union/reoperation equivalence shown in earlier RCTs.
- Structures at risk: the superior gluteal nerve/vessels with an overly proximal or medially aggressive entry-point dissection, and the femoral neurovascular bundle with an errant distal locking drill trajectory (use the perfect-circle freehand technique carefully, particularly anteriorly).
- Unrecognized rotational malalignment is easy to miss on standard AP/lateral fluoroscopy alone — use the lesser trochanter profile technique or direct clinical comparison of rotation before closing.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) immediately for stable patterns — the sliding/telescoping mechanism is designed for it.
- For unstable/comminuted patterns, WBAT is still generally pursued with the CMN load-sharing construct, but with closer radiographic follow-up (2, 6, 12 weeks) to detect early collapse/varus migration; touch-down weight-bearing for 4–6 weeks is reserved for cases with poor bone quality, inadequate fixation, or major comminution where the construct is judged marginal.
- Union is typically confirmed by bridging callus by 12–16 weeks; nonunion is uncommon (<5%) given the metaphyseal/cancellous vascularity of this region.
- Physiotherapy progression: POD0–1 bed mobility/transfers/ankle pumps; week 1–2 gait training and hip/knee AROM with isometric quad/gluteal sets; week 2–6 progressive resistive exercise and device weaning; week 6–12 abductor/extensor strengthening and single-leg stance work; beyond 12 weeks continued strengthening and fall-prevention.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing.
