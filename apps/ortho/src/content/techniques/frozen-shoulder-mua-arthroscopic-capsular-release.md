## Indications

Reserved for frozen shoulder patients who remain functionally limited after a genuine trial (typically 3–6 months) of the non-operative ladder — reassurance/home ROM program, structured physiotherapy, and at least one intra-articular corticosteroid injection (± hydrodilatation).

## Positioning & setup

MUA is performed under general or regional anaesthesia. Arthroscopic capsular release (ACR) is performed under direct arthroscopic visualization and is frequently combined with MUA to complete the release. No further positioning detail is specified in the source.

## Steps

1. **Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA)**: controlled, sequential passive stretching release of the capsule — forward elevation first, then external rotation, then internal rotation/extension. Lowest cost and fastest to perform, but relies on capsular tissue tearing in a controlled pattern rather than a directed release.
2. **Arthroscopic capsular release (ACR)**: directed release of the rotator interval, coracohumeral ligament, and anterior/inferior/posterior capsule under direct visualization; allows simultaneous inspection and treatment of intra-articular pathology (biceps, labrum, partial cuff tears); more costly and technically demanding than MUA alone.
3. **Combine MUA with ACR** when a directed release needs completing after arthroscopic work, or reserve ACR for cases with suspected concomitant intra-articular pathology or after failed MUA.

## Pitfalls

- MUA carries a theoretical (low-incidence) risk of humeral fracture, particularly in osteopenic or older patients, plus risk of cuff or labral injury from the uncontrolled tearing pattern; transient neurapraxia has been reported.
- ACR risks nerve injury (the axillary nerve is at risk during inferior capsular release), incomplete release/recurrent stiffness, and rarely instability from over-release.
- UK FROST directly compared MUA and ACR head-to-head with early structured physiotherapy and found no clinically important difference between them at 12 months, with MUA being the more cost-effective choice and ACR carrying higher cost/complication burden without a meaningfully better outcome.
- Diabetic patients and those with a prolonged frozen phase (>12 months) should be counseled on a higher risk of incomplete recovery and recurrence regardless of which operative technique is chosen.
- If frozen shoulder coexists with cuff pathology or early glenohumeral OA, treat the capsular stiffness first (injections, PT, MUA/ACR as needed) before committing to an arthroplasty decision, since capsular contracture can obscure true passive ROM and cuff strength assessment.

## Post-op protocol

- Immediate aggressive passive ROM within 24–48 hours to prevent re-adhesion — motion, not protection, is the postoperative priority (fundamentally different from arthroplasty protocols).
- Daily supervised or intensive home PT for 2–6 weeks.
- Interscalene catheter or frequent multimodal analgesia in the first days facilitates early motion tolerance.
- Return to full function typically 6–12 weeks; recurrence of stiffness is the main risk if therapy is not sustained.
