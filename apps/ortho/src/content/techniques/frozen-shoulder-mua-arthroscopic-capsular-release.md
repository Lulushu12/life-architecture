## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.**
- Frozen shoulder patients who remain functionally limited after a genuine trial (typically 3–6 months) of the non-operative ladder — reassurance/home ROM program, structured physiotherapy, and at least one intra-articular corticosteroid injection (± hydrodilatation).
- ACR is specifically preferred over isolated MUA when there is suspected concomitant intra-articular pathology (biceps, labrum, partial cuff tear) warranting direct inspection/treatment, or after a failed MUA.

**Contraindications.**
- Absolute: active glenohumeral infection, and recent (<3–6 months) humeral fracture or other bony pathology where forceful manipulation risks fracture propagation.
- Relative: significant osteopenia/osteoporosis (raises MUA-related humeral fracture risk — consider ACR instead, which achieves release without a forceful manipulation moment); short duration of symptoms/still in an active early freezing phase where continued non-operative treatment may still succeed; secondary frozen shoulder from an unaddressed underlying cause (e.g., unrecognized rotator cuff tear, instability, or glenohumeral OA) where capsular release alone will not resolve symptoms — evaluate and address concomitant pathology; uncontrolled diabetes (does not preclude surgery but should be optimized and factored into recovery-time counseling, given slower/less complete recovery in this population).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: plain radiographs to exclude other pathology (usually normal in idiopathic frozen shoulder — the diagnosis is clinical); MRI reserved for atypical presentations or when concomitant rotator cuff/labral pathology is suspected and ACR (rather than isolated MUA) is being planned so it can be addressed in the same setting.
- **Confirm non-operative trial adequacy**: document at least 3–6 months of structured treatment including PT and at least one corticosteroid injection (± hydrodilatation) before proceeding, and confirm the diagnosis is primary/idiopathic or a well-characterized secondary cause rather than an unaddressed structural lesion.
- **Choice of technique**: decide preoperatively between isolated MUA, isolated ACR, or combined MUA+ACR based on suspected concomitant pathology, bone quality (favor ACR in osteopenic patients), and patient/surgeon preference — counsel using UK FROST data that outcomes are equivalent at 12 months, with MUA being more cost-effective.
- **Instrumentation/equipment**: standard arthroscopy tower, pump, and radiofrequency (RF) ablation/electrocautery device for ACR; no special implants are needed since this is a soft-tissue release procedure.
- **Analgesia planning**: arrange an interscalene catheter or robust multimodal analgesia plan in advance, since early aggressive postoperative motion is the cornerstone of preventing re-adhesion and depends on adequate pain control.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia (for adequate muscle relaxation during manipulation) is standard, often combined with an interscalene regional block or catheter placed for postoperative analgesia to enable early aggressive motion.
- **Position**: beach-chair position is most common for both MUA and ACR, allowing arthroscopic access to the glenohumeral joint and subacromial space; lateral decubitus with traction is an alternative for ACR per surgeon preference.
- **Table setup**: a shoulder positioner allowing free passive manipulation through full flexion, abduction, and both internal and external rotation; for ACR, standard arthroscopic fluid-management setup with the pump pressure kept as low as effective to reduce fluid extravasation into the periscapular/deltoid soft tissues.
- **Draping**: standard arthroscopic shoulder draping with the arm free for full passive ROM testing/manipulation before and after any release.
- **Landmarks**: mark the acromion (posterolateral and anterolateral corners), coracoid, and clavicle before establishing portals; the posterior viewing portal is placed roughly 2 cm inferior and 1–2 cm medial to the posterolateral acromial corner ("soft spot"), with anterior and lateral working portals placed under direct/spinal-needle-confirmed visualization — the anterior portal skin incision is typically made about 2 cm lateral to the anterolateral edge of the acromion, using prior spinal-needle localization to confirm the trajectory before committing to the portal.
- **Documentation**: record baseline passive ROM (forward elevation, external rotation at the side, internal rotation) under anesthesia before any manipulation or release begins, and again after — this before/after comparison is the objective record of what was achieved.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Baseline exam under anesthesia**: with the patient fully relaxed under general anesthesia, document passive forward elevation, external rotation at the side, and internal rotation (hand-behind-back level) — this establishes the true capsular restriction free of guarding.
2. **Manipulation under anesthesia (MUA)** (performed alone or as the first stage before/after ACR):
   - Stabilize the scapula with one hand while applying controlled, progressive passive force with the other.
   - Sequence: forward elevation first, then external rotation (with the arm at the side and then in abduction), then internal rotation/extension — releasing the capsule in a controlled, sequential pattern rather than a single maximal-force maneuver.
   - Feel and, where audible, listen for the sensation/sound of capsular release (a "give") at each restricted arc rather than forcing through a hard endpoint, and reassess ROM between steps.
3. **Arthroscopic setup for capsular release**: establish the posterior viewing portal, then confirm anterior and lateral portal trajectories with a spinal needle before making the working portal incisions.
4. **Diagnostic arthroscopy**: survey the glenohumeral joint (biceps anchor, labrum, cuff undersurface, rotator interval) and subacromial space to confirm the diagnosis and identify any concomitant pathology to be addressed in the same setting.
5. **Rotator interval and anterior capsular release**: using a 3.0 mm 90° RF electrocautery device through the anterior portal, begin release at the rotator interval and middle glenohumeral ligament, then continue the anterior capsular release below the long head of the biceps tendon origin, preserving the glenoid labrum throughout.
6. **Inferior capsular release**: extend the release inferiorly toward the axillary pouch, working from the anterior portal with the scope posterior (or switching scope/instrument portals as needed for angle); this is the technically highest-risk step for the axillary nerve.
7. **Posterior capsular release**: switch the arthroscope to the anterior portal and the RF probe to the posterior portal to complete the posterior capsular release; confirm an adequate release by visualizing exposed muscle tissue (deltoid/infraspinatus fibers) deep to the released capsule.
8. **Protect the axillary nerve during the 5–7 o'clock release**: when releasing capsule from the 5 o'clock to 7 o'clock position (right shoulder; mirror for left), stay within about 10 mm of the glenoid rim, keep the RF probe face under direct vision and oriented toward the arthroscope, and avoid violating the glenoid labrum — this is the standard technique for avoiding iatrogenic axillary nerve injury during this portion of the release.
9. **Completion (360° release)**: confirm circumferential capsular mobility by reassessing passive ROM arthroscopically and clinically; if a residual restriction remains in a specific arc, direct additional release or a final gentle manipulation at that arc.
10. **Combine with MUA to complete the release** if a directed arthroscopic release alone does not achieve the target ROM, or reserve isolated MUA for straightforward cases without suspected intra-articular pathology.
11. **Final ROM documentation**: record final passive ROM in all planes before waking the patient, both to confirm an adequate release was achieved and to set the postoperative therapy target.
12. **Portal closure**: close arthroscopic portals with simple sutures or adhesive strips; no formal deep closure is needed since this is not an open procedure.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always document a formal before/after ROM exam under anesthesia — it is the single best objective marker that an adequate release was achieved and anchors the immediate postoperative therapy prescription.
- Sequence MUA in the same order every time (elevation, then external rotation, then internal rotation/extension) so that the surgeon develops a consistent feel for what a normal versus abnormal release pattern feels like at each step.
- Favor ACR (or combined MUA+ACR) over isolated MUA in osteopenic or elderly patients, since a directed arthroscopic release avoids the uncontrolled tearing pattern that carries fracture risk with forceful manipulation.
- Keep the RF probe within roughly 10 mm of the glenoid rim and facing the arthroscope during the 5–7 o'clock inferior capsular release — this is the most consistently cited technical safeguard against axillary nerve injury in the literature.
- Confirm posterior release adequacy by visualizing exposed deltoid/infraspinatus muscle fibers, not merely by the RF device having passed through the expected plane.
- Use a low-pressure arthroscopic pump setting sufficient for visualization — excessive pressure drives fluid extravasation into the deltoid and periscapular soft tissues, which can obscure landmarks and complicate the immediate postoperative exam.
- Arrange the interscalene catheter or analgesia plan before the case, not as an afterthought — pain control in the first 24–48 hours directly determines whether the patient can participate in the aggressive early motion that prevents re-adhesion.
- Address any concomitant intra-articular pathology (partial cuff tear, labral fraying, biceps pathology) found on diagnostic arthroscopy in the same setting when feasible, since this is often the reason ACR was chosen over isolated MUA in the first place.
- Set expectations with diabetic patients and those with a prolonged (>12 month) frozen phase before the procedure — counsel on a higher risk of incomplete recovery and recurrence regardless of which technique is used, so this is not mistaken for a technical failure postoperatively.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- MUA carries a theoretical (low-incidence) risk of humeral fracture, particularly in osteopenic or older patients, plus risk of cuff or labral injury from the uncontrolled tearing pattern; transient neurapraxia has been reported.
- ACR risks nerve injury (the axillary nerve is at risk during inferior capsular release), incomplete release/recurrent stiffness, and rarely instability from over-release.
- UK FROST directly compared MUA and ACR head-to-head with early structured physiotherapy and found no clinically important difference between them at 12 months, with MUA being the more cost-effective choice and ACR carrying higher cost/complication burden without a meaningfully better outcome.
- Diabetic patients and those with a prolonged frozen phase (>12 months) should be counseled on a higher risk of incomplete recovery and recurrence regardless of which operative technique is chosen.
- If frozen shoulder coexists with cuff pathology or early glenohumeral OA, treat the capsular stiffness first (injections, PT, MUA/ACR as needed) before committing to an arthroplasty decision, since capsular contracture can obscure true passive ROM and cuff strength assessment.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Immediate aggressive passive ROM within 24–48 hours to prevent re-adhesion — motion, not protection, is the postoperative priority (fundamentally different from arthroplasty protocols).
- Daily supervised or intensive home PT for 2–6 weeks.
- Interscalene catheter or frequent multimodal analgesia in the first days facilitates early motion tolerance.
- Return to full function typically 6–12 weeks; recurrence of stiffness is the main risk if therapy is not sustained.
