## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Smillie **stage III** Freiberg disease — further central collapse of the metatarsal head with a **viable dorsal or plantar hinge of articular cartilage** remaining. This is the joint-preserving procedure of choice at this stage.
- Symptomatic stage II disease (central articular depression) refractory to conservative management, in a patient with sufficient remaining plantar cartilage to reconstruct a functional joint surface.
- Young, active patients in whom joint preservation is strongly preferred over salvage arthroplasty given the long functional lifespan required of the joint.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Smillie stage IV (loose intra-articular fragmentation) or stage V (flattened, arthritic collapse) — there is no viable cartilage hinge to rotate into the load-bearing zone; debridement/loose body excision or salvage (interpositional, implant, or resection) arthroplasty is indicated instead.
- Inadequate viable plantar cartilage on preoperative MRI, even if the plain radiographic stage appears favorable — cartilage viability, not radiographic stage alone, determines candidacy.
- Active infection or an inadequate soft-tissue envelope over the dorsal forefoot.
- Very low-demand or elderly patients in whom a simpler debridement/salvage procedure may achieve adequate symptom relief with less operative complexity.

## Preoperative Planning

- AP and lateral weight-bearing foot radiographs to stage the disease (Smillie) and assess the degree of metatarsal head collapse and shortening.
- **MRI is important and often decisive** — plain radiographic collapse can understate or lag the true extent of cartilage viability, and MRI confirms bone marrow edema pattern and the amount of viable dorsal versus plantar articular cartilage remaining, which directly determines whether a Gauthier osteotomy is feasible and how large a wedge is needed.
- Confirm the affected ray (overwhelmingly the 2nd, occasionally the 3rd) and assess the overall metatarsal parabola — a Gauthier osteotomy shortens the metatarsal to some degree, and this should be planned against the neighboring rays to avoid creating a new transfer-load pattern.
- Instrumentation: mini oscillating saw, small K-wires for provisional fixation, small-fragment screws or a mini plate/staple for definitive fixation per surgeon preference, and mini C-arm for intraoperative confirmation of wedge closure and joint congruity.
- Template the approximate size of the dorsal closing wedge needed based on the degree of collapse seen on MRI/radiographs — larger collapse requires a larger wedge to rotate a sufficient amount of viable plantar cartilage into the load-bearing position.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Regional (ankle/popliteal block) with sedation, or general anesthesia; short procedure well suited to regional block for postoperative analgesia.
- Supine, foot free at the end of the table, thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination.
- Prep and drape to allow full visualization of the dorsal forefoot and free passive motion testing of the affected MTP joint.
- Mini C-arm positioned for AP and lateral imaging of the lesser metatarsal head without breaking sterility — confirm image quality of the specific ray involved before incision, since lesser metatarsal heads can be difficult to isolate on fluoroscopy with overlapping adjacent rays.
- Mark the dorsal longitudinal incision centered over the affected metatarsal (2nd, occasionally 3rd) and mark the adjacent ray for reference to avoid wrong-level surgery.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a dorsal longitudinal incision centered over the affected metatarsal, between the extensor tendons to the affected and adjacent toe.
2. Develop the interval, retract the extensor tendons, and incise the MTP joint capsule longitudinally to expose the metatarsal head.
3. Inspect the articular surface directly and correlate with preoperative MRI to **confirm a viable dorsal or plantar cartilage hinge** on the metatarsal head — this is the prerequisite for proceeding with joint preservation; if the cartilage is not viable as anticipated, be prepared to convert to debridement or salvage arthroplasty.
4. Débride any loose osteochondral fragments and synovitis from the joint to improve visualization.
5. Mark the planned osteotomy on the metatarsal neck, oriented so the dorsal-based wedge, when closed, rotates the viable plantar (or, less commonly, dorsal) cartilage into the load-bearing position.
6. Perform the **dorsiflexion closing-wedge osteotomy** at the metatarsal neck (Gauthier/Kinnard technique) using a mini oscillating saw, removing a dorsally-based wedge of bone sized to the degree of collapse.
7. Close the osteotomy, **rotating the viable plantar articular cartilage dorsally** into the load-bearing zone of the joint — this substitutes healthy cartilage for the collapsed/necrotic dorsal segment and is the central maneuver of the procedure.
8. Provisionally pin the osteotomy with a K-wire and take the joint through passive motion to confirm the newly rotated cartilage surface articulates congruently with the phalangeal base without residual impingement.
9. Confirm wedge closure, alignment, and joint congruity with fluoroscopy (AP and lateral) before definitive fixation.
10. Fix the osteotomy per surgeon preference and construct stability — options include crossed K-wires, a small-fragment screw, or a mini plate/staple; construct choice should balance rigidity against the limited bone stock in a small metatarsal head fragment.
11. Recheck final position and joint motion with fluoroscopy after definitive fixation.
12. Irrigate, repair the capsule, close in layers, and apply a supportive but non-restrictive forefoot dressing.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Correlate the intraoperative articular inspection directly with the preoperative MRI before committing to the osteotomy — cartilage that looks marginal on direct inspection but was mapped as viable on MRI can still be salvageable; conversely, be willing to abandon joint preservation if the cartilage is clearly non-viable despite favorable imaging.
- Size the dorsal wedge to the degree of collapse — under-sizing fails to bring enough viable cartilage into the load-bearing zone, while over-sizing excessively shortens the metatarsal and risks transfer problems to adjacent rays.
- Take the joint through a full passive ROM arc after wedge closure, before final fixation, to confirm the rotated cartilage surface tracks congruently — this is the best intraoperative check that the maneuver has achieved its goal.
- Plan the resultant metatarsal length against the overall parabola; a Gauthier osteotomy does shorten the ray to some extent, and this should be weighed against the adjacent rays to avoid iatrogenic transfer metatarsalgia.
- Use a mini C-arm liberally — lesser metatarsal heads are small and the wedge/fixation is easy to misjudge on direct visualization alone.
- Keep the periosteal/soft-tissue envelope around the metatarsal head as intact as reasonably possible during exposure to protect the remaining blood supply to an already vascular-compromised bone.
- Have a salvage plan (debridement, interpositional arthroplasty, or implant) ready to proceed to in the same setting if intraoperative inspection reveals the cartilage is not viable enough for the planned osteotomy.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The procedure depends entirely on having a **viable cartilage hinge** — attempting it in stage IV–V disease (loose bodies, flattened arthritic collapse) is not appropriate; debridement/loose body excision or salvage (interpositional/implant/resection) arthroplasty is indicated instead.
- **Avascular progression despite osteotomy** can still occur.
- Hardware prominence and residual stiffness are recognized complications.
- Under-sizing the wedge leaves inadequate viable cartilage in the load-bearing zone and risks early recurrence of symptoms; over-sizing excessively shortens the ray and risks transfer metatarsalgia to adjacent rays.
- Small bone stock in the metatarsal head fragment limits fixation options and can make the construct less rigid than desired — protected weight-bearing until early consolidation is important.
- If salvage arthroplasty/implant is later required, it carries the same general lesser-MTP arthroplasty complication profile (instability, transfer metatarsalgia) as other lesser-ray salvage procedures.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–4**: protected weight-bearing in a postoperative shoe/boot per fixation stability.
- **Weeks 4–8**: progressive weight-bearing and shoe transition once early union/consolidation is evident radiographically.
- **Return to activity**: typically **8–12 weeks**, guided by radiographic healing.
