## Indications

Judet III–IV radial neck fractures (60–90° or >90°/complete displacement), and Judet II fractures with angulation >30–45° or failing simple closed manipulation. Percutaneous reduction is preferred even at the most severe (Judet IV) grade; open reduction is reserved as a last resort when percutaneous techniques fail.

## Positioning & setup

The radial neck physis is intracapsular and the radial head is a secondary ossification center supplied by an anastomotic ring vulnerable to disruption with high-energy displacement or aggressive open manipulation — this is the rationale for avoiding open reduction whenever a percutaneous option exists. When open reduction is unavoidable, it is performed via a **lateral/Kocher approach**, minimizing periosteal stripping and preserving the annular ligament and posterior soft-tissue attachments as much as possible.

## Steps

1. Attempt manipulation with a percutaneous joystick/elevator for lower-grade or less angulated fractures.
2. **Métaizeau technique ("Israeli technique")**: pass an intramedullary flexible nail retrograde from the distal radial metaphysis, advance it across the fracture.
3. Rotate and use the nail as a lever against the radial head fragment to reduce it.
4. Leave the nail in place as an intramedullary splint for 4–6 weeks.
5. Reserve open reduction (lateral/Kocher approach) for failed percutaneous attempts; minimize periosteal stripping and preserve the annular ligament and posterior soft-tissue attachments.

## Pitfalls

- Open reduction substantially increases the risk of radial head avascular necrosis (up to 20–30% after open reduction of severely displaced fractures), radioulnar synostosis, and stiffness.
- Loss of forearm rotation is often the more functionally limiting sequela than residual angulation itself.
- Up to 30–45° of residual angulation is often accepted in children under ~10 years with preserved forearm rotation rather than pursuing open reduction, because radial neck remodeling (unlike malrotation) is real.

## Post-op protocol

- The intramedullary nail is left in place as a splint for **4–6 weeks**.
- Sling or above-elbow cast in a position of comfort for the first 1–2 weeks, then early motion; **forearm rotation**, not extension/flexion, is the functional parameter to track.
- Broader elbow-region recovery pattern applies: active ROM after immobilization/nail removal, motion regained over several weeks, return to sport typically at 6–8 weeks once union and function have returned.
