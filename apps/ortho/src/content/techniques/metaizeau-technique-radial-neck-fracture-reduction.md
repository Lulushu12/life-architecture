## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Judet III–IV radial neck fractures (60–90° angulation, or >90°/complete displacement of the radial head).
- Judet II fractures with angulation >30–45° that fail simple closed manipulation.
- Percutaneous reduction is preferred even at the most severe (Judet IV) grade, including fractures associated with elbow dislocation or a Monteggia-equivalent pattern — open reduction is reserved as a genuine last resort.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Judet I–II fractures with ≤30° angulation — treat with sling/cast and early motion; do not subject a mildly angulated, functionally acceptable fracture to an invasive reduction technique.
- Angulation up to 30–45° in a child under approximately 10 years old with preserved forearm rotation — real remodeling potential exists at the radial neck (unlike malrotation), so accepting residual angulation is often preferable to any reduction maneuver, percutaneous or open.
- A radial head/neck fracture so comminuted that no coherent fragment exists to lever against — a percutaneous nail has nothing to push, and open assessment (with a low threshold to accept a suboptimal result rather than resect the radial head in a child) may be unavoidable.
- Do not persist with repeated percutaneous reduction attempts in a tight forearm — escalating attempts are a recognized cause of iatrogenic forearm compartment syndrome (see Pitfalls); know when to stop and reassess rather than pushing for a perfect reduction.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP and lateral elbow radiographs to grade Judet angulation and translation; assess for an associated elbow dislocation, olecranon fracture, or Monteggia-equivalent ulna fracture, which changes sequencing (typically reduce/stabilize the ulna or elbow dislocation first, then address the radial neck).
- **Implant selection**: a 1.6 mm (0.062") or 2.0 mm titanium elastic nail or a heavy Kirschner wire sized to roughly 40–50% of the radial medullary canal diameter at the planned entry site — undersized implants provide inadequate leverage against the radial head fragment, oversized implants are difficult to pass and control.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: image intensifier, elastic nail set (or heavy K-wires) with a T-handle for controlled rotation, nail bender, and pin cutter. Have a lateral/Kocher approach set (self-retaining retractor, periosteal elevator) opened but not necessarily used, in case percutaneous reduction fails.
- **Sequencing plan**: if there is an associated elbow dislocation, plan to reduce the dislocation first (this often partially reduces the radial neck fracture as well), then proceed to the Métaizeau maneuver for any residual radial neck displacement.
- **Anesthesia/compartment discussion**: alert the team preoperatively that repeated reduction attempts carry a recognized association with forearm compartment syndrome, so postoperative compartment monitoring should be planned for, particularly after a technically difficult or prolonged reduction.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard; avoid a dense regional block that could mask early compartment syndrome symptoms if the reduction was difficult or prolonged, similar to the concern in supracondylar humerus surgery.
- **Positioning**: supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, forearm pronated to expose the entry site for the nail (typically the distal radial metaphysis, dorsal or dorsoradial).
- **Table setup**: rather than repeatedly repositioning the arm to obtain AP, oblique, and lateral fluoroscopic views during the reduction maneuver, keep the limb still and move the C-arm around it — this is a specific, practical technique tip for this procedure, since the nail-levering maneuver itself is sensitive to losing position if the arm is disturbed between imaging passes.
- **Tourniquet**: a pneumatic tourniquet applied high on the arm is standard, available for inflation if conversion to open reduction is needed.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire forearm and elbow circumferentially to allow access to the distal radial entry site and, if needed, extension to a lateral/Kocher approach at the elbow.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the distal radial entry site (either a distal lateral start point roughly 1.5 cm proximal to the physis, or a dorsal start point in line with Lister's tubercle roughly 1.5 cm proximal to the physis, per surgeon preference) before incision, and mark the lateral epicondyle/radial head region in case an open approach becomes necessary.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Entry site exposure**: make a small (1.5–2 cm) incision over the chosen distal radial entry point, bluntly dissect to bone protecting the superficial radial nerve branches and extensor tendons.
2. **Create the entry portal**: use an awl or drill to open the cortex at roughly a 45° angle to the shaft, angled to allow the nail/wire to pass proximally up the medullary canal.
3. **Insert the nail and advance to the fracture site**: insert the pre-bent nail or wire and advance it proximally using a T-handle, applying gentle oscillating (back-and-forth rotational) movements rather than pure axial push to ease progression through the canal — forcing straight axial pressure risks the tip catching the cortex or creating a false passage, and this oscillating-advance technique is a specifically described technical pearl for this procedure.
4. **Cross the fracture site**: advance the nail tip to the level of the fracture and gently pass it into the radial head fragment, using imaging to confirm the tip has actually engaged the fragment (rather than simply abutting it) before attempting to lever.
5. **Reduce the radial head fragment**: with the nail tip engaged in the fragment, use the nail as a lever — advance and rotate it (often described as a roughly 180° rotation of the tip) to push and rotate the displaced/angulated fragment back into an anatomically reduced position relative to the radial shaft; a gentle stabilizing counterforce applied externally over the radial head (with a thumb, through the skin) as the nail is levered helps hold the fragment while the reduction is achieved, rather than relying on the nail alone.
6. **Confirm reduction on fluoroscopy**: obtain AP, oblique, and lateral views (moving the C-arm around the stationary, undisturbed arm) to confirm the radial head is realigned with the radial neck/shaft, checking Judet angulation has been restored to an acceptable range.
7. **Advance the nail into final position**: once reduction is satisfactory, advance the nail further into the radial head to act as an intramedullary splint, stopping short of penetrating the articular surface, and confirm final position on both projections.
8. **Assess forearm rotation intraoperatively**: passively pronate and supinate the forearm to confirm the reduction is stable through the functional range and there is no mechanical block.
9. **Trim the nail**: cut the nail leaving a short segment outside the cortex (bent away from soft tissue) to allow later removal, or bury it fully per surgeon preference and local practice for planned removal timing.
10. **Assess for and rule out compartment syndrome intraoperatively/immediately postoperatively**: given the recognized association between repeated reduction attempts and forearm compartment syndrome, palpate forearm compartment tension before leaving the OR, particularly if the reduction required multiple passes, and maintain a low threshold for close postoperative compartment monitoring.
11. **Open reduction (last resort, after failed percutaneous attempts)**: use a lateral/Kocher interval approach, minimizing periosteal stripping and carefully preserving the annular ligament and posterior soft-tissue attachments; reduce the fragment under direct vision and stabilize with the same intramedullary nail technique or a small implant as the fracture pattern allows, accepting that this carries substantially higher AVN/synostosis/stiffness risk than the percutaneous route.
12. **Closure**: close the entry-site incision in layers; apply a well-padded splint or cast per surgeon preference.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Move the C-arm, not the arm, when checking AP/oblique/lateral views during the reduction maneuver — repositioning the limb between images risks losing the partially-achieved reduction and forces you to restart the levering maneuver.
- Use gentle oscillating rotational movements of the T-handle to advance the nail through the canal rather than pure axial force — this reduces the risk of the tip catching the cortex or creating a false track, particularly at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal transition.
- Apply gentle external counterforce over the radial head with your other hand while levering the nail — this two-handed technique (nail levering internally, thumb stabilizing externally) improves control of the fragment during the critical reduction moment compared with relying on the nail alone.
- Confirm the nail tip has actually engaged the radial head fragment (not just abutted it) before attempting the levering rotation — a nail that slips off the fragment during the lever maneuver wastes an attempt and adds to cumulative soft-tissue trauma.
- Set a mental limit on the number of reduction attempts before pausing — persistent, repeated attempts at a technically difficult reduction are specifically associated with the development of forearm compartment syndrome, and recognizing when to stop (accept a less-than-perfect reduction within known remodeling tolerance, or convert to a different strategy) is a genuine technical skill in this procedure.
- If an elbow dislocation is present, reduce the dislocation first — this frequently improves or even substantially reduces the radial neck fracture on its own, sometimes making the subsequent nailing maneuver easier or unnecessary to fully complete.
- When open reduction is unavoidable, resist the urge to fully expose and mobilize the fragment for a "perfect" visual reduction — minimizing periosteal stripping and preserving the posterior soft-tissue attachments and annular ligament matters more to the vascular outcome than achieving textbook-anatomic alignment under direct vision.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Open reduction substantially increases the risk of radial head avascular necrosis (up to 20–30% after open reduction of severely displaced fractures), radioulnar synostosis, and stiffness.
- Loss of forearm rotation is often the more functionally limiting sequela than residual angulation itself.
- Up to 30–45° of residual angulation is often accepted in children under ~10 years with preserved forearm rotation rather than pursuing open reduction, because radial neck remodeling (unlike malrotation) is real.
- Repeated intraoperative reduction attempts in a technically challenging fracture are a recognized cause of forearm compartment syndrome, particularly (though not exclusively) when there is an associated elbow dislocation — maintain a high index of suspicion and a low threshold for postoperative compartment checks after a difficult or prolonged case.
- A nail sized too small for the canal provides inadequate leverage to reduce the fragment; a nail sized too large is difficult to advance and risks iatrogenic comminution at the entry site or along the canal.
- Losing engagement between the nail tip and the radial head fragment mid-maneuver is a common technical frustration — confirm true engagement fluoroscopically before committing to the levering rotation rather than assuming contact equals engagement.

## Postoperative Protocol

- The intramedullary nail is left in place as a splint for **4–6 weeks**.
- Sling or above-elbow cast in a position of comfort for the first 1–2 weeks, then early motion; **forearm rotation**, not extension/flexion, is the functional parameter to track.
- Broader elbow-region recovery pattern applies: active ROM after immobilization/nail removal, motion regained over several weeks, return to sport typically at 6–8 weeks once union and function have returned.
