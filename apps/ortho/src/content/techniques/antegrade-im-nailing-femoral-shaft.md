## Indications

First-line treatment for essentially all skeletally mature diaphyseal femoral shaft fractures (AO/OTA 32-A/B/C) in stable or resuscitated polytrauma patients — the workhorse technique for the femoral shaft.

## Positioning & setup

Supine on a fracture table (with traction) or lateral decubitus. Start point at the piriformis fossa or the greater trochanteric tip. Trochanteric entry nails have a lateral bow matched to the proximal femur and reduce the risk of iatrogenic varus malreduction and femoral neck fracture compared with piriformis entry — generally preferred, especially with a narrow proximal canal or a concomitant femoral neck fracture requiring cephalomedullary fixation.

## Steps

1. Confirm no ipsilateral femoral neck fracture before reaming (dedicated fine-cut CT or true AP hip/pelvis film with internal rotation) — this must be done before an antegrade start point is reamed.
2. Choose start point and positioning (piriformis fossa vs trochanteric tip; fracture table vs lateral decubitus), favoring trochanteric entry for narrow canals or concomitant neck fracture.
3. Ream sequentially to 1-1.5 mm over the selected nail diameter to improve endosteal contact and allow a larger/stronger nail; confirm the guidewire and reamers track the femur's anterior bow to avoid anterior cortical perforation.
4. Achieve reduction: traction-table longitudinal traction for most fractures. For segmental or comminuted (Winquist III/IV) patterns, use manual/semi-open reduction (ball-spike, femoral distractor, or provisional plate) or blocking (Poller) screws to control alignment before nail passage, since traction alone may not correct rotational or coronal deformity through comminuted zones.
5. Pass the nail and lock statically (proximal and distal) — standard for essentially all closed diaphyseal nailings regardless of comminution grade; static locking controls rotation and length without meaningfully raising nonunion risk when reaming is adequate.
6. Assess rotation fluoroscopically before final locking: compare cortical diameter ratios (lesser trochanter profile) or use the "perfect circle" technique of the contralateral distal femur; malrotation >15° is symptomatic and a recognized, largely preventable complication.
7. Check leg length and rotation against the contralateral limb, and obtain full-length fluoroscopic or radiographic confirmation of alignment before leaving the OR.

## Pitfalls

- Missed ipsilateral femoral neck fracture — screen with CT before reaming/nailing; historically missed in up to 19-31% of cases on initial films.
- Malrotation — the most common preventable technical error; confirm intraoperatively against the contralateral limb.
- Anterior cortical perforation from a mismatched nail bow, particularly with long or stiff reamers in short-statured patients (the femur has a gentle anterior bow, radius of curvature ~120 cm).
- Iatrogenic comminution at the entry site with trochanteric start points if guidewire placement is imprecise.
- Fracture-table traction carries a recognized, largely preventable risk of pudendal nerve palsy and perineal soft-tissue injury from the perineal post — use a well-padded post, limit traction time, and consider a flat (non-traction) table or lateral technique for prolonged or difficult reductions.
- In anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients, closed injuries can typically be delayed 24-48 h for reversal/clearance without meaningfully worsening outcome provided the limb is well splinted and neurovascular status is monitored; open fracture, vascular compromise, or compartment syndrome override this and require urgent intervention.
- Dynamization (removing one set of interlocking screws) is reserved for delayed union without progression at 3-6 months on serial films — not performed prophylactically.

## Post-op protocol

- **0-2 weeks**: mobilize out of bed day 1 when medically stable. Weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is standard after a statically locked reamed nail in Winquist 0-II patterns; protected/toe-touch weight bearing considered for Winquist III-IV or segmental patterns with tenuous fixation. Early hip/knee active-assisted ROM, quadriceps sets, ankle pumps plus pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis (LMWH or equivalent).
- **2-6 weeks**: progress weight bearing to full as tolerated in most patterns; gait training from walker/crutches to cane; active ROM, closed-chain strengthening as tolerated; radiographic check at ~6 weeks for early callus.
- **6-12 weeks**: progressive resistive strengthening of hip abductors/quadriceps/hamstrings; proprioceptive and balance training; single-leg stance work; solid bridging callus typically by 12-16 weeks in simple patterns.
- **3-6 months**: full unrestricted activity, running, and impact sport generally deferred until radiographic union (bridging callus on ≥3 of 4 cortices) and clinical union (painless full weight bearing, no motion/tenderness at the fracture site), typically achieved by 4-6 months (union rates ~90-98% by 6-12 months).
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often possible by 6-8 weeks; physically demanding/manual labor typically requires 4-6+ months and confirmed radiographic union.
- **Return to sport**: pivoting/contact sport deferred until full radiographic union and restoration of at least ~90% of contralateral strength on isokinetic/functional testing, typically 5-9 months.
