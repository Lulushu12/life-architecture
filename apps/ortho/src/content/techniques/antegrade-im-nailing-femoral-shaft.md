## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- First-line treatment for essentially all skeletally mature diaphyseal femoral shaft fractures (AO/OTA 32-A/B/C) in stable or resuscitated polytrauma patients — the workhorse technique for the femoral shaft.
- Winquist-Hansen 0-IV comminution patterns are all amenable to reamed statically locked nailing; the classification informs anticipated rotational/axial stability and nonunion vigilance rather than gating the indication itself.
- Segmental fractures, fractures with an ipsilateral shaft-neck pattern (once the neck component is addressed or fixed with a cephalomedullary construct), and floating knee patterns (as the femoral half of a nail-nail construct) are all appropriate indications.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Active local or systemic infection at the planned entry site or along the intended canal.
- Very proximal (subtrochanteric extension needing separate implant strategy) or very distal (metaphyseal/supracondylar extension precluding adequate distal interlock purchase) fractures — consider retrograde nailing or a locked plate instead.
- Narrow or dysplastic femoral canal (e.g., some skeletal dysplasias, prior malunion) where standard reamed nail diameters cannot be safely accommodated without cortical perforation risk.
- Open physis (skeletally immature patients) — piriformis/trochanteric entry nailing risks avascular necrosis of the femoral head and growth disturbance; use alternative pediatric-specific fixation.
- Unscreened ipsilateral femoral neck fracture is a relative contraindication until excluded by CT or dedicated imaging — reaming through an undiagnosed nondisplaced neck fracture can convert it to a displaced one.
- Severe active pulmonary compromise (ARDS, severe chest trauma) in the physiologically unstable polytrauma patient — favor damage-control external fixation first (see that technique) rather than definitive reamed nailing.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP/lateral femur radiographs including the hip and knee joints above and below the fracture. Dedicated fine-cut (2 mm) CT of the femoral neck, or a true AP hip/pelvis film with internal rotation, is mandatory in every case before an antegrade start point is reamed — ipsilateral neck fractures are missed on initial films in up to 19-31% of cases and are frequently vertically oriented (Pauwels III) and nondisplaced.
- **Templating**: measure canal diameter at the isthmus on the contralateral (uninjured) femur if the injured side is too deformed to template reliably; estimate nail length from greater trochanter tip to the knee joint line, adjusted for the chosen entry point (piriformis vs trochanteric). Note the femur's anterior bow (radius of curvature ~120 cm) and confirm the selected nail's bow is appropriate for the patient's height/canal length — mismatched bow is a leading cause of anterior cortical perforation.
- **Implant selection**: choose entry-point-specific nail design (piriformis-entry nails are straighter proximally; trochanteric-entry nails have a lateral proximal bow matched to the greater trochanter). Have a range of diameters (typically 9-13 mm) and lengths available, plus proximal and distal interlocking options (standard vs multiplanar/spiral blade distal interlocks) on the shelf.
- **Equipment**: radiolucent fracture table with a well-padded perineal post (or a flat radiolucent table for a lateral/manual-traction technique), C-arm positioned for orthogonal AP/lateral imaging of the hip, femur, and knee, femoral distractor or large reduction (ball-spike) instruments for comminuted patterns, a set of blocking (Poller) screws for segmental/comminuted zones, and a cannulated reaming system with graduated reamers.
- **Case-specific planning**: for Winquist III/IV or segmental fractures, plan the reduction strategy in advance (semi-open reduction, femoral distractor placement sites, blocking screw trajectory) since traction alone will not reliably correct rotational or coronal deformity through a comminuted zone. For anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients with a closed injury, plan timing with the anesthesia/hematology team — a 24-48 h delay for reversal or clearance is usually acceptable provided the limb is splinted and monitored, but open fracture, vascular compromise, or compartment syndrome overrides this.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard for fracture-table antegrade nailing (muscle relaxation aids traction/reduction); consider a regional block (fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block) as a perioperative analgesic adjunct to reduce opioid requirement, placed after confirming no planned compartment monitoring conflict.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent fracture table with a well-padded perineal post, injured leg in traction with the foot secured in a boot attachment, contralateral leg abducted and flexed out of the fluoroscopic field (or positioned in a leg holder) to allow true lateral imaging of the hip. Alternatively, lateral decubitus on a flat radiolucent table with manual or distractor-assisted traction — favored by some surgeons for trochanteric entry and in patients where perineal post complications are a particular concern (obesity, prior pelvic/perineal injury).
- **Table setup**: confirm full fluoroscopic access from hip to knee before draping — rotate the C-arm through a full arc to ensure the image intensifier is not obstructed by the traction post or contralateral limb attachment at any point needed during the case.
- **Draping**: wide circumferential prep and drape from the iliac crest to the knee, keeping the entire limb free for intraoperative rotation and length assessment; drape to allow direct palpation of the greater trochanter and access to a possible separate distal incision for interlocking.
- **Landmarks**: mark the greater trochanter tip, the anticipated entry portal (piriformis fossa medial to the trochanteric tip, or the tip/lateral facet of the trochanter for trochanteric entry), and the planned proximal interlocking incision before prepping.
- **Tourniquet**: not used for femoral shaft nailing (proximal thigh tourniquet is impractical and would obscure the operative field/entry site).
- **Perineal post precaution**: use a well-padded, appropriately sized post, limit total traction time, and periodically release traction during setup/reduction troubleshooting to reduce pudendal nerve and perineal soft-tissue injury risk.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm neck integrity.** Before any guidewire passes into the canal, re-confirm on the OR fluoroscopy (and preoperative CT) that there is no ipsilateral femoral neck fracture.
2. **Position and apply traction.** Position the patient on the fracture table (or lateral decubitus on a flat table), apply longitudinal traction, and obtain a preliminary AP/lateral fluoroscopic reduction check. Adjust rotation of the traction boot to approximate normal femoral version.
3. **Choose and establish the entry portal.** For trochanteric entry, make a 3-4 cm incision proximal to the tip of the greater trochanter, split the gluteus medius fibers in line, and place the awl or guidewire at the tip/lateral facet of the trochanter, aiming down the medullary axis in both planes. For piriformis entry, the portal is more medial, at the piriformis fossa in line with the femoral canal axis on the AP view and in line with the anterior femoral neck axis on the lateral view. Confirm starting position and trajectory on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy before advancing — an off-axis start point is difficult to correct later and predisposes to malalignment or eccentric reaming.
4. **Pass the ball-tipped guidewire** across the fracture site into the distal metaphysis under fluoroscopic control, confirming central position on both views. In comminuted zones, use a femoral distractor, provisional reduction clamp, or manual manipulation to align the fragments before the guidewire crosses.
5. **Ream sequentially**, typically starting at 8-8.5 mm and advancing in 0.5 mm increments to 1-1.5 mm over the selected nail diameter. Confirm the reamer is tracking the anterior bow of the femur on lateral fluoroscopy at each pass, particularly in short-statured patients or when using long/stiff reamers — resistance or a change in reamer "feel" partway through a pass suggests eccentric reaming or impending perforation and should prompt a fluoroscopic check.
6. **Address comminution before nail passage.** For segmental or Winquist III/IV patterns, achieve provisional length, rotation, and alignment using a femoral distractor, a percutaneously placed reduction clamp, or blocking (Poller) screws placed just outside the intended nail path to narrow the effective canal and direct the nail into a corrected position — traction alone frequently fails to correct rotational or coronal deformity through a comminuted segment.
7. **Pass the nail** over the guidewire (or reamed canal) to the predetermined depth, seating it just proud of or flush with the entry point per the specific implant's design, while maintaining the reduction achieved in step 6.
8. **Lock proximally** through the targeting jig, placing the standard proximal interlocking screw(s) per the chosen nail design (some systems also offer a cephalomedullary/oblique proximal option if concurrent neck fixation is needed).
9. **Lock distally**, generally using free-hand fluoroscopic "perfect circle" technique (align the C-arm so the distal interlocking holes project as true circles, then drill/insert through the projected center) or a distal targeting attachment if the system provides one with acceptable accuracy at this nail length. Place at least two distal screws (more if using the nail as the sole fixation across a comminuted or short distal segment).
10. **Assess rotation before final tightening/end-cap placement.** Compare the cortical diameter (lesser trochanter profile) or use the "perfect circle" technique on the contralateral distal femur intraoperatively; malrotation >15° is clinically apparent and one of the most common preventable technical errors — check before releasing traction and again after final fixation.
11. **Check leg length** against the contralateral limb (clinically and fluoroscopically) before final tightening, correcting for any over-distraction from prolonged traction.
12. **Static lock as the default.** Static interlocking (proximal and distal) is standard for essentially all closed diaphyseal nailings regardless of comminution grade; it controls rotation and length without meaningfully raising nonunion risk when reaming is adequate. Reserve dynamization (removal of one set of interlocking screws) for delayed union without radiographic progression at 3-6 months, not as a primary construct choice.
13. **Final imaging and closure.** Release traction, obtain full-length fluoroscopic or radiographic confirmation of alignment, rotation, and hardware position from hip to knee. Irrigate and close the entry and interlocking incisions in layers; apply a soft dressing (bulky compressive dressings are not routinely needed for a closed nailing).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm the femoral neck is intact on live fluoroscopy immediately before reaming begins, even if preoperative CT was reviewed — a last verification costs seconds and prevents an unrecoverable error.
- Use the tip of the guidewire, not the reamer, to negotiate a comminuted or angulated segment; the guidewire is more forgiving and easier to redirect than a reamer head.
- In short-statured patients or those with an accentuated anterior bow, favor a shorter/more curved nail design and watch the lateral fluoroscopic image continuously during the last several reamer passes — anterior perforation tends to occur near the isthmus-to-distal-metaphysis transition.
- When using blocking (Poller) screws, place them on the concave side of the deformity, just outside the path the nail should travel, to narrow the effective canal and push the nail into corrected alignment; this is especially useful for very distal or very proximal shaft fractures with a short working segment.
- For rotational assessment, obtain the lesser trochanter profile view on the injured side and compare directly against a matched fluoroscopic view of the uninjured contralateral hip before final locking — this single step meaningfully reduces the incidence of clinically apparent malrotation.
- A trochanteric-entry nail is generally the safer default in patients with a narrow proximal canal, osteoporotic bone, or any concomitant femoral neck injury requiring cephalomedullary fixation, since its lateral proximal bow reduces iatrogenic varus and neck-fracture risk relative to piriformis entry.
- Periodically release fracture-table traction during setup and troubleshooting rather than maintaining it continuously through a long or difficult reduction — this reduces cumulative pudendal nerve and perineal soft-tissue load.
- When reaming a canal with retained hardware or a prior nonunion, have the previous imaging available in the room and confirm reamer compatibility/removal plan before starting.
- Consider tranexamic acid (IV at induction ± topical at closure) as a blood-loss-reduction adjunct, particularly in higher-comminution or longer cases, screening for standard contraindications first.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Missed ipsilateral femoral neck fracture** — screen with CT before reaming/nailing; historically missed in up to 19-31% of cases on initial films. This is the single most consequential avoidable complication of antegrade nailing.
- **Malrotation** — the most common preventable technical error; confirm intraoperatively against the contralateral limb before final locking. Malrotation >15° is symptomatic and clinically apparent.
- **Anterior cortical perforation** from a mismatched nail bow, particularly with long or stiff reamers in short-statured patients (the femur has a gentle anterior bow, radius of curvature ~120 cm) — watch the reamer trajectory on lateral fluoroscopy throughout, not just at the end of reaming.
- **Iatrogenic comminution at the entry site** with trochanteric start points if guidewire/awl placement is imprecise or forced.
- **Perineal post complications** — fracture-table traction carries a recognized, largely preventable risk of pudendal nerve palsy and perineal soft-tissue injury; use a well-padded post, limit traction time, and consider a flat (non-traction) table or lateral technique for prolonged or difficult reductions.
- **Anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients** — closed injuries can typically be delayed 24-48 h for reversal/clearance without meaningfully worsening outcome provided the limb is well splinted and neurovascular status is monitored; open fracture, vascular compromise, or compartment syndrome override this and require urgent intervention.
- **Nonunion/delayed union** — approximately 4-8% after reamed nailing overall, higher with unreamed nailing, smoking, NSAID use, high BMI, diabetes, and open fracture. Manage delayed/nonunion with exchange reamed nailing (see the dedicated exchange nailing technique) once infection is excluded.
- **Leg-length discrepancy** — can occur with comminuted patterns treated by nailing without careful intraoperative length restoration (traction-table over-distraction or under-reduction); confirm length against the contralateral limb before final locking.
- **Infection** — deep infection after closed femoral shaft nailing is low (well under 1-2% in most series); rates rise substantially with open fracture severity.
- **Dynamization performed too early** or prophylactically is not indicated — reserve for delayed union without radiographic progression at 3-6 months.
- **Fat embolism syndrome/ARDS** — incidence ~1-7.9% after femoral shaft fracture; classic triad of hypoxemia, neurologic changes, and petechial rash; case-fatality reported up to ~10-17% in symptomatic cases. Early stabilization (<24 h in the physiologically tolerant patient) is protective.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0-2 weeks**: mobilize out of bed day 1 when medically stable. Weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is standard after a statically locked reamed nail in Winquist 0-II patterns; protected/toe-touch weight bearing considered for Winquist III-IV or segmental patterns with tenuous fixation. Early hip/knee active-assisted ROM, quadriceps sets, ankle pumps plus pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis (LMWH or equivalent).
- **2-6 weeks**: progress weight bearing to full as tolerated in most patterns; gait training from walker/crutches to cane; active ROM, closed-chain strengthening as tolerated; radiographic check at ~6 weeks for early callus.
- **6-12 weeks**: progressive resistive strengthening of hip abductors/quadriceps/hamstrings; proprioceptive and balance training; single-leg stance work; solid bridging callus typically by 12-16 weeks in simple patterns.
- **3-6 months**: full unrestricted activity, running, and impact sport generally deferred until radiographic union (bridging callus on ≥3 of 4 cortices) and clinical union (painless full weight bearing, no motion/tenderness at the fracture site), typically achieved by 4-6 months (union rates ~90-98% by 6-12 months).
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often possible by 6-8 weeks; physically demanding/manual labor typically requires 4-6+ months and confirmed radiographic union.
- **Return to sport**: pivoting/contact sport deferred until full radiographic union and restoration of at least ~90% of contralateral strength on isokinetic/functional testing, typically 5-9 months.
