## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.**
- Primary or post-traumatic elbow osteoarthritis in active patients with mechanical symptoms (loss of terminal extension/flexion, locking from loose bodies, impingement pain) and reasonably preserved joint space.
- Best results occur when symptoms are predominantly osteophyte/impingement-driven rather than from diffuse chondral loss — this population is exactly where debridement-type surgery should be tried before total elbow arthroplasty, especially in throwers, laborers, and other young, active patients.
- Coexisting or expected ulnar neuropathy is an indication to add nerve decompression/transposition to the same procedure rather than a reason to avoid it.

**Contraindications.**
- Absolute: active joint infection.
- Relative: diffuse advanced chondral loss/joint-space obliteration (debridement alone will not durably relieve pain from bone-on-bone arthritis — consider TEA or, in the young high-demand patient, interposition arthroplasty instead); significant elbow instability or collateral ligament insufficiency (debridement/fossa fenestration does not address instability and may worsen symptomatic laxity); severe fixed flexion contracture or heterotopic ossification bridging the joint, where the mechanical block is not primarily osteophyte-related; and patients with unrealistic expectations of achieving a normal, pain-free arc when the underlying disease is advanced.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP/lateral (± oblique) elbow radiographs to characterize osteophyte location (coronoid/olecranon tips, coronoid/olecranon fossae), loose bodies, and joint-space preservation; CT for detailed loose-body mapping and preoperative planning of the debridement/fenestration extent, particularly before an open OK procedure.
- **Nerve assessment**: obtain nerve conduction studies when ulnar neuropathy is suspected clinically (up to 20–40% coexistence in elbow OA), and examine specifically for a subluxating/dislocating ulnar nerve, which changes the transposition decision.
- **Approach selection**: decide preoperatively between open (classic OK — ulnohumeral arthroplasty with olecranon fossa fenestration) and arthroscopic ("column procedure" — anterior and posterior compartment debridement, osteophyte excision, capsular release, loose-body removal) based on surgeon experience, loose-body location/number, and the need for concurrent open ulnar nerve work.
- **Instrumentation/equipment**: for the open approach — osteotomes, curettes, and a fenestration burr/trephine for the olecranon fossa; for the arthroscopic approach — standard elbow arthroscopy tower with anterior and posterior portal instrumentation, a shaver and burr, and fluoroscopy on standby for confirming adequate bony resection if needed.
- **HO-risk stratification**: identify patients at elevated heterotopic ossification risk (prior HO, head injury, burns) preoperatively so indomethacin or single-dose radiation prophylaxis can be arranged for the immediate postoperative period.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia or regional block (supraclavicular/infraclavicular brachial plexus block), often combined for postoperative analgesia; a regional block also facilitates the early aggressive motion protocol central to this procedure's success.
- **Position**: for the arthroscopic column procedure, lateral decubitus or supine with a arm-positioning device/traction tower is most common, allowing access to both anterior and posterior compartments; for the open OK procedure, supine or lateral decubitus with the arm supported on a hand table or bolster, elbow flexed, is typical.
- **Table setup**: tourniquet applied high on the arm after exsanguination; an arm-positioning tower or padded bolster to hold the elbow at the desired flexion angle for each stage of the procedure (extension for posterior work, flexion for anterior compartment access).
- **Draping**: circumferential prep/drape of the entire upper extremity above the tourniquet to allow free intraoperative flexion/extension.
- **Landmarks**: mark the olecranon tip, medial and lateral epicondyles, radial head, and the course of the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel before incision/portal placement; for arthroscopy, standard anterior (proximal anteromedial/anterolateral) and posterior (posterolateral, straight posterior, posterocentral/trans-triceps) portal sites are marked with the elbow held in the position that maximizes safe distance from the neurovascular structures at each portal.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy available intraoperatively to confirm adequate osteophyte resection and fossa fenestration, particularly for the open approach or when arthroscopic visualization of bony landmarks is limited by synovitis/scarring.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach selection and initial exposure**:
   - **Open OK procedure**: a posterior triceps-splitting or paratricipital approach exposes the distal humerus and olecranon; alternatively, a limited posterolateral (column) approach can be used.
   - **Arthroscopic column procedure**: establish standard anterior portals first (with the joint distended by saline injection through the soft spot to displace neurovascular structures anteriorly before portal placement) to address the anterior compartment, followed by posterior portals for the posterior compartment.
2. **Anterior compartment debridement** (arthroscopic) or anterior exposure (open): identify and excise anterior osteophytes at the coronoid tip and coronoid fossa, remove anterior loose bodies, and perform an anterior capsular release/capsulectomy as needed to restore terminal extension — work methodically and stay on bone when working near the anterior capsule to protect the median nerve, brachial artery, and radial nerve, which lie in the anterior soft tissues.
3. **Posterior compartment debridement**: excise posterior/olecranon-tip osteophytes and remove posterior loose bodies from the olecranon fossa and posterior gutters; address posteromedial and posterolateral impingement, common in throwing athletes, by removing osteophytes along the posteromedial olecranon and trochlea.
4. **Olecranon fossa fenestration** (the defining step of the classic OK procedure): create a bony window (fenestration/trephination) through the floor of the olecranon fossa connecting the anterior and posterior compartments — this allows the coronoid and coronoid fossa to be debrided through the same working window as the olecranon tip and olecranon fossa, addressing terminal extension and flexion impingement simultaneously; in the arthroscopic column procedure the same principle is achieved via a burr working through both anterior and posterior portals without a single dedicated bony window.
5. **Coronoid and coronoid fossa debridement**: remove coronoid-tip osteophytes and deepen/clear the coronoid fossa so the coronoid can seat fully in terminal flexion without bony impingement.
6. **Radiocapitellar assessment**: inspect and address radiocapitellar osteophytes/loose bodies if contributing to symptoms, particularly forearm rotation-related pain.
7. **Confirm restored motion intraoperatively**: take the elbow through a full passive flexion-extension arc and pronosupination after debridement to confirm mechanical impingement has been relieved; use fluoroscopy if bony clearance is in question.
8. **Ulnar nerve decompression/transposition** (when indicated): decompress the cubital tunnel by releasing the overlying fascia/Osborne's ligament; transpose the nerve anteriorly (subcutaneous or submuscular) when pre-op neuropathy is present or when the flexion gain achieved by the debridement is large enough to risk traction neuropathy on an untransposed nerve.
9. **Hemostasis and closure**: release the tourniquet and achieve hemostasis; for the open approach, repair the triceps-splitting interval (if used) and close in layers; for arthroscopic portals, close with simple sutures/adhesive strips.
10. **Dressing and initial immobilization**: apply a soft, bulky compressive dressing (rather than a rigid immobilizing splint) to permit the early aggressive motion protocol to begin within the first few postoperative days.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Distend the joint with saline through the soft spot before establishing the first anterior arthroscopy portal — this displaces the neurovascular bundle anteriorly away from the capsule and creates a safer working space for portal placement.
- Address both compartments through the fenestration/fossa window (or via combined anterior and posterior portals arthroscopically) in the same setting — treating only the compartment causing the most obvious symptoms and leaving the other under-debrided is a common cause of incomplete relief.
- Take the elbow through a full passive arc intraoperatively after debridement, not just a visual inspection of the resected bone, to confirm the mechanical block has actually been relieved.
- Have a low threshold to add ulnar nerve decompression when the anticipated flexion gain is substantial — a nerve that tolerated a limited preoperative arc may not tolerate the new range without decompression/transposition.
- Stay directly on bone when debriding near the anterior capsule to protect the median nerve, brachial artery, and radial nerve, which are in close proximity anteriorly.
- Use fluoroscopy intraoperatively when in doubt about the adequacy of osteophyte resection or fossa fenestration, especially in revision cases or where synovitis obscures arthroscopic bony landmarks.
- In throwing athletes, specifically inspect and address posteromedial olecranon/trochlear impingement, which is a common and easily under-treated pain generator distinct from the central fossa osteophytes.
- Start motion early and aggressively (day 1–3) — this procedure's success depends as much on the postoperative motion protocol as on the bony work itself, since recurrent stiffness and heterotopic ossification are the main threats to a good result.
- Pre-identify high-HO-risk patients (prior HO, head injury, burns) so prophylaxis (indomethacin or single-dose radiation) is arranged proactively rather than reactively.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Heterotopic ossification is a recognized complication and a specific driver of the early-motion post-op protocol.
- Incomplete relief results if diffuse chondral loss is present and unaddressed by debridement alone.
- Transient ulnar neuropathy can occur, including from the debridement/decompression itself if the nerve is retracted or handled excessively.
- Recurrent stiffness/loose body formation can develop over time; reoperation rate is reported at ≈4%.
- Candidacy hinges on generally preserved joint space and impingement/osteophyte-predominant symptoms rather than diffuse chondral loss.
- Neurovascular injury (median nerve, brachial artery, radial nerve anteriorly; ulnar nerve posteromedially) is a risk during aggressive anterior or posterior debridement if instruments stray off bone.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Early motion protocol — continuous passive motion or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM starting day 1–3 postoperatively to prevent recurrent stiffness and heterotopic-ossification-related motion loss.
- Indomethacin or single-dose radiation prophylaxis considered in high-HO-risk patients (prior HO, head injury, burns).
- Strengthening deferred to 6 weeks.
- Return to manual labor/sport 3–4 months.
- Reported outcomes (2024 series): Mayo Elbow Performance Score improvement from ≈60.7 to ≈84.6, with low complication (≈2.8%) and reoperation (≈4.2%) rates.
