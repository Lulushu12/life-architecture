## Indications

- Lewis-Rorabeck II (implant stable, displaced) — ORIF.
- Lewis-Rorabeck III (implant loose or failing) — revision TKA.
- Retrograde intramedullary nailing only when the femoral component has an open (posterior-stabilized box or cruciate-retaining) design permitting nail passage (Su type I, occasionally II).

## Positioning & setup

- Confirm the specific implant's box geometry from operative records or manufacturer specifications before planning a nailing strategy — do not assume compatibility from the component's appearance on plain film.

## Steps

1. **Lewis-Rorabeck II:** locked distal femoral plating is the workhorse.
2. Consider retrograde intramedullary nailing only when confirmed compatible with an open-box femoral component design (Su type I, occasionally II).
3. Use dual-plating (medial + lateral) or plate-nail combinations for comminuted or osteoporotic patterns needing added stability.
4. **Lewis-Rorabeck III:** proceed to revision TKA, typically with a stemmed femoral component bridging the fracture, metaphyseal augments/cones for associated bone loss, and fracture-specific fixation (cerclage, plate) as an adjunct to the revision construct.
5. Reserve distal femoral replacement (hinged tumor-style prosthesis) for unreconstructable comminution, severe bone loss, or the frail elderly patient in whom immediate full weight-bearing is the priority.
6. In revision for LR III, restore the joint line and use a rotating-hinge design when collateral ligament integrity cannot be confirmed or restored.
7. At the index TKA, avoid anterior femoral notching — the single most modifiable risk factor for this fracture pattern.

## Pitfalls

- Assuming retrograde nail compatibility from plain-film component appearance is an avoidable planning error — always confirm box geometry from operative records or manufacturer specifications.
- Undertreating comminuted or osteoporotic patterns with a single plate — consider dual-plating or a plate-nail combination.
- Balancing early ROM work against fracture protection is a genuine tension after ORIF — pace individually with the treating surgeon rather than defaulting to prolonged immobilization, which risks superimposed stiffness.

## Post-op protocol

**ORIF (Lewis-Rorabeck II):**
- **Weeks 0–4:** hinged brace locked in extension for ambulation, partial weight-bearing; ROM exercises (passive/active-assisted) begin early (within days) to avoid superimposed stiffness while balancing fracture protection.
- **Weeks 4–8:** progression to full weight-bearing with radiographic progress; brace unlocked incrementally as ROM and fracture stability allow.
- **Weeks 8+:** continued ROM progression targeting functional flexion (≥90–100°); union assessment at 3 months.

**Revision TKA (Lewis-Rorabeck III):** weight-bearing as tolerated if stemmed components achieve solid diaphyseal fixation, ROM progression targeting 90° flexion by 6 weeks, full recovery trajectory 6–12 months.

Complications to watch for: nonunion/malunion (higher with ORIF around a loose or osteoporotic construct), hardware failure and recurrent periprosthetic fracture at the proximal end of the construct, dislocation, and periprosthetic joint infection. Periprosthetic fractures in the elderly carry hip-fracture-equivalent mortality risk and should be treated with the same medical optimization urgency.
