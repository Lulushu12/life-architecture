## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Lewis-Rorabeck II (implant stable, displaced) — ORIF.
- Lewis-Rorabeck III (implant loose or failing) — revision TKA.
- Retrograde intramedullary nailing only when the femoral component has an open (posterior-stabilized box or cruciate-retaining) design permitting nail passage (Su type I, occasionally II).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Lewis-Rorabeck I (non-displaced, implant stable) fractures — treat with a hinged brace and protected weight-bearing; ORIF is unnecessary morbidity for a stable, non-displaced pattern.
- A closed-box femoral component design or a fracture line through the intercondylar box (Su type II/III) — retrograde nailing is mechanically precluded; do not attempt to force nail passage.
- Active periprosthetic joint infection — work up and treat infection before committing to either ORIF or revision; fixation into an infected bed will not achieve union or a durable revision.
- Severe comminution or bone loss unreconstructable by plating or a standard stemmed revision — proceed directly to distal femoral replacement rather than attempting a construct likely to fail.
- Medically unfit patients for whom the longer operative time of revision TKA is prohibitive — consider distal femoral replacement (shorter, single-stage, immediate weight-bearing) as the pragmatic salvage option in the frail elderly.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** full-length femur (not knee-only) radiographs to characterize fracture location relative to the femoral component box, assess implant fixation (loosening, subsidence, new radiolucency versus prior films), and plan plate/nail length. CT is useful for comminution and for confirming component rotation if malposition is suspected as a contributing factor.
- **Confirm implant compatibility before planning any nailing strategy.** Obtain the operative record or contact the manufacturer to confirm the specific femoral component's box geometry (open versus closed intercondylar box) — do not assume compatibility from the component's appearance on plain film; this is an avoidable planning error that can only be caught preoperatively or very early intraoperatively, before a nail has been attempted.
- **Implant/graft selection:** for ORIF, plan a locked distal femoral plate long enough to achieve solid proximal fixation well above the fracture; have a second (medial) plate or a plate-nail combination available for comminuted or osteoporotic patterns. For revision, have a stemmed femoral component system, metaphyseal augments/cones, and a rotating-hinge option available, since the definitive construct is often decided only after intraoperative assessment of collateral ligament integrity and bone loss.
- **Equipment list:** distal femoral locking plate set (with polyaxial/variable-angle locking screw capability preferred for periprosthetic fixation around an implant), retrograde nail system (only if box geometry has been confirmed compatible), cerclage cable set, revision TKA tray with stemmed components and augments, rotating-hinge system on standby, and structural allograft for significant bone loss.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or combined general/regional (spinal + peripheral nerve block, e.g., adductor canal) anesthesia; a tourniquet is optional — many surgeons avoid it for ORIF around a periprosthetic fracture to allow real-time assessment of bleeding and fracture reduction, but it can be available high on the thigh if needed for revision exposure.
- Supine position on a radiolucent table with a bump under the ipsilateral hip if needed for limb rotation control, and the knee free to flex over a bolster or with an assistant/positioner supporting the leg for intraoperative fluoroscopy and range-of-motion assessment.
- Prep and drape circumferentially from mid-thigh to the foot to allow free manipulation of the leg for reduction, trialing, and fluoroscopic imaging throughout the case.
- Position the C-arm to image the distal femur and knee in orthogonal planes without repositioning the leg; confirm image quality before draping is finalized.
- Mark the prior incision, plan to incorporate the most lateral previous longitudinal incision if multiple exist, and mark the planned proximal extent of plate/nail fixation using preoperative full-length films.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### ORIF for Lewis-Rorabeck II

1. **Confirm implant stability and box compatibility before incision.** Reconfirm from imaging and operative records that the femoral component is well-fixed and, if nailing is being considered, that the box design permits nail passage (Su type I, occasionally II).
2. **Approach and exposure.** Use a lateral submuscular or standard lateral approach to the distal femur, extending proximally to allow adequate plate length and distally to expose the fracture and, where needed, the joint for direct visualization of reduction relative to the femoral component.
3. **Fracture reduction.** Reduce the fracture with a combination of manual traction, a femoral distractor or bone hook, and provisional clamps; favor indirect/minimally invasive reduction technique where comminution allows, preserving periosteal blood supply at the fracture site — this matters as much for periprosthetic distal femur fractures as it does for native supracondylar fractures.
4. **Locked distal femoral plating (workhorse for LR II).** Apply a precontoured locking distal femoral plate laterally, achieving multiple locking screws in the distal (often short, metaphyseal) segment around or adjacent to the femoral component, and bicortical fixation proximally along the shaft. Use polyaxial locking screws where available to navigate around the existing implant's pegs/lugs.
5. **Retrograde intramedullary nailing (only if confirmed compatible).** If the box design is confirmed open and permits nail passage, prepare the entry point through the intercondylar box under fluoroscopic guidance, ream and pass the nail retrograde, and achieve interlocking fixation proximally and distally per standard retrograde nailing technique — do not proceed with this step until box compatibility is fluoroscopically and radiographically reconfirmed at this point in the case.
6. **Augment fixation for comminuted/osteoporotic patterns.** Add a second (medial) plate in a dual-plating construct, or combine a plate with a retrograde nail (plate-nail combination), when a single lateral plate alone is judged insufficient for the comminution or bone quality encountered.
7. **Provisional and final fixation check.** Confirm reduction and hardware position fluoroscopically in orthogonal planes, check coronal and rotational alignment against the contralateral limb, and take the knee through a gentle range of motion to confirm no hardware-implant impingement before final tightening.
8. **Closure.** Close the vastus lateralis/fascia over the plate in layers, place a drain if extensive dissection occurred, and reconfirm distal neurovascular status.

### Revision TKA for Lewis-Rorabeck III

1. **Exposure and component removal.** Extend the prior incision as needed; remove the loose/failing femoral component (and any other components requiring revision) with osteotomes and, if cemented, cement-removal instrumentation, preserving as much host bone as possible.
2. **Fracture-site and bone-loss assessment.** Debride the fracture site and assess associated metaphyseal bone loss directly — this often exceeds what preoperative imaging suggested, particularly with comminution at the fracture itself.
3. **Prepare the canal and select a stemmed femoral component.** Ream/broach the femoral canal to solid diaphyseal engagement and select a stemmed component that bridges the fracture, transferring load past the deficient/fractured metaphyseal-diaphyseal zone to intact diaphyseal bone.
4. **Address metaphyseal bone loss.** Use metaphyseal augments or cones for AORI-graded bone deficiency associated with the fracture, following the same defect-preparation principles as elective revision (sequential reaming/broaching to a tight circumferential press-fit, avoiding over- or under-preparation).
5. **Fix the fracture as an adjunct to the revision construct.** Secure fracture fragments around the stemmed component with cerclage cables and/or a supplementary plate — the revision stem provides the primary load-bearing bypass, while cerclage/plate fixation controls rotation and holds fragments reduced during healing.
6. **Restore the joint line and select constraint level.** Use the fibular head, epicondylar axis, and patellar tracking to help restore the joint line when native landmarks are distorted by fracture or prior bone loss. Assess collateral ligament integrity directly (it frequently cannot be confirmed or trusted after a displaced periprosthetic fracture with associated soft-tissue injury) — use a rotating-hinge design whenever collateral competence cannot be confirmed or restored, rather than assuming a lower-constraint construct will suffice.
7. **Distal femoral replacement (segmental) as a fallback.** Reserve for unreconstructable comminution, severe bone loss, or the frail elderly patient in whom immediate full weight-bearing without a prolonged protected phase is the clinical priority.
8. **Trial and final fixation check.** Trial components, assess stability through range of motion, confirm fracture fragment reduction is maintained, and check patellar tracking before final implantation.
9. **Final implantation and closure.** Cement or press-fit final components per system protocol, secure fracture fixation, close in layers over a drain, and reconfirm neurovascular status.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Verify femoral component box geometry from the operative record or manufacturer specification before draping — this single confirmation step prevents committing to a nailing strategy intraoperatively only to discover the box is closed.
- Use polyaxial/variable-angle locking screws in the distal fragment when plating around a retained femoral component — standard fixed-angle locking screws often cannot avoid the component's fixation pegs/lugs, and variable-angle technology solves this without sacrificing locked fixation.
- Favor indirect reduction and minimally invasive plate passage where fracture comminution allows — preserving periosteal blood supply at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction meaningfully affects union rates in this fracture-prone stress-riser zone.
- When adding a second plate for a comminuted pattern, place it in a true orthogonal (medial) plane rather than simply doubling lateral fixation — this most effectively controls the sagittal-plane deforming forces typical of supracondylar periprosthetic fractures.
- In revision for LR III, do not assume collateral competence from preoperative imaging — assess directly after component removal and fracture reduction, and have a rotating-hinge system available even when a lower-constraint plan seems reasonable on paper.
- Use the uninjured, contralateral limb as an intraoperative reference for rotational alignment when plating a comminuted distal femur — rotational malreduction is easy to miss on a single fluoroscopic view.
- At the index primary TKA (the preventive counterpart to this technique), avoid anterior femoral notching — the single most modifiable risk factor for this fracture pattern; more than 3 mm of notching is associated with a substantially increased fracture risk in multiple series.
- When early ROM work is needed to prevent superimposed stiffness but fracture stability is a concern, communicate an explicit, individualized pacing plan with the physiotherapy team rather than defaulting to either extreme (full immobilization or unrestricted early motion).

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Assuming retrograde nail compatibility from plain-film component appearance is an avoidable planning error — always confirm box geometry from operative records or manufacturer specifications.
- Undertreating comminuted or osteoporotic patterns with a single plate — consider dual-plating or a plate-nail combination.
- Balancing early ROM work against fracture protection is a genuine tension after ORIF — pace individually with the treating surgeon rather than defaulting to prolonged immobilization, which risks superimposed stiffness.
- Placing distal locking screws that inadvertently conflict with the femoral component's fixation pegs, or that violate the implant-bone interface, can compromise both fracture fixation and implant fixation simultaneously — plan screw trajectories against known component geometry.
- Underestimating metaphyseal bone loss at the fracture site during revision planning — as with elective aseptic revision, direct intraoperative assessment frequently exceeds what preoperative imaging suggested.
- Nonunion/malunion (higher with ORIF around a loose or osteoporotic construct), hardware failure and recurrent periprosthetic fracture at the proximal end of the construct, dislocation, and periprosthetic joint infection are all recognized complications. Periprosthetic fractures in the elderly carry hip-fracture-equivalent mortality risk and should be treated with the same medical optimization urgency.

## Postoperative Protocol

**ORIF (Lewis-Rorabeck II):**
- **Weeks 0–4:** hinged brace locked in extension for ambulation, partial weight-bearing; ROM exercises (passive/active-assisted) begin early (within days) to avoid superimposed stiffness while balancing fracture protection.
- **Weeks 4–8:** progression to full weight-bearing with radiographic progress; brace unlocked incrementally as ROM and fracture stability allow.
- **Weeks 8+:** continued ROM progression targeting functional flexion (≥90–100°); union assessment at 3 months.

**Revision TKA (Lewis-Rorabeck III):** weight-bearing as tolerated if stemmed components achieve solid diaphyseal fixation, ROM progression targeting 90° flexion by 6 weeks, full recovery trajectory 6–12 months.

Complications to watch for: nonunion/malunion (higher with ORIF around a loose or osteoporotic construct), hardware failure and recurrent periprosthetic fracture at the proximal end of the construct, dislocation, and periprosthetic joint infection. Periprosthetic fractures in the elderly carry hip-fracture-equivalent mortality risk and should be treated with the same medical optimization urgency.
