## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Unstable Weber B ankle fracture: positive stress test (manual external rotation or gravity stress radiograph), talar shift >2 mm on mortise view, or a bimalleolar/trimalleolar-equivalent pattern (fibula fracture plus deltoid rupture, even without a medial malleolus fracture).
- All Weber C and Maisonneuve patterns — the syndesmosis is disrupted by definition and always requires fibular fixation plus syndesmosis stabilization.
- Displaced medial malleolus fracture (>2 mm) regardless of fibular pattern.
- Talar shift on stress view even in an apparently isolated, non-displaced Weber A/B fracture.
- Open fracture, fracture-dislocation with skin compromise, or failure of closed reduction to restore/maintain the mortise.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active soft-tissue infection or unresolved fracture blisters over the planned incision — delay until re-epithelialized.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease or uncontrolled diabetes with poor perfusion — consider staged or minimally invasive fixation (fibular nailing) or discuss primary tibiotalocalcaneal nailing/external fixation with the patient.
- Non-ambulatory or medically unfit patients in whom the surgical risk exceeds the functional benefit — cast/boot immobilization or a syndesmosis-sparing salvage nail may be preferable.
- Isolated Weber A or a truly stable Weber B (negative stress test, intact deltoid) — these are non-operative regardless of fibular displacement, since instability, not displacement alone, drives the surgical decision.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP, mortise (15–20° internal rotation), and lateral ankle radiographs; full-length tibia-fibula films whenever a Maisonneuve pattern is suspected (disproportionate medial tenderness with an unremarkable-looking ankle). CT is obtained for any intra-articular extension, comminution, or posterior malleolus involvement — it is essentially mandatory whenever a posterior malleolus fragment is visible on the lateral radiograph, since morphology (Mason & Molloy) drives fixation strategy.
- **Templating**: measure fibular length and rotation against the contralateral uninjured ankle if the pattern is complex; note plate length needed to span comminution and screw trajectory for the medial malleolus.
- **Implant selection**: one-third tubular or precontoured anatomic locking fibular plate; consider an intramedullary fibular nail (with locking end-caps) for osteoporotic bone, thin/compromised soft tissue, or a low-energy spiral fracture in an elderly patient. Have both quadricortical/tricortical syndesmotic screws (3.5 mm and 4.5 mm) and a suture-button implant opened and available — the intraoperative stress test, not the preoperative plan, decides which is used and whether one is needed at all.
- **Equipment**: mini and large pointed reduction (Weber) clamps, a lamina spreader or small distractor for comminuted patterns, image intensifier positioned for unobstructed AP/mortise/lateral views, and — if available — intraoperative CT/O-arm or a plan for contralateral-comparison fluoroscopy.
- **Case-specific planning**: in diabetic neuropathy/Charcot risk, plan an "overtreated" construct (bridge plating, additional screws, or combined nail-plus-plate) from the outset rather than a standard construct. In frail/non-ambulatory patients, discuss a syndesmosis-only nail or tibiotalocalcaneal nail salvage construct with the patient before draping.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal anesthesia, supplemented with a popliteal sciatic ± saphenous regional block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the ipsilateral hip/buttock to internally rotate the limb and bring the ankle into a neutral, easily accessible position for the lateral approach; alternatively a lateral decubitus or "sloppy lateral" position with a bean bag if the posterior malleolus will also be approached posterolaterally.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet applied but often not inflated for a routine ORIF unless visualization demands it; if used, exsanguinate with an Esmarch and limit inflation time.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm brought in from the contralateral side (or foot of bed), positioned to obtain true AP, mortise (15–20° internal rotation), and lateral views without repositioning the leg. Confirm a clean mortise view of the *uninjured* contralateral ankle can also be obtained on the same draped field or from stored pre-op images for comparison.
- **Draping**: standard extremity drape with the leg free to allow full internal/external rotation intraoperatively for stress testing; prep the entire limb from above the tourniquet to the toes to allow foot manipulation.
- **Landmarks**: mark the fibular shaft, lateral malleolus tip, anterior/posterior tibial tubercles (incisura), and — if a posterior malleolus or posteromedial approach is planned — the Achilles tendon border and posteromedial neurovascular bundle course, before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Position and prep** as above; apply and inflate the tourniquet if planned.
2. **Approach the fibula** through a direct lateral or posterolateral incision centered over the fracture, protecting the superficial peroneal nerve (crosses the field anteriorly, roughly 10–12 cm proximal to the tip of the fibula) and the sural nerve if the incision extends posteriorly.
3. **Reduce the fibula**: clear hematoma and interposed periosteum from the fracture site, then reduce using a combination of a pointed reduction clamp, provisional K-wires as joysticks, and manual traction/rotation. Restore length, rotation, and coronal alignment by referencing the fibular fracture pattern (interdigitation of a spiral/oblique fracture line is often the best guide to rotation) and checking the fibula against the incisura on fluoroscopy.
4. **Fix the fibula**: for a simple oblique/spiral fracture, place one or two interfragmentary lag screws through or independent of a neutralization one-third tubular or precontoured locking plate; for comminuted or very distal fractures, use a locking plate in bridging mode. If IM nailing is chosen, ream/broach the medullary canal from a lateral entry point at the tip of the fibula and lock proximally and distally.
5. **Confirm fibular reduction fluoroscopically** on true mortise and lateral views — check for restoration of Shenton-type continuity of the lateral tibiotalar joint space and symmetric medial/superior clear space compared to the contralateral side or preinjury films.
6. **Address the medial malleolus** (if fractured) through a separate anteromedial incision, protecting the saphenous vein/nerve. Clear the fracture of interposed periosteum/deltoid fibers (a common block to anatomic reduction), reduce under direct vision, and fix with two parallel partially threaded 4.0 mm cancellous screws for a simple oblique/transverse fragment, a tension-band construct for a small avulsion fragment, or a small buttress/antiglide plate for a vertical shear (supination-adduction) fragment that resists screw-only fixation.
7. **Assess and fix the posterior malleolus** if the fragment meets fixation criteria (see the dedicated posterior malleolus technique for approach selection by Mason & Molloy type) — perform this step before final syndesmosis assessment, since restoring the posterior malleolus (which carries the PITFL insertion) often restores syndesmotic stability directly.
8. **Stress-test the syndesmosis** on every Weber B/C ankle after fibula (and posterior malleolus, if fixed) fixation: perform the Cotton test (grasp the fibula with a bone hook or clamp and translate it anteriorly/posteriorly relative to the tibia under fluoroscopy, watching for >2–3 mm of translation or visible gapping) and an external rotation stress test with the knee flexed to relax the gastrocnemius.
9. **If the stress test is positive**, reduce the fibula into the incisura with a large pointed reduction clamp — place the medial tine on the anterior third of the tibia (to avoid inducing posterior fibular translation, a well-described source of malreduction) roughly 2 cm proximal to the joint line, parallel to the joint, and the lateral tine directly on the fibula.
10. **Stabilize the syndesmosis** while holding the clamp reduction:
    - *Screw fixation*: place one or two 3.5 mm or 4.5 mm quadricortical (or tricortical, sparing the far medial cortex) screws 2–4 cm above the joint line, directed 25–30° from posterolateral to anteromedial (parallel to the plafond, from the fibula into the tibia), through or just proximal to the plate.
    - *Suture-button fixation*: drill from the fibula into the tibia at a similar trajectory and tension, seating the device to allow physiologic micromotion without over-tightening (which recreates a rigid, screw-like construct and risks malreduction).
11. **Verify the reduction directly**, not by fluoroscopy alone — compare mortise fluoroscopy to the contralateral ankle, reference the anterior and posterior tibial tubercles for symmetric fibular seating in the incisura, or use intraoperative CT/O-arm if available. Release the reduction clamp before final imaging to confirm the construct — not the clamp — is holding the reduction.
12. **Final fluoroscopic check**: AP, mortise, and lateral views confirming anatomic fibular length/rotation, restored medial clear space (≤4 mm, equal to the superior clear space), tibiofibular clear space ≤6 mm, and tibiofibular overlap ≥1 mm on mortise view.
13. **Irrigate, achieve hemostasis, deflate the tourniquet before closure if it was inflated**, and close in layers (deep fascia, subcutaneous, skin) over a drain if extensive dissection or bleeding is anticipated. Apply a well-padded splint with the ankle at neutral dorsiflexion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the spiral/oblique fracture line of the fibula itself as a reduction guide — anatomic interdigitation of the fracture edges is often a more reliable indicator of rotation than fluoroscopy alone.
- When placing the syndesmotic reduction clamp, put the tibial tine on the anterior third of the tibia rather than centered or posterior — a posteriorly placed tibial tine tends to translate the fibula posteriorly relative to the tibia, a well-documented cause of syndesmotic malreduction.
- Always obtain (or have stored) a true mortise view of the contralateral, uninjured ankle before draping — comparing tibiofibular overlap and clear space to the patient's own normal anatomy is far more reliable than relying on population-average numbers, since normal incisura morphology varies.
- Direct visualization of the incisura (through the fibular fracture site or a small anterior arthrotomy) to confirm fibular seating is a simple, low-cost adjunct that catches malreduction fluoroscopy alone misses.
- Externally rotate the foot 15–20° while placing syndesmotic screws/suture-button — this reduces the risk of iatrogenic syndesmotic narrowing/overcompression that can occur if the screw is placed with the foot in neutral or internal rotation.
- When comminution or a very distal fibula fracture limits screw purchase, choose a locking plate in bridge mode rather than forcing interfragmentary screws that further destabilize the fragments.
- If a posterior malleolus fragment is present, fix it before final syndesmosis assessment — it frequently obviates the need for a separate syndesmotic implant altogether, sparing the patient a construct and a hardware-removal decision.
- In osteoporotic bone, favor a fibular IM nail or locked plating construct over a standard one-third tubular plate with unicortical purchase, and consider a buttress plate rather than screws alone for the medial malleolus.
- Do not over-tension a suture-button construct — snug it to close visible gapping without whittling out physiologic motion; over-tightening reintroduces the malreduction risk the device is meant to avoid.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Malreduction of even 1–2 mm of fibular shortening/rotation measurably increases tibiotalar contact pressure and accelerates post-traumatic arthritis — always compare intraoperative fluoroscopy to the contralateral uninjured mortise view when in doubt.
- Fluoroscopy alone under-detects syndesmotic malreduction, reported in a substantial minority to majority (up to ~25–50%) of screw-fixed cases when judged by fluoroscopy alone; this is strongly linked to worse patient-reported outcomes at 2 years and is considered the single most preventable technical error in ankle fracture surgery.
- Assess syndesmosis stability *after* posterior malleolus fixation, not before — fixing an adequately sized fragment (carries the PITFL insertion) can obviate a separate trans-syndesmotic implant.
- Malpositioned reduction clamps (tibial tine placed posteriorly) are a recognized, avoidable cause of iatrogenic posterior fibular translation — place the tibial tine anteriorly on the tibia.
- Missing a Maisonneuve pattern (proximal fibula fracture with disproportionate medial ankle tenderness) leaves an unstable syndesmosis untreated — obtain full-length tibia-fibula films whenever suspected, and palpate the proximal fibula/common peroneal nerve territory in every ankle injury with medial-sided tenderness out of proportion to the visible ankle injury.
- Routine syndesmotic screw removal is not required if the patient is asymptomatic; a rigid screw allows no physiologic fibular motion during dorsiflexion and breakage is common but usually benign — counsel patients about this preoperatively to avoid alarm if a screw breaks on follow-up imaging.
- Superficial peroneal nerve injury during the lateral approach and saphenous nerve/vein injury during the medial approach are avoidable with careful subcutaneous dissection and nerve identification before deep dissection.
- In osteoporotic bone, consider fibular IM nailing, locked plating, or buttress-augmented medial malleolar fixation; in diabetic neuropathy/Charcot risk, "overtreat" with bridge plating, additional screws, or combined intramedullary + plate constructs, extended NWB, and a low threshold for prolonged immobilization, given substantially higher malunion/nonunion/wound complication rates in this population.
- Wound dehiscence/infection risk rises with excessive retraction and thin flap elevation over the lateral malleolus — handle skin edges gently and avoid prolonged tourniquet time in patients with compromised soft tissue.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks**: splint/boot, strict elevation, NWB or toe-touch, early toe/knee ROM, wound check at 10–14 days.
- **2–6 weeks**: transition to removable boot; the WAX trial (UK RCT) found weight-bearing initiated at 2 weeks post-ORIF was non-inferior (trending superior) to the traditional 6-week protocol for functional outcomes with no increase in complications — supported by the 2024 AAOS guideline update for anatomically reduced, rigidly plated bimalleolar/isolated lateral malleolar fractures. Begin active/active-assisted ankle ROM (avoid aggressive inversion in medial-sided injuries) once the wound is healed.
- **6–12 weeks**: progressive WBAT to full weight-bearing, gait training, proprioception/balance work, progressive strengthening.
- **12 weeks onward**: return to impact/sport once radiographic union confirmed, full painless ROM, and strength ≥90% of contralateral side. Return to sedentary work typically 2–6 weeks, manual labor 3–4 months depending on fixation stability and job demands.
- **Syndesmosis-specific**: suture-button constructs generally tolerate the same early WB protocols as isolated fibula fixation given their dynamic nature; screw constructs historically prompted more conservative/delayed WB until screw removal or radiographic consolidation, though this gap is narrowing with rigid, well-reduced contemporary constructs. Return to sport for a fixed syndesmosis injury is typically 4–6 months.
