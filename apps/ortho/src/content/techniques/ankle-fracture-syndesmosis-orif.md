## Indications

ORIF is indicated for unstable Weber B ankle fractures (positive stress test, talar shift >2 mm, bimalleolar/trimalleolar equivalent) and for all Weber C/Maisonneuve patterns, which always require fibular fixation plus syndesmosis stabilization. Displaced medial malleolus fracture or talar shift on stress view is a surgical indication even for otherwise stable-appearing Weber A/B patterns.

## Positioning & setup

Standard lateral approach to the fibula with intraoperative fluoroscopy available for AP, mortise, and lateral views; contralateral uninjured mortise views should be available for comparison when reduction quality is in doubt. In the elderly, osteoporotic, or diabetic/neuropathic patient, plan for augmented fixation (locked plating, IM nailing, buttress constructs) rather than standard technique.

## Steps

1. **Reduce and fix the fibula first** via a standard lateral approach — restore length, rotation, and coronal alignment against the incisura. Use a one-third tubular or precontoured locking plate as a neutralization or lag-screw-plus-neutralization construct depending on fracture obliquity. Intramedullary fibular nailing is a good alternative in osteoporotic bone or compromised soft tissue (diabetics, elderly), with lower wound complication rates.
2. **Fix the medial malleolus** — two partially threaded cancellous screws or a tension band construct for small/avulsion fragments; a small buttress plate for vertical shear (supination-adduction) patterns.
3. **Assess the posterior malleolus** — if a fragment meeting fixation criteria (≥25% articular surface or CT-defined incisural involvement/step-off) is present, fix it via the approach dictated by its Mason & Molloy morphology before final syndesmosis assessment, since fixing it often restores syndesmotic stability directly.
4. **Stress-test the syndesmosis** after fibula (and posterior malleolus, if fixed) fixation using the Cotton test (anterior-posterior fibular translation with a reduction clamp/pointed instrument) and external rotation stress — perform this on every Weber B/C ankle.
5. **If positive, stabilize the syndesmosis.** Screw fixation: one or two 3.5/4.5 mm quadricortical (or tricortical) screws, 2–4 cm above the joint line, angled 25–30° anterior-to-posterior (parallel to the joint), through or just above the plate. Suture-button (dynamic) fixation is increasingly preferred based on level-I evidence and is favored in athletes and higher-demand patients; screws remain reasonable in comminuted/very unstable syndesmoses where added rigidity is desired or where suture-button implants are unavailable.
6. **Verify reduction directly**, not by fluoroscopy alone — use direct visualization/reduction clamp technique referencing the anterior and posterior tibial tubercles, contralateral-comparison fluoroscopy, or intraoperative CT/O-arm/3D imaging where available.

## Pitfalls

- Malreduction of even 1–2 mm of fibular shortening/rotation measurably increases contact pressure and accelerates post-traumatic arthritis — always compare intraoperative fluoroscopy to the contralateral uninjured mortise view when in doubt.
- Fluoroscopy alone under-detects syndesmotic malreduction, reported in a substantial minority to majority of screw-fixed cases when judged by fluoroscopy alone; this is strongly linked to worse patient-reported outcomes at 2 years and is considered the single most preventable technical error in ankle fracture surgery.
- Assess syndesmosis stability *after* posterior malleolus fixation, not before — fixing an adequately sized fragment (carries the PITFL insertion) can obviate a separate trans-syndesmotic implant.
- Routine syndesmotic screw removal is not required if the patient is asymptomatic; a rigid screw allows no physiologic fibular motion during dorsiflexion and breakage is common but usually benign.
- Missing a Maisonneuve pattern (proximal fibula fracture with disproportionate medial ankle tenderness) leaves an unstable syndesmosis untreated — obtain full-length tibia-fibula films whenever suspected.
- In osteoporotic bone, consider fibular IM nailing, locked plating, or buttress-augmented medial malleolar fixation; in diabetic neuropathy/Charcot risk, "overtreat" with bridge plating, additional screws, or combined intramedullary + plate constructs, extended NWB, and low threshold for prolonged immobilization, given substantially higher malunion/nonunion/wound complication rates.

## Post-op protocol

- 0–2 weeks: splint/boot, strict elevation, NWB or toe-touch, early toe/knee ROM, wound check at 10–14 days.
- 2–6 weeks: transition to removable boot; the WAX trial (UK RCT) found weight-bearing initiated at 2 weeks post-ORIF was non-inferior (trending superior) to the traditional 6-week protocol for functional outcomes with no increase in complications — supported by the 2024 AAOS guideline update for anatomically reduced, rigidly plated bimalleolar/isolated lateral malleolar fractures. Begin active/active-assisted ankle ROM (avoid aggressive inversion in medial-sided injuries) once the wound is healed.
- 6–12 weeks: progressive WBAT to full weight-bearing, gait training, proprioception/balance work, progressive strengthening.
- 12 weeks onward: return to impact/sport once radiographic union confirmed, full painless ROM, and strength ≥90% of contralateral side. Return to sedentary work typically 2–6 weeks, manual labor 3–4 months depending on fixation stability and job demands.
- Syndesmosis-specific: suture-button constructs generally tolerate the same early WB protocols as isolated fibula fixation given their dynamic nature; screw constructs historically prompted more conservative/delayed WB until screw removal or radiographic consolidation, though this gap is narrowing with rigid, well-reduced contemporary constructs. Return to sport for a fixed syndesmosis injury is typically 4–6 months.
