## Indications

Young/active patients participating in pivoting/cutting sports, objective instability (positive pivot-shift) that fails or is unlikely to respond to rehabilitation, and combined ligament injury. Per the AAOS 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline, surgery within 3 months of injury is now favored over the prior 5-month window, because the risk of secondary meniscal/chondral injury rises with delay and with the number of instability episodes.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed at the setup/room level in this monograph. The pivot-shift exam — the most specific test for functional instability — is best performed under anesthesia and should be graded (0–III) as part of the intraoperative assessment. Preoperative imaging review should specifically flag a Segond fracture, ramp lesion, or lateral meniscal root/posterior horn tear, since these change the operative plan (augmentation, concurrent meniscal repair, tunnel sequencing).

## Steps

1. **Select graft by age/sport/demand.** Bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft is the historical "gold standard" for high-level pivoting athletes (bone-to-bone healing, lower graft-rupture rates in young high-demand cohorts) but carries anterior knee pain, kneeling pain, and small patellar fracture/rupture risk. Hamstring tendon (semitendinosus ± gracilis) autograft has lower donor-site morbidity but historically higher graft-failure rates in young high-risk patients used alone. Quadriceps tendon autograft use has risen sharply (from <5% pre-2014 to ~20% of primary ACLR by 2023), favored for larger cross-sectional area/strength and lower anterior knee pain than BPTB, and is increasingly used in revision settings. Allograft is reasonable for low-demand/older patients or as an adjunct in multiligament reconstruction, but should be avoided as the sole graft in young high-demand pivoting athletes given higher registry re-rupture rates.
2. **In skeletally immature patients**, use physeal-sparing (all-epiphyseal or extra-physeal/over-the-top with iliotibial band) technique for boys with bone age <12y and girls <10y; use transphyseal technique (small-diameter, vertically-oriented tunnels avoiding central physeal violation, soft-tissue graft, hardware kept off the physis) as skeletal maturity approaches (bone age ≥15y male / ≥13y female). Age-specific "over-the-top" strategies have been described across prepubescent, young-adolescent, and older-adolescent growth stages to balance graft stability against growth-disturbance risk.
3. **Decide on lateral extra-articular augmentation (LET or anterolateral ligament reconstruction)** in patients with a high-risk profile: age <25, high-grade pivot-shift (grade 2–3), pivoting/contact sport, generalized ligamentous laxity, revision setting, or a Segond fracture signaling anterolateral structure injury. STABILITY 1 (618 patients, age 14–25) showed ACLR+LET reduced clinical graft failure from 40% to 25% at 2 years (RRR 0.38) with no significant increase in adverse events and only a transient reduction in quadriceps strength at 6–12 months that normalizes by 1 year.
4. **Place tunnels anatomically**, avoiding the historically "too-vertical" femoral tunnel that under-controls rotation.
5. **Choose fixation.** Femoral-side suspensory fixation (cortical button) is favored with soft-tissue grafts and smaller tunnel diameters; interference screw (metal or bioabsorbable) fixation is used, particularly for BPTB bone plugs, for bone-to-bone healing. Tibial-side fixation ranges from interference screw to staple/washer backup, chosen by bone quality and graft type. Confirm graft tensioning at the flexion angle appropriate to the graft type.
6. **Address concomitant meniscal and chondral pathology concurrently**, including recognition of ramp lesions (posteromedial meniscocapsular tears, best seen on MRI or via a posteromedial portal, associated with residual rotational laxity if unrepaired) and lateral meniscal root/posterior horn tears.
7. **In combined-ligament reconstructions**, sequence tunnels and fixation order to avoid tunnel convergence.

## Pitfalls

- Missing a concomitant PLC or PCL injury preoperatively leads to early ACL graft attritional failure — the cruciate graft stretches out if the corner or PCL is not addressed.
- Missing a ramp lesion or lateral meniscal root tear causes residual instability despite a technically sound ACLR.
- Over-aggressive early rehabilitation in combined-ligament reconstructions risks secondary-restraint overload.
- In revision ACLR, failing to address tunnel widening/malposition may require staged bone-grafting before re-reconstruction.
- Growth disturbance (limb-length discrepancy, angular deformity) is a real but low-frequency risk even with physeal-sparing technique (~2–3% in recent systematic reviews); pediatric graft rupture rates (~8% in recent meta-analysis) generally exceed adult rates, reinforcing the need for close family counseling and adherence-focused rehab. Physeal-sparing technique reduces but does not eliminate growth-disturbance risk, and graft survivorship is comparable between physeal-sparing and transphyseal approaches when technique is properly matched to skeletal maturity.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches (brace locked in extension for ambulation per surgeon protocol), ROM goal 0–90°, quad activation, patellar mobilization, cryotherapy.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progress to full weight-bearing without brace lock, ROM to full flexion, closed-chain strengthening (mini-squats, leg press), stationary bike once ROM allows, gait normalization.
- **Weeks 6–12**: open- and closed-chain progressive resistance training, proprioceptive/balance training, begin light jogging around 10–12 weeks if quadriceps strength and effusion criteria are met.
- **Months 3–6**: sport-specific agility drills, plyometrics introduced with strict quality criteria (avoid dynamic valgus), non-contact/change-of-direction training progression.
- **Months 6–9+**: return-to-sport testing battery — quadriceps and hamstring limb symmetry index (LSI) ≥90%, single-leg/triple/crossover/6-m timed hop tests ≥90% LSI, ACL-RSI psychological readiness score, and movement-quality screening for dynamic valgus. Earliest RTS clearance is 9 months, since return before 9 months carries up to a 7-fold higher second-ACL-injury risk even at 90% LSI. Passing the RTS battery reduces graft-rupture risk but has not been shown to reliably predict contralateral or overall subsequent knee injury — continue neuromuscular training after clearance, not just a one-time pass/fail test.
- **Return to work**: desk work 1–2 weeks with crutches as needed; light manual 4–8 weeks; heavy manual/labor 4–6 months.
