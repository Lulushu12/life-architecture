## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: young/active patients participating in pivoting/cutting sports; objective instability (positive pivot-shift) that fails or is unlikely to respond to rehabilitation; combined ligament injury requiring cruciate stability as part of a coordinated repair; and, per the AAOS 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline, surgery within 3 months of injury is now favored over the prior 5-month window, because the risk of secondary meniscal/chondral injury rises with delay and with the number of instability episodes.

**Add lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) or anterolateral ligament (ALL) reconstruction when**: age <25 years, high-grade pivot-shift (grade 2–3), participation in pivoting/contact sport, generalized ligamentous laxity, revision ACLR, or a Segond fracture signaling anterolateral structure injury.

**Contraindications**:
- Active knee sepsis or overlying skin/soft-tissue infection — treat and clear before elective reconstruction.
- Uncorrected significant limb malalignment (e.g., severe varus with medial compartment overload) or advanced tricompartmental osteoarthritis, where isolated ACLR is unlikely to improve function and may need to be staged with or deferred for realignment.
- Skeletally immature patients requiring an *unmodified adult-technique* transphyseal reconstruction — use physeal-sparing technique instead (see Preoperative Planning).
- Uncontrolled loss of motion/arthrofibrosis-prone knee (acute, markedly swollen, <90° flexion) — restore motion with a prehabilitation period first; operating into a stiff, inflamed knee is a leading avoidable driver of postoperative arthrofibrosis.
- Low-demand, sedentary, or older patients without functional instability — a trial of structured quadriceps rehabilitation is a reasonable first-line alternative and should be discussed as a genuine option, not a fallback.
- Relative: uncontrolled diabetes, active tobacco/nicotine use (impairs graft incorporation and wound healing — counsel on cessation), and unaddressed compliance/rehabilitation barriers, since outcome depends heavily on adherence to a multi-month protocol.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to characterize the ACL tear pattern, meniscal status (ramp lesion, lateral meniscal root/posterior horn tear), articular cartilage, and classic bone-bruise pattern (lateral femoral condyle + posterolateral tibial plateau). Plain radiographs to check for Segond fracture, tibial eminence avulsion (pediatric-equivalent injury), physeal status, and overall alignment; a long-leg alignment view is worthwhile if varus/valgus malalignment is suspected clinically.
- **Graft selection**, matched to age/sport/demand:
  - **Bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft**: historical "gold standard" for high-level pivoting athletes (bone-to-bone healing, lower graft-rupture rates in young high-demand cohorts in several registries); trade-offs include anterior knee pain, kneeling pain, and a small risk of patellar fracture/patellar tendon rupture.
  - **Hamstring tendon (semitendinosus ± gracilis) autograft**: lower donor-site morbidity, preserves the extensor mechanism; historically higher graft-failure rates in young high-risk patients when used alone without augmentation — this is the population in which STABILITY 1 demonstrated the benefit of adding LET.
  - **Quadriceps tendon autograft**: usage rose from <5% (pre-2014) to ~20% of primary ACLR by 2023 (ACL Study Group survey); favored for larger cross-sectional area/strength and lower anterior knee pain than BPTB; increasingly the leading choice in revision ACLR.
  - **Allograft**: reasonable for low-demand/older patients or as an adjunct in multiligament reconstruction to limit operative time and donor morbidity; avoid as the sole graft in young, high-demand pivoting athletes given consistently higher registry re-rupture rates.
  - **Pediatric/skeletally immature**: physeal-sparing (all-epiphyseal or extra-physeal/over-the-top with iliotibial band) technique for boys with bone age <12y and girls <10y; transphyseal technique (small-diameter, vertically-oriented tunnels avoiding central physeal violation, soft-tissue graft, hardware kept off the physis) as skeletal maturity approaches (bone age ≥15y male / ≥13y female).
- **LET graft**: typically a strip of iliotibial band (distally based, modified Lemaire) harvested separately from the intra-articular graft; if using a free tendon (e.g., gracilis) instead, plan tunnel trajectory to avoid convergence with the ACL femoral tunnel.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: standard arthroscopy tower, tourniquet, ACL tibial and femoral aiming guides, graft-specific harvest set (open-ended stripper for hamstring, oscillating saw/patellar tendon harvest set for BPTB), suspensory cortical button and/or interference screw sets (metal or bioabsorbable) sized to the tunnel, guide pins and cannulated reamers, and a graft-tensioning device if used. For LET, a separate small fragment set/anchor or an interference screw for femoral fixation of the tenodesis strip.
- **Case-specific planning**: review MRI specifically for ramp lesions and lateral meniscal root tears preoperatively so meniscal repair instruments/implants (all-inside or inside-out sets) are open and available; confirm whether a combined ligament procedure is planned so tunnel sequencing can be planned to avoid convergence.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia or spinal/regional anesthesia, frequently combined with a femoral nerve block or adductor canal block for postoperative analgesia (adductor canal preferred over femoral block when quadriceps strength preservation is prioritized for early rehab).
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard operating table with a well-padded lateral post or leg-holder positioned at mid-thigh to allow the knee to be flexed to 90° and beyond during arthroscopy and to allow varus stress for lateral compartment visualization; the contralateral leg is padded and slightly abducted/flexed in a well-leg holder.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh pneumatic tourniquet placed proximally, padded, with the limb exsanguinated before inflation; many surgeons keep it available but uninflated unless visualization requires it.
- **Fluoroscopy**: not routinely required for isolated ACLR; keep available if concurrent tibial eminence or other fracture fixation is planned.
- **Exam under anesthesia**: perform first, before prepping — Lachman, pivot-shift (graded 0–III, most specific for functional instability and best assessed under anesthesia), varus/valgus stress at 0° and 30°, and dial test, to confirm the preoperative diagnosis and detect any missed combined-ligament injury before committing to the surgical plan.
- **Draping**: circumferential limb prep from mid-thigh (above tourniquet) to the toes; a sterile tourniquet or bump may be used to allow the leg to hang free for AP/PA translation testing during the case.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: patella and patellar tendon borders, tibial tubercle, joint line, Gerdy's tubercle, fibular head, and planned anteromedial/anterolateral arthroscopic portal sites; for LET, mark the lateral epicondyle and the path of the iliotibial band.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy.** Establish standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals. Perform a systematic 13-point examination: suprapatellar pouch, medial and lateral gutters, medial and lateral compartments, intercondylar notch, and posteromedial/posterolateral recesses as indicated. Confirm the ACL tear pattern and probe the menisci circumferentially, specifically evaluating the posteromedial meniscocapsular junction for a ramp lesion (best seen via a posteromedial portal or by viewing through the notch with the scope angled posteriorly) and the lateral meniscal posterior horn/root.
2. **Address meniscal and chondral pathology first.** Repair ramp lesions (inside-out or all-inside technique) and lateral meniscal root/posterior horn tears before tunnel drilling, since instrumentation and fluid extravasation are easier to control in an unreconstructed knee, and an unrepaired ramp lesion is a well-documented cause of residual rotational laxity even after a technically sound ACLR.
3. **Harvest the graft.** For hamstring: oblique incision over the pes anserinus, identify and harvest semitendinosus (± gracilis) with an open tendon stripper, whipstitch the ends, and prepare on the back table to the required diameter (typically 7.5–9 mm total construct). For BPTB: harvest the central third of the patellar tendon with bone plugs from patella and tibial tubercle. For quadriceps tendon: harvest through a small proximal incision, with or without a bone block from the patella. Prepare the graft to final length and diameter while the notch is prepared.
4. **Notchplasty as needed** — a limited notchplasty improves visualization and avoids graft impingement, but avoid over-resection, which can weaken the lateral wall and destabilize femoral tunnel fixation.
5. **Drill the femoral tunnel first (or tibial tunnel first, per surgeon technique/preference), placing it anatomically** within the native ACL femoral footprint — avoid the historically "too-vertical" (over-the-top-adjacent, 11–12 o'clock right knee) position, which under-controls rotation and reproduces a persistent pivot-shift even with a technically "intact-looking" graft. Use an anteromedial portal or outside-in technique for independent, more horizontal femoral tunnel placement when performing an anatomic single-bundle reconstruction.
6. **Drill the tibial tunnel** aimed at the anatomic tibial ACL footprint (anterior and medial to the PCL, posterior to the anterior horn of the lateral meniscus), checking that the guide pin does not impinge the PCL or notch roof in extension.
7. **Pass the graft** from tibial to femoral tunnel under direct arthroscopic visualization, confirming smooth passage without soft-tissue snagging.
8. **Fix the femoral side first.** Suspensory cortical-button fixation is favored with soft-tissue grafts and smaller tunnel diameters; interference screw fixation (metal or bioabsorbable) is typically used for BPTB bone plugs to achieve bone-to-bone healing.
9. **Cycle the knee** through a full range of motion 20–30 times to pre-tension the graft and seat the fixation before final tibial-side fixation.
10. **Tension and fix the tibial side** at the flexion angle appropriate to the graft/technique (commonly 10–20° of flexion with firm posterior-drawer-neutral tibial reduction for standard single-bundle ACLR), applying steady longitudinal tension while confirming full extension is achieved without graft impingement and full flexion is achieved without excess laxity. Back up interference screw fixation with a staple/washer if bone quality is poor.
11. **Perform LET if indicated.** Harvest a distally based strip of iliotibial band (roughly 7–9 cm × 1 cm), leaving it attached distally at Gerdy's tubercle. Pass it deep to the lateral collateral ligament, from posterior to anterior, and fix it to the femur just proximal and posterior to the lateral epicondyle — a position distal and posterior to the typical ACL femoral tunnel trajectory — using a small anchor or interference screw. Tension the tenodesis with the knee at 60–90° of flexion (technique-dependent; many modified-Lemaire protocols use roughly 60° flexion) and the tibia held in neutral-to-slight-internal rotation, taking care not to overconstrain the joint or force it into hyperextension. Directing the femoral LET socket proximally (~30°) and anteriorly (~20°) relative to the ACL femoral tunnel has been described as a specific way to minimize tunnel convergence between the two constructs.
12. **Final arthroscopic and clinical checks.** Re-probe the graft for isometry and impingement through a full ROM arc, repeat Lachman and pivot-shift under anesthesia to confirm restored stability, and inspect meniscal repairs for stability.
13. **Closure.** Irrigate the joint, close portals and any open incisions in layers, infiltrate local anesthetic for postoperative analgesia, and apply a bulky dressing with a hinged brace locked in extension for ambulation per protocol.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Perform the exam under anesthesia before prepping/draping — it is your last chance to detect a missed combined-ligament injury before the operative plan is locked in.
- Address meniscal ramp lesions and lateral root tears *before* tunnel drilling; once tunnels are placed, instrument access and visualization for posteromedial repair become more difficult.
- Use an independent (anteromedial portal or outside-in) femoral tunnel technique rather than a transtibial femoral tunnel when anatomic, more horizontal femoral positioning is the goal — a transtibial tunnel trajectory frequently forces a too-vertical, rotationally insufficient femoral position.
- Cycle the knee before final tibial fixation — this removes creep from the graft-fixation construct and prevents early postoperative laxity from tunnel/graft "settling."
- When placing an LET femoral tunnel, keep the socket shallow and direct it proximal and anterior relative to the ACL femoral tunnel to minimize the risk of tunnel convergence — a recognized technical pitfall of the modified Lemaire technique.
- Match tibial-side graft fixation angle to graft type and associated procedures: standard single-bundle ACLR is commonly fixed near 10–20° flexion, but a combined MLKI case may require fixation closer to full extension to avoid overconstraining a knee also undergoing PCL/PLC work — sequence and communicate this explicitly when multiple grafts are tensioned in the same case.
- In revision ACLR, image and plan for tunnel widening/malposition before the case — a single-stage revision may not be feasible if prior tunnels are grossly enlarged or malpositioned, and staged bone-grafting should be arranged in advance rather than discovered intraoperatively.
- Keep LET tensioning conservative (do not over-tighten) — an overtensioned lateral tenodesis can restrict tibial internal rotation and knee flexion, producing lateral compartment overload.
- In quadriceps tendon graft harvest, protect the suprapatellar fat pad and avoid violating the anterior cortex of the patella if not intending a bone block, to reduce postoperative anterior knee pain.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Missing a concomitant PLC or PCL injury preoperatively leads to early ACL graft attritional failure — the cruciate graft stretches out if the corner or PCL is not addressed.
- Missing a ramp lesion or lateral meniscal root tear causes residual instability despite a technically sound ACLR.
- Over-aggressive early rehabilitation in combined-ligament reconstructions risks secondary-restraint overload.
- In revision ACLR, failing to address tunnel widening/malposition may require staged bone-grafting before re-reconstruction.
- Growth disturbance (limb-length discrepancy, angular deformity) is a real but low-frequency risk even with physeal-sparing technique (~2–3% in recent systematic reviews); pediatric graft rupture rates (~8% in recent meta-analysis) generally exceed adult rates, reinforcing the need for close family counseling and adherence-focused rehab. Physeal-sparing technique reduces but does not eliminate growth-disturbance risk, and graft survivorship is comparable between physeal-sparing and transphyseal approaches when technique is properly matched to skeletal maturity.
- Graft failure/re-rupture: roughly 5–15% in primary ACLR depending on age/graft/activity level, substantially higher (~40% at 2 years) in young high-risk patients treated with hamstring autograft alone versus ~25% at 2 years when LET is added (STABILITY 1, RRR 0.38) — a strong argument for routine augmentation in the high-risk profile described above.
- Contralateral ACL injury risk is comparable to, or in some cohorts exceeds, ipsilateral re-rupture risk — counsel patients that "the other knee" is also at meaningful risk and should factor into neuromuscular training decisions.
- Arthrofibrosis/cyclops lesion: avoid operating into an acutely inflamed, motion-limited knee; a cyclops lesion (fibroproliferative nodule anterior to the graft) presents as loss of terminal extension and may require arthroscopic debridement.
- Anterior knee pain is more common with BPTB graft; counsel patients preoperatively when this graft is chosen.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis develops regardless of treatment strategy — 44% radiographic OA at 11 years in the KANON cohort irrespective of arm — surgery restores stability but has not been shown to prevent long-term OA.
- Tunnel malposition is the single most correctable cause of both persistent instability and graft impingement/loss of extension; if in doubt intraoperatively, re-check tunnel position against anatomic landmarks before finalizing fixation.
- Infection is rare (<1%) but should prompt early aggressive arthroscopic washout if suspected, given the risk to graft viability.
- Peroneal nerve is at theoretical risk during LET harvest/fixation dissection posterior to the iliotibial band — stay anterior to the biceps femoris and lateral intermuscular septum during dissection.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches (brace locked in extension for ambulation per surgeon protocol), ROM goal 0–90°, quad activation, patellar mobilization, cryotherapy.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progress to full weight-bearing without brace lock, ROM to full flexion, closed-chain strengthening (mini-squats, leg press), stationary bike once ROM allows, gait normalization.
- **Weeks 6–12**: open- and closed-chain progressive resistance training, proprioceptive/balance training, begin light jogging around 10–12 weeks if quadriceps strength and effusion criteria are met.
- **Months 3–6**: sport-specific agility drills, plyometrics introduced with strict quality criteria (avoid dynamic valgus), non-contact/change-of-direction training progression.
- **Months 6–9+**: return-to-sport testing battery — quadriceps and hamstring limb symmetry index (LSI) ≥90%, single-leg/triple/crossover/6-m timed hop tests ≥90% LSI, ACL-RSI psychological readiness score, and movement-quality screening for dynamic valgus. Earliest RTS clearance is 9 months, since return before 9 months carries up to a 7-fold higher second-ACL-injury risk even at 90% LSI. Passing the RTS battery reduces graft-rupture risk but has not been shown to reliably predict contralateral or overall subsequent knee injury — continue neuromuscular training after clearance, not just a one-time pass/fail test.
- **Return to work**: desk work 1–2 weeks with crutches as needed; light manual 4–8 weeks; heavy manual/labor 4–6 months.
