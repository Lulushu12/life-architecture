## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Thoracolumbar burst fracture with TLICS ≥5, or TLICS 4 with surgeon judgment favoring surgery.
- Flexion-distraction (Chance) injury with a disrupted posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) confirmed on MRI or clear translational/distraction morphology on CT.
- Neurologic deficit with structural canal compromise at any TLICS score, warranting decompression and stabilization.
- Progressive kyphotic deformity or loss of reduction on interval imaging in an initially nonoperatively-managed fracture.
- Severe (AO A4/C) fractures with major anterior column loss where structural reconstruction is anticipated in addition to posterior stabilization.

**Contraindications / relative cautions**
- **Purely bony Chance fracture with intact PLC on MRI in a reliable patient** — can be managed nonoperatively in a hyperextension TLSO; surgery is not mandatory, though the threshold to proceed to fixation is low if compliance or follow-up is in doubt, since progressive kyphosis is the failure mode of missed instability.
- **TLICS ≤3 injuries without neurologic deficit or PLC disruption** — treated with bracing; surgery is not indicated.
- **Active systemic infection or uncorrected coagulopathy** precluding safe instrumentation.
- **Severely osteoporotic bone where standard short-segment fixation is anticipated to fail** — favor long-segment fixation or cement augmentation rather than a standard short-segment construct.
- **Unrecognized associated intra-abdominal injury** (in Chance-pattern seatbelt injuries) should be excluded/addressed before or in coordination with spine fixation, since a missed hollow-viscus injury is a life-threatening omission that takes priority.

## Preoperative Planning

- **CT (thin-cut, sagittal/coronal reconstructions)** to measure sagittal (kyphotic) angle, percentage vertebral body height loss, and canal compromise (percentage of canal diameter occupied by retropulsed fragment) — these feed directly into the decompression and construct-length decision.
- **MRI** to assess PLC integrity (critical for Chance-pattern injuries and for TLICS scoring when morphology alone is borderline), cord/conus signal, and epidural hematoma.
- **Abdominal/pelvic CT with IV contrast** in any restrained occupant with a seatbelt sign, given the well-described association between Chance-mechanism injuries and hollow-viscus injury (roughly 15–20% in some series) — coordinate timing with general/trauma surgery as needed.
- **Construct planning**: decide short- versus long-segment fixation based on comminution severity, bone quality, and whether an index-level screw is feasible; template screw length/diameter at each planned level on preoperative CT, particularly pedicle diameter at the fractured level itself if an index-level screw is planned.
- **Decompression planning**: determine whether ligamentotaxis (indirect reduction via posterior distraction) is likely to be adequate based on fracture age (effectiveness drops sharply after roughly 72 hours from injury) and fragment characteristics (a rotated or free fragment not attached to the PLL is less likely to reduce indirectly), or whether direct decompression (laminectomy, transpedicular corpectomy, or combined anterior corpectomy/strut graft or cage) should be planned from the outset.
- **Implant selection**: standard pedicle screw-rod system for posterior fixation; anterior corpectomy cage/strut graft system on standby (or planned as a staged/combined procedure) for severe (AO A4/C) anterior column loss; consider cement-augmented or expandable pedicle screws for osteoporotic bone.
- **Equipment**: intraoperative fluoroscopy (biplanar or single C-arm), high-speed burr and Kerrison rongeurs if direct decompression is anticipated, and a distraction/compression-capable rod-reduction instrumentation set (compression capability is specifically needed for Chance-pattern tension-band injuries).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia** with standard spine-trauma monitoring; consider neuromonitoring (motor/somatosensory evoked potentials) when a neurologic deficit is present or decompression near the cord/conus is planned, per surgeon and institutional practice.
- **Prone positioning** on a radiolucent spine frame (e.g., Jackson table or bolsters) that allows the abdomen to hang free, reducing epidural venous pressure/bleeding and facilitating ventilation; the frame should also permit gentle extension across the fracture level to aid reduction of a kyphotic deformity.
- All pressure points padded (chest, iliac crests, knees, feet); eyes protected and periodically checked given prone positioning risk; arms positioned to avoid brachial plexus stretch (typically flexed at the shoulder <90° and elbow padded).
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm to allow true AP and lateral views of the instrumented levels without repositioning the frame — confirm this before final positioning is locked and before prepping.
- **Prep and drape** a posterior midline field extending well beyond the planned instrumented levels in both directions, in case intraoperative findings (e.g., unexpectedly poor bone quality, additional level of instability) require extending the construct.
- If a combined anterior-posterior procedure is planned, position and prep for the posterior stage first in most protocols (posterior stabilization followed by anterior reconstruction, or vice versa per surgeon preference and injury pattern) with appropriate repositioning/redraping between stages.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm construct length before incision**: for burst fractures, posterior short-segment pedicle screw fixation (one level above/below the fracture) is the workhorse for most burst fractures without severe comminution, often including an index-level screw at the fractured level itself to improve construct stiffness and reduce hardware failure/kyphosis recurrence versus classic short-segment constructs without it. Posterior long-segment fixation (2 levels above/below) is reserved for severe comminution, osteoporotic bone, or when index-level screw placement is not feasible.
2. **Make a posterior midline incision** centered over the injured level(s), and expose subperiosteally along the spinous processes and laminae bilaterally out to the transverse processes at each instrumented level, maintaining meticulous hemostasis in the often hyperemic, injured paraspinal tissue.
3. **Identify pedicle entry points** at each planned level using standard anatomic landmarks (transverse process/facet junction, per level-specific technique) confirmed with AP and lateral fluoroscopy or, where available, navigation.
4. **Place pedicle screws at the planned levels**, including the index-level screw at the fractured vertebra if the pedicle is intact enough to support it — probe/tap the pedicle tract carefully in the fractured vertebra, since pedicle wall integrity here is less predictable than in an uninjured vertebra, and confirm trajectory on fluoroscopy before final seating.
5. **Decide on decompression approach and timing**: if ligamentotaxis is planned, this occurs as part of the reduction maneuver (step 6) rather than as a separate direct step; if direct decompression is planned (significant canal compromise with neurologic deficit, or a fracture judged unlikely to respond to ligamentotaxis, e.g., >72 hours old or a free rotated fragment), proceed to laminectomy and/or transpedicular decompression at this stage, removing retropulsed fragments under direct vision while protecting the thecal sac/cord.
6. **Achieve reduction**: for burst fractures, apply controlled distraction across the pedicle screw-rod construct to restore height and achieve ligamentotaxis-mediated indirect reduction of retropulsed fragments via tensioning of the intact posterior longitudinal ligament; reassess canal clearance on lateral fluoroscopy (and intraoperative ultrasound or direct visualization after laminectomy, if performed) before final rod locking. For Chance/flexion-distraction injuries, apply controlled compression across the injured segment instead — the tension-band failure pattern of these injuries makes compression instrumentation biomechanically appropriate, closing down the posterior distraction while restoring sagittal alignment.
7. **If canal compromise persists after ligamentotaxis/reduction and a neurologic deficit is present**, proceed to direct decompression (laminectomy ± transpedicular corpectomy) at this stage rather than accepting residual compression.
8. **For severe (AO A4/C) fractures with major anterior column loss**, plan combined anterior-posterior reconstruction: after posterior stabilization, proceed (same setting or staged) to anterior corpectomy of the comminuted body, with placement of a structural strut graft or expandable cage restoring anterior column height and load-sharing, particularly prioritized in younger patients where restoring sagittal alignment for long-term outcomes matters most.
9. **For minimally invasive percutaneous pedicle screw fixation** (an option for TLICS 4–5 injuries without need for direct decompression): place screws through percutaneous fascial incisions under fluoroscopic or navigated guidance, achieving indirect reduction/ligamentotaxis through the percutaneously-placed rod construct without formal midline exposure — this reduces blood loss and muscle injury versus open approaches with comparable fusion/alignment outcomes in appropriately selected patients.
10. **Contour and lock final rods**, confirming maintained reduction and appropriate compression (Chance) or distraction/neutral loading (burst) across the construct.
11. **Decorticate the posterior elements at the instrumented (non-fractured) levels and pack autograft/allograft** to promote fusion, avoiding grafting directly over an open laminectomy defect if one has been created.
12. **Obtain final AP and lateral fluoroscopy** confirming alignment, screw position, and adequacy of reduction/decompression.
13. **Irrigate, achieve hemostasis, and close in layers** over a subfascial drain (commonly used given the extensive posterior dissection and epidural venous bleeding risk), with meticulous fascial closure given the biomechanical loads across a thoracolumbar construct.
14. **Screen (or confirm screening already completed) for concomitant intra-abdominal injury** in any Chance fracture from a seatbelt mechanism if not already addressed preoperatively — document the seatbelt sign and coordinate with general/trauma surgery as needed.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Add the index-level screw whenever pedicle integrity at the fractured vertebra allows it — this is one of the most consistent, technique-modifiable ways to reduce postoperative kyphosis recurrence and hardware failure in short-segment constructs.
- Match the reduction maneuver to the injury biomechanics: distraction/ligamentotaxis for burst fractures (restoring height, indirectly reducing retropulsed fragments via PLL tensioning), compression for Chance/flexion-distraction injuries (closing the posterior tension-band failure) — using the wrong maneuver for the injury pattern will not achieve the intended reduction and can worsen alignment.
- Reassess canal clearance on fluoroscopy (or direct vision if a laminectomy has been performed) after reduction, before final rod locking — do not assume ligamentotaxis has adequately cleared the canal without confirming it, particularly in fractures approaching or beyond the 72-hour window where ligamentotaxis effectiveness is known to decline.
- In fractures older than roughly 72 hours or with a rotated/free fragment not attached to the PLL, plan for direct rather than indirect decompression from the outset rather than relying on ligamentotaxis to fail intraoperatively before switching strategies.
- Favor long-segment fixation (or cement-augmented/expandable screws) over standard short-segment constructs in osteoporotic or severely comminuted bone — a construct that appears adequate on intraoperative fluoroscopy in poor-quality bone is a common setup for delayed hardware failure.
- Position the patient prone with the abdomen hanging free — this measurably reduces epidural venous bleeding and improves visualization, particularly important during laminectomy/direct decompression stages.
- Consider minimally invasive percutaneous fixation for TLICS 4–5 injuries not requiring direct decompression — comparable alignment/fusion outcomes with less blood loss and muscle injury in appropriately selected patients.
- Use a subfascial drain liberally given the extent of posterior dissection and epidural venous bleeding risk in thoracolumbar trauma cases — a postoperative epidural hematoma is a preventable cause of neurologic deterioration.
- Do not let spine fixation planning delay identification and management of an associated hollow-viscus injury in Chance-pattern seatbelt trauma — this workup runs in parallel with, not after, spine surgical planning.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Omitting the index-level screw in comminuted (A4) or osteoporotic burst fractures increases the risk of post-traumatic kyphosis and hardware failure** — add the index-level screw or convert to a long-segment construct in these cases.
- **Ligamentotaxis loses effectiveness after roughly 72 hours from injury** — beyond this window, plan for direct rather than indirect decompression if canal compromise persists.
- **Purely bony Chance fractures can be braced, but reliability of follow-up is the deciding factor** — compression fixation should be favored over bracing whenever compliance or follow-up is in doubt, since progressive kyphosis is the failure mode.
- **Do not overlook the seatbelt sign** — up to 15–20% of seatbelt-sign-positive patients have an associated hollow-viscus injury, and this workup should not be deferred while focused on the spine fracture itself.
- **Using a distraction maneuver on a Chance/flexion-distraction injury (or compression on a pure burst fracture) mismatches the reduction technique to the injury biomechanics** and can worsen alignment rather than correct it.
- **Dural tear/CSF leak** during laminectomy or transpedicular decompression, particularly in the setting of pre-existing dural injury from bone fragments — recognized by CSF in the field intraoperatively; repaired primarily or with a patch/sealant as needed, with postoperative flat bed rest per surgeon protocol if a repair is tenuous.
- **Epidural hematoma** from inadequate hemostasis of the epidural venous plexus, particularly with extensive posterior dissection — a subfascial drain and meticulous intraoperative hemostasis are the primary mitigations; postoperative neurologic deterioration should prompt urgent imaging and, if a compressive hematoma is confirmed, emergent evacuation.
- **Pedicle screw malposition/breach** at the index (fractured) level, where pedicle wall integrity is less predictable than in uninjured bone — careful probing/tapping and fluoroscopic confirmation before final screw seating reduce this risk.
- **Postoperative pseudarthrosis/hardware failure** if construct length or index-level screw use does not match bone quality and comminution severity.

## Postoperative Protocol

Wound care and pain control acutely (0–2 weeks), with mobilization typically beginning per surgeon protocol within 24–72 hours (bed rest is avoided given pneumonia, DVT, and deconditioning risk). In the subacute phase (2–12 weeks), patients mobilize with brace support per protocol, with interval imaging at 6–12 weeks to confirm fixation stability. Progressive resistance exercise and dynamic core stabilization follow once fusion is trending (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable fixation; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months, longer with extensive fusion levels or persistent neurologic deficit. Return-to-sport/high-demand clearance requires solid radiographic fusion (typically ≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, normalized strength, and clearance imaging showing no hardware failure or progressive deformity.
