## Indications

Applies to thoracolumbar burst fractures with TLICS ≥5 (or ≥4 with surgeon judgment favoring surgery), and to flexion-distraction (Chance) injuries with a disrupted posterior ligamentous complex (PLC) on MRI. Purely bony Chance fractures with an intact PLC in a reliable patient can instead be braced nonoperatively, but the threshold for surgery is low given the risk of progressive kyphosis with compliance/follow-up failure.

## Positioning & setup

Posterior midline exposure for pedicle screw instrumentation; anterior corpectomy/strut graft or cage access is added for combined reconstructions in severe anterior column loss.

## Steps

1. **For burst fractures — select construct length**: posterior short-segment pedicle screw fixation (one level above/below) is the workhorse for most burst fractures without severe comminution, often including an index-level screw at the fractured level itself to improve construct stiffness and reduce hardware failure/kyphosis recurrence versus classic short-segment constructs without it. Posterior long-segment fixation (2 levels above/below) is reserved for severe comminution, osteoporotic bone, or when index-level screw placement is not feasible.
2. **Decide on decompression**: indirect decompression via ligamentotaxis (distraction/reduction through posterior instrumentation) is often sufficient for fractures without complete canal-blocking retropulsion; ligamentotaxis effectiveness drops sharply after roughly 72 hours from injury, so timing matters. Direct decompression (laminectomy, transpedicular corpectomy, or combined anterior corpectomy/strut graft or cage) is added when significant canal compromise persists with a neurologic deficit, or when ligamentotaxis is judged unlikely to adequately clear the canal.
3. **Add combined anterior-posterior reconstruction** for severe (AO A4/C) fractures with major anterior column loss requiring structural reconstruction (corpectomy + cage/strut graft) in addition to posterior stabilization, particularly in younger patients where restoring anterior load-sharing and sagittal alignment is prioritized.
4. **Consider minimally invasive percutaneous pedicle screw fixation** for TLICS 4–5 injuries without need for direct decompression — reduces blood loss/muscle injury versus open approaches with comparable fusion/alignment outcomes in appropriately selected patients.
5. **For Chance/flexion-distraction injuries with PLC disruption**: use posterior segmental compression fixation (pedicle screw-rod construct placed in compression across the injured segment) — a fusion-preserving alternative in patients without body-fracture comminution; the tension-band failure pattern makes compression instrumentation biomechanically ideal.
6. **Screen for concomitant intra-abdominal injury** in any Chance fracture from a seatbelt mechanism — document the seatbelt sign (abdominal wall ecchymosis) and obtain abdominal/pelvic CT with IV contrast given the well-described association with hollow-viscus injury.

## Pitfalls

- Omitting the index-level screw in comminuted (A4) or osteoporotic burst fractures increases the risk of post-traumatic kyphosis and hardware failure — add the index-level screw or convert to a long-segment construct in these cases.
- Ligamentotaxis loses effectiveness after roughly 72 hours from injury — beyond this window, plan for direct rather than indirect decompression if canal compromise persists.
- Purely bony Chance fractures can be braced, but reliability of follow-up is the deciding factor — compression fixation should be favored over bracing whenever compliance or follow-up is in doubt, since progressive kyphosis is the failure mode.
- Do not overlook the seatbelt sign — up to 15–20% of seatbelt-sign-positive patients have an associated hollow-viscus injury, and this workup should not be deferred while focused on the spine fracture itself.

## Post-op protocol

Wound care and pain control acutely (0–2 weeks), with mobilization typically beginning per surgeon protocol within 24–72 hours (bed rest is avoided given pneumonia, DVT, and deconditioning risk). In the subacute phase (2–12 weeks), patients mobilize with brace support per protocol, with interval imaging at 6–12 weeks to confirm fixation stability. Progressive resistance exercise and dynamic core stabilization follow once fusion is trending (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable fixation; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months, longer with extensive fusion levels or persistent neurologic deficit. Return-to-sport/high-demand clearance requires solid radiographic fusion (typically ≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, normalized strength, and clearance imaging showing no hardware failure or progressive deformity.
