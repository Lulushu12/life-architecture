## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

Knee OA (mild-moderate, K-L 2–3) as a second-line option, particularly LP-PRP, in patients wanting to defer surgery. Lateral epicondylitis and patellar tendinopathy (LR-PRP, moderate-strong evidence, superior to steroid at 6–24 months). Rotator cuff tendinopathy (non-tear) and, more equivocally, rotator cuff repair augmentation. Plantar fasciitis as a steroid-sparing alternative (slower onset, more durable). Hip OA (limited dedicated evidence, extrapolated from knee data, image guidance mandatory). Achilles tendinopathy as an adjunct to eccentric loading (weak-moderate evidence).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Not indicated as a substitute for arthroplasty in severe (K-L 4) knee OA — evidence is weak/negative and expectations should be set accordingly.
- Active local or systemic infection at the intended injection site.
- Uncorrected severe thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction disorder — the autologous product depends on adequate baseline platelet number/function; screen if there is a known hematologic condition.
- Active malignancy (relative) — theoretical concern about growth-factor stimulation of a local or systemic malignant process; individualize with the treating oncology team.
- Anticoagulation is not an absolute contraindication (autologous product, low bleeding risk from the injection itself) but should be reviewed per usual injection-safety practice for the specific target (e.g., deeper hip injections).
- Undiagnosed or unclear source of pain — do not commit a patient to a time- and cost-intensive PRP series before imaging (US/MRI) has reasonably confirmed the target diagnosis; a classic pitfall is treating presumed lateral epicondylitis that is actually PIN entrapment or radial tunnel syndrome.
- Recent (within days) NSAID use is a relative timing consideration rather than a contraindication — NSAIDs can theoretically blunt platelet function and the intended inflammatory cascade; ask about recent use and counsel on avoidance around the injection window.
- Inflamed/effusive joint as a target for LR-PRP specifically — use LP-PRP instead in this setting (see Pitfalls).
- Unrealistic patient expectations of disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating benefit — recalibrate before proceeding.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the diagnosis with imaging (US/MRI) before committing to a PRP series — PRP is time- and cost-intensive and should not substitute for a missed alternate diagnosis.
- Decide and document the preparation type (LR-PRP vs LP-PRP) and target platelet-concentration multiple appropriate to the indication before the draw — LR-PRP for tendon targets, LP-PRP for intra-articular use, with a generally assumed therapeutic threshold of ≥3–5× baseline platelet count (supraphysiologic concentrations >8× may be inhibitory in vitro, so more concentrated is not automatically better).
- Select and confirm availability of the specific PRP preparation system/kit in advance (single-spin vs double-spin systems concentrate platelets to different degrees and separate leukocytes differently) — do not switch systems between visits in a planned series without noting the change.
- Plan the injection series length in advance where applicable: a single injection is standard for most tendon targets; 2–3 weekly injections is a common (though not consensus-mandated) protocol for intra-articular knee use.
- For tendon targets, plan for ultrasound availability — accurate targeting of the pathologic tendon segment (and avoidance of the adjacent joint) is materially easier with real-time imaging than by palpation alone.
- Counsel the patient in advance about the expected post-injection flare, the NSAID-avoidance window, and — for shoulder PRP specifically — the reported association with post-injection adhesive capsulitis, so this is part of informed consent rather than a surprise finding later.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- No general or regional anesthesia is used; the venous blood draw is performed with standard phlebotomy technique, and the injection itself is done under local skin anesthesia only (a local anesthetic wheal is generally avoided immediately around the injection site itself for intra-articular series since some protocols prefer to avoid diluting or altering the biologic product with local anesthetic, though ethyl chloride spray or anesthetizing the overlying skin only is commonly used).
- Blood draw: patient seated or supine, arm extended, standard antecubital venipuncture technique, drawing into the specific anticoagulated collection tubes supplied by the chosen PRP kit (volume typically 15–60 mL depending on system).
- Processing: performed chairside/tableside in a point-of-care centrifuge; patient can remain in the treatment room or step out briefly depending on workflow.
- Injection positioning is target-specific:
  - **Knee (IA):** supine, knee extended, superolateral or anterolateral/anteromedial parapatellar approach — the superolateral approach with the knee extended has the best-supported blind accuracy among landmark techniques, and ultrasound guidance further improves accuracy for any approach when available.
  - **Lateral epicondyle:** seated, elbow flexed and forearm pronated on a supporting surface, point of maximal tenderness palpated and marked at or just distal to the lateral epicondyle; ultrasound probe positioned to visualize the common extensor origin/ECRB in long axis.
  - **Patellar tendon:** supine or seated with knee flexed over the table edge, tendon visualized in long axis with ultrasound.
  - **Shoulder (subacromial/rotator cuff):** seated, arm relaxed at side, ultrasound-guided approach to the target tendon/bursa.
  - **Plantar fascia:** prone with the foot hanging off the table end, or supine with hip/knee flexed and externally rotated to expose the plantar heel, medial approach under ultrasound to the fascia origin at the calcaneal tuberosity.
- Standard sterile skin prep at each injection site; no tourniquet used.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Draw whole blood (typically 15–60 mL depending on system).
2. Centrifuge (single or double spin) and harvest the buffy coat to prepare the concentrate.
3. Classify and document the preparation as leukocyte-rich (LR-PRP) or leukocyte-poor (LP-PRP) with its platelet-concentration multiple on the order/consent — this determines the appropriate indication (LR-PRP for tendon targets, LP-PRP for intra-articular use).
4. Activate — endogenous activation via collagen/thrombin at the injection site is standard; exogenous activation is less common.
5. Confirm the target under ultrasound for tendon injections: identify the hypoechoic/heterogeneous tendinopathic segment (e.g., the ECRB origin at the lateral epicondyle) before needle introduction.
6. Inject under US guidance for tendon targets: use a peritendinous, needle fenestration ("dry needling") technique — pass the needle through the pathologic tendon tissue multiple times in different trajectories (fanning pattern) to mechanically disrupt degenerative tissue, depositing small aliquots of PRP with each pass, especially for epicondylitis/patellar tendinopathy.
7. Avoid injecting deep to the tendon into the adjacent joint (e.g., radiocapitellar joint at the lateral epicondyle) unless joint pathology is also being addressed as a separate, deliberate target.
8. For intra-articular knee use, confirm needle position (free flow, low resistance, aspirate a small amount of synovial fluid if present) before depositing the full volume; deliver as a single injection or as a 2–3 weekly series depending on protocol — there is no consensus on optimal series length.
9. Withdraw the needle, apply a dressing, and observe the patient briefly before discharge.
10. Document preparation type, platelet multiple (if measured by the kit), target(s), technique (fenestration vs simple deposition), and volume delivered.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm the pathologic tendon segment on ultrasound before the first needle pass — treating the point of maximal tenderness alone, without imaging correlation, risks missing the true focus of tendinosis.
- Use a fanning, multi-pass fenestration technique through the pathologic tendon rather than a single deposition — the mechanical disruption of degenerative tissue is considered part of the therapeutic mechanism, not just a delivery method.
- Keep the needle tip visible in-plane throughout the fenestration passes; losing the needle tip off the ultrasound image while fanning through tendon risks straying into an adjacent joint or neurovascular structure.
- For intra-articular knee series, use the same approach (superolateral vs anteromedial) across the series when possible — consistency reduces variability in a repeated-dose protocol.
- Process and inject the same day as the draw — PRP is not banked or stored for later use; plan the visit so processing time does not leave a large gap between draw and injection.
- Warn patients that the expected flare after PRP is often more pronounced than after a corticosteroid injection — set expectations at the time of injection, not only in a post-visit handout.
- For shoulder PRP specifically, discuss the reported association with adhesive capsulitis as part of informed consent, and have a low threshold to start early gentle ROM work if stiffness begins to develop post-injection.
- Do not chase a higher platelet-concentration multiple than the evidence supports for the indication — supraphysiologic concentrations (>8×) may be inhibitory in vitro, and for rotator cuff repair augmentation specifically, platelet concentration has not been shown to influence outcomes or retear rates.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not pool LR- and LP-PRP as a single "PRP" category when counseling or documenting — outcomes and adverse-event rates are preparation-specific; specify LR vs LP and the platelet multiple on every order.
- Avoid LR-PRP intra-articularly in an inflamed/effusive joint — higher pro-inflammatory cytokine load increases flare risk without clear benefit over LP-PRP in that setting.
- For rotator cuff repair augmentation, platelet concentration does not significantly influence clinical outcomes or retear rates (2026 meta-analysis) — augmentation may aid pain/healing biology signals but is not proven to reduce structural retear; counsel on an associated higher rate of post-injection adhesive capsulitis.
- Supraphysiologic platelet concentrations (>8× baseline) may be inhibitory in vitro — more concentrated is not necessarily better; therapeutic effect generally assumes ≥3–5× baseline.
- Do not promise PRP as disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy — current evidence supports symptomatic benefit in appropriately selected patients, not structural disease reversal.
- Post-injection pain/flare is expected, higher with LR-PRP (~20–30% at 48–72h); theoretical infection risk is very low (autologous product); no systemic allergy risk.
- Blind (landmark-only) IA knee injection accuracy, while reasonable with an experienced injector (roughly 85–93% pooled across techniques), is meaningfully lower than ultrasound-guided injection (95–100%) — for a costly, time-intensive series, the accuracy gain from ultrasound guidance is worth the added setup time whenever available.
- Straying deep to a tendon target into the adjacent joint (e.g., radiocapitellar joint during lateral epicondyle fenestration) is a recognized technical error — keep the needle tip visualized and stop fenestration at the deep tendon margin unless intentionally also treating joint pathology.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 1 (Day 0–3):** Relative rest of the treated structure for 24–48 hours. Ice for the expected inflammatory flare, which typically resolves in 48–72 hours — counsel that this flare is part of the intended mechanism, not a complication. Avoid NSAIDs for 3–7 days after PRP if feasible (theoretical blunting of the intended inflammatory/platelet-mediated cascade) — this differs from corticosteroid injection, where NSAID use is not restricted.
- **Phase 2 (Week 1–2):** Resume gentle active ROM and low-load ADLs. For tendinopathy protocols specifically, delay initiation/resumption of the eccentric loading program 5–10 days post-injection to allow the local inflammatory/remodeling phase to establish.
- **Phase 3 (Week 2–6):** Progress strengthening per underlying diagnosis.
- **Return to sport/high-impact activity:** Individualized; most tendinopathy PRP series use a structured criterion-based return (pain-free functional testing, symmetric strength ≥90% of contralateral limb) rather than a fixed calendar date, typically not before 4–6 weeks post-injection. Manual labor/heavy lifting: 1–2 weeks post-injection to allow the expected inflammatory phase to settle before heavy loading.
