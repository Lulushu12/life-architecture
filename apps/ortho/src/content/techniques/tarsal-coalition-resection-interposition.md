## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Symptomatic tarsal coalition (calcaneonavicular or talocalcaneal) that has failed a non-operative trial (activity modification/NSAIDs, short-leg cast or boot immobilization for a flare, then a semi-rigid or UCBL orthotic).
- Resection with interposition is first-line surgery for a symptomatic calcaneonavicular coalition.
- For a talocalcaneal (middle facet) coalition, resection with interposition is appropriate when the coalition involves **less than ~50% of the joint surface area**, with minimal secondary degenerative change or talar beaking on imaging, in an appropriately selected older child/adolescent.
- Subtalar or triple arthrodesis is reserved for extensive coalition, significant degenerative change, failed prior resection, or associated fixed hindfoot deformity — growth-sparing resection is exhausted first given the permanence of fusion.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Coalition involving more than roughly 50% of the talocalcaneal joint surface, or with substantial secondary degenerative change/talar beaking, is a relative contraindication to resection alone — arthrodesis is more likely to be the durable answer in this group.
- A fixed hindfoot valgus or varus deformity accompanying the coalition may need concurrent or staged osteotomy/reconstruction, and resection alone without addressing the fixed deformity is unlikely to relieve symptoms.
- Active infection or skin compromise over the planned approach.
- An asymptomatic, incidentally discovered coalition is not an indication for surgery — many coalitions are found incidentally and do not require intervention; treat symptomatic patients, not radiographic findings alone.
- A patient/family unwilling or unable to comply with the postoperative immobilization and structured ROM protocol is a relative contraindication, since re-adhesion of the resected bridge is directly related to early, guided motion.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** foot radiographs (AP/lateral/oblique) as an initial screen — calcaneonavicular coalition is visible on the 45° oblique view as the classic "anteater sign"; talocalcaneal coalition is suggested indirectly by the "C-sign" on the lateral view and talar beaking, though plain films frequently miss it, particularly when the coalition is still fibrous or cartilaginous. **CT is the gold standard** for definitively characterizing coalition extent, precise anatomy, and surgical planning — essential for talocalcaneal (middle facet) coalitions to estimate the percentage of joint surface involved, which directly drives the resection-versus-arthrodesis decision. MRI adds value specifically for coalitions still fibrous/cartilaginous (not yet visible on CT/plain films) and for assessing secondary cartilage/joint changes not apparent on CT alone; ultrasound has also been explored as a lower-cost, radiation-free first-line screening option in some centers, particularly in the incompletely ossified foot, though CT remains standard for definitive surgical planning.
- **Surface area estimation:** on preoperative CT, estimate the coalition's percentage of the total middle facet/subtalar joint surface — this single measurement is the primary determinant of resection candidacy for talocalcaneal coalitions.
- **Interposition material selection:** decide preoperatively (while remaining willing to adapt intraoperatively) between fat graft (autologous, typically harvested locally from the surgical field or a separate small incision), extensor digitorum brevis (EDB) muscle belly (harvested locally, well-suited to the calcaneonavicular location where it lies directly in the operative field), or bone wax/cement/other synthetic spacer, and — where available — juvenile hyaline cartilage allograft, which has been described specifically for middle-facet talocalcaneal coalition interposition. Fat grafting is the most widely used option for talocalcaneal middle-facet coalitions.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list:** standard bone/soft-tissue set with osteotomes, curettes, and a high-speed burr for coalition resection; a small saw may assist marking the resection margins; fluoroscopy for intraoperative confirmation of complete resection and hindfoot alignment; and, for a talocalcaneal coalition, consider preoperative planning support (e.g., 3D-printed models or mixed-reality/navigation planning, increasingly described in the literature) if the coalition's anatomy is complex or the surgeon's experience with the middle facet approach is limited.
- **Approach planning:** for calcaneonavicular coalition, plan a dorsolateral (Ollier-type) incision centered over the sinus tarsi region. For talocalcaneal (middle facet) coalition, plan a **posteromedial approach** — a longitudinal incision medial to the middle facet, roughly 1.5–2 cm distal to the medial malleolus, or a horizontal incision along the medial hindfoot centered over the sustentaculum tali, from the anterior margin of the Achilles tendon to the navicular tuberosity — chosen for direct, unobstructed access to the middle facet without disturbing the posterior facet.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia, commonly combined with a popliteal/ankle block or local infiltration for postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning:** supine for a calcaneonavicular (dorsolateral) approach, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to internally rotate the limb slightly and improve lateral foot access; for a talocalcaneal (posteromedial) approach, the leg is typically externally rotated (a bump under the contralateral hip, or the hip simply allowed to externally rotate) to expose the medial hindfoot, sometimes with the knee flexed and the foot resting in a figure-4-type position for access.
- **Tourniquet:** a thigh or calf tourniquet is standard, inflated after limb exsanguination, to provide a bloodless field for precise identification of the coalition margins and adjacent neurovascular structures.
- **Fluoroscopy:** available in the room to confirm the extent and completeness of bony resection intraoperatively, and to assess hindfoot alignment at the end of the case.
- **Draping:** prep and drape the entire foot and distal leg free to allow full assessment of hindfoot position and subtalar motion intraoperatively.
- **Landmarks marked before incision:** the sinus tarsi and anterior process of the calcaneus for a calcaneonavicular approach; the medial malleolus, sustentaculum tali, posterior margin of the Achilles tendon, and navicular tuberosity for a talocalcaneal (posteromedial) approach.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### A. Calcaneonavicular coalition resection with EDB interposition

1. Make a dorsolateral (Ollier-type) incision centered over the sinus tarsi, extending from just distal to the lateral malleolus toward the base of the fourth metatarsal.
2. Identify and elevate the extensor digitorum brevis (EDB) muscle belly off the underlying coalition, preserving its proximal attachment/vascularity so it can be used as a vascularized interposition flap later in the case — this is a key reason the calcaneonavicular approach lends itself so naturally to EDB interposition.
3. Identify the coalition between the anterior process of the calcaneus and the navicular, confirmed by direct palpation of the abnormal bony/fibrous bridge and correlated with the preoperative CT.
4. Resect the coalition using osteotomes and a high-speed burr, removing a generous block of bone/fibrous tissue to create a gap of adequate width (generally at least 1 cm) — under-resection is a common technical error that predisposes to bridge reformation.
5. Confirm complete resection by direct visualization of a mobile, unobstructed gap between the calcaneus and navicular, and by passively testing subtalar/midtarsal motion, which should be visibly improved compared to the pre-resection exam.
6. Round and smooth the resected bony margins to minimize the chance of re-adhesion and to reduce the risk of impingement.
7. Transpose the preserved EDB muscle belly into the resection gap, securing it (typically with a suture anchor or transosseous suture) to hold it in position within the defect.
8. Confirm hindfoot alignment and motion on fluoroscopy before closure; close in layers.

### B. Talocalcaneal (middle facet) coalition resection with fat graft interposition

1. Make the posteromedial incision as planned — longitudinal, medial to the middle facet, roughly 1.5–2 cm distal to the medial malleolus, or a horizontal incision centered over the sustentaculum tali.
2. Develop the interval carefully, retracting the flexor digitorum longus (FDL) either dorsally or plantarward depending on the coalition's exact location and the ease of exposure it affords, and retracting the flexor hallucis longus (FHL) plantarward, away from the sustentaculum tali — correct identification and protection of both tendons, and of the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle running with them in this interval, is essential before proceeding to bony resection.
3. Expose the sustentaculum tali and the coalition at the middle facet, confirming its extent against the preoperative CT.
4. Resect the coalition with osteotomes/curettes and a high-speed burr, working from the exposed margin toward the posterior facet, taking care to **stay within the confirmed extent of the coalition and avoid violating the adjacent normal posterior facet articular cartilage** — inadvertent injury to the posterior facet is a recognized technical pitfall of this resection.
5. Use intraoperative fluoroscopy to confirm the resection has adequately decompressed the middle facet and that no residual bony bridge remains.
6. Confirm restoration of subtalar motion by passive inversion/eversion testing before proceeding to interposition.
7. Harvest a local or regional fat graft (or, alternatively, prepare a juvenile hyaline cartilage allograft if that interposition material was selected) and pack it into the resection defect, securing it as needed (suture, fibrin glue) to prevent early displacement.
8. Close in layers, protecting the FDL/FHL and neurovascular bundle during closure.

### C. General principles applicable to both coalition types

1. Confirm resection extent and completeness on fluoroscopy before committing to interposition — a coalition that has not been fully resected will predictably re-adhere regardless of the interposition material chosen.
2. Test subtalar/midtarsal range of motion directly on the table after resection and again after interposition placement to confirm the interposition material has not itself blocked the newly restored motion.
3. Achieve meticulous hemostasis before closure, particularly around the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle in the talocalcaneal approach.
4. Apply a well-padded splint or cast in a neutral, corrected hindfoot position at the end of the case, anticipating the early postoperative immobilization period.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- For calcaneonavicular coalitions, preserve the EDB muscle belly's proximal blood supply from the outset of dissection — a vascularized EDB interposition graft integrates more reliably than a free graft and is a natural fit given the muscle's anatomic position directly over this coalition.
- For talocalcaneal coalitions, favor the posteromedial approach for its direct, unobstructed access to the middle facet — it avoids working through or disturbing the posterior facet and keeps working instruments confined to the coalition site itself, which reduces the risk of both tendon injury and neurovascular injury compared to approaches that require more extensive retraction.
- Resect generously rather than conservatively — a resection gap that is too narrow is a common, avoidable cause of bridge reformation; aim for a clearly visible, mobile gap of at least about 1 cm once the coalition is removed.
- Use intraoperative fluoroscopy liberally to confirm resection completeness before placing the interposition graft — this is far easier to correct at the time of the index procedure than to discover on postoperative imaging after symptoms recur.
- When resecting a talocalcaneal coalition, work from the exposed (usually anterior/medial) margin toward the posterior facet rather than starting centrally — this orientation makes it easier to recognize when you are approaching the normal posterior facet articular cartilage and to stop the resection at the correct plane.
- Test subtalar motion passively both before and after interposition placement — an interposition graft that is packed too tightly can itself restrict the very motion the resection was meant to restore.
- Time the surgery with an eye to symptom emergence patterns — calcaneonavicular coalitions typically become symptomatic around age 8–12 as they ossify, and talocalcaneal around age 12–16; understanding this natural history helps counsel families on why a coalition seen incidentally at a younger age may not yet be the source of current symptoms.
- Counsel patients/families that early, guided postoperative motion (not prolonged immobilization) is what protects against re-adhesion of the interposition graft — set this expectation before surgery so the family understands the rationale for the structured weight-bearing and ROM progression that follows.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Recurrence of the coalition/bar after resection** is a recognized failure mode — most reliably prevented by a generous, fluoroscopically confirmed resection and appropriate interposition, rather than relying on interposition material alone to prevent reformation of an incompletely resected bridge.
- **Persistent pain despite adequate resection** should prompt consideration of an undiagnosed additional coalition (multiple coalitions in the same or contralateral foot are not rare) or underlying degenerative change, rather than assuming inadequate surgery — reimaging (CT) is reasonable in this scenario.
- **Interposition graft failure** can undermine an otherwise technically adequate resection — this includes graft resorption, displacement, or (for EDB) devascularization if the muscle's proximal pedicle was compromised during harvest.
- **Injury to the FDL, FHL, or posterior tibial neurovascular bundle** is a specific risk of the talocalcaneal (posteromedial) approach given their close proximity to the sustentaculum tali and middle facet — careful identification and retraction, rather than blind dissection, is the key preventive step.
- **Inadvertent violation of the posterior facet articular cartilage** during talocalcaneal middle-facet resection is a recognized technical pitfall that can introduce new joint pathology — stay within the confirmed coalition margins on preoperative CT and stop resection at the first sign of normal articular cartilage.
- **Under-resection** (a gap too narrow, or bony margins left rough/unrounded) predisposes to re-adhesion — resect generously and smooth the margins.
- Symptoms characteristically emerge as the coalition ossifies (calcaneonavicular ~age 8–12; talocalcaneal ~age 12–16) — timing of presentation should inform suspicion even when initial radiographs are equivocal.
- Reported outcomes for talocalcaneal coalition resection show roughly 80% good-to-excellent results in the literature, useful as a benchmark figure for surgical counseling, with poorer results generally associated with more extensive coalitions, greater degenerative change, or incomplete resection.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Non-weight-bearing cast/boot for 2–3 weeks.
- Progressive weight-bearing in a removable boot with early subtalar ROM exercises to prevent re-adhesion of the interposition graft.
- Formal physiotherapy for peroneal strengthening and gait retraining, weeks 6–12.
- Return to sport typically 3–4 months.
