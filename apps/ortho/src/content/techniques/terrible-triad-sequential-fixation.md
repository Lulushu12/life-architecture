## Indications

Terrible triad = elbow dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture. This sequential fixation protocol addresses the full "circle of Horii" injury pattern (lateral-to-medial soft-tissue disruption: LCL first, then anterior capsule/coronoid, with the radial head fracturing from impaction against the capitellum) and is indicated for any terrible triad requiring operative stabilization.

## Positioning & setup

Examination under anesthesia (EUA) with fluoroscopic stress testing is the definitive stability assessment and should be planned as part of the operative workup, not relied upon in the awake ED patient where guarding limits accuracy. Have both lateral (Kocher or EDC-splitting) and medial approach setups available, plus fluoroscopy for intraoperative stability testing.

## Steps

1. **Examine under anesthesia and reduce the elbow first**; confirm concentric reduction on fluoroscopy/true lateral.
2. **Fix the coronoid** — address before the radial head when accessible. For anterior-approach exposures or fragments visible through the radial head defect, use a suture lasso through drill holes (capturing the anterior capsule) for small/comminuted tip fragments, or screws for larger O'Driscoll type 1 subtype-2/type 3 fragments. Anteromedial facet fractures (O'Driscoll type 2) need a separate medial buttress approach.
3. **Fix or replace the radial head** — ORIF if reconstructable (≤3 fragments), RHA if comminuted; the radial head must be addressed (essentially never excised alone) because it is a key lateral/valgus and longitudinal stabilizer in this unstable pattern.
4. **Repair the LCL/LUCL** — through the lateral (Kocher or EDC-splitting) approach, using a suture anchor or transosseous tunnel repair back to the isometric point on the lateral epicondyle (near the center of the capitellum's arc of rotation). This step is essential in virtually all terrible triads since the LUCL is the injury's primary soft-tissue lesion.
5. **Intraoperative stability test** — take the elbow through a full flexion-extension arc under fluoroscopy/direct vision after the lateral-sided repair; if concentric and stable through **30°–130°**, close.
6. **If still unstable** (subluxates or requires >30–45° flexion to stay reduced): repair the MCL through a medial approach, apply a **hinged external fixator** to maintain concentric reduction while allowing protected early motion, or both.
7. **Post-fixation:** hinged brace, begin protected active motion within the first 1–2 weeks — motion, not prolonged immobilization, is what prevents the stiffness that historically gave this injury its name.

An alternative sequencing — **coronoid-first repair through an anterior approach** — has shown good mid-term results (minimum 2-year follow-up) and is useful when coronoid fragment morphology favors direct anterior exposure.

## Pitfalls

- Skipping the intraoperative stability arc test (30°–130° flexion) risks closing an elbow that is not truly concentrically stable.
- Under-recognized or inadequately repaired LCL/coronoid injury leads to chronic instability, recurrent subluxation, or frank redislocation — often requiring revision ligament reconstruction ± coronoid reconstruction later.
- Anteromedial facet (O'Driscoll type 2) coronoid fractures are NOT adequately addressed by LCL repair alone — they require a separate medial buttress approach.
- Heterotopic ossification is the most common complication after high-energy elbow fracture-dislocations, especially with delayed fixation, concomitant head injury, or burns — consider HO prophylaxis (indomethacin/NSAID 2–3 weeks, or single-fraction 700 cGy radiotherapy in very high-risk cases).
- A technically excellent fixation followed by prolonged casting "to be safe" is a common, preventable cause of poor outcome — early motion is part of the fixation strategy, not an afterthought.
- Prospective outcome data confirm that this systematic, sequential fixation (coronoid → radial head → LCL) yields good 1-year functional results (MEPS improving from ~41 to ~90; DASH improving from ~66 to ~17).

## Post-op protocol

- Hinged elbow brace set to block the last 20–30° of extension initially, with the arc progressively opened over 2–4 weeks as stability is confirmed clinically.
- Begin protected AROM within 1–2 weeks of fixation.
- Static progressive or dynamic splinting introduced at 6–8 weeks if a flexion or extension contracture is developing despite therapy.
- Formal hand/elbow physiotherapy: AAROM → AROM progression, edema control, scar mobilization once wounds are healed, gentle overpressure stretching from 3–4 weeks once fixation/repair is confirmed stable.
- **Stiffness prevention bundle:** rigid-enough fixation to permit early motion, HO prophylaxis in high-risk cases, early (days, not weeks) initiation of motion, hinged bracing rather than static casting whenever stability allows, and aggressive early physiotherapy engagement.
- **Return to work:** light/sedentary duty at 2–4 weeks with stable fixation; manual labor/heavy lifting typically 3–4 months, contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing.
- **Return to sport:** non-contact sport once a painless functional arc (30°–130° flexion, adequate rotation) and satisfactory strength are achieved, typically 3–4 months; contact/collision sport deferred to ~4–6 months and individualized to fixation stability and residual laxity on exam.
