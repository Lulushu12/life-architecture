## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Terrible triad = elbow dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture, requiring operative stabilization to restore the full "circle of Horii" injury pattern (lateral-to-medial soft-tissue disruption: LCL first, then anterior capsule/coronoid, with the radial head fracturing from impaction against the capitellum).
- Any terrible triad in which the elbow is not concentrically stable on examination under anesthesia, or which involves a coronoid fracture large enough to threaten anterior stability, or a radial head fracture requiring fixation/replacement.
- Monteggia-variant and other combined fracture-dislocation patterns that share the same instability architecture — sequential fixation principles apply even when the injury does not meet the strict "triad" definition.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Truly stable elbow after closed reduction with only a small (O'Driscoll type 1 subtype 1, <2mm) coronoid tip fragment and a Mason I radial head fracture — may be managed non-operatively with early protected motion if concentric stability is confirmed on stress fluoroscopy.
- Active infection or severely compromised soft-tissue envelope — treat/stage before definitive reconstruction.
- Medically unstable polytrauma patient — temporize with a splint or hinged external fixator alone until definitive multi-structure reconstruction can be safely performed.
- Grossly comminuted, unreconstructable coronoid and radial head in a low-demand patient with severe preexisting arthrosis — occasionally better served by a primary hinged fixator/limited reconstruction strategy with a frank discussion of expected residual stiffness, rather than an ambitious multi-structure repair unlikely to hold.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** CT with 2D/3D reconstruction for essentially all terrible triad injuries — required to accurately classify the coronoid fracture (O'Driscoll type) and count/characterize radial head fragments, both of which materially change the fixation plan.
- **Coronoid classification (O'Driscoll):** Type 1 (tip, subtypes by height <2mm vs ≥2mm), Type 2 (anteromedial facet — associated with varus posteromedial rotatory instability and often requiring a separate medial buttress approach), Type 3 (basal, including transolecranon variants) — determine before the case which fragments are fixable through the lateral/anterior exposure alone and which will require a planned medial approach.
- **Implant selection:** have both a coronoid fixation option (suture lasso/suture anchor system for small or comminuted fragments, and small-fragment/headless screws or a mini-plate for larger O'Driscoll type 1 subtype-2/type 3 fragments) and a full radial head ORIF/RHA system available, since the intraoperative fragment assessment frequently changes the plan from what was anticipated on imaging. A comparative case series (2025) found the suture lasso technique for coronoid fixation produced greater intraoperative and final-follow-up stability than alternative fixation methods in terrible triad reconstruction, particularly for small or comminuted fragments where screws/microplates risk splitting the bone; reserve screws/microplates for genuinely large, structurally substantial fragments.
- **LCL repair implants:** suture anchors (2.4–3.5mm) or a drill-hole/transosseous tunnel technique for the lateral epicondyle footprint; have both available depending on bone quality and surgeon preference.
- **Hinged external fixator:** have a hinged elbow fixator available and opened (or immediately obtainable) for any case with preoperative imaging suggesting high-grade instability (large coronoid deficit, combined medial and lateral injury) — do not let instrument availability delay the decision to apply one intraoperatively.
- **Case-specific planning:** plan approach sequencing (lateral first for radial head/LCL, with a separate medial or anterior approach staged in if the coronoid requires it) before the patient is positioned; discuss with the patient/family that the definitive construct (ORIF vs. RHA, suture fixation vs. screw fixation of the coronoid, need for MCL repair or external fixation) may only be finalized intraoperatively once instability is directly assessed.
- **Equipment checklist:** EUA/fluoroscopy setup, lateral (Kocher/EDC-splitting) and medial approach instrument sets, anterior approach set if coronoid-first anterior sequencing is planned, suture lasso/passer instruments, small-fragment and headless screw sets, radial head ORIF/RHA implant system, suture anchors for LCL/MCL repair, hinged external fixator.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia — required to allow a reliable examination under anesthesia (EUA) with fluoroscopic stress testing, which is the definitive stability assessment for this injury and should be planned as part of the operative workup rather than relied upon in the awake ED patient, where guarding limits accuracy.
- **Position:** supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table (for lateral-only work) or lateral decubitus over a padded bolster (preferred when a combined lateral-plus-medial, or posterior/osteotomy, exposure may be needed) — choose a position that allows conversion to a second approach without repositioning if the plan may change intraoperatively.
- **Tourniquet:** pneumatic tourniquet on the upper arm, exsanguinate, inflate to standard limb pressure; plan for a case that may run long (multi-structure reconstruction commonly exceeds 2 hours) and deflate for hemostasis before final closure.
- **Fluoroscopy:** mini C-arm draped into the field and used repeatedly through the case — for the initial EUA stress test, to confirm coronoid and radial head fixation/implant position, and for the critical final intraoperative stability arc test through 30°–130° flexion.
- **Draping:** circumferential extremity prep from above the tourniquet to the fingertips, with the drape arranged to allow conversion between lateral, medial, and (if needed) anterior or posterior exposures without a full re-prep.
- **Landmarks marked:** lateral epicondyle and Kocher interval, medial epicondyle and the course of the ulnar nerve (if a medial approach is anticipated), and the antecubital crease/brachialis-coronoid interval if anterior coronoid-first sequencing is planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Examine under anesthesia and reduce the elbow first.** With the patient fully relaxed, perform gentle closed reduction of the ulnohumeral joint if not already reduced, and stress-test through flexion-extension and varus-valgus under fluoroscopy to characterize the instability pattern before making any incision. Confirm concentric reduction on a true lateral view (symmetric ulnohumeral joint space).
2. Approach laterally through the **Kocher interval** (between anconeus and ECU) or an **EDC-splitting** approach; pronate the forearm during dissection anterior to the radiocapitellar joint to protect the PIN.
3. **Fix the coronoid before the radial head when accessible through the existing exposure** — the coronoid is frequently visible and reachable through the radial head fracture defect itself once the comminuted radial head fragments are temporarily removed, avoiding the need for a separate approach in many cases:
   - For small or comminuted **O'Driscoll type 1** tip fragments, use a **suture lasso technique**: pass sutures through drill holes in the coronoid base, lasso the anterior capsule and any bony fragment together, and shuttle the suture ends to be tied either directly over the anterior ulnar cortex or incorporated into the radial head/LCL fixation construct later in the sequence. This technique has been shown in comparative series to provide greater intraoperative and final-follow-up stability than screw or microplate fixation for small/comminuted fragments, which risk splitting the bone.
   - For larger, structurally substantial **O'Driscoll type 1 subtype-2 or type 3** fragments with adequate bone stock, use small-fragment or headless **screws** placed antegrade or retrograde, or a **mini-plate**, for more rigid fixation of a fragment large enough to tolerate hardware without splitting.
   - **O'Driscoll type 2 (anteromedial facet) fractures are a distinct pattern** and are NOT adequately addressed through the lateral exposure or by suture lasso/LCL repair alone — plan and perform a **separate medial buttress approach** (over the flexor-pronator origin, with ulnar nerve identification/protection) for direct plate fixation of the facet, since this fragment is critical for varus stability.
4. **Fix or replace the radial head.** ORIF if reconstructable (≤3 major fragments) using screws in the "safe zone" or a low-profile mini-plate; RHA if comminuted, sizing carefully to the resected native fragments (trial down, not up, if uncertain) — the radial head must be addressed (essentially never excised alone) because it is a key lateral/valgus and longitudinal stabilizer in this unstable pattern.
5. **Repair the LCL/LUCL.** Through the same lateral exposure, identify the ligament origin (usually avulsed from the lateral epicondyle) and repair it back to the isometric point on the lateral epicondyle — near the center of the capitellum's arc of rotation — using a suture anchor or a transosseous tunnel technique. This step is essential in virtually all terrible triads since the LUCL is the injury's primary soft-tissue lesion; if coronoid sutures were left long in step 3, they can be incorporated into this repair for added anterior-lateral capsular tensioning.
6. **Perform the intraoperative stability test.** Take the elbow through a full flexion-extension arc under fluoroscopy and direct vision after the lateral-sided repair is complete. If the joint tracks concentrically and remains stable through **30°–130°** flexion without subluxation, proceed to closure.
7. **If still unstable** (the joint subluxates, or requires holding in >30–45° flexion to stay reduced): proceed to address the medial side and/or apply supplemental fixation:
   - **Repair the MCL** through a medial approach (identify and protect the ulnar nerve first) if valgus instability persists despite adequate lateral and coronoid fixation.
   - **Apply a hinged external fixator** spanning the elbow to maintain concentric reduction while allowing protected early motion, when soft-tissue repair alone cannot achieve reliable stability, or as an adjunct alongside MCL repair in the most unstable patterns.
8. Take a final fluoroscopic and direct-vision check of full elbow congruency and the stability arc once all structures are addressed.
9. Irrigate, close each approach in layers, and apply a hinged elbow brace (or incorporate the external fixator into the postoperative plan) set to protect the repair while allowing early protected motion — begin this within the first 1–2 weeks per the rehabilitation protocol below, since motion, not prolonged immobilization, is what prevents the stiffness that historically gave this injury its name.

**Alternative sequencing:** a **coronoid-first repair through an anterior approach** (developing the interval over the brachialis to access the coronoid directly, before any lateral work) has shown good mid-term results (minimum 2-year follow-up) and is a reasonable alternative when coronoid fragment morphology and preoperative imaging favor direct anterior exposure over access through the radial head defect.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the radial head fracture defect as a "window" to the coronoid whenever the fragments are already displaced/comminuted — this frequently avoids the need for a separate coronoid-specific approach and is faster than a formal anterior exposure.
- Favor the suture lasso technique over screws/microplates for small or comminuted coronoid fragments — comparative series show it achieves greater construct stability in this specific setting, and screws risk splitting an already-fragmented small piece of bone.
- Reserve screw or microplate coronoid fixation for genuinely large O'Driscoll type 1 subtype-2 or type 3 fragments with adequate bone stock to tolerate hardware.
- If coronoid repair sutures are left long, incorporate them into the subsequent LCL repair construct for added anterior-capsular tensioning — this "layers" the soft-tissue reconstruction rather than treating each structure in complete isolation.
- Always perform the intraoperative stability arc test (30°–130° flexion) under both fluoroscopy and direct vision after the lateral-sided repair — do not rely on a "feels stable" impression alone; a subtle subluxation at a specific arc segment is easy to miss without formally cycling the joint.
- Do not skip a dedicated medial approach for O'Driscoll type 2 (anteromedial facet) fractures — this is the single most common technical error that leads to residual varus instability despite an apparently thorough lateral-sided repair.
- Have the hinged external fixator genuinely ready to apply (not merely "available in the department") for any case with high-grade instability anticipated on preoperative imaging — a delay in obtaining/assembling the fixator after determining the joint is unstable prolongs the case and tourniquet time unnecessarily.
- Counsel the patient/team before the case that the coronoid-first, radial-head-second, LCL-third sequence may be modified intraoperatively (e.g., to coronoid-first-via-anterior-approach) based on fragment morphology actually encountered — flexibility in sequencing, applied consistently with the underlying stability principles, produces good outcomes in either order.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Skipping the intraoperative stability arc test (30°–130° flexion) risks closing an elbow that is not truly concentrically stable.
- Under-recognized or inadequately repaired LCL/coronoid injury leads to chronic instability, recurrent subluxation, or frank redislocation — often requiring revision ligament reconstruction ± coronoid reconstruction later.
- Anteromedial facet (O'Driscoll type 2) coronoid fractures are NOT adequately addressed by LCL repair alone — they require a separate medial buttress approach.
- Screw or microplate fixation of small or comminuted coronoid fragments risks splitting the fragment and achieving less reliable fixation than a suture lasso technique in this specific setting — match the fixation method to fragment size and quality, not habit.
- Suture lasso and suture anchor techniques, while generally more stable in comparative series, carry their own risks of suture laceration through fragile bone or capsule and can still under-perform biomechanically if not tensioned adequately — ORIF overall has been associated with higher rates of implant failure in some series, and suture anchors with higher rates of malunion/nonunion of the underlying fragment, reinforcing that no single coronoid fixation method is failure-proof and technique/fragment-matching matters.
- Heterotopic ossification is the most common complication after high-energy elbow fracture-dislocations, especially with delayed fixation, concomitant head injury, or burns — consider HO prophylaxis (indomethacin/NSAID 2–3 weeks, or single-fraction 700 cGy radiotherapy in very high-risk cases).
- A technically excellent fixation followed by prolonged casting "to be safe" is a common, preventable cause of poor outcome — early motion is part of the fixation strategy, not an afterthought.
- Prospective outcome data confirm that this systematic, sequential fixation (coronoid → radial head → LCL) yields good 1-year functional results (MEPS improving from ~41 to ~90; DASH improving from ~66 to ~17); case series of the suture lasso variant report a mean postoperative arc of motion of 115° (range 75°–140°), average DASH of 16 (range 0–43), and average Broberg-Morrey score of 90 (range 64–100) — broadly consistent, reinforcing outcomes across technique variants when sequencing principles are followed.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Hinged elbow brace set to block the last 20–30° of extension initially, with the arc progressively opened over 2–4 weeks as stability is confirmed clinically.
- Begin protected AROM within 1–2 weeks of fixation.
- Static progressive or dynamic splinting introduced at 6–8 weeks if a flexion or extension contracture is developing despite therapy.
- Formal hand/elbow physiotherapy: AAROM → AROM progression, edema control, scar mobilization once wounds are healed, gentle overpressure stretching from 3–4 weeks once fixation/repair is confirmed stable.
- **Stiffness prevention bundle:** rigid-enough fixation to permit early motion, HO prophylaxis in high-risk cases, early (days, not weeks) initiation of motion, hinged bracing rather than static casting whenever stability allows, and aggressive early physiotherapy engagement.
- **Return to work:** light/sedentary duty at 2–4 weeks with stable fixation; manual labor/heavy lifting typically 3–4 months, contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing.
- **Return to sport:** non-contact sport once a painless functional arc (30°–130° flexion, adequate rotation) and satisfactory strength are achieved, typically 3–4 months; contact/collision sport deferred to ~4–6 months and individualized to fixation stability and residual laxity on exam.
