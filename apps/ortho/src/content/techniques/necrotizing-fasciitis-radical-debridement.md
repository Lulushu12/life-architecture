## Indications

Any patient with a clinical picture compatible with necrotizing fasciitis: pain out of proportion to exam, pain crossing fascial planes beyond visible erythema, skin anesthesia, hemorrhagic bullae, crepitus, rapid progression over hours, or systemic toxicity disproportionate to skin findings. Necrotizing fasciitis is a surgical emergency confirmed only in the operating room — do not wait for imaging or a LRINEC cut-off.

## Positioning & setup

Primarily a clinical diagnosis. Cross-sectional imaging (CT showing fascial gas or fluid tracking, non-enhancing fascia, or MRI with fascial hyperintensity) may support the diagnosis but must never delay operative exploration in a patient with a compatible clinical picture.

## Steps

1. Once suspected, proceed directly to emergent operative exploration — not further diagnostic testing; imaging or scoring systems must not delay the trip to the OR.
2. Confirm intraoperatively via the "finger test" (lack of resistance to blunt finger dissection along the fascial plane), dishwater-grey exudate, thrombosed subcutaneous vessels, and lack of bleeding from the tissue.
3. Perform radical debridement of all non-viable fascia and overlying skin/subcutaneous tissue to a margin of visibly healthy, bleeding, adherent fascia.
4. Consider amputation as the only source-control option for a rapidly progressive, limb-threatening extremity infection.
5. Start empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics as an adjunct to (never a substitute for) surgery — e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem plus clindamycin, ± vancomycin/linezolid for MRSA coverage; clindamycin is included specifically for its toxin-suppressive effect via protein synthesis inhibition in streptococcal/staphylococcal toxin-mediated disease.
6. Plan a mandatory re-look at 24–48h (often more than once), continuing until the wound bed is clean and progression has stopped.
7. Proceed to delayed reconstruction (skin grafting, flap coverage) only after source control has been achieved.

## Pitfalls

- Time-to-debridement is the dominant modifiable predictor of mortality — door-to-OR <12h is associated with markedly lower mortality; overall mortality is ~20–30%, rising toward ~70% with delay beyond 24h.
- Skin findings dramatically underestimate the extent of necrosis, since sparse fascial blood supply enables rapid, silent proximal-distal spread — do not judge the debridement margin by visible erythema alone.
- A low LRINEC score must never override clinical suspicion or delay surgical exploration — sensitivity is only ~70–80%, and up to 10–20% of surgically confirmed cases present with LRINEC <6.
- IVIG remains an unresolved adjunct (evidence mixed even in a 2025 international consensus) — it is not a substitute for source control via early radical debridement, which remains the only intervention with unambiguous mortality benefit.

## Post-op protocol

**Days 0–14 (acute/source-control phase):** Dressing changes or negative-pressure wound therapy between staged debridements; joint positioning/splinting to prevent contracture even while systemically unwell; nutritional support is a rehabilitation priority given the catabolic burden of extensive tissue loss and repeated operative trips.

**Weeks 2–8:** Once the wound bed is clean and reconstruction (graft/flap) has been performed, progressive mobilization per flap-protection protocol (typically a period of immobilization for free-flap take, then graded activity); scar and contracture management begins as soon as the reconstruction is stable.

**Months 3–12:** Long-term functional and cosmetic rehabilitation, often with plastic-surgery-led scar therapy, compression garments, and staged secondary reconstructive procedures. Return to work is highly variable — weeks for a limited-debridement survivor to many months for extensive limb reconstruction or after amputation, where prosthetic rehabilitation becomes the primary pathway.
