## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Any patient with a clinical picture compatible with necrotizing fasciitis: pain out of proportion to exam, pain crossing fascial planes beyond visible erythema, skin anesthesia, hemorrhagic bullae, crepitus, rapid progression over hours, or systemic toxicity disproportionate to skin findings.
- Necrotizing fasciitis is a surgical emergency **confirmed only in the operating room** — clinical suspicion alone is the indication for exploration; do not wait for imaging or a LRINEC cut-off, both of which lack the sensitivity to safely rule the diagnosis out.
- A positive intra-operative finding (see Step-by-Step) confirms the diagnosis and mandates proceeding directly to radical debridement in the same setting — there is no role for "closing and reassessing" once necrotizing fasciitis is confirmed at exploration.

**Contraindications**
- There is no medical contraindication that should delay exploration in a patient with a compatible clinical picture — even a critically unstable patient should generally go to the OR for source control with concurrent resuscitation, since ongoing tissue necrosis and toxin production are themselves drivers of the instability.
- The only real "contraindication" is a confidently alternative diagnosis established by direct inspection (e.g., simple cellulitis, an abscess without fascial involvement) — but this determination is made intra-operatively via exploration, not pre-operatively via imaging or scoring, given the poor sensitivity of both.
- Amputation, while sometimes necessary, is not indicated as a default first step — attempt debridement with limb preservation first unless the limb is unsalvageable or amputation is the only way to achieve source control in a rapidly progressive, life-threatening infection.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Do not let planning delay the trip to the OR.** The single most important "planning" step is recognizing that time-to-debridement is the dominant modifiable predictor of mortality and mobilizing the OR, anesthesia, and surgical team immediately on clinical suspicion.
- **Imaging** (only if it does not delay surgery): CT showing fascial gas, fluid tracking along fascial planes, or non-enhancing fascia, or MRI with fascial hyperintensity, may support the diagnosis in a genuinely equivocal presentation — but must never be pursued at the expense of prompt exploration in a patient with a compatible clinical picture.
- **Marking the extent of disease**: if time allows and the patient is being moved to the OR promptly, mark the visible margin of erythema/induration with a skin marker before incision — this provides a baseline reference, since the actual fascial necrosis typically extends well beyond it and the marked line helps communicate the discrepancy to the team and for serial re-look comparison.
- **Blood products and resuscitation**: alert the blood bank and anticipate significant blood loss and fluid shifts, particularly for a large or truncal/perineal debridement; coordinate with anesthesia and, where available, critical care for peri-operative and post-operative management of septic physiology.
- **Multidisciplinary team**: involve plastic surgery early for anticipated soft-tissue reconstruction, general/colorectal surgery for perineal (Fournier's) disease with potential need for fecal diversion, and critical care/infectious disease concurrently — necrotizing fasciitis is rarely a single-specialty case beyond the index debridement.
- **Equipment**: have electrocautery, a full complement of debridement instruments, and — for an anticipated amputation-level infection — amputation instrumentation available in the room rather than needing to send for it mid-case.
- **Antibiotics**: ensure empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem plus clindamycin, ± vancomycin/linezolid for MRSA coverage) are started as soon as the diagnosis is suspected, in parallel with — never as a substitute for or delay to — surgical mobilization.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with full hemodynamic monitoring (arterial line strongly considered given expected physiologic instability); these patients frequently meet criteria for septic shock and require concurrent resuscitation intra-operatively, so close coordination with anesthesia before induction is essential, including discussion of vasopressor readiness.
- **Position**: dictated entirely by the anatomic site of disease — supine for most limb and abdominal wall disease, lithotomy or modified lithotomy for perineal/Fournier's disease (allows access to the perineum, scrotum/labia, and perianal region simultaneously), lateral decubitus for posterior trunk disease. Plan positioning to allow access to the full extent of suspected disease plus a margin, since the debridement frequently extends further than the pre-operative exam suggested.
- **Table setup**: standard OR table; for perineal disease, stirrups for lithotomy positioning with careful padding given often-prolonged case duration and unstable physiology.
- **Tourniquet**: not applicable to fascial/soft-tissue debridement across large, often multi-compartmental areas of the trunk or proximal limb; even for a distal limb presentation, a tourniquet is generally avoided since assessment of tissue bleeding/viability (a core diagnostic and margin-assessment tool) requires perfused tissue.
- **Draping**: wide, generous draping well beyond the visibly affected area and the pre-marked margin — the debridement extent frequently exceeds what is visible on skin exam, and the drape field must not become the limiting factor on how far the surgeon can extend debridement once truly non-viable tissue is found intra-operatively.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the visible margin of erythema/skin change before incision (see Preoperative Planning) as a reference point, understanding that the true fascial margin will very likely extend beyond it.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Proceed directly to emergent operative exploration** on clinical suspicion — imaging, LRINEC, or further diagnostic testing must not delay the trip to the OR.
2. **Make an initial incision** through skin and subcutaneous tissue at the area of greatest clinical suspicion (often the most tender, most erythematous, or most rapidly changing area) down to the level of the deep fascia.
3. **Perform the "finger test"** — attempt blunt finger dissection along the fascial plane. A positive test (lack of normal resistance, tissue that separates with minimal force) strongly supports the diagnosis. Concurrently assess for **dishwater-grey exudate**, **thrombosed subcutaneous vessels**, and a **lack of bleeding** from the tissue at exploration — the combination of these findings, not any single one, confirms the diagnosis intra-operatively.
4. **Extend the incision along the fascial plane** in the direction the finger test tracks freely, following the actual extent of disease rather than being anchored to the pre-marked skin margin — the fascial necrosis, once confirmed, dictates the debridement extent, not the initial skin incision.
5. **Classify tissue into zones as debridement proceeds**: frankly necrotic, non-bleeding, dishwater-draining tissue is excised completely; adjacent tissue of uncertain viability is assessed carefully and trimmed back only as far as needed to remove non-viable tissue while preserving what is salvageable (this zone is reassessed at the planned re-look rather than being maximally resected at the index procedure, since viability can be difficult to judge definitively under acute inflammation); clearly healthy, bleeding, normally-adherent tissue beyond the disease margin is left alone.
6. **Debride all non-viable fascia and the overlying non-viable skin/subcutaneous tissue** to a margin of visibly healthy, bleeding, adherent fascia on all sides — do not stop at the pre-marked skin margin if the finger test and tissue findings indicate further extension; conversely, do not extend indiscriminately into clearly healthy, well-perfused tissue beyond what the intra-operative findings support.
7. **Send debrided tissue for microbiology (Gram stain and culture) and histopathology** — histopathology can help confirm the diagnosis and characterize the depth/extent of necrosis when the intra-operative picture is borderline.
8. **Assess limb/segment viability continuously through the debridement.** If source control cannot realistically be achieved through debridement alone in a rapidly progressive, limb-threatening extremity infection (e.g., disease tracking proximally faster than it can be controlled, or the limb is unsalvageable), proceed to amputation as the definitive source-control measure — this decision should not be deferred if the alternative is ongoing, uncontrolled sepsis.
9. **Achieve hemostasis** and irrigate the wound bed thoroughly.
10. **Do not attempt primary closure.** Leave the wound open, dress with saline-moistened gauze or apply negative-pressure wound therapy per surgeon preference and wound characteristics, anticipating further debridement.
11. **Continue empiric broad-spectrum IV antibiotics** (adjusted as culture results return) as an adjunct to, never a substitute for, the surgical source control already performed.
12. **Explicitly plan and schedule a mandatory re-look at 24–48h** before leaving the OR — this is not a contingency, it is a standard, expected part of the operative sequence for confirmed necrotizing fasciitis.
13. **At each re-look**, repeat the same assessment: re-examine tissue viability, extend debridement as needed for any newly demarcated non-viable tissue, and continue serial re-looks (often more than once) until the wound bed is clean, bleeding, and progression has definitively stopped.
14. **Proceed to delayed reconstruction** (split-thickness skin grafting, local or free flap coverage per defect size and location) only once source control is confirmed and the wound bed is clean at a re-look — reconstruction attempted before infection is controlled predictably fails.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Let the finger test, not the pre-marked skin margin, dictate how far to extend the debridement — the fascial disease routinely extends well beyond visible skin changes, and anchoring the debridement to the skin margin is a common cause of inadequate initial source control.
- Use the three-zone framework (necrotic/excise, uncertain/trim conservatively and reassess, healthy/preserve) explicitly during the case — it gives a structured way to balance the competing goals of complete source control and tissue/limb preservation, and provides a clear rationale to document in the operative note for the next surgeon at re-look.
- Photograph the wound bed at the end of each debridement — this is invaluable for the re-look team (who may not be the index surgeon) to judge interval progression or regression.
- Do not close fascial defects or attempt any primary skin closure at the index procedure, even in areas that look clean — leave everything open and reassess at the planned re-look.
- For perineal (Fournier's) disease, involve general/colorectal surgery early for a low threshold to fecal diversion if there is any concern about ongoing fecal contamination of the wound bed, which will otherwise undermine every subsequent debridement effort.
- When in doubt about the viability of a segment of tissue at the margin, err toward re-look reassessment rather than either extreme (neither maximal resection of possibly-salvageable tissue nor leaving obviously questionable tissue in place) — the planned 24–48h re-look exists precisely to resolve this uncertainty safely.
- Keep the wound generously open with negative-pressure wound therapy or moist dressings between debridements — this both manages the large fluid/protein losses from an open wound and keeps the wound bed accessible and easy to reassess at the next look.
- Coordinate nutrition support (often early enteral, sometimes parenteral) from the first 24–48h — these patients are markedly catabolic from both the infection and repeated operative trips, and inadequate nutritional support measurably slows wound-bed progress toward being reconstruction-ready.
- Do not view amputation as a last resort to be avoided at all costs when it is the fastest route to definitive source control in a rapidly progressive extremity infection — delayed amputation after multiple failed limb-salvage debridements is a recognized, avoidable driver of excess mortality.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Time-to-debridement is the dominant modifiable predictor of mortality — door-to-OR <12h is associated with markedly lower mortality; overall mortality is ~20–30%, rising toward ~70% with delay beyond 24h.
- Skin findings dramatically underestimate the extent of necrosis, since sparse fascial blood supply enables rapid, silent proximal-distal spread — do not judge the debridement margin by visible erythema alone.
- A low LRINEC score must never override clinical suspicion or delay surgical exploration — sensitivity is only ~70–80%, and up to 10–20% of surgically confirmed cases present with LRINEC <6.
- IVIG remains an unresolved adjunct (evidence mixed even in a 2025 international consensus) — it is not a substitute for source control via early radical debridement, which remains the only intervention with unambiguous mortality benefit.
- Inadequate initial debridement (stopping at the pre-marked skin margin rather than following the true fascial extent) is a common technical error that necessitates a more extensive, higher-morbidity re-look than would have been needed with an adequate index procedure.
- Attempting primary closure at the index procedure, or closing too early at a re-look before the wound bed is truly clean, risks recurrent uncontrolled infection under a closed space.
- Delayed or repeatedly deferred amputation in an unsalvageable, rapidly progressive extremity infection — out of reluctance to amputate — is an avoidable contributor to mortality; the decision should be made on source-control grounds, not deferred emotionally.
- Fecal contamination of a perineal wound bed from an undiverted, ongoing fecal stream will undermine every subsequent debridement in Fournier's gangrene — involve colorectal surgery early rather than after repeated failed local wound control.
- Post-operative catabolic decompensation from inadequate nutritional support slows wound-bed readiness for reconstruction and prolongs the overall hospital course — treat nutrition as a rehabilitation priority from day one, not an afterthought once the acute surgical phase is over.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Days 0–14 (acute/source-control phase):** Dressing changes or negative-pressure wound therapy between staged debridements; joint positioning/splinting to prevent contracture even while systemically unwell; nutritional support is a rehabilitation priority given the catabolic burden of extensive tissue loss and repeated operative trips.

**Weeks 2–8:** Once the wound bed is clean and reconstruction (graft/flap) has been performed, progressive mobilization per flap-protection protocol (typically a period of immobilization for free-flap take, then graded activity); scar and contracture management begins as soon as the reconstruction is stable.

**Months 3–12:** Long-term functional and cosmetic rehabilitation, often with plastic-surgery-led scar therapy, compression garments, and staged secondary reconstructive procedures. Return to work is highly variable — weeks for a limited-debridement survivor to many months for extensive limb reconstruction or after amputation, where prosthetic rehabilitation becomes the primary pathway.
