## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:

- Stable OCD lesions (intact overlying cartilage) failing 3–6 months of non-operative care (activity restriction, unloader brace, non-weight-bearing trial): arthroscopic drilling.
- Unstable/partially detached but viable fragment: fragment fixation — preferred whenever fragment viability allows, since it preserves native hyaline cartilage.
- Non-viable fragment or a full-thickness defect remaining after fragment excision: treat as a focal chondral/osteochondral defect on the cartilage restoration ladder.
- Staging relies on MRI instability criteria (high-signal/fluid line ≥5 mm deep to the fragment on T2, a peripheral high-signal rim, multiple breaches/cysts beneath the fragment, a focal osteochondral defect with fluid extending through the cartilage surface) together with arthroscopic ICRS OCD staging (I–IV).
- The classic location is the posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle (~70–80% of cases); lesion size >2 cm² and involvement of the weight-bearing lateral femoral condyle, or an unstable/detached fragment, predict failure of conservative care.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:

- Skeletally immature patients with a stable lesion and no MRI instability criteria — non-operative management remains first-line, since healing rates are favorable in this group; surgery is reserved for failure of an adequate non-operative trial.
- An incidental, asymptomatic "ossification variant" mistaken for OCD — distinguish by lesion location, symmetry (variants are often bilateral/symmetric), and absence of MRI instability criteria before committing to surgery.
- A fragment judged non-viable (comminuted, fully detached with poor bone stock, or chronically resorbed) is not a fixation candidate — proceed directly to excision and cartilage restoration planning rather than attempting fixation.
- Diffuse chondral degeneration of the involved compartment beyond the focal OCD lesion — treat as advanced cartilage disease rather than isolated OCD.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP, lateral, and notch/tunnel view radiographs (notch view best profiles the classic posterolateral medial condyle location); MRI is essential for staging stability using the instability criteria above. Compare with the contralateral knee if an ossification variant is a diagnostic consideration.
- **Decide the operative pathway in advance where possible**, but plan to confirm intraoperatively — arthroscopic ICRS OCD staging (I: stable, intact cartilage; II: cartilage breach with underlying fracture/fluid; III: partially detached fragment; IV: fully detached/loose body) frequently refines or changes the plan made from MRI alone.
- **Implant/equipment selection**:
  - *Drilling*: a set of fine (0.045–0.062 in) K-wires or drill bits; decide preoperatively between a transarticular approach (through the joint surface, technically simpler, used when a discrete point of visualized/probed cartilage softening is present) and a retroarticular/extra-articular approach (from outside the joint into the lesion without crossing intact cartilage, requiring fluoroscopic guidance).
  - *Fixation*: bioabsorbable pins/screws (avoid a second surgery for hardware removal) or metallic compression screws (headless or low-profile, allow compression across the fragment-bed interface but typically require a planned removal procedure); have both available, since the choice may depend on fragment size/orientation discovered intraoperatively.
  - *Bone graft*: have a source of autograft (arthroscopic drilling debris, or a small iliac crest or proximal tibial harvest) available for grafting the crater beneath a fixed fragment.
- **Equipment/instrumentation checklist**: standard arthroscopy tower; C-arm fluoroscopy set up and draped in if retroarticular drilling or screw/pin placement under fluoroscopic guidance is planned; a small curette/shaver set for debriding the fibrous interface of an unstable fragment; a compression screw or bioabsorbable pin system sized appropriately for the fragment.
- **Positioning aid**: for retroarticular drilling, plan a sandbag or thigh-post to hold the knee flexed 30–60° (the specific angle depends on how posterior the lesion is on the condyle — more posterior lesions require more flexion), since this position is used to direct the guide wire from the extra-articular epiphyseal cortex to the lesion without crossing the joint.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia, often combined with a femoral or adductor canal nerve block.
- **Positioning**: supine with a leg holder or lateral post allowing full flexion beyond 120°; for retroarticular drilling specifically, position the knee flexed 30–60° and hold it with a sandbag and thigh post so the flexion angle stays constant and reproducible while the guide wire trajectory is planned and drilled — the exact flexion angle is adjusted based on how posterior the lesion sits on the condyle.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh tourniquet applied and available.
- **Fluoroscopy**: a sterile-draped C-arm is required in the room for retroarticular/extra-articular drilling and is helpful for confirming screw/pin trajectory and depth during fragment fixation; position the C-arm and confirm clear AP and lateral views of the knee before prepping, since intraoperative repositioning is disruptive once draped.
- **Setup**: standard knee arthroscopy fluid inflow/outflow system; keep the C-arm monitor and image intensifier oriented for easy surgeon viewing without breaking sterile technique.
- **Landmarks**: mark standard anteromedial/anterolateral arthroscopy portals; for retroarticular drilling, mark the planned percutaneous guide-wire entry point at the medial or lateral edge of the femoral epiphysis (determined from the preoperative notch-view radiograph and MRI localization of the lesion) before draping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm stability status with MRI instability criteria and/or arthroscopic ICRS OCD staging before choosing between drilling, fixation, and excision.
2. Perform diagnostic arthroscopy through standard portals; directly visualize and probe the lesion to confirm cartilage integrity (stable, intact surface vs. softening/fissuring vs. partial or complete detachment) — this arthroscopic assessment frequently refines the MRI-based stability call.
3. **Stable lesion failing 3–6 months of non-operative care**: perform arthroscopic drilling.
   - *Transarticular*: under direct arthroscopic visualization, pass a fine K-wire or drill perpendicular to the articular surface through the softened/intact cartilage into the lesion at multiple points spaced a few millimeters apart, penetrating into healthy subchondral bone beyond the lesion-bone interface to stimulate a vascular healing response.
   - *Retroarticular/extra-articular*: with the knee held flexed 30–60° by the sandbag/thigh post, place a percutaneous K-wire at the medial or lateral edge of the epiphysis and direct it under fluoroscopic (and arthroscopic, for confirmation of trajectory relative to the joint surface) guidance toward the center of the lesion, drilling multiple parallel passes without penetrating the articular cartilage — this preserves the intact overlying surface entirely, which is the main advantage of this approach; a larger-diameter wire (e.g., 0.062 in) can be used safely here since it does not cross the articular cartilage.
4. **Unstable/partially detached but viable fragment**: elevate the fragment carefully, debride the fibrous interface tissue from both the fragment's undersurface and the crater bed with a curette or shaver until healthy bleeding bone is exposed on both surfaces.
5. Micro-drill the sclerotic crater base to stimulate bleeding, and pack the crater with autograft bone (drilling debris, or a small harvested graft) to restore the subchondral contour and provide a vascular healing bed beneath the fragment.
6. Reduce the fragment anatomically to the crater and provisionally hold it with a K-wire or probe while confirming congruity with the surrounding native cartilage surface.
7. Fix the fragment with bioabsorbable pins/screws or metallic compression screws, placed perpendicular to the fragment surface (or slightly countersunk to avoid prominence) and spaced to achieve stable, multi-point fixation without fracturing the fragment.
8. Confirm fixation stability by probing the fragment margin and cycling the knee through range of motion; verify on fluoroscopy (if used) that hardware does not breach the articular surface or extend into the joint.
9. **Non-viable fragment or full-thickness defect after fragment excision**: excise the non-viable fragment, debride the crater to a stable, healthy rim, and treat per the cartilage restoration ladder — OAT for smaller lesions, OCA for larger lesions or when subchondral bone stock is deficient, ACI/MACI reserved for chondral-only defects with adequate subchondral bone.
10. Throughout, distinguish true OCD from an incidental "ossification variant" in skeletally immature patients by lesion location, symmetry (variants are often bilateral/symmetric), and absence of MRI instability criteria before committing to surgery.
11. Close portal and any percutaneous drilling sites in standard fashion; document lesion stage, technique used, and fixation construct in the operative note for postoperative protocol planning.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use arthroscopic direct visualization/probing to confirm or revise the MRI-based stability stage before committing to a technique — the two do not always agree, and the intraoperative finding should govern the final decision.
- For retroarticular drilling, adjust the knee flexion angle to the specific posterior extent of the lesion on the condyle rather than using a single fixed angle for every case — more posterior lesions require more flexion to achieve a perpendicular, on-target trajectory from the extra-articular entry point.
- A larger-diameter K-wire (e.g., 0.062 in) is appropriate for the retroarticular approach since it never crosses the articular cartilage, whereas the transarticular approach should use a finer wire to minimize cartilage injury at each pass.
- When fixing a fragment, always debride the fibrous interface completely from both surfaces before grafting — fixing a fragment over residual fibrous tissue without a bleeding bone interface is a common cause of nonunion.
- Countersink metallic compression screw heads slightly below the articular surface, or confirm bioabsorbable pin heads are flush, to reduce the risk of symptomatic hardware prominence — the most common reason for reoperation even when the fragment goes on to unite.
- Have a low threshold to convert intraoperatively from fixation to excision-plus-restoration if the fragment is found to be more comminuted or less viable than the preoperative imaging suggested — do not force fixation of a fragment that will not survive.
- Fluoroscopic confirmation of pin/screw trajectory (even briefly, even for a primarily arthroscopic case) is inexpensive insurance against an intra-articular hardware breach that is otherwise easy to miss on arthroscopic view alone, particularly at the fragment periphery.
- In adults, maintain a lower surgical threshold than in skeletally immature patients — healing rates decline sharply after physeal closure, so a shorter trial of non-operative care (toward the 3-month end of the 3–6 month range) is reasonable in an adult with a stable but symptomatic, non-improving lesion.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not rely on radiographs alone to stage stability — MRI is required for surgical decision-making.
- Hardware prominence/synovitis requiring removal is the most common reason for reoperation even with fragment union.
- Nonunion of the fixed fragment, or progression to a full-thickness defect if fixation fails, converts the case to a salvage cartilage-restoration procedure.
- Healing rates in skeletally immature, stable OCD treated non-operatively are favorable but decline sharply in skeletally mature patients — use a much lower threshold for surgery in adults.
- Fixing a fragment without adequately debriding the fibrous interface or grafting the crater is a preventable cause of nonunion.
- Retroarticular drilling performed without correct flexion-angle planning risks a guide wire trajectory that misses the lesion or inadvertently breaches the articular surface — confirm trajectory with fluoroscopy and/or arthroscopic visualization before completing the drilling pass.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Drilling (intact cartilage)**: weight-bearing as tolerated with brace protection, ROM progressed early; return to sport at 3–4 months once healing is confirmed on imaging.
- **Fragment fixation**: protected weight-bearing for 6 weeks, ROM progression similar to the cartilage-restoration protocols; return to sport at 6–9 months, pending radiographic/MRI union of the fragment.
- The 2024 AAOS Osteochondritis Dissecans Clinical Practice Guideline Rapid Update reaffirms MRI-based instability staging and supports drilling for stable lesions failing non-operative care, with fixation preferred over excision for unstable-but-viable fragments.
