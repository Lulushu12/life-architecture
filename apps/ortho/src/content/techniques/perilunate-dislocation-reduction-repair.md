## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Any confirmed perilunate dislocation or fracture-dislocation (Mayfield stage II–IV, lesser- or greater-arc) — this is a surgical emergency in essentially all cases.
- A "wrist sprain" with persistent pain and swelling after high-energy trauma should raise suspicion, since up to 25% of perilunate dislocations are missed on the initial ED film (look for disrupted Gilula's arcs, "spilled teacup" lunate, capitate overlap).
- Median nerve compression symptoms or a tense carpal tunnel after high-energy wrist trauma mandate emergent reduction regardless of definitive-fixation timing.

**Contraindications**
- **Absolute**: none to emergent closed reduction — it should be performed as soon as the diagnosis is made, even before full imaging workup is complete, if there are neurologic or skin-compromise signs.
- **Relative to open definitive repair**: chronic (>6 weeks) neglected dislocation where closed or even open anatomic reduction is not achievable and cartilage damage is already established — proceed toward a salvage strategy (PRC/4-corner fusion) rather than attempting anatomic ligament reconstruction; medically unstable polytrauma patient in whom definitive open repair must be staged after emergent closed reduction and splinting.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: PA, lateral, and oblique wrist radiographs with specific attention to Gilula's carpal arcs, the lunate's articular relationship to the radius and capitate ("spilled teacup" sign on lateral), and capitate overlap on the AP view. CT is strongly recommended to characterize any greater-arc (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial-styloid) fracture component and plan concurrent fracture fixation.
- **Implant planning**: K-wires (0.045–0.062 in) for SL and LT joint fixation; suture anchors for direct dorsal SLIL/LTIL repair or internal-brace augmentation with high-strength nonabsorbable suture tape; headless compression screw set on standby for trans-scaphoid variants.
- **Equipment**: mini C-arm, small-joint retractor set, K-wire driver, suture anchor system, carpal tunnel release instrument set (in case volar decompression/exposure is needed), external fixator as a backup option for severely comminuted/unstable patterns.
- **Case-specific planning**: determine lesser- vs greater-arc pattern preoperatively from CT — greater-arc variants need the scaphoid (or other fractured carpal bone) fixation added to the ligamentous repair plan, not substituted for it.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

**Emergent closed reduction** can typically be performed under procedural sedation in the emergency department or immediately in the operating room under regional/general anesthesia if proceeding directly to definitive fixation. For **definitive open repair**, general anesthesia or a regional block (supraclavicular/axillary) is standard, supine position with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, tourniquet applied after exsanguination. Prep and drape circumferentially to allow both dorsal and volar access, since a combined approach is frequently required. Position the mini C-arm for orthogonal wrist imaging before draping is finalized. Mark the dorsal wrist landmarks (Lister's tubercle, third/fourth extensor compartment interval) and the volar carpal tunnel landmarks (palmaris longus, distal wrist crease) before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Emergent closed reduction** (performed first, regardless of eventual definitive plan): apply longitudinal traction to the hand with countertraction on the forearm for several minutes to fatigue the musculature and disimpact the carpus, then apply direct thumb pressure over the volar-prominent lunate while extending the wrist, following the reverse of the Mayfield injury sequence, then flex the wrist to complete the reduction. Confirm restoration of the normal lunate-capitate-radius relationship on fluoroscopy/lateral film, then splint the wrist in slight flexion.

2. **Definitive open repair**, commonly performed within the first 24–96 hours to ~2 weeks (earlier repair is generally associated with better outcomes): plan a combined dorsal (primary) ± volar approach.

3. **Dorsal exposure.** Make a longitudinal dorsal incision from the third metacarpal base extending proximally to the distal radioulnar joint. Identify and transpose the extensor pollicis longus radially out of the third compartment; retract the second and fourth extensor compartments to expose the dorsal capsule. Perform a ligament-sparing (Berger-type) capsulotomy to preserve the dorsal intercarpal and dorsal radiocarpal ligaments while exposing the SL and LT joints and the midcarpal row.

4. **Reduce the carpus under direct vision using joystick K-wires.** Place a joystick K-wire into the scaphoid and another into the lunate. Use the scaphoid wire to extend the scaphoid (correcting its flexed, pronated position) and the lunate wire to flex the lunate (correcting its extended DISI-type position), then close the SL gap and hold provisional reduction with pointed reduction (tenaculum) forceps across the SL interval. Preliminary lunate reduction and pinning is a useful technical sequence — it restores carpal height early and gives a stable reference for subsequent scaphoid and triquetral reduction.

5. **Repair the SL and LT ligaments.** With the reduction held provisionally, repair the dorsal SLIL and LTIL directly with suture anchors placed into the lunate and scaphoid (and triquetrum for the LT side). Where tissue quality allows augmentation, an internal-brace technique may be added: drill three bone tunnels (lunate, proximal scaphoid, distal scaphoid), pass high-strength nonabsorbable suture tape through them, and tension it to hold the scaphoid-lunate relationship while the native ligament repair heals.

6. **Definitive intercarpal K-wire fixation.** Place K-wires across the SL joint and across the LT joint to maintain reduction through the healing period; cut the wires short beneath the skin or leave them percutaneous per surgeon preference, planned for removal at 8–10 (up to 12) weeks.

7. **Volar approach (added when needed):** carpal tunnel exposure for median nerve decompression (mandatory if any preoperative median nerve symptoms or persistent compression signs), direct repair of the volar radioscaphocapitate (RSC) ligament, and/or completion of reduction if the dorsal approach alone cannot achieve it. Release the transverse carpal ligament fully and inspect the nerve for contusion.

8. **Greater-arc (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial-styloid) variants:** fix the associated carpal bone fracture (typically the scaphoid, with a headless compression screw placed via the same dorsal or an added percutaneous trajectory) in addition to completing the ligamentous repair described above — the bony injury must be fixed, not treated as if it were purely ligamentous.

9. **Confirm final reduction and hardware position** on multi-view fluoroscopy — restored carpal height, corrected SL/LT angles, and centrally placed K-wires/screws — before closure.

10. **Closure.** Repair the dorsal capsulotomy, close extensor retinaculum loosely to avoid bowstringing, close skin in layers, and apply a well-padded splint/cast with the wrist in neutral to slight flexion, incorporating the forearm to control rotation if a trans-scaphoid component was fixed.

11. **Missed/chronic (>6 weeks) injury:** open reduction becomes progressively harder as the volar capsule contracts and cartilage degenerates; if anatomic reduction is not achievable intraoperatively or cartilage damage is already present, proceed directly toward a salvage strategy (PRC/4-corner fusion) rather than persisting with an anatomic ligament reconstruction that is unlikely to hold.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Attempt closed reduction immediately on diagnosis, even in the emergency department under sedation, rather than waiting for the operating room — this decompresses the median nerve and skin and buys time for definitive planning.
- Use a ligament-sparing (Berger) dorsal capsulotomy rather than an unstructured capsular incision — preserving the dorsal intercarpal and radiocarpal ligaments protects secondary stabilizers you will rely on postoperatively.
- Reduce and pin the lunate first using a joystick wire before tackling scaphoid or triquetral reduction — restoring carpal height early gives a stable platform for the rest of the reduction sequence.
- Joystick wires in both the scaphoid and lunate let you correct the DISI pattern directly and under control — extend the scaphoid, flex the lunate, then close the SL gap, rather than trying to force reduction with reduction forceps alone.
- Consider suture-tape internal-brace augmentation of the SL/LT repair in higher-demand patients or when native tissue quality is marginal — it protects the repair during the critical early healing window without replacing the biologic repair itself.
- Always evaluate and treat the volar side (carpal tunnel release, RSC repair) when there are any preoperative median nerve symptoms — do not defer decompression to a second stage.
- Get CT-quality characterization of any bony (greater-arc) component before the case — discovering an unplanned scaphoid or triquetral fracture intraoperatively changes the fixation plan and equipment needs.
- Counsel the patient before surgery that residual stiffness and a meaningful long-term risk of SLAC-pattern arthritis persist even after technically excellent, appropriately timed repair — this is a feature of the injury, not necessarily a marker of technical failure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Delayed decompression risks persistent median nerve neuropathy from acute compression — emergent closed reduction should not be delayed while arranging definitive surgery.
- Even technically adequate, appropriately timed repair is associated with persistent moderate residual disability (grip strength deficits, reduced ROM, later SLAC-pattern arthritis) — patient counseling about long-term prognosis is essential regardless of timing.
- Up to 25% of perilunate dislocations are missed on initial imaging — a normal-seeming "wrist sprain" film after high-energy trauma warrants specific review of Gilula's arcs and the lunate's articular relationships.
- Greater-arc (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial styloid) variants require fracture fixation in addition to ligamentous repair; treating them as purely ligamentous injuries under-treats the bony component.
- Failure to use a ligament-sparing capsulotomy can sacrifice the dorsal intercarpal/radiocarpal ligaments needed as secondary stabilizers, undermining the long-term stability of an otherwise well-executed repair.
- Incomplete carpal tunnel release when median nerve symptoms are present risks persistent compressive neuropathy — release the entire transverse carpal ligament, not a partial window.
- K-wire migration or pin-tract infection is a recognized complication of the 8–10 week fixation period; monitor pin sites and remove promptly at the planned interval.
- Chronic/neglected (>6 week) injuries are progressively harder to reduce anatomically; forcing an anatomic reconstruction against contracted, degenerated tissue produces a poor result compared with transitioning to a planned salvage procedure.

## Postoperative Protocol

Cast/brace with K-wires in place for 8–10 weeks, followed by wire removal and gradual AROM. Hand therapy focuses on regaining the flexion/extension arc, which is often permanently reduced 20–30% versus the contralateral side. Strengthening begins at 3 months; return to heavy labor/sport at 4–6 months, with counseling that residual stiffness and long-term SLAC-pattern arthritis risk remain even with optimal treatment.
