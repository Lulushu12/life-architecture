## Indications

Perilunate dislocation is a surgical emergency — definitive treatment is surgical in essentially all cases. A "wrist sprain" with persistent pain and swelling after high-energy trauma should raise suspicion, since up to 25% of perilunate dislocations are missed on the initial ED film (look for disrupted Gilula's arcs, "spilled teacup" lunate, capitate overlap).

## Positioning & setup

Emergent closed reduction is performed under sedation. Trans-scaphoid (greater-arc) variants require additional setup for scaphoid screw fixation alongside ligament repair.

## Steps

1. **Emergent closed reduction**: traction-countertraction with thumb pressure on the lunate, following the reverse Mayfield sequence, under sedation — performed immediately to decompress the median nerve and skin regardless of the eventual definitive plan, followed by splinting.
2. **Definitive open repair**, commonly performed within the first 24–96 hours to ~2 weeks (earlier repair is generally associated with better outcomes): combined dorsal (primary) ± volar approach.
3. Dorsal approach: repair/pin the SL and LT ligaments and assess/repair the dorsal capsule.
4. Volar approach (carpal tunnel exposure): added for median nerve decompression, volar ligament (RSC) repair, or when closed reduction fails.
5. Fix with K-wires across the SL and LT joints (± suture anchors for direct ligament repair).
6. For trans-scaphoid (greater-arc) variants, add scaphoid screw fixation to the ligamentous repair.
7. **Missed/chronic (>6 weeks) injury**: open reduction becomes progressively harder; if reduction is not achievable or cartilage damage is already present, proceed directly toward a salvage strategy (PRC/4-corner fusion) rather than persisting with reconstruction.

## Pitfalls

- Delayed decompression risks persistent median nerve neuropathy from acute compression — emergent closed reduction should not be delayed while arranging definitive surgery.
- Even technically adequate, appropriately timed repair is associated with persistent moderate residual disability (grip strength deficits, reduced ROM, later SLAC-pattern arthritis) — patient counseling about long-term prognosis is essential regardless of timing.
- Up to 25% of perilunate dislocations are missed on initial imaging — a normal-seeming "wrist sprain" film after high-energy trauma warrants specific review of Gilula's arcs and the lunate's articular relationships.
- Greater-arc (trans-scaphoid, trans-triquetral, trans-radial styloid) variants require fracture fixation in addition to ligamentous repair; treating them as purely ligamentous injuries under-treats the bony component.

## Post-op protocol

Cast/brace with K-wires in place for 8–10 weeks, followed by wire removal and gradual AROM. Hand therapy focuses on regaining the flexion/extension arc, which is often permanently reduced 20–30% versus the contralateral side. Strengthening begins at 3 months; return to heavy labor/sport at 4–6 months, with counseling that residual stiffness and long-term SLAC-pattern arthritis risk remain even with optimal treatment.
