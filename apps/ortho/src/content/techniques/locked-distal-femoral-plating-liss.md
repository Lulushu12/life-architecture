## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Comminuted metaphyseal (33-A3) and, especially, complex intra-articular fractures (33-C2/C3) where articular reconstruction with lag screws must precede metaphyseal bridging, and where a nail cannot achieve adequate distal fixation because the distal segment is too short or too comminuted.
- Partial articular fractures (33-B1/B2) requiring buttress or neutralization support in addition to lag screw fixation.
- Periprosthetic distal femur fractures with an intact, well-fixed femoral component and adequate distal bone stock for locking screws around the prosthesis.
- Fractures in osteoporotic bone where fixed-angle locking screw fixation is needed to resist the varus-collapse failure mode seen with older non-locking condylar/blade plate designs.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Severe medial column comminution/bone loss without a plan for medial augmentation (bone graft, medial plate, or nail-plate combination) — isolated lateral locked plating alone is at high risk of varus collapse in this scenario.
- Active infection at the planned incision site or along the intended submuscular plate path.
- Fracture patterns better suited to load-sharing fixation with adequate bone stock (simple 33-A/C1 with good metaphyseal purchase available) — a retrograde nail may achieve comparable or superior stability with a smaller footprint and earlier weight bearing in these patterns.
- Vascular injury requiring urgent revascularization — sequence rapid temporary stabilization (external fixation) and vascular repair before a definitive, longer plating procedure.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: dedicated knee AP/lateral/oblique radiographs plus CT with coronal/sagittal reconstruction in essentially all AO/OTA 33-C fractures — CT is considered standard of care preoperatively for intra-articular distal femur fractures and frequently reveals coronal (Hoffa) fragments not visible on plain film, which changes the fixation strategy (need for separate anteroposterior lag screws) in a substantial proportion of cases.
- **Templating**: assess medial column integrity/comminution on CT specifically, since this is the single factor most predictive of whether isolated lateral locked plating will be sufficient or whether a medial-assist strategy is needed. Template plate length (longer plates spanning more of the comminuted zone reduce stress concentration at the plate-bone interface) and screw trajectories for the articular block.
- **Implant selection**: select a lateral distal femoral locking plate/LISS system sized to the patient's anatomy, with an adequate range of locking and non-locking (cortical) screw options, and confirm availability of headless compression screws for anteroposterior fixation of any coronal (Hoffa) fragment. If preoperative planning suggests medial deficiency, have a medial plating set or plan for a combined nail-plate construct available as a backup or primary strategy.
- **Equipment**: radiolucent table, C-arm, the manufacturer's MIPO insertion handle/aiming guide for the chosen plate system, standard lag screw and headless compression screw sets for articular fixation, large and small pointed reduction clamps, and a femoral distractor for provisional length/alignment control before plate application.
- **Case-specific planning**: plan the sequence explicitly — articular reduction and provisional fixation must be completed and confirmed anatomic (≤1-2 mm step-off) before metaphyseal bridging begins, since the plate application step depends on the articular block already being a single, stable unit to which the plate can be fixed.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard; regional block adjuncts (femoral/adductor canal or fascia iliaca block) reduce postoperative opioid requirement, particularly relevant in older patients where minimizing systemic opioid load reduces delirium risk.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the ipsilateral hip if needed for limb rotation, and a bolster or triangle under the distal thigh/knee to allow the knee to rest in slight flexion, which relaxes the gastrocnemius and facilitates reduction of the classic apex-posterior (extension) deformity of the distal fragment.
- **Draping**: wide prep and drape from mid-thigh to well below the knee, keeping the limb free to allow intraoperative flexion/extension for both articular exposure and submuscular plate passage.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm for true AP and lateral imaging of the knee and distal femur before draping; confirm the image can capture both the articular reduction and the full length of the planned submuscular plate simultaneously or with minimal repositioning.
- **Landmarks**: mark the lateral epicondyle, the joint line, and the planned distal (articular-adjacent) and proximal (submuscular insertion) incision sites before prepping — the MIPO technique uses two limited incisions rather than a single extensile exposure.
- **Tourniquet**: used selectively; many surgeons apply a high thigh tourniquet for the articular reduction portion of the case and release it before or during plate insertion/screw placement to confirm adequate perfusion and avoid prolonged inflation times in a case that can run long.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach the articular surface** through a limited lateral parapatellar or direct lateral incision centered over the distal fracture, sufficient to visualize and reduce the articular block without extensile exposure of the metaphyseal comminution zone.
2. **Reduce the articular fragments anatomically**, using pointed reduction clamps and provisional K-wires, restoring the joint surface to ≤1-2 mm step-off — this must be completed and confirmed (fluoroscopically and, where feasible, by direct visualization) before proceeding to metaphyseal bridging, since all subsequent steps depend on the articular block behaving as a single stable unit.
3. **Provisionally and then definitively fix the articular block** with lag screws (interfragmentary or headless compression screws for coronal/Hoffa fragments, placed anteroposterior and buried beneath the cartilage surface), converting the articular fracture into effectively a single "distal fragment" that the plate will subsequently span to the shaft.
4. **Create a separate, limited proximal incision** along the lateral thigh, well proximal to the fracture, for submuscular plate insertion — do not connect this to the distal incision with a continuous extensile dissection, since the biology-preserving benefit of MIPO depends on maintaining an intact soft-tissue/periosteal bridge over the comminuted metaphyseal zone.
5. **Pass the plate submuscularly** from proximal to distal (or distal to proximal depending on system/preference) deep to the vastus lateralis, using the manufacturer's insertion handle to control plate trajectory and rotation as it slides along the lateral femoral cortex.
6. **Confirm plate position on the distal femur** — the plate should lie flat against the lateral condyle. On the AP fluoroscopic view, a guide pin placed through the aiming sleeve of the insertion handle should be parallel to the tibiofemoral joint line; if there is any doubt about proximal plate position (a common challenge with the minimally invasive technique, where even fluoroscopy does not guarantee optimal position), enlarge the proximal incision slightly and palpate the plate's upper rim directly with a finger on the anterior femoral cortex to confirm it is not rotated or translated.
7. **Provisionally pin or clamp the plate** to both the articular block and the proximal shaft, then reduce the metaphyseal comminution indirectly — restore length, rotation, and coronal/sagittal alignment using the plate itself as a reduction tool (with a femoral distractor or a pointed reduction clamp through a stab incision if needed) rather than direct exposure and manipulation of each comminuted fragment.
8. **Place distal locking screws first** into the articular block through the plate, using fixed-angle locking screws to achieve multiplanar distal fixation — placing distal screws before proximal screws allows fine-tuning of plate position relative to the joint before the construct is "locked" to the shaft.
9. **Reassess coronal and sagittal alignment** on fluoroscopy after distal screw placement, and correct any residual varus/valgus or extension/flexion malalignment of the distal fragment before proceeding proximally — this is the last convenient opportunity to adjust alignment before the plate is rigidly fixed to the shaft.
10. **Place proximal screws** through the submuscular incision (typically a mix of locking and non-locking screws, per the working length/bridging-plate biomechanics principle — leaving some empty holes near the fracture to create a longer working length reduces stress concentration and can favor a more flexible, callus-permissive construct).
11. **Assess the medial column** directly (via the CT already obtained, and intraoperatively if any medial exposure is used) — if a deficient medial buttress is present or becomes apparent, proceed to a medial-assist construct (medial plate placed through a separate medial incision, or conversion/augmentation to a nail-plate combination) or bone-graft the medial defect rather than relying on the lateral plate alone.
12. **Final fluoroscopic check** of articular reduction, plate position, screw length (confirm no intra-articular screw penetration), and overall coronal/sagittal/rotational alignment compared to the contralateral limb.
13. **Release the tourniquet (if used) and achieve hemostasis**, then close both incisions in layers; a drain is optional per surgeon preference.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Insert the proximal-most guidewire (used to confirm plate trajectory) through one cortex only rather than bicortically — this reduces the risk of guidewire breakage during subsequent manipulation and plate passage.
- Verify plate position is neither too posterior nor too anterior on the lateral condyle before final proximal fixation — a plate placed too posteriorly is the classic cause of the "golf club" deformity, where over-medialization of the articular block at the proximal fixation stage produces varus malalignment and rotational deformity, increasing nonunion risk; when in doubt, enlarge the proximal incision and palpate the plate's position directly rather than relying on fluoroscopy alone.
- Complete and confirm anatomic articular reduction before starting metaphyseal bridging — sequence matters, and metaphyseal bridging performed around a still-mobile or malreduced articular block will not self-correct.
- Use the plate itself, together with a femoral distractor or provisional clamp, as the primary reduction tool for the metaphyseal segment — resist the temptation to expose and directly manipulate every comminuted fragment, which sacrifices the biology-preserving advantage of the MIPO technique.
- Deliberately leave a longer working length near the fracture (skip a hole or two immediately adjacent to the comminution) rather than clustering all screws tightly around the fracture site — a longer working length distributes strain more favorably for callus formation in a comminuted bridging construct.
- Actively assess and address the medial column at the time of index surgery rather than waiting for varus collapse to declare itself on follow-up imaging — medial deficiency identified preoperatively or intraoperatively should prompt an augmentation strategy up front.
- Flex the knee over a bolster during the case to relax the gastrocnemius pull on the distal fragment, which otherwise contributes to the classic apex-posterior (extension) deformity and makes sagittal-plane reduction more difficult.
- Consider releasing the tourniquet before final proximal screw tightening in a long case to confirm perfusion and avoid an unnecessarily prolonged inflation time.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Medial column comminution/void is the Achilles heel of isolated lateral locked plating — the main technical risk is varus collapse with medial deficiency and malunion in comminuted patterns.
- The "golf club" deformity — distal plate placed too posteriorly, over-medializing the articular block when the proximal plate is secured to the shaft, producing varus malalignment and rotational deformity and increasing nonunion risk; prevent by confirming plate position both fluoroscopically and, when uncertain, by direct palpation through an enlarged proximal incision.
- Confirming correct plate position proximally is genuinely challenging with the minimally invasive technique, and even routine use of an image intensifier does not guarantee an optimal position — build in a low threshold to open the proximal incision further for direct confirmation.
- Older non-locking condylar/blade plates are inferior to locking-plate technology in osteoporotic bone.
- Blood loss from open plating of comminuted patterns is generally higher than for retrograde nailing, a practical factor favoring nailing when the fracture pattern allows either option.
- Heterotopic ossification risk is higher with extensive quadriceps/vastus lateralis dissection — the MIPO approach reduces this risk relative to extensile open approaches.
- Intra-articular screw penetration from a distal locking screw placed without careful attention to trajectory and length, particularly in the coronal-plane (Hoffa) fixation — confirm screw length and position on multiple fluoroscopic views before final tightening.
- Missed coronal (Hoffa) fragments on plain film alone — CT is required to reliably detect these and plan the necessary anteroposterior lag/headless compression screw fixation.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0-2 weeks**: knee ROM started early (0-90°) when fixation stability allows, to prevent arthrofibrosis. Comminuted metaphyseal patterns fixed with a single lateral locked plate are often kept touch-down/toe-touch weight bearing (10-15 kg) for 6-8 weeks given documented varus-collapse risk with early loading of comminuted, medially-deficient constructs.
- **2-6 weeks**: progressive knee flexion toward 90-110°, quadriceps activation, patellar mobilization; continue protected weight bearing per construct until radiographic healing progression.
- **6-12 weeks**: progress weight bearing to full as callus/healing progresses on serial radiographs, typically begun around 6-8 weeks for comminuted patterns; ROM progression toward ≥110-120° flexion.
- **3-6 months**: full weight bearing, strengthening progression, gait normalization. Nonunion is more frequent after plating of severely comminuted patterns without adequate medial support (reported 10-17% in some nail-vs-plate comparative cohorts, higher for plate-only constructs with medial deficiency).
- **Return to work**: sedentary 6-10 weeks; manual labor 4-6+ months, contingent on union and functional knee ROM/strength.
- **Return to sport**: high-demand/pivoting sport generally deferred 6-9 months, contingent on radiographic union, painless full ROM, and quadriceps/hamstring strength ≥85-90% of the contralateral limb.
