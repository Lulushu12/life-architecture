## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Pathologic fractures (nail adds prophylactic fixation length along the entire shaft).
- Segmental fractures where plating both fracture lines would require extensive exposure and soft-tissue morbidity.
- Polytrauma patients, where faster fixation, less blood loss, and earlier mobilization are prioritized over the marginal biomechanical advantages of plating.
- Select proximal-third or very distal-third patterns where plate fixation is technically difficult due to limited working length.
- Simple (AO/OTA 12-A) and wedge (12-B) fracture patterns with adequate bone stock for proximal and distal interlocking.

**Contraindications**:
- Preoperative radial nerve palsy where exploration is independently planned/desired at the time of fixation — nailing does not allow direct nerve visualization (relative contraindication; some surgeons still nail and explore separately if indicated).
- Comminuted/segmental (12-C) patterns without adequate proximal and distal bone for secure locking, where a nail alone cannot control rotation.
- Narrow medullary canal precluding an appropriately sized implant (rare, but relevant in small-stature patients).
- Active local infection at the planned entry site.
- Very proximal or very distal metaphyseal fractures close to the shoulder or elbow joint where locking screw purchase would be inadequate — plating or a hybrid construct is preferred.
- Concomitant glenohumeral pathology (e.g., rotator cuff tear requiring repair) that an antegrade entry portal would aggravate — consider retrograde nailing or plating instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP/lateral humerus radiographs including the shoulder and elbow joints (rule out floating elbow); CT is not routinely required for simple diaphyseal patterns but is useful for segmental/comminuted fractures or pathologic fractures to plan screw trajectory around lesions.
- **Canal templating**: measure medullary canal diameter and length on the injury/contralateral film to select nail diameter and length; confirm the implant system offers proximal locking options (including a spiral-blade or multiplanar option in osteoporotic/pathologic bone) and adequate distal locking options for the chosen entry (antegrade vs. retrograde).
- **Entry choice planning**: decide antegrade versus retrograde before the day of surgery based on fracture location, shoulder pathology, elbow pathology, and polytrauma logistics/positioning constraints (e.g., a patient who cannot be positioned prone/lateral for a retrograde approach).
- **Equipment**: image intensifier (full C-arm, not just mini C-arm, is often preferable for humeral length), radiolucent arm table or hand table, cannulated reaming system if reaming is planned, and both proximal and distal targeting/aiming jigs for the chosen nail system.
- **Radial nerve status**: document a detailed preoperative neurologic exam; if a palsy is already present, decide whether nailing alone (nerve not explored) or a combined/staged approach with nerve exploration is appropriate.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia; regional block (interscalene or supraclavicular) as an adjunct for postoperative analgesia, used cautiously if a baseline nerve exam still needs to be reliably assessed postoperatively.
- **Antegrade nailing position**: modified beach-chair or semi-lateral "sloppy lateral" position with the arm draped free, allowing full adduction of the arm across the chest for humeral head access and fluoroscopic visualization from shoulder to elbow.
- **Retrograde nailing position**: lateral decubitus or prone position with the arm supported on a padded arm holder, elbow flexed, allowing posterior access to the olecranon fossa and full fluoroscopic visualization.
- **Table setup**: radiolucent table/arm board is mandatory; position the C-arm to scroll freely along the entire humeral length without repositioning the patient.
- **Draping**: prep and drape circumferentially from the base of the neck (antegrade) or scapula (retrograde) to the elbow, keeping the entire limb free for manipulation and length/rotation assessment.
- **Tourniquet**: generally not used for humeral shaft surgery (proximal location and need for wide exposure/fluoroscopy make a tourniquet impractical); rely on careful hemostasis instead.
- **Landmarks**: mark the acromion and planned superior entry portal (antegrade) or the tip of the olecranon and posterior midline (retrograde) before incision; mark the fracture level as localized by preoperative imaging and confirm with fluoroscopy after positioning.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm entry choice.** Reconfirm antegrade versus retrograde based on fracture pattern, associated shoulder/elbow pathology, and polytrauma logistics.
2. **Antegrade approach and entry.** Make a small incision from the anterolateral acromion extending proximally; split the deltoid in line with its fibers. Identify the rotator cuff interval and incise the supraspinatus tendon in line with its fibers (a longitudinal split, not a transverse detachment) to expose the entry site at or just medial to the apex of the humeral head, in line with the humeral shaft axis and just posterior to the biceps tendon. A straight nail's entry point is intentionally medialized relative to a curved nail's, which helps preserve the supraspinatus footprint by passing through more of the muscular portion rather than the tendon itself — even so, up to roughly a third of humeri have anatomy where some violation of the footprint is difficult to avoid, so counsel patients that some postoperative shoulder discomfort is expected. Place stay sutures in the supraspinatus split edges for later repair.
3. **Antegrade guidewire and reaming.** Pass a guidewire down the canal across the reduced fracture under fluoroscopic control; ream sequentially if the system calls for reaming, monitoring for excessive resistance that may signal off-axis reaming or canal mismatch.
4. **Retrograde approach and entry.** With the arm positioned and the elbow flexed, make a posterior midline incision proximal to the olecranon, split the triceps in line with its fibers, and create the entry portal in the olecranon fossa (typically an oval entry point angled slightly proximally to avoid a stress riser at the thin supracondylar bone) under fluoroscopic guidance — avoid an entry portal that is too distal or too large, which risks propagating a supracondylar fracture.
5. **Reduce the fracture.** Achieve closed or minimally-open reduction of the fracture under fluoroscopy — longitudinal traction, manual manipulation, or a percutaneous reduction tool/joystick as needed; confirm length, alignment, and rotation (compare the contralateral limb's rotational profile if available) before passing the nail across the fracture site.
6. **Pass the nail.** Advance the nail across the reduced fracture site under continuous fluoroscopic guidance, watching for iatrogenic fracture propagation or comminution at both the entry portal and the fracture site itself — advance gently, back out and reassess trajectory if unexpected resistance is encountered rather than forcing the implant.
7. **Proximal locking.** Place proximal interlocking screws via the targeting jig, using multiplanar locking options (including calcar/oblique screws in proximal-third fractures or osteoporotic bone) to maximize proximal fragment purchase.
8. **Distal locking.** Place distal interlocking screws, generally free-hand under fluoroscopic "perfect circle" technique for antegrade nails or via a jig for retrograde systems; ensure at least two distal locking screws for segmental/comminuted patterns to control rotation.
9. **Confirm reduction and hardware position.** Obtain final AP and lateral fluoroscopic images of the entire humerus, confirming acceptable alignment (see functional bracing thresholds: <20° varus/valgus, <20° AP angulation, <3 cm shortening, <30° malrotation as general benchmarks even for operative fixation), nail seated appropriately proximally and distally, and no screw malposition.
10. **Repair the entry portal.** For antegrade nailing, repair the supraspinatus split side-to-side with the previously placed stay sutures; for retrograde nailing, repair the triceps split.
11. **Layered closure.** Close deltoid fascia (antegrade) or triceps fascia (retrograde), subcutaneous tissue, and skin in standard fashion.
12. **Postoperative neurovascular check.** Document a detailed radial nerve exam in the recovery room and compare against the preoperative baseline.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- For antegrade entry, incise the supraspinatus in line with its fibers and place secure stay sutures before splitting — this makes a clean, reliable side-to-side repair possible at closure and measurably reduces postoperative cuff-related shoulder pain.
- Use the medialized "straight nail" entry point (in line with the humeral shaft axis, just posterior to the biceps tendon, at the apex of the head) rather than a laterally-biased entry — this passes through more of the supraspinatus muscle belly and less of its tendon footprint.
- If canal reaming meets unexpected resistance, stop and re-check guidewire position and trajectory on fluoroscopy before continuing — forcing a reamer through a malpositioned or off-axis guidewire is a common mechanism of iatrogenic fracture propagation.
- For retrograde entry, keep the olecranon fossa portal oval rather than round and avoid over-widening it — a portal that is too large or too distal measurably increases supracondylar fracture risk at the entry site.
- Use longitudinal traction and a folded bump under the fracture site to assist closed reduction before nail passage; percutaneous reduction clamps or a joystick pin can convert a difficult closed reduction into a controlled one without opening the fracture.
- Always confirm rotational alignment against the contralateral limb (or clinical landmarks such as the epicondylar axis relative to the shoulder) — rotational malunion is easy to miss on AP/lateral fluoroscopy alone since it does not show up as an obvious angular deformity.
- For pathologic or very osteoporotic bone, favor a nail system with multiplanar or spiral-blade proximal locking options — standard single-plane locking screws are more prone to cut-out/toggle in weak bone.
- Use at least two distal interlocking screws in segmental or comminuted (12-C) patterns to control rotation; a single distal screw is inadequate for these unstable patterns even with a well-seated nail.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Antegrade nailing: shoulder pain is the most common patient-reported complaint post-nailing (reported in up to 10–20%), driven largely by rotator cuff footprint violation at the entry portal — a medialized entry point and meticulous supraspinatus repair mitigate but do not eliminate this.
- Retrograde nailing: risk of supracondylar fracture propagation at the entry site (portal too large/too distal) and postoperative elbow stiffness from the posterior triceps-splitting approach and proximity to the joint.
- The radial nerve is not directly visualized/exposed with nailing, which lowers but does not eliminate iatrogenic palsy risk from reaming/insertion, particularly at the fracture site during closed reduction maneuvers.
- Iatrogenic fracture propagation/comminution at the entry portal or along the shaft during nail passage is a technique-dependent risk — avoid forcing the nail or reamer through resistance without reassessing trajectory.
- Recent comparative literature (2023–2025) is mixed: some registry/propensity-matched series show higher nonunion with plating versus nailing, contradicting older teaching; no single implant is unequivocally superior — individualize to fracture morphology (segmental/comminuted favors plate for length/rotation control unless polytrauma logistics favor nail), surgeon experience, and whether radial nerve exploration is independently indicated.
- Rotational malunion is under-recognized intraoperatively because standard AP/lateral fluoroscopy does not reliably show rotation — always cross-check against the contralateral limb.
- Distal interlocking with a single screw in an unstable (segmental/comminuted) pattern risks loss of rotational control and nonunion; use at least two distal screws in these patterns.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Elbow, wrist, hand motion from day 0; shoulder pendulum exercises early.
- Active shoulder/elbow ROM progressed as fracture stability allows.
- Resisted strengthening deferred until union confirmed (radiographic union expected by 12–16 wk).
- Return to heavy lifting/contact sport typically 4–6 months.
