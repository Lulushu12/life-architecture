## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- **Weil osteotomy**: isolated or multi-ray lesser metatarsal overload (metatarsalgia), and concurrent MTP subluxation/dislocation (crossover toe) to decompress the joint and permit reduction.
- **DMMO (distal metatarsal minimally invasive osteotomy)**: percutaneous extra-articular alternative to open Weil for appropriately selected multi-ray overload, when less soft-tissue morbidity is prioritized over precise control of final metatarsal position.
- **Direct plantar plate repair**: Coughlin/Nery grade II–III plantar plate tears with instability persisting despite conservative care; grade IV tears combined with fixed MTP dislocation.
- **Rigid hammer/claw/mallet toe correction** (PIP resection arthroplasty or arthrodesis) is added when a fixed lesser toe deformity accompanies the MTP-level pathology.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Isolated flexible lesser toe deformity without MTP-level pathology — this should be managed with soft-tissue procedures (flexor tenotomy, Girdlestone-Taylor transfer) alone, not a Weil osteotomy.
- Vascular insufficiency of the toe, given the risk that aggressive soft-tissue release combined with an osteotomy can compromise a marginal blood supply.
- Active infection or an inadequate dorsal soft-tissue envelope.
- Attempting to correct an overloaded ray in isolation without assessing the overall metatarsal parabola — operating on one ray without reference to its neighbors risks simply transferring the overload problem laterally.
- DMMO in cases requiring precise, predictable control of final metatarsal head position (e.g., significant fixed deformity needing exact repositioning) — the "floating" healing process of DMMO gives less positional control than an open, fixed Weil osteotomy.

## Preoperative Planning

- Weight-bearing AP foot radiograph to assess the metatarsal parabola and relative metatarsal protrusion (Maestro method), identifying which ray(s) are relatively long/overloaded and by how much.
- Clinical **MTP dorsal drawer/Lachman test** to grade plantar plate instability, and ultrasound or MRI to formally grade the plantar plate tear (Coughlin/Nery 0–IV) when surgical planning depends on the grade.
- Assess for a positive "V-sign" (gap between toes) suggesting crossover deformity, and document which MTP joint(s) are subluxed or frankly dislocated.
- Plan the amount of shortening needed **per ray relative to its neighbors**, not in isolation — the goal is a smooth, corrected parabola, and over-shortening one ray relative to its neighbors is a direct cause of transfer metatarsalgia.
- Decide fixation strategy for the Weil osteotomy (typically a single cortical screw) and, if PIP-level correction is planned concurrently, whether K-wire, intramedullary implant, or peg-in-hole fixation will be used.
- If plantar plate repair is planned, confirm availability of the specific anchor/suture-passing instrumentation for a dorsal-approach repair, or plan the plantar approach if that is the surgeon's preferred exposure.
- Instrumentation: mini oscillating saw, small phalangeal reduction clamp, single cortical screws (or K-wires for temporary/definitive fixation), and mini C-arm.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Regional (ankle/popliteal block) with sedation, spinal, or general anesthesia.
- Supine, foot free at the end of the table, ipsilateral hip bump for neutral rotation.
- Thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination.
- Mini C-arm positioned for AP and lateral views of the lesser metatarsals/MTP joints — confirm the specific ray of interest is clearly visualized without excessive overlap from adjacent rays.
- Mark the dorsal longitudinal incision(s) over the affected ray(s) for open Weil/plantar plate repair, or the percutaneous portal position for DMMO; mark all rays planned for surgery preoperatively to avoid wrong-level confusion when multiple rays are addressed in one setting.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach (open Weil/plantar plate repair)**: make a dorsal longitudinal incision over the affected MTP joint, between the extensor tendons; retract the extensor tendon(s), incise the MTP capsule, and expose the metatarsal head and neck.
2. **Weil osteotomy — cut orientation**: orient the saw cut from dorsal-distal to plantar-proximal along the metatarsal shaft, angled roughly 30° so the cut plane runs nearly parallel to the weight-bearing plantar surface — this orientation is what allows the capital fragment to translate proximally along the cut without plantarflexing.
3. **Weil osteotomy — cut and translate**: perform the osteotomy, then slide the metatarsal head fragment proximally along the osteotomy surface by the planned amount, respecting the overall metatarsal parabola rather than the symptomatic ray in isolation. **Limit shortening to the minimum needed for symptom relief** — over-shortening (generally regarded as >3–4 mm beyond what is needed) is a leading cause of floating toe.
4. **Weil osteotomy — provisional fixation**: use a K-wire placed as a "joystick" through the phalanx/metatarsal head to control rotation and confirm congruent joint tracking as the fragment is provisionally held in position; a phalangeal reduction clamp can maintain compression at the osteotomy site and prevent inadvertent distraction while fixation is placed. Two-pin (or pin-plus-clamp) provisional control, rather than relying on a single point of fixation, limits unwanted rotation of the metatarsal head as the definitive screw is tightened.
5. **Weil osteotomy — definitive fixation**: place a single cortical screw across the osteotomy, confirming position on fluoroscopy; remove provisional K-wires once definitive fixation is secure.
6. **DMMO alternative**: through a small percutaneous portal, perform a percutaneous extra-articular osteotomy of the metatarsal neck with a burr under fluoroscopic guidance, without formal internal fixation in most techniques — the metatarsal head is allowed to "float" into a corrected position, stabilized by the surrounding soft-tissue envelope and periosteum, and heals by callus formation over a longer time course than a fixed, open osteotomy.
7. **Plantar plate repair — dorsal approach**: after the Weil osteotomy has decompressed the joint and provided access, retract the capital fragment to expose the plantar plate from above; identify the tear pattern, pass sutures through the plate edges (with an anchor in the phalangeal base or transosseous tunnels), and repair before reducing and fixing the osteotomy, or repair after fixation depending on surgeon sequence preference and exposure.
8. **Plantar plate repair — plantar approach (alternative)**: through a plantar longitudinal incision directly over the involved MTP joint, expose and repair the plantar plate under direct visualization without needing to work through the dorsal osteotomy exposure; this affords excellent direct access at the cost of a weight-bearing scar.
9. **Rigid toe correction (when concurrent)**: through a separate dorsal incision over the PIP joint, resect the head/condyles of the proximal phalanx (resection arthroplasty) or prepare both surfaces for formal arthrodesis; fix with a K-wire, intramedullary implant, or peg-in-hole technique, adding extensor tenotomy/capsulotomy if needed to correct residual MTP extension contracture.
10. **Flexible deformity (when isolated, no MTP-level pathology)**: for flexible hammer toe, perform a flexor-to-extensor tendon transfer (Girdlestone-Taylor) or simple flexor tenotomy; for flexible claw toe, perform an extensor tenotomy/dorsal MTP capsulotomy ± flexor tenotomy.
11. Take the MTP joint through passive motion to confirm the toe sits in a plantigrade, non-subluxed position without excessive tension on the repair before final fixation of any K-wire used for toe positioning.
12. If a K-wire is used to hold the toe in a corrected, slightly plantarflexed position postoperatively, place it now and plan for removal in clinic typically at 3–4 weeks.
13. Irrigate, close the capsule and extensor mechanism, then skin, and apply a compressive dressing with a dorsal splint component holding the toe plantigrade — this splinting detail specifically helps prevent early dorsal contracture of the toe during healing.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Keep the Weil osteotomy cut as close to parallel to the weight-bearing surface as the ~30° dorsal-distal-to-plantar-proximal orientation allows — a cut that is too oblique is a specific, described mechanism for plantar displacement of the capital fragment and subsequent floating toe.
- Use a K-wire as a joystick through the metatarsal head/phalanx during reduction — beyond controlling rotation, it also serves as a visual landmark for judging exactly how far the capital fragment has been shifted plantarward/proximally, which is otherwise difficult to judge by eye.
- Limit shortening strictly to what is needed for decompression; when in doubt, shorten less rather than more, since under-correction is far easier to revise than a floating toe from over-shortening.
- Use two points of provisional fixation control (K-wire plus a phalangeal reduction clamp, or two pins) rather than a single pin before placing the definitive screw — this limits the metatarsal head rotating under torque as the screw is tightened, a subtle error that can silently degrade the correction.
- Add extensor tendon lengthening or a flexor-to-extensor transfer at the same setting when the MTP has significant preoperative extension contracture — this proactively protects flexion power and reduces floating-toe incidence rather than treating it only if it develops.
- Splint the toe in mild plantarflexion for the first 2–3 weeks (dressing or dorsal splint) regardless of the specific fixation used — this is a simple, low-cost measure with a described role in preventing dorsal contracture and floating toe.
- Start aggressive, early postoperative toe ROM exercises and consider taping the toe in a plantarflexed position during recovery — both are described strategies to further reduce the incidence of floating toe.
- Always plan the shortening of the symptomatic ray against its neighbors on the preoperative radiograph, not in isolation — the target is a smooth parabola, not simply relieving the index ray's overload at the expense of creating a new one next door.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Weil osteotomy carries a well-recognized risk of **floating toe deformity** (loss of plantar flexion power/MTP subluxation) if the metatarsal is **over-shortened**, if the cut is **too oblique**, if the osteotomy is started **too proximally**, or if the plantar plate/flexor balance is not respected.
- DMMO has a **significantly longer time to bony healing** than open Weil (2024 meta-analysis), while Weil has more wound problems and MTP stiffness — the two are roughly equally effective (AOFAS/VAS) but trade different complication profiles.
- **Simple soft-tissue balancing without addressing plate incompetence has a high recurrence rate** for higher-grade (II–III/IV) plantar plate tears — do not substitute balancing alone when a formal repair is indicated.
- **Transfer metatarsalgia to adjacent (usually more lateral) rays** occurs if a single ray is overcorrected relative to its neighbors — plan against the full metatarsal parabola, not the symptomatic ray in isolation.
- Pin-tract infection is a recognized risk with K-wire fixation of PIP correction; recurrent deformity results from undercorrection or failure to address MTP-level pathology.
- Vascular compromise of the toe can result from overly aggressive soft-tissue release in a toe with borderline perfusion.
- Screw rotation of the capital fragment during tightening (if provisional fixation is inadequate) can silently lose the intended, carefully judged correction.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: postoperative shoe; K-wire (if used for PIP fixation) protected until removal, typically **3–4 weeks**; weight-bear as tolerated with attention to keeping load off the operated ray where a Weil osteotomy has been performed.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing; buddy-taping of the toe once the K-wire is removed to maintain alignment during early healing; gentle ROM exercises.
- **Weeks 6–8**: transition to regular shoes as swelling allows; strengthening and gait normalization.
- **Return to sport**: typically **8–12 weeks**, longer if plantar plate repair was performed given the need for soft-tissue healing under tension.
