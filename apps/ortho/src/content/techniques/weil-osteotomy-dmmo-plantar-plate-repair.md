## Indications

- **Weil osteotomy**: isolated or multi-ray lesser metatarsal overload (metatarsalgia), and concurrent MTP subluxation/dislocation (crossover toe) to decompress the joint and permit reduction.
- **DMMO (distal metatarsal minimally invasive osteotomy)**: percutaneous extra-articular alternative to open Weil for appropriately selected multi-ray overload, when less soft-tissue morbidity is prioritized over precise control of final metatarsal position.
- **Direct plantar plate repair**: Coughlin/Nery grade II–III plantar plate tears with instability persisting despite conservative care; grade IV tears combined with fixed MTP dislocation.
- **Rigid hammer/claw/mallet toe correction** (PIP resection arthroplasty or arthrodesis) is added when a fixed lesser toe deformity accompanies the MTP-level pathology.

## Positioning & setup

Dorsal approach to the affected lesser MTP joint(s) for open Weil and plantar plate repair; percutaneous stab approach for DMMO. Preoperative plantar plate grading (Coughlin/Nery, via exam — MTP dorsal drawer/Lachman test — and ultrasound/MRI) and assessment of the overall metatarsal parabola (Maestro method) to avoid overcorrecting one ray relative to its neighbors.

## Steps

1. **Weil osteotomy**: oblique intra-articular shortening ± elevating osteotomy of the metatarsal neck, decompressing the overloaded MTP joint. Respect the overall metatarsal parabola rather than planning the cut in isolation.
2. **DMMO alternative**: percutaneous extra-articular osteotomy without formal fixation in most techniques — the metatarsal head is allowed to "float" into a corrected position and heal by callus formation.
3. **Plantar plate repair**: for grade II–III tears, repair via a dorsal MTP approach, typically with an implant fixed by anchor or transosseous suture; a direct plantar-approach repair is an alternative, better for visualization but with a weight-bearing scar.
4. **Rigid toe correction (when concurrent)**: PIP joint resection arthroplasty (removal of the proximal phalanx condyles) or formal PIP arthrodesis, fixed with K-wire, intramedullary implant, or peg-in-hole technique ± extensor tenotomy/capsulotomy.
5. **Flexible deformity (when isolated, no MTP-level pathology)**: flexor-to-extensor tendon transfer (Girdlestone-Taylor) or simple flexor tenotomy for flexible hammer toe; extensor tenotomy/dorsal MTP capsulotomy ± flexor tenotomy for flexible claw toe.

## Pitfalls

- Weil osteotomy carries a well-recognized risk of **floating toe deformity** (loss of plantar flexion power/MTP subluxation) if the metatarsal is **over-shortened** or if the plantar plate/flexor balance is not respected.
- DMMO has a **significantly longer time to bony healing** than open Weil (2024 meta-analysis), while Weil has more wound problems and MTP stiffness — the two are roughly equally effective (AOFAS/VAS) but trade different complication profiles.
- **Simple soft-tissue balancing without addressing plate incompetence has a high recurrence rate** for higher-grade (II–III/IV) plantar plate tears — do not substitute balancing alone when a formal repair is indicated.
- **Transfer metatarsalgia to adjacent (usually more lateral) rays** occurs if a single ray is overcorrected relative to its neighbors — plan against the full metatarsal parabola, not the symptomatic ray in isolation.
- Pin-tract infection is a recognized risk with K-wire fixation of PIP correction; recurrent deformity results from undercorrection or failure to address MTP-level pathology.
- Vascular compromise of the toe can result from overly aggressive soft-tissue release in a toe with borderline perfusion.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: postoperative shoe; K-wire (if used for PIP fixation) protected until removal, typically **3–4 weeks**; weight-bear as tolerated with attention to keeping load off the operated ray where a Weil osteotomy has been performed.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing; buddy-taping of the toe once the K-wire is removed to maintain alignment during early healing; gentle ROM exercises.
- **Weeks 6–8**: transition to regular shoes as swelling allows; strengthening and gait normalization.
- **Return to sport**: typically **8–12 weeks**, longer if plantar plate repair was performed given the need for soft-tissue healing under tension.
