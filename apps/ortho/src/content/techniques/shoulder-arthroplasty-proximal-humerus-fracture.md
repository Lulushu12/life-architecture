## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Head-splitting or otherwise unreconstructable proximal humerus fracture patterns (severe comminution, disrupted medial hinge/calcar) not amenable to ORIF.
- Displaced 3-/4-part fractures in patients >65–70 years, particularly valgus-impacted or head-split variants with comminution, where rTSA has increasingly become primary (not salvage) treatment.
- Fracture-dislocations with unreconstructable head fragments or significant articular impaction.
- Hemiarthroplasty remains a reasonable option specifically in younger patients with an intact rotator cuff and glenoid and an unreconstructable head, where preserving native glenoid bone stock and avoiding baseplate-related complications is prioritized.
- Failed ORIF with collapse, screw penetration, or established AVN causing painful, unreconstructable joint destruction (salvage rTSA).

**Contraindications**:
- Reconstructable fracture patterns in younger, higher-demand patients — ORIF is preferred to preserve native anatomy and avoid the long-term wear/loosening considerations of arthroplasty.
- Active local or systemic infection.
- Axillary nerve palsy with no evidence of recovery potential and a glenoid/deltoid insufficient to support a reverse construct (relative contraindication — assess deltoid function carefully, since rTSA is uniquely deltoid-dependent).
- Glenoid bone loss or deficiency too severe to support stable baseplate fixation without staged bone grafting.
- Very young, high-demand patients for whom the long-term durability of any shoulder arthroplasty (anatomic or reverse) is a significant concern — exhaust reconstructive (ORIF) options first.
- Medical comorbidity precluding a longer arthroplasty procedure when a shorter fixation procedure would suffice.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: true AP (Grashey), scapular-Y, and axillary lateral views; CT with 2D/3D reconstruction is essentially routine for fracture arthroplasty planning — assess glenoid bone stock and version (relevant to baseplate planning for rTSA), tuberosity fragment size/comminution, and humeral canal diameter for stem sizing.
- **Implant selection**: choose hemiarthroplasty versus rTSA based on age, rotator cuff/glenoid status, and tuberosity reconstructability (see Indications); template humeral stem size/height and, for rTSA, glenosphere size and lateralization/inferior offset options against the CT-based glenoid assessment. Favor a fracture-specific stem with dedicated suture holes/fins for tuberosity fixation.
- **Fixation strategy planning**: decide in advance whether the stem will be cemented or press-fit (uncemented, metaphyseal-fixation stems have shown good outcomes even with calcar involvement in comparative series) based on bone quality and canal fit; plan the specific tuberosity cerclage technique (see Technical Tips) before the case, since conventional four/five-suture cerclage healing rates in the literature run only around 60–70% and technique-specific improvements are meaningful.
- **Concomitant injury/soft-tissue assessment**: confirm rotator cuff integrity on preoperative imaging where feasible, and document axillary nerve function preoperatively (motor and sensory) as a baseline, since deltoid/axillary nerve function is the dominant determinant of rTSA outcome.
- **Equipment**: full arthroplasty instrument trays (trial and definitive stems, broaches/reamers, glenoid reaming/baseplate instrumentation for rTSA), heavy non-absorbable suture or suture tape in quantity for tuberosity cerclage, and — if using a tensioning device for cerclage — confirm availability, since tensioned cerclage constructs have shown improved biomechanical stability over manual knotting.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically combined with an interscalene regional block for intraoperative muscle relaxation and postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: beach-chair position, torso elevated 30–60°, head secured in a padded headrest, operative shoulder brought to the edge of the table to allow full arm excursion, adduction/extension for glenoid exposure (rTSA), and axillary fluoroscopic imaging if used.
- **Table setup**: a mechanical arm positioner or sterile arm holder is useful, particularly for rTSA glenoid exposure, which requires sustained arm positioning (adduction, extension, external rotation) that is fatiguing for an assistant to hold manually through a long case.
- **Draping**: drape widely from the base of the neck to distal humerus, keeping the arm free for full range of motion testing and stem trialing.
- **Tourniquet**: not used (proximal location).
- **Landmarks**: mark the coracoid, acromion, and deltopectoral groove; mark the planned deltopectoral incision before prepping.
- **Fluoroscopy**: available but used more selectively than in ORIF — primarily to confirm stem height/version if landmarks are distorted by fracture comminution, and to confirm final tuberosity position.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach.** Standard deltopectoral incision and interval, identifying and protecting the cephalic vein (retracted consistently medially or laterally) and the axillary nerve, which is at risk throughout dissection and retraction, particularly with inferior capsular release and during humeral head delivery/extraction.
2. **Identify and tag the tuberosities.** Before removing the humeral head fragment, place heavy non-absorbable traction sutures through the tendon-bone junction of the greater tuberosity (supraspinatus/infraspinatus) and lesser tuberosity (subscapularis) — these sutures are essential both for controlling the fragments during exposure and for the final cerclage repair, and are far easier to place before the fragments are further destabilized by head removal.
3. **Remove the humeral head fragment(s).** Deliver and remove the unreconstructable head fragment(s), preserving the tuberosities on their soft-tissue (rotator cuff) attachments as intact as possible rather than freeing them completely from surrounding muscle.
4. **Prepare the humeral canal.** Broach/ream the humeral canal to the templated size, referencing height and version off the remaining proximal humeral anatomy (the pectoralis major insertion is a commonly used version landmark — roughly 20–30° of retroversion relative to the forearm axis, and roughly level with or slightly proximal to the humeral head articular surface for height) since normal proximal landmarks (calcar, tuberosities) are disrupted by the fracture itself.
5. **Trial reduction and height/version check.** Perform a trial reduction, assessing soft-tissue tension (a gentle "70/30" or similar rule of thumb balancing stability against overstuffing), passive range of motion, and — for rTSA — deltoid tension and stability through a full arc of motion before committing to final implants.
6. **For rTSA — prepare and fix the glenoid.** Expose the glenoid (posterior capsular release, humeral head/proximal shaft retraction), ream to a corrected version/inclination as templated, and place the baseplate with peripheral screw fixation into the best available bone (typically directing at least one screw toward the base of the coracoid/scapular pillar for superior fixation strength). Impact the glenosphere, choosing a lateralized and/or inferior-offset position to reduce the risk of scapular notching.
7. **Seat the definitive humeral stem.** Cement or press-fit the definitive stem at the templated height and version, incorporating the tuberosities' previously placed sutures through dedicated suture holes/fins on a fracture-specific stem design where available.
8. **Reduce the construct.** Reduce the humeral component onto the glenosphere (rTSA) or native/reamed glenoid (hemiarthroplasty), and reassess stability, ROM, and deltoid tension.
9. **Reduce and fix the tuberosities.** Reposition the greater and lesser tuberosities anatomically around the stem, restoring bone-to-bone contact with the shaft and with each other where possible (a medial cerclage between the greater and lesser tuberosities, in addition to sutures through the stem, meaningfully improves rotational and vertical stability of the construct per contemporary cerclage techniques). Pass and tension sutures: through the stem's dedicated fixation points, as a medial cerclage between the two tuberosities, and through drill holes in the proximal shaft for a vertical tension-band effect — tie sequentially, checking that the tuberosities sit reduced against the stem/shaft without gapping, ideally using a suture-tensioning device rather than manual knot tension alone if available, since tensioned constructs have shown improved biomechanical stability over hand-tied knots.
10. **Assess final stability and tuberosity position.** Passively range the shoulder through internal/external rotation and forward flexion/abduction to confirm the tuberosity repair holds without gapping and the joint (or reverse construct) is stable through the achievable arc; obtain fluoroscopy if tuberosity position or stem height/version is in question.
11. **Irrigate and achieve hemostasis.** Copious irrigation and meticulous hemostasis, particularly given the difficulty of managing infection after shoulder arthroplasty if it later develops.
12. **Layered closure.** Repair the subscapularis (if taken down) or confirm the interval is intact, close the deltopectoral interval, subcutaneous tissue, and skin; consider a drain per surgeon preference.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Tag both tuberosities with heavy suture through the tendon-bone junction before removing the head fragment — waiting until after head removal makes fragment control and later cerclage passage significantly harder.
- Restore a medial cerclage directly between the greater and lesser tuberosities (not just each tuberosity separately to the stem/shaft) — contemporary biomechanical and clinical data show this added rotational/vertical stability meaningfully improves tuberosity healing rates compared with stem-only suture fixation, which historically achieves tuberosity healing in only around 60–70% of cases with conventional technique.
- Use a suture-tensioning device for the cerclage construct where available — tensioned repairs have measurably higher construct stability than manually knotted sutures, and a well-tensioned repair is one of the few technically modifiable factors that reliably improves tuberosity healing.
- Reference stem height and version off the pectoralis major insertion when native proximal landmarks are destroyed by comminution — this is the most reliable intraoperative landmark available in a fracture case, unlike calcar or tuberosity position, which are frequently disrupted.
- For rTSA, lateralize and/or inferiorize the glenosphere position deliberately — this is the single most effective technical lever for reducing scapular notching risk, and is a decision made at baseplate/glenosphere selection, not something correctable afterward.
- Avoid excessive humeral lengthening when setting stem height — overlengthening in pursuit of soft-tissue tension risks axillary nerve traction injury and does not reliably improve outcomes.
- Preserve the tuberosities' soft-tissue (rotator cuff) attachments as intact as possible during head fragment removal — a tuberosity still tethered by its cuff insertion is easier to reduce accurately and has better healing potential than one that has been completely stripped free.
- Consider a fracture-specific stem design with dedicated suture holes/fins rather than a standard elective arthroplasty stem — these are purpose-built to simplify and strengthen the tuberosity cerclage construct.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Hemiarthroplasty functional outcome is unpredictable because it depends on tuberosity healing — a major reason it has been supplanted by rTSA in the elderly; conventional cerclage-only tuberosity fixation achieves anatomic healing in only about 60–70% of cases in the literature, and healed tuberosities correlate directly with better active elevation and external rotation.
- Excessive humeral lengthening risks axillary nerve traction injury.
- Malpositioned (non-lateralized/non-inferior) glenosphere placement increases scapular notching risk.
- Deep infection after proximal humerus arthroplasty is a feared complication given the difficulty of staged revision in this anatomic region.
- Tuberosity malunion, resorption, or nonunion after fracture arthroplasty is a major driver of poor rotational strength and function independent of implant position or stability — this is the dominant technical failure mode specific to fracture arthroplasty (as opposed to elective arthroplasty).
- Manual knot-tensioned cerclage constructs are biomechanically less stable than tensioning-device-assisted constructs — a technique-dependent, correctable factor in tuberosity healing rates.
- Loss of native proximal landmarks (calcar, tuberosities) from comminution makes stem height/version judgment more error-prone than in elective arthroplasty — relying on a single soft-tissue landmark (e.g., pectoralis major insertion alone) without cross-checking trial stability and ROM can still result in a malpositioned stem.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Post-rTSA**: passive/active-assisted ROM from day 1–2 (implant is inherently more stable than anatomic reconstruction); avoid active internal rotation/extension behind the back for 6 wk if subscapularis was repaired; active strengthening from 6 wk; full activity by 3–4 months. Deltoid and rotator cuff (teres minor) strength are the main determinants of final functional outcome, not ROM alone.
- A 2024–2025 multicenter RCT found rTSA superior to locked plate fixation at 2 years for displaced fractures in the elderly, reinforcing the trend toward rTSA as primary (not salvage) treatment in appropriately selected older patients. However, a PLOS One systematic review/meta-analysis (2025, ~10 studies, 244 plate vs 287 rTSA patients) found functional superiority of either treatment remains statistically unresolved — individualize by patient factors rather than assuming a blanket protocol shift.
