## Indications

Head-splitting or unreconstructable proximal humerus fracture patterns. Hemiarthroplasty was historically favored in younger patients with an intact rotator cuff and glenoid. Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) has progressively supplanted it in the elderly (>65–70y) for unreconstructable 3-/4-part and head-splitting patterns, because outcomes are far less dependent on tuberosity healing than hemiarthroplasty.

## Positioning & setup

Deltopectoral approach (standard), protecting the cephalic vein in the deltopectoral interval and the axillary nerve throughout dissection and retraction.

## Steps

1. Approach via the deltopectoral interval.
2. For rTSA, restore the greater tuberosity around the prosthesis when possible — improves external rotation and abduction strength even with a semi-constrained implant.
3. Position the glenosphere lateralized and inferior to reduce the risk of scapular notching.
4. Avoid excessive humeral lengthening, which risks axillary nerve traction injury.
5. For hemiarthroplasty, recognize that final function depends heavily on tuberosity healing, which is unpredictable, especially in osteoporotic, comminuted tuberosities.

## Pitfalls

- Hemiarthroplasty functional outcome is unpredictable because it depends on tuberosity healing — a major reason it has been supplanted by rTSA in the elderly.
- Excessive humeral lengthening risks axillary nerve traction injury.
- Malpositioned (non-lateralized/non-inferior) glenosphere placement increases scapular notching risk.
- Deep infection after proximal humerus arthroplasty is a feared complication given the difficulty of staged revision in this anatomic region.

## Post-op protocol

- **Post-rTSA**: passive/active-assisted ROM from day 1–2 (implant is inherently more stable than anatomic reconstruction); avoid active internal rotation/extension behind the back for 6 wk if subscapularis was repaired; active strengthening from 6 wk; full activity by 3–4 months. Deltoid and rotator cuff (teres minor) strength are the main determinants of final functional outcome, not ROM alone.
- A 2024–2025 multicenter RCT found rTSA superior to locked plate fixation at 2 years for displaced fractures in the elderly, reinforcing the trend toward rTSA as primary (not salvage) treatment in appropriately selected older patients. However, a PLOS One systematic review/meta-analysis (2025, ~10 studies, 244 plate vs 287 rTSA patients) found functional superiority of either treatment remains statistically unresolved — individualize by patient factors rather than assuming a blanket protocol shift.
