## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Grade III isolated PCL injury with persistent symptomatic instability after an adequate non-operative trial; combined ligament injury (virtually all multiligament knee injury patterns with PCL involvement); bony avulsion with displacement (often amenable to primary fragment fixation instead of ligament reconstruction); and chronic PCL insufficiency with functional limitation or early degenerative change.

**Contraindications**:
- Grade I–II isolated PCL injury, and most isolated Grade III injuries in low-to-moderate demand patients — these are managed first-line with bracing and quadriceps-focused rehabilitation given generally favorable long-term patient-reported outcomes with non-operative treatment.
- Active infection or compromised soft-tissue envelope over planned approach/portal sites.
- Uncorrected varus malalignment with chronic combined PCL/PLC insufficiency — consider staged or combined proximal tibial osteotomy first (or concurrently), since reconstructing the PCL alone into a varus-thrust knee predisposes to graft stretch and failure.
- Fixed flexion contracture or advanced degenerative change limiting expected functional benefit — manage expectations and consider whether arthroplasty is a more appropriate eventual pathway in the older, arthritic knee rather than isolated ligament reconstruction.
- Uncontrolled diabetes, active nicotine use, or other factors materially impairing graft incorporation should be optimized before an elective reconstruction.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to confirm PCL tear location/pattern and assess for combined PLC, ACL, or meniscal injury (a large proportion of "isolated" PCL tears on initial presentation are in fact under-recognized combined injuries). Stress radiographs (Telos device or manual stress views at 90° flexion) to quantify posterior tibial translation objectively and grade laxity (Grade I: 0–5 mm; Grade II: 5–10 mm; Grade III: >10 mm), which helps confirm the operative indication and provides a reproducible baseline for postoperative comparison. Plain radiographs to assess for bony avulsion and overall alignment; obtain a long-leg alignment view if varus malalignment is suspected clinically, given its relevance to graft longevity.
- **Technique selection**:
  - **Single-bundle vs. double-bundle** (anterolateral + posteromedial bundle) reconstruction — double-bundle theoretically better restores both flexion and extension stability but is technically demanding with limited proven clinical superiority over single-bundle technique; most surgeons default to single-bundle.
  - **Transtibial tunnel vs. tibial inlay** technique — transtibial creates an oblique tibial tunnel with a sharp posterior "killer turn" as the graft turns from the tibial tunnel exit into the joint, a stress riser implicated in graft thinning and attritional failure under cyclic loading; the tibial inlay technique (graft/bone block seated directly into a trough at the native tibial PCL footprint via a posterior approach, avoiding the tunnel turn entirely) was developed specifically to eliminate the killer turn, at the cost of a separate posterior approach (and, in the open version, a position change during the case). Arthroscopically-assisted inlay techniques have been described to avoid a full position change. Favor inlay for revision PCL reconstruction or when combined posterior instability makes graft durability a particular concern.
- **Graft selection**: autograft (hamstring, quadriceps tendon, or BPTB) or allograft (Achilles tendon with a bone block is a common choice for inlay technique, given its length and the ability to seat a bone plug in the tibial trough); allograft is frequently favored in combined MLKI cases to limit total donor-site morbidity when multiple grafts are needed in the same sitting.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: standard arthroscopy tower; PCL-specific femoral and tibial aiming guides; a curved tibial guide designed to minimize tunnel obliquity if a transtibial technique is used; graft preparation set including whipstitch suture and an EndoPearl or equivalent graft-end augmentation device to enhance femoral-side soft-tissue graft fixation; interference screws (sized to graft/tunnel) and a suture-anchor/washer set for supplemental tibial fixation; for inlay technique, a dedicated inlay instrument set (slot cutter, bone-plug positioner, impactor) and a posterior retractor set.
- **Case-specific planning**: if a displaced bony avulsion is present, plan primary fragment fixation (screw or suture-anchor technique) as an alternative to soft-tissue graft reconstruction. If combined PLC or posteromedial corner injury is present, plan a single coordinated procedure (or tightly staged sequence) rather than treating the PCL in isolation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, often combined with regional block (adductor canal or popliteal sciatic block, the latter used selectively given the need to monitor for peroneal nerve function postoperatively if combined PLC work is also performed) for postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard or radiolucent table with a leg-holder or lateral post enabling flexion beyond 90°; if an open tibial inlay approach is planned, be prepared to reposition to prone (or lateral decubitus, technique-dependent) for the posterior approach portion of the case, or use an arthroscopically-assisted inlay technique to avoid the position change — confirm and plan this explicitly with the OR team in advance, since an intraoperative position change affects draping, timing, and equipment setup.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh pneumatic tourniquet, padded, limb exsanguinated; use judiciously given case length, particularly if combined procedures are planned.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm available, particularly useful for confirming tunnel trajectory/guide-pin position on the transtibial technique and for confirming inlay bone-plug seating.
- **Exam under anesthesia**: posterior drawer at 90° flexion (graded by tibial step-off relative to the femoral condyles), posterior sag (Godfrey test), quadriceps active test, and dial test at 30° and 90° (asymmetry at both angles implicates combined PCL+PLC injury, not isolated PLC) — performed before prepping to confirm the injury pattern and detect any missed combined injury.
- **Draping**: circumferential prep from mid-thigh to toes; if a posterior approach is planned, prep circumferentially to allow position change/re-draping mid-case, or prep and drape for a combined anterior-arthroscopic-plus-posterior-open field if using a single-position technique.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: joint line, popliteal crease (if posterior approach planned), fibular head and peroneal nerve course (particularly if combined PLC work is planned), and standard arthroscopic portal sites.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy.** Establish anterolateral and anteromedial portals; systematically evaluate the ACL, menisci, and articular cartilage, then specifically evaluate the PCL — its femoral and tibial attachments, tear pattern, and remnant quality. A posteromedial portal is often useful for direct visualization of the tibial PCL footprint and for working in the posterior compartment.
2. **Debride the PCL remnant** as needed while preserving a viable stump where possible (some techniques preserve remnant tissue for proprioceptive and vascular contribution to graft healing), and prepare the femoral and tibial footprints.
3. **Harvest and prepare the graft** on the back table concurrently: whipstitch the tendinous ends, size the graft to the planned tunnel/inlay dimensions, and attach an EndoPearl (or equivalent) to the femoral end of a soft-tissue graft to improve femoral fixation strength and reduce the "bungee" effect of a soft graft pulling through a suspensory construct.
4. **Prepare the femoral tunnel(s).** For single-bundle reconstruction, place a single femoral tunnel reproducing the anterolateral bundle footprint (the primary restraint, taut in flexion); for double-bundle, place a second, smaller tunnel for the posteromedial bundle footprint. Femoral tunnels are typically drilled from an accessory anteromedial or medial portal for direct visualization and accurate footprint placement.
5. **Prepare the tibial fixation site — this is the key technique-defining step.**
   - *Transtibial technique*: use a PCL tibial guide set to the desired angle (aim for as shallow/less oblique a trajectory as the anatomy allows) to drill from the anteromedial tibia to the posterior tibial PCL footprint, staying just below the posterior tibial "sulcus" under direct posteromedial-portal or fluoroscopic visualization to avoid a neurovascular injury and to avoid an unnecessarily acute tunnel angle. Minimizing the posterior tibial tunnel angle — rather than accepting the steepest angle that is technically achievable — is the single most important modifiable step in reducing the killer-turn effect.
   - *Tibial inlay technique*: via a posterior approach (open, or arthroscopically-assisted with the patient remaining supine in some described modifications), develop the interval to expose the native PCL tibial footprint, protecting the neurovascular bundle throughout; create a bone trough/slot at the footprint using the dedicated slot-cutter instrument, and prepare a corresponding bone plug on the graft (commonly Achilles allograft) sized to match.
6. **Pass the graft.** For transtibial technique, pass the graft from posterior tibial tunnel exit, through the joint, to the femoral tunnel(s), taking care that the graft glides smoothly around the tunnel exit without kinking — resistance felt during passage is a warning sign of an excessively acute killer-turn angle that should prompt tunnel revision rather than forceful graft passage. For inlay technique, seat the bone plug directly into the prepared trough and provisionally fix with a cannulated screw or staple before passing the free tendinous end anteriorly to the femoral tunnel(s).
7. **Provisional femoral fixation.** Fix the femoral side first (interference screw and/or suspensory button with EndoPearl augmentation), leaving the tibial/inlay side free for tensioning.
8. **Reduce the tibia and tension the graft near full extension** (technique-typical range approximately 0–20° of flexion) while applying an anteriorly directed force on the proximal tibia to correct posterior sag and restore normal tibial step-off relative to the femoral condyles — this "anterior drawer reduction" maneuver during tensioning is essential to avoid fixing the graft with residual posterior laxity built in.
9. **Final tibial fixation.** For transtibial technique, fix with an interference screw, backed up with a suture-anchor/post-and-washer construct on the anteromedial tibial cortex for additional security, particularly with soft-tissue grafts. For inlay technique, confirm final bone-plug seating and screw fixation.
10. **Address associated PLC/posteromedial corner injury in the same setting** (combined or staged, per the multiligament reconstruction framework) — an unaddressed posterolateral/posteromedial injury leaves the PCL graft overloaded and it will stretch out over time even with technically perfect PCL-side technique.
11. **Final checks.** Cycle the knee through range of motion, re-check posterior drawer/sag under anesthesia to confirm restored stability, and inspect graft tensioning/isometry arthroscopically before closure.
12. **Closure.** Irrigate, close portals and any open posterior incision in layers, and apply a hinged brace locked or supported in extension for ambulation per protocol, specifically to protect against unprotected posterior tibial sag in the early postoperative period.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Grade posterior laxity objectively with stress radiographs preoperatively — this gives an evidence-based threshold for the operative decision and a reproducible number to compare against postoperatively, rather than relying on exam grading alone.
- Attach an EndoPearl (or equivalent graft-end button) to a soft-tissue graft's femoral end before passage — it meaningfully improves femoral fixation strength for suspensory constructs and reduces graft "windshield-wiper" motion within the tunnel.
- When using the transtibial technique, prioritize minimizing the tibial tunnel angle over any other single technical variable — resist the temptation to accept a steeper, easier-to-drill trajectory, since this is the direct driver of the killer-turn stress riser.
- If the graft does not pass smoothly around the tibial tunnel exit during transtibial passage, treat this as a signal to revise the tunnel rather than force the graft — forced passage against resistance is a direct mechanism for graft fraying at exactly the point most vulnerable to attritional failure.
- Reserve tibial inlay technique for revision cases, combined posterior instability, or whenever graft durability is a particular concern — the trade-off of a posterior approach (and possible position change) is justified by the definitive elimination of the killer-turn stress riser.
- Tension the graft with an active anterior-drawer/tibial-reduction maneuver, not passive tensioning alone — simply pulling the graft taut without correcting posterior tibial sag first will fix the graft with residual laxity built in.
- Preserve a viable PCL remnant where feasible rather than routinely debriding it completely — remnant tissue can contribute proprioceptive input and a vascular/scaffold benefit to graft incorporation.
- Back up tibial-side interference screw fixation with a suture-anchor/post construct, especially in soft-tissue grafts or lower bone quality — the tibial side is the weak link in early postoperative fixation strength for most PCL reconstruction constructs.
- Sequence a combined PCL + PLC reconstruction so that peripheral (PLC) structures are addressed before final PCL tensioning, then verify PCL laxity is corrected with the corner already stabilized, since an unaddressed PLC will make PCL tensioning appear falsely adequate intraoperatively while remaining functionally overloaded postoperatively.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Failing to address associated PLC/posteromedial corner injury concurrently is a primary driver of PCL graft overload and attritional failure.
- Excessive posterior tibial tunnel angle worsens the transtibial "killer turn" effect and predisposes to graft stretch/failure.
- Double-bundle technique adds technical demand without clearly demonstrated clinical superiority over single-bundle reconstruction.
- Residual posterior laxity is common even after technically adequate reconstruction, and persistent posterior tibial translation can alter joint loading, contributing to patellofemoral and medial compartment OA over time.
- Forcing graft passage through a too-acute transtibial tunnel risks graft fraying at the tunnel exit, setting up early attritional failure even if initial fixation appears secure.
- Posterior neurovascular injury is a recognized risk during tibial tunnel drilling (transtibial) and especially during the posterior exposure for tibial inlay technique — meticulous, direct visualization/protection of the popliteal neurovascular bundle is essential throughout posterior dissection.
- Tensioning the graft without correcting posterior tibial sag first fixes in residual laxity that will read clinically as an under-corrected, "stretched-out"-feeling reconstruction from the earliest postoperative visits.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weight-bearing**: touch-down/protected weight-bearing in an extension brace at 2 weeks, progressing to full weight-bearing by 6 weeks.
- **ROM**: goal of 0–90° by 6 weeks, avoiding unprotected posterior sag (i.e., avoid positions/loads that allow the tibia to sag posteriorly against gravity or hamstring pull while unprotected).
- **Brace-free ambulation**: approximately 6 weeks.
- General PCL principles that apply through the healing phase: early quadriceps activation and avoidance of isolated open-chain hamstring exercise, which generates a posteriorly directed tibial force and increases posterior tibial sag; progressive weight-bearing and ROM per the above milestones; periodic laxity reassessment, with stress radiographs if there is concern for progression.
- The monograph's detailed phased return-to-sport timeline (weeks 0–4, 4–12, months 3–6) is described for the non-operative PCL pathway specifically; for reconstructed PCL, sport-specific and cutting/pivoting progression follows restoration of stability and strength on the milestones above, with periodic laxity reassessment guiding progression.
