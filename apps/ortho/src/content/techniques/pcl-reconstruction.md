## Indications

Grade III isolated PCL injury with persistent symptomatic instability after an adequate non-operative trial; combined ligament injury (virtually all multiligament knee injury patterns with PCL involvement); bony avulsion with displacement (often amenable to primary fragment fixation instead of ligament reconstruction); and chronic PCL insufficiency with functional limitation or early degenerative change.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed at the setup/room level in this monograph.

## Steps

1. **Choose single-bundle vs. double-bundle (anterolateral + posteromedial) technique.** Double-bundle offers theoretically better restoration of both flexion and extension stability but is technically demanding with limited proven clinical superiority.
2. **Choose tunnel technique.** The transtibial tunnel technique carries a recognized "killer turn" graft-bending risk that can predispose to attritional graft failure; the tibial inlay technique is favored in some surgeons' hands to avoid this, particularly for revision or combined posterior instability cases. If using a transtibial approach, avoid an excessive posterior tibial tunnel angle to minimize the killer-turn effect.
3. **Address associated PLC/posteromedial corner injury in the same setting** (combined or staged) — an unaddressed posterolateral/posteromedial injury leaves the PCL graft overloaded and it will stretch out.
4. **Tension the graft near full extension** for most techniques.
5. **For displaced bony avulsion**, consider primary fragment fixation as an alternative to ligament reconstruction.

## Pitfalls

- Failing to address associated PLC/posteromedial corner injury concurrently is a primary driver of PCL graft overload and attritional failure.
- Excessive posterior tibial tunnel angle worsens the transtibial "killer turn" effect and predisposes to graft stretch/failure.
- Double-bundle technique adds technical demand without clearly demonstrated clinical superiority over single-bundle reconstruction.
- Residual posterior laxity is common even after technically adequate reconstruction, and persistent posterior tibial translation can alter joint loading, contributing to patellofemoral and medial compartment OA over time.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weight-bearing**: touch-down/protected weight-bearing in an extension brace at 2 weeks, progressing to full weight-bearing by 6 weeks.
- **ROM**: goal of 0–90° by 6 weeks, avoiding unprotected posterior sag (i.e., avoid positions/loads that allow the tibia to sag posteriorly against gravity or hamstring pull while unprotected).
- **Brace-free ambulation**: approximately 6 weeks.
- General PCL principles that apply through the healing phase: early quadriceps activation and avoidance of isolated open-chain hamstring exercise, which generates a posteriorly directed tibial force and increases posterior tibial sag; progressive weight-bearing and ROM per the above milestones; periodic laxity reassessment, with stress radiographs if there is concern for progression.
- The monograph's detailed phased return-to-sport timeline (weeks 0–4, 4–12, months 3–6) is described for the non-operative PCL pathway specifically; for reconstructed PCL, sport-specific and cutting/pivoting progression follows restoration of stability and strength on the milestones above, with periodic laxity reassessment guiding progression.
