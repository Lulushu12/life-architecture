## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Jefferson (C1 ring) fracture with transverse atlantal ligament (TAL) disruption (Dickman type II, or Spence overhang >6.9mm) in a fracture pattern that allows safe C1 lateral mass screw placement.
- Type II odontoid fracture not amenable to anterior screw fixation — comminution (Type IIA), reverse-oblique fracture line, associated TAL injury, or unfavorable anterior trajectory (barrel chest, severe kyphosis).
- Primary treatment choice in many elderly/osteoporotic Type II odontoid patients, given lower nonunion risk from rigid segmental fixation independent of precise fracture-line geometry.
- Chronic odontoid nonunion or delayed instability after failed nonoperative treatment.
- Combined C1-C2 instability from any cause (os odontoideum with instability, rheumatoid pannus with atlantoaxial subluxation) where segmental screw-rod fixation is preferred over wiring/transarticular alternatives.

**Contraindications (absolute or strongly relative)**
- **Anatomy precluding safe C1 lateral mass screw placement** — severely comminuted C1 lateral mass, aberrant or high-riding vertebral artery, or a narrow/dysplastic lateral mass identified on preoperative CT — favors occipitocervical fusion instead.
- **Occipital condyle fracture or occipitocervical dissociation** — requires extension of fixation to the occiput rather than isolated C1-C2 fusion.
- **Isolated posterior C1 arch fracture with intact anterior arch and intact TAL** — rarely unstable and rarely needs surgery at all.
- **Active local or systemic infection**, or bone quality too poor to anticipate reasonable screw purchase without augmentation (relative — cement augmentation or alternative constructs may salvage some cases).
- **Prohibitive perioperative risk in a frail, non-ambulatory patient** where nonoperative management (accepted fibrous nonunion) is a reasonable alternative — shared decision-making is essential rather than a reflexive operative approach based on fracture type alone.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Thin-cut CT with sagittal, coronal, and (ideally) CT-angiography or MR-angiography** of the craniocervical junction — assess C1 lateral mass bone stock and screw trajectory, C2 pedicle/pars width and height, and — critically — **vertebral artery course**, including any high-riding or medialized vertebral artery at C2 (present in a meaningful minority of patients) that would make a standard C2 pedicle screw unsafe.
- **MRI** to confirm TAL status when the rule-of-Spence overhang is borderline, and to assess cord/canal status.
- **Templating**: measure C1 lateral mass screw length/diameter and starting point, and C2 pedicle/pars/translaminar screw trajectory on the axial and sagittal CT; decide preoperatively (per patient's imaging) whether standard C2 pedicle screws, pars screws, translaminar screws, or a sub-laminar/sub-facet variant is safest given the individual vertebral artery anatomy.
- **Implant selection**: polyaxial screw-rod system sized for C1 lateral mass (typically 3.5–4.0mm diameter) and C2 (pedicle screws generally larger/longer than pars screws; translaminar screws as an alternative when pedicle/pars anatomy is unfavorable); have structural or morselized bone graft available (autograft from posterior iliac crest, local autograft, or allograft) to pack the C1-C2 joint/decorticated posterior elements for fusion.
- **Equipment**: intraoperative fluoroscopy (biplanar or single C-arm with position changes) or, where available, image-guided navigation — navigation is increasingly used for C2 screw placement specifically to reduce vertebral artery risk in anatomically unfavorable patients; high-speed burr, C1-C2 reduction/distraction instrumentation, and a Mayfield head clamp for rigid intraoperative positioning and reduction control.
- **Consider whether concomitant occiput fixation may be needed intraoperatively** (i.e., prep and drape a field that allows extension to occipitocervical fusion) if the preoperative imaging leaves any doubt about C1 screw feasibility.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia with awake fiberoptic intubation** (or careful in-line stabilization if asleep) given the potential for an unstable C1-C2 segment; avoid neck flexion/extension during induction and transfer.
- **Prone positioning on a radiolucent table**, head rigidly fixed in a Mayfield three-pin head clamp (essential — this procedure requires absolute head/neck immobility during screw placement and cannot be safely done in a horseshoe headrest alone).
- Neutral or slight flexion of the head relative to the neck is generally used to open the interlaminar space for exposure, while avoiding either fixed flexion (worsens any anterior subluxation) or extension (can narrow the space available for the cord in an unstable segment) — the specific position is guided by the fracture pattern and any pre-positioning reduction achieved under fluoroscopy.
- Bolsters/chest rolls to allow free abdominal excursion and reduce venous congestion/bleeding; arms tucked or padded at the sides; all pressure points padded (axillae, elbows, knees).
- Confirm satisfactory C1-C2 alignment/reduction under lateral fluoroscopy after final head-clamp positioning, before prepping — this is the last easy opportunity to adjust position-dependent reduction.
- **Fluoroscopy or navigation array setup**: position the C-arm (or navigation reference frame, if used) to allow true lateral and AP/open-mouth-equivalent views of the occiput-C1-C2 complex without needing to move the head clamp during the case.
- Prep and drape a posterior midline field from the occiput to at least C4, wide enough laterally to allow for extension to occipitocervical instrumentation if intraoperative findings require it.
- Consider posterior iliac crest as a secondary sterile field if autograft harvest is planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm indication and screw feasibility one final time** intraoperatively — for Jefferson fractures, confirm the fracture pattern still allows safe C1 lateral mass screw placement; for odontoid fractures, confirm anterior screw fixation remains judged not feasible/not preferred.
2. **Make a posterior midline incision** from the occiput to C3-C4, and expose subperiosteally along the midline raphe to minimize bleeding.
3. **Expose the posterior arch of C1 carefully and subperiosteally**, staying within ~1.5cm of midline laterally on the superior surface of the arch — the vertebral artery runs in the groove on the superior/posterior aspect of the C1 lateral mass just lateral to this zone, so dissection here should be gentle, subperiosteal, and non-electrocautery-dependent to avoid a vertebral artery injury.
4. **Identify the C2 nerve root and ganglion**, which crosses the C1-C2 joint and often needs to be retracted, coagulated, or (per surgeon preference) sacrificed to expose the C1 lateral mass entry point and the C1-C2 joint for later graft placement — sacrifice is generally well tolerated (occipital numbness) and often improves visualization.
5. **Expose the C1 lateral mass entry point**, typically at the junction of the posterior arch and the lateral mass, confirmed under fluoroscopy or navigation.
6. **Place C1 lateral mass screws bilaterally**: after confirming the starting point, drill/tap under image guidance with a trajectory angled slightly medially and cephalad toward the anterior tubercle of C1, avoiding penetration of the anterior cortex near the internal carotid artery/hypoglossal nerve; screw length and diameter per preoperative templating.
7. **Expose the C2 pars/pedicle/lamina** and select the entry point and trajectory based on preoperative vertebral artery mapping — standard C2 pedicle screw entry is at the superomedial aspect of the C2 lateral mass/isthmus; an alternative, artery-sparing entry point described in the literature is roughly 3mm below the midpoint of the C1-C2 joint, directed medially and downward into the C2 body, largely bypassing the pars/pedicle isthmus and further reducing vertebral artery risk in borderline anatomy — consider this or a translaminar screw when the standard pedicle trajectory is judged unsafe.
8. **Place C2 pedicle, pars, or translaminar screws** under fluoroscopic or navigated guidance, confirming trajectory on both AP and lateral views before final seating; Magerl-type C1-C2 transarticular screws are an alternative fixation strategy for the same segment when Goel-Harms-style segmental screws are not feasible, though transarticular screws carry their own vertebral artery risk profile and require a specific fixed C1-C2 reduction before drilling.
9. **Achieve provisional reduction** of C1 on C2 (correcting any residual translation or rotation) using the screw heads/reduction instrumentation before final rod placement, confirmed under lateral fluoroscopy.
10. **Contour and place rods bilaterally**, connecting the C1 lateral mass and C2 screws; apply compression or neutral loading across the construct as the fracture/injury pattern dictates, and torque all set screws to final specification.
11. **Decorticate the posterior elements of C1 (arch) and C2 (lamina/isthmus)** and pack autograft or allograft across the decorticated bed and into the C1-C2 joint space (some techniques place structural graft directly into the joint, others rely on posterolateral onlay graft) to promote fusion.
12. **Obtain final AP and lateral fluoroscopic images** (or CT if navigation-assisted and available intraoperatively) confirming screw position, absence of vertebral artery-concerning trajectory, and satisfactory C1-C2 alignment.
13. **Irrigate, achieve meticulous hemostasis** (the posterior C1-C2 venous plexus can bleed briskly), and close in layers over a drain if indicated, with attention to a secure fascial closure given the biomechanical stress across this region.
14. **Postoperative CT** is obtained in most centers before mobilization out of a rigid collar to confirm screw position, particularly to exclude an occult vertebral artery-concerning trajectory not fully appreciated on fluoroscopy.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Dissect the posterior arch of C1 subperiosteally and gently, staying within the safe zone medial to the vertebral artery groove — this single habit is the most important technical safeguard against vertebral artery injury during exposure.
- Sacrificing the C2 nerve root/ganglion (rather than aggressively retracting it) often improves visualization of the C1 lateral mass entry point and the C1-C2 joint with an acceptable, usually well-tolerated trade-off of occipital numbness.
- Use preoperative CT-angiography or MR-angiography specifically to map the C2 vertebral artery course — a high-riding or medialized artery on one or both sides should change the planned C2 screw trajectory (or fixation type) before you are in the operating room, not after.
- Consider the modified, more caudal/medial C2 screw entry point (approximately 3mm below the C1-C2 joint midpoint, directed medially and downward into the C2 body) as an alternative to the standard pedicle trajectory in patients with borderline vertebral artery anatomy — it largely bypasses the pars/pedicle isthmus.
- Achieve provisional reduction through the screw-head/reduction instrumentation before final rod tightening — this gives more controlled, incremental correction of translation/rotation than attempting to force reduction with the rod alone.
- A rigid Mayfield head clamp is not optional for this procedure — attempting C1-C2 segmental screw placement without absolute head fixation risks screw malposition with head micromotion during drilling.
- Have image guidance (navigation) available, or at minimum a low threshold to use it, in any case with unfavorable or atypical vertebral artery anatomy — this is one of the clearest evidence-based uses of navigation in spine trauma.
- Pack graft into the C1-C2 joint itself, not just onto the decorticated posterior elements — joint-space fusion adds an additional biological fusion surface beyond the posterolateral onlay bed.
- Confirm construct alignment on a true lateral fluoroscopic view before you leave the OR — subtle residual C1-C2 subluxation is easy to miss on an imperfect lateral shot and is far easier to correct before closure than after.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Vertebral artery injury** is the most feared complication of this technique — occurs from straying lateral during C1 arch dissection, from a C2 screw trajectory that violates the pars/pedicle isthmus in a patient with anomalous (high-riding/medialized) vertebral artery anatomy, or from overly aggressive drilling without image confirmation. Preoperative CTA/MRA mapping and intraoperative fluoroscopy/navigation are the primary mitigations; if injury occurs, tamponade with hemostatic material, avoid bilateral vertebral artery injury (do not proceed with a symmetric contralateral trajectory that risks the same complication), and obtain urgent vascular/interventional consultation.
- **Reserve occipitocervical fusion (rather than C1-C2 fusion) for fractures precluding safe C1 screw placement** (severely comminuted lateral mass) or when there is concomitant occipital condyle/occipitocervical dissociation.
- **Isolated posterior arch fractures with an intact anterior arch and intact TAL rarely need surgery** — confirm TAL status before proceeding to fusion for a Jefferson-pattern fracture.
- **The construct sacrifices approximately 50% of cervical rotation** — a relevant counseling point, particularly for younger, active patients being treated for an odontoid fracture where an anterior screw might otherwise have preserved rotation.
- **Screw malposition into the spinal canal or transverse foramen** — mitigated by careful preoperative templating, image guidance in difficult anatomy, and intraoperative fluoroscopic/CT confirmation before leaving the OR.
- **C1-C2 venous plexus bleeding** during C1 lateral mass exposure can be brisk — have hemostatic agents (thrombin-soaked gelfoam/patties) ready and avoid electrocautery near the vertebral artery groove; tamponade rather than aggressive cauterization is usually the safer response.
- **Occipital neuralgia/numbness** from C2 nerve root sacrifice — usually well tolerated but should be part of informed consent.
- **Nonunion** — less common than with anterior screw fixation given rigid segmental fixation, but still occurs, particularly with poor bone quality, inadequate decortication/grafting, or construct loosening.
- **In frail elderly patients, this technique achieves substantially higher fusion rates than nonoperative management but carries real perioperative morbidity/mortality** — shared decision-making incorporating frailty, baseline mobility, and patient goals is essential rather than defaulting to surgery based on fracture type alone.
- **Adjacent-segment stress and long-term rotational limitation** — patients should be counseled preoperatively about the permanent loss of roughly half of normal cervical rotation.

## Postoperative Protocol

Post-op management follows the general upper cervical fracture rehabilitation pathway: wound care and pain control acutely (0–2 weeks) with brace-protected mobilization typically beginning within 24–72h; formal ROM restrictions until early fusion consolidation (~6–8 weeks) in the subacute phase (2–12 weeks), with interval imaging at 6–12 weeks; progressive loading and brace weaning once radiographic fusion is confirmed (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable fixation; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months. Return-to-sport/contact-activity clearance requires solid radiographic fusion (≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, and normalized strength, and remains individualized and conservative for cervical injuries, particularly those involving C1-C2.
