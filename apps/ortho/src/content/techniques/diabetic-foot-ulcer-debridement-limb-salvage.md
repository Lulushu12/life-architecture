## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Diabetic foot ulcer with clinical or radiographic evidence of infection (per IDSA/IWGDF grading) requiring surgical source control.
- Non-healing ulcer despite optimized offloading and wound care over an adequate trial period.
- Exposed bone, tendon, or joint requiring debridement and/or coverage planning.
- Suspected or confirmed osteomyelitis requiring resection of infected/necrotic bone.
- Recalcitrant forefoot ulcer with a demonstrated gastrocnemius/Achilles contracture on Silfverskiöld testing, where tendon lengthening can reduce forefoot plantar pressure and support healing.
- Non-salvageable tissue (extensive gangrene, uncontrolled sepsis) requiring amputation at an appropriate level.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Uncorrected critical limb ischemia (WIfI ischemia grade ≥2, ABI <0.4, absolute toe pressure <30 mmHg, or TcPO2 <30 mmHg) — do not debride without a perfusion plan; obtain same-week vascular surgery referral first, since debridement in an ischemic bed will not heal and can precipitate further tissue loss.
- Uncontrolled systemic sepsis without prior resuscitation — stabilize the patient before elective-length debridement, though emergent source control for life-threatening infection should not be delayed.
- Overlengthening risk in patients with an already low/normal calcaneal pitch or prior heel pad compromise — a gastrocnemius recession or Achilles lengthening in this context risks calcaneal gait and heel ulceration.
- Poorly controlled glycemic status and active smoking are correctable risk factors for wound failure; address as feasible without unduly delaying limb-threatening interventions.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Vascular assessment**: ABI, toe-brachial index, or formal arterial duplex/angiography in any patient with diminished pulses or a WIfI ischemia component ≥1; coordinate timing of revascularization (endovascular or open bypass) with debridement — before or concurrent, per WIfI stage and clinical urgency.
- **Imaging**: plain radiographs first-line to assess for osteomyelitis (cortical destruction, periosteal reaction) and bony deformity; MRI when radiographs are equivocal, understanding that both osteomyelitis and Charcot changes can produce marrow edema — clinical correlation (sinus tract, ulcer location, trend) is essential; consider bone biopsy for culture-directed antibiotic therapy when osteomyelitis is suspected and the diagnosis/organism is not otherwise established.
- **Infection workup**: probe-to-bone test at the bedside (high positive predictive value for osteomyelitis in high-prevalence settings); inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, procalcitonin) to support but not confirm infection; wound and, where feasible, bone cultures to guide antibiotic selection per IDSA/IWGDF severity grading.
- **Equipment**: standard debridement instrumentation (curettes, rongeurs, saw for bone resection), pulse lavage for irrigation, culture swabs/bone biopsy instruments, tendon lengthening/recession instrumentation (available even when the primary indication is debridement, since concurrent equinus correction is frequently added), amputation level-specific instrumentation on standby (ray resection, transmetatarsal, below-knee sets) in case intraoperative findings require escalation beyond the planned level.
- **Case-specific planning**: define in advance, with the multidisciplinary team, the anticipated debridement extent and amputation "ladder" fallback level if intraoperative findings (tissue viability, extent of infection) are worse than anticipated — this avoids returning to the operating room under time pressure with an unplanned escalation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic block (± saphenous/ankle block) combined with sedation or general anesthesia is common; regional anesthesia is often favored in this population given frequent cardiovascular comorbidity, though the choice is individualized with anesthesia input on cardiac risk.
- **Positioning**: supine for the majority of forefoot/midfoot debridement and amputation-level procedures; prone or lateral positioning may be used for isolated heel/posterior work or combined tendon lengthening procedures. The leg is prepped free to allow intraoperative assessment of perfusion (e.g., checking capillary refill/bleeding at debridement margins) and manipulation for tendon balancing.
- **Tourniquet**: use is individualized — many surgeons avoid a tourniquet in ischemic or borderline-perfused limbs so that bleeding at debridement margins can be used as a real-time indicator of tissue viability; when perfusion is adequate and revascularization is not imminent, a tourniquet may still be used for a defined portion of the case (e.g., bone resection) and released before final assessment of margins.
- **Fluoroscopy**: available for cases involving bone resection (exostectomy, partial calcanectomy, ray/transmetatarsal amputation) to confirm adequate resection margins and residual bone contour.
- **Landmarks**: mark the ulcer boundaries, planned debridement margins, and (if tendon work planned) the Achilles/gastrocnemius incision sites before prepping; mark planned amputation level flap lines if amputation is anticipated as a possible outcome.
- **Draping/prep**: isolate the ulcer/infected field from clean skin during prep (wound often prepped last or with a separate barrier); given frequent malodorous or heavily colonized wounds, plan instrument and glove changes between the "dirty" debridement phase and any subsequent clean closure or tendon work.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Sharp debridement**: excise all nonviable, necrotic, and heavily contaminated tissue back to a bed of bleeding, viable tissue — including callused/hyperkeratotic wound margins, which perpetuate a chronic, non-healing wound edge if left in place. Send tissue and/or bone for culture at this stage, before antibiotics are broadened empirically if not already started.
2. **Revascularization**: coordinate endovascular or open bypass revascularization before or concurrent with debridement whenever the WIfI ischemia grade indicates benefit — in practice this is frequently staged with vascular surgery performing revascularization first (or on the same day) followed by debridement, since wound healing potential is perfusion-limited above nearly all other factors.
3. **Bone resection for osteomyelitis**: resect infected/necrotic bone back to a margin of grossly and (where feasible) histologically/culture-negative bone, sending a clear margin specimen for culture; discuss with infectious disease whether the resection achieved adequate source control or whether prolonged antibiotic therapy alone is a reasonable alternative when radical resection is not feasible or would be excessively morbid (e.g., resection that would compromise a major weightbearing structure).
4. **Achilles tendon lengthening or gastrocnemius recession**: for a recalcitrant forefoot ulcer with a confirmed equinus contracture on Silfverskiöld testing, perform a percutaneous or open tendo-Achilles lengthening (triple hemisection technique is common) or an isolated gastrocnemius recession (Strayer-type, preferred when the contracture is isolated to the gastrocnemius) to reduce forefoot plantar pressure — this is frequently performed in the same setting as debridement to address the underlying biomechanical driver of ulcer recurrence.
5. **Amputation level selection** (when tissue loss or infection extent precludes limb-preserving debridement alone): select the most distal level consistent with adequate perfusion, viable soft-tissue coverage, and a durable, shoeable or prosthetic-fitting residual limb — toe, ray resection, transmetatarsal amputation, below-knee, or above-knee, in ascending order of proximal extent. Confirm bleeding, viable margins intraoperatively at the planned level before committing to closure; be prepared to revise proximally if margins are unhealthy.
6. **Closure/coverage decision**: close primarily only over a clean, well-vascularized bed with tension-free approximation; otherwise pack open, plan for delayed primary closure, negative pressure wound therapy, or staged coverage (local flap, skin graft, or free flap in a multidisciplinary limb salvage setting) once the wound bed is clean and granulating.
7. **Multidisciplinary handoff**: document the debridement extent, culture results pending, and planned antibiotic course; hand off explicitly to wound care nursing for dressing selection and to endocrinology/vascular surgery for continued optimization — limb salvage is iterative and rarely completed in a single operative encounter.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Debride the entire callused wound margin, not just the visibly necrotic center — a chronic diabetic ulcer's edge is biologically "stuck" in a non-healing state and periodic sharp debridement of the rim is often what allows the wound to restart healing.
- Use bleeding as your real-time viability indicator at debridement margins, especially when a tourniquet has been deflated for this purpose — tissue that does not bleed briskly is unlikely to heal and should be considered for further resection.
- Sequence revascularization before or concurrent with debridement rather than after — debriding an ischemic wound first, planning to revascularize later, risks worsening a marginal perfusion state and converting a salvageable wound into a non-healing one.
- When performing a gastrocnemius recession versus a full Achilles lengthening, base the choice on the Silfverskiöld test result — an isolated gastrocnemius contracture is adequately addressed with recession alone and carries a lower overlengthening/calcaneal gait risk than a full tendo-Achilles lengthening.
- Take a true margin culture (not just superficial wound swab) when resecting for osteomyelitis — superficial swabs are frequently colonized with organisms that do not reflect the deep infecting organism and can misdirect antibiotic therapy.
- Plan the amputation level conservatively but honestly — preserving a marginal, poorly perfused distal level to "save length" often results in a second, more proximal amputation; a well-healed transmetatarsal amputation is a better functional outcome than a failed toe/ray amputation requiring revision.
- Engage the "toe and flow" model explicitly — pair aggressive local wound/bone management (the "toe") with aggressive perfusion optimization (the "flow"); cohort data associate this combined approach with reduced major amputation rates compared with either component alone.
- Do not treat glycemic optimization, smoking cessation, and nutrition optimization as afterthoughts — they materially affect healing potential and should be actively managed in parallel with surgical treatment, not deferred until after the wound closes.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not debride an ischemic wound without a perfusion plan; a WIfI ischemia grade ≥2 with a wound warrants same-week vascular surgery referral.
- Overlengthening the Achilles/gastrocnemius carries a small but real risk of calcaneal gait or heel ulceration — balance forefoot offloading benefit against this trade-off, and favor isolated gastrocnemius recession over full Achilles lengthening when the Silfverskiöld test supports it.
- Recurrence after healing is high without ongoing offloading/footwear intervention — **up to 40% at 1 year, 60%+ at 3–5 years** — and can progress to osteomyelitis or major amputation in patients with uncontrolled infection or critical limb ischemia not amenable to revascularization; offloading and footwear are lifelong interventions, not episodic ones.
- Incomplete bone resection for osteomyelitis (leaving grossly or microbiologically infected margins) is a common cause of treatment failure and need for reoperation — send a true margin culture and correlate intraoperative appearance with imaging.
- Superficial wound swab cultures frequently misrepresent the deep infecting organism and can misdirect antibiotic therapy — prefer deep tissue or bone cultures obtained at debridement.
- Amputation at an inadequately perfused or marginally viable level risks wound breakdown at the new margin, precipitating a second, more proximal amputation — err toward the level with confirmed viable, bleeding margins.
- Corticosteroid injections and repeated soft-tissue interventions are not part of this pathway; treat glycemic optimization, smoking cessation, and nutrition optimization as core adjuncts, not afterthoughts, given their material effect on healing.

## Postoperative Protocol

Total contact casting (TCC) or instant-TCC changed weekly to every 2 weeks; typical healing trajectory for a well-offloaded, adequately perfused plantar forefoot ulcer is 6–8 weeks. Failure to reduce wound area by ≥40–50% at 4 weeks predicts non-healing and should trigger reassessment of perfusion, infection, offloading compliance, and adjunctive therapy.

After amputation or bone resection, protect the residual limb/foot in a splint or boot until soft-tissue healing is secure, then progress weightbearing per level-specific tolerance with orthotics/prosthetics involvement early for level-appropriate footwear or prosthetic planning. Continue multidisciplinary follow-up (vascular, endocrinology, wound care, orthotics) indefinitely given the high lifetime recurrence risk in this population.
