## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Refractory sesamoiditis, osteonecrosis, or symptomatic fracture nonunion of the tibial or fibular sesamoid after failure of non-operative management (rigid orthotic with sesamoid cutout, activity modification, CAM boot immobilization 4–6 weeks).
- The tibial sesamoid is the far more common site of surgical pathology given its greater axial load transmission during push-off.
- **ORIF/bone grafting with screw or tension-band fixation** is reserved instead for a symptomatic fracture nonunion in a young, active patient who wishes to avoid sesamoidectomy altogether and preserve the native sesamoid's biomechanical function.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- An asymptomatic bipartite sesamoid discovered incidentally — this is a normal anatomic variant (prevalence ~10–33%, often bilateral) with smooth, corticated margins and no marrow edema, and should never be treated as pathology.
- Uncertain diagnosis — proceeding to sesamoidectomy without MRI/bone scan confirmation in an equivocal case risks operating on an asymptomatic variant rather than the true pain generator.
- Bilateral or multifocal forefoot pain suggesting an alternative diagnosis (stress fracture of the metatarsal, plantar plate pathology, systemic/inflammatory disease) not yet excluded.
- Planned **complete** excision of a sesamoid in a patient without a compelling reason — partial excision should be the default whenever the pathology is confined to one pole, given the meaningfully higher iatrogenic malalignment risk of complete excision.
- Active infection or an inadequate plantar soft-tissue envelope at the planned incision site.

## Preoperative Planning

- Obtain AP, lateral, and a dedicated **sesamoid axial (skyline) view**; differentiate a bipartite variant (smooth, rounded, sclerotic margins) from an acute fracture (sharp, jagged, non-sclerotic margins) — comparison views of the contralateral foot are useful when uncertain.
- MRI or bone scan when radiographs are equivocal, to confirm marrow edema (fracture/stress reaction/osteonecrosis) versus a quiescent asymptomatic bipartite variant.
- Confirm which sesamoid (tibial versus fibular) and which pole is symptomatic — this determines both the approach and how much of the sesamoid can be preserved.
- Plan the extent of resection preoperatively based on imaging: a clearly demarcated fractured or necrotic pole supports a straightforward partial excision; diffuse involvement of the whole sesamoid should prompt reconsideration of whether complete excision is truly unavoidable and, if so, a frank preoperative discussion of the associated hallux valgus/varus risk.
- Instrumentation: fine osteotomes or a mini saw for a controlled partial resection (rather than a rongeur alone, which can leave an irregular remnant), and — if ORIF is planned instead — small-fragment screws or wire/suture for a tension-band construct plus bone graft (autograft or allograft) for the nonunion site.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Ankle block or popliteal regional block with sedation, or general/spinal anesthesia per preference; short outpatient procedure well suited to regional anesthesia.
- Supine, foot free at the end of the table.
- Thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination for a bloodless field, important given the small working space plantar to the metatarsal head.
- Choice of **medial plantar approach** for the tibial sesamoid or **lateral plantar approach** for the fibular sesamoid, avoiding a direct plantar (weight-bearing) incision when feasible; mark the planned incision preoperatively based on which sesamoid is involved.
- No fluoroscopy is routinely required for excision, but have it available intraoperatively if ORIF of a nonunion is planned, to confirm screw/wire position.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the diagnosis (fracture, osteonecrosis, or refractory sesamoiditis) and document that non-operative management has failed before proceeding.
2. Make a medial (tibial sesamoid) or lateral (fibular sesamoid) plantar-based longitudinal incision, staying off the direct weight-bearing plantar skin where possible; protect the plantar digital nerves, which run close to the sesamoid at this level.
3. Develop the interval through subcutaneous tissue to the flexor hallucis brevis (FHB) tendon sling, which envelops the sesamoid — incise the tendon sheath longitudinally, in line with its fibers, to expose the sesamoid without transecting the sling itself.
4. Identify the fractured or necrotic pole of the sesamoid directly, correlating with preoperative imaging.
5. Perform a **partial excision** — remove only the fractured or necrotic pole with a fine osteotome or mini saw for a controlled, smooth resection margin, preserving the remainder of the sesamoid and, critically, the intact portion of the FHB sling attaching to it.
6. Smooth the residual sesamoid surface/edges to avoid a sharp bony prominence that could itself become symptomatic against the flexor tendon or plantar soft tissue.
7. Repair the FHB tendon sheath/sling incision meticulously — this preserves the static and dynamic restraint function of the sesamoid apparatus and is a key step in avoiding iatrogenic hallux malalignment.
8. For a symptomatic nonunion in a young, active patient wishing to avoid excision, instead expose the fracture site, débride the nonunion interface to bleeding bone, pack with autograft or allograft bone, and fix with a small-fragment screw or a tension-band wire/suture construct spanning the fracture, confirming reduction and fixation with fluoroscopy.
9. Irrigate, close the FHB sling/tendon sheath, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers.
10. Apply a compressive dressing and a stiff-soled postoperative shoe to offload the forefoot.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Incise the FHB sheath longitudinally, in line with its fibers, rather than transversely — this makes an anatomic, low-tension repair straightforward at closure and helps preserve sesamoid function.
- Use a controlled osteotome or saw cut rather than piecemeal rongeur bites for the partial resection — a smooth, planned resection margin heals and remodels better than an irregular one and is less likely to leave a symptomatic bony edge.
- Smooth the cut edge of the remaining sesamoid before closure; a sharp residual corner can abrade the adjacent flexor tendon or plantar fat pad postoperatively.
- Repair the FHB sling meticulously and without excessive tension — this is the structure most responsible for preserving the medial (tibial sesamoid) or lateral (fibular sesamoid) static restraint that prevents iatrogenic hallux malalignment.
- Route the incision off the direct weight-bearing plantar skin whenever the pathology location allows — a scar directly under the metatarsal head/sesamoid load-bearing zone is more likely to be symptomatic postoperatively.
- If diffuse sesamoid involvement makes partial excision impossible and complete excision is unavoidable, counsel the patient explicitly and in advance about the meaningfully higher risk of iatrogenic hallux valgus (complete tibial) or hallux varus (complete fibular) so this is not a surprise postoperative discussion.
- For ORIF of a nonunion, confirm rigid fixation intraoperatively with gentle stress testing before closure — a construct that is not truly rigid risks progressing to a symptomatic re-nonunion despite grafting.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Partial (not complete) sesamoidectomy is strongly preferred** whenever a partial excision can achieve symptom relief while preserving the FHB sling.
- **Complete tibial sesamoidectomy** materially increases the risk of iatrogenic **hallux valgus** from loss of the medial static/dynamic restraint.
- **Complete fibular sesamoidectomy** increases the risk of iatrogenic **hallux varus**.
- Excising the wrong sesamoid, or both sesamoids without clear indication, risks persistent pain and weakness of push-off.
- Any procedure that destabilizes the 1st ray sesamoid apparatus (including complete sesamoidectomy) redistributes load laterally and is a recognized mechanism for iatrogenic transfer metatarsalgia under the lesser metatarsal heads.
- An inadequately repaired FHB sling after partial excision undermines the intended benefit of preserving the sling in the first place.
- Plantar digital nerve injury from careless dissection near the sesamoid is a recognized approach-related risk.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: protected weight-bearing in a stiff-soled postoperative shoe/boot, off-loading the forefoot.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing, transition toward regular supportive/stiff-soled shoes.
- **Return to running/impact/push-off-intensive sport**: often **8–12 weeks**, since the FHB sling and 1st MTP push-off mechanics need to accommodate the altered sesamoid anatomy.
