## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:

- Peripheral (red-red or red-white zone) tear location.
- Tear length >1 cm.
- Patient age generally <40–50 (relative — biology matters more than chronologic age).
- Acute or subacute tear.
- Concurrent ACL reconstruction — a biologically favorable environment; repair success rates are meaningfully higher when performed with concomitant ACLR than in an ACL-stable/isolated knee.
- A locked knee (fixed flexion deformity, block to full extension) should be treated as a bucket-handle tear until proven otherwise and taken to urgent MRI/arthroscopy rather than a prolonged conservative trial.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:

- Degenerative, complex, multi-planar tears in white-white (avascular) tissue with poor intrinsic healing potential — better served by limited debridement of the unstable flap than by a repair likely to fail.
- Chronic, severely retracted, or deformed bucket-handle fragments that cannot be adequately reduced to their anatomic bed.
- Diffuse chondral degeneration in the same compartment (a repair placed into an already degenerative environment has a lower chance of a clinically meaningful benefit).
- Uncorrected ligamentous instability if the meniscus repair is being considered in isolation — an unstable knee places excessive shear on the repair and predicts failure unless stability is restored concurrently.
- Active infection.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to characterize tear pattern (vertical longitudinal vs. bucket-handle vs. complex), length, location relative to the vascular zones, and to screen for a concurrent root or ramp lesion, ACL injury, or chondral pathology that changes the operative plan.
- **Assess vascular zone** (red-red, red-white, white-white) preoperatively from imaging and confirm intraoperatively — this is the single biggest determinant of repair candidacy and should be explicitly documented.
- **Implant/instrumentation selection**: decide in advance (or plan to decide intraoperatively based on tear location) among inside-out (cannulated zone-specific suture passers, a posteromedial or posterolateral retractor/incision tray), all-inside (a device-based suture-anchor or suture-hook system compatible with the tear location), and outside-in (spinal needles, suture-passing wires) — have more than one system opened/available, since intraoperative findings often change the plan from what was anticipated on MRI.
- **Concurrent ACLR planning**: if the meniscus tear is found at the time of ACL reconstruction, plan tunnel and graft-preparation sequencing so the meniscal repair does not conflict with tibial/femoral tunnel trajectories, and plan to take advantage of the marrow-venting/hemarthrosis biology of the ACLR case.
- **Equipment/instrumentation checklist**: arthroscopy tower, cannulated suture-passing devices for the chosen technique(s), high-strength non-absorbable or slowly-absorbable suture, an all-inside device magazine if using a modern implant-based system, and a posteromedial/posterolateral retractor set (with a padded mallet/spoon retractor to protect the neurovascular structures) for inside-out cases.
- **Plan the accessory incision site** in advance based on tear location (posteromedial for medial-sided tears, posterolateral for lateral-sided tears) and mark it before draping.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia; a femoral or adductor canal nerve block is frequently added for postoperative analgesia, particularly when an accessory open incision is planned.
- **Positioning**: supine with a lateral post or leg holder allowing full flexion beyond 120° and a figure-4 (flexed, externally rotated) position for posterior horn access on both the medial and lateral sides.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh tourniquet applied and available; many surgeons leave it deflated during diagnostic arthroscopy and inflate selectively if bleeding limits visualization, particularly during the accessory-incision dissection for inside-out repair.
- **Setup**: standard arthroscopic fluid inflow/outflow system; prep and drape wide enough to allow a posteromedial or posterolateral accessory incision if inside-out repair is selected, with the knee positioned to allow the surgeon (or an assistant) safe access to place a protective retractor between the joint capsule and the neurovascular structures during suture retrieval.
- **Landmarks**: mark standard anteromedial/anterolateral portals; mark the posteromedial (medial-sided tears) or posterolateral (lateral-sided tears) accessory incision site, typically 2–3 cm behind the respective collateral ligament, before draping — planning this incision precisely reduces dissection time and neurovascular risk.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Establish standard anteromedial and anterolateral viewing/working portals and perform diagnostic arthroscopy.
2. Probe not just the torn segment but the posterior horn of both menisci, to identify a concurrent ramp or root lesion before proceeding — this changes the operative plan and should not be discovered after the primary repair is already underway.
3. Characterize the tear: length, pattern (vertical longitudinal vs. bucket-handle vs. complex/degenerative), and vascular zone (red-red, red-white, white-white) by direct visualization and probing.
4. For a chronic bucket-handle tear, fully reduce the displaced fragment before fixation using a probe or grasper to walk the fragment back into its anatomic bed — an incompletely reduced fragment heals in a foreshortened, non-anatomic position and remains symptomatic; confirm reduction by checking that the fragment's contour matches the native meniscal rim circumferentially, not just at the point of initial reduction.
5. Lightly rasp/abrade the tear edges and the adjacent synovial fringe (in red-red/red-white tissue) to stimulate a vascular healing response before placing sutures.
6. Select the repair technique based on tear location and available exposure:
   - **Inside-out** — gold standard for posterior horn access and fixation strength; place cannulas through the anteromedial or anterolateral portal directed at the tear, pass paired sutures across the tear in a vertical or horizontal mattress pattern, and retrieve the needles through the protected posteromedial or posterolateral accessory incision, where a spoon/retractor separates the capsule from the neurovascular structures before any needle is passed.
   - **All-inside** — device-based, faster, avoids the accessory incision; deploy suture-anchor implants or a suture-hook/relay system directly through the working portal, taking care with implant trajectory near the posterior capsule and popliteal neurovascular bundle, particularly on the lateral side.
   - **Outside-in** — favored for anterior horn/body tears; pass a spinal needle from outside the joint through the tear under direct visualization, shuttle suture through the needle, and tie the suture over the capsule outside the joint; lowest neurovascular risk given the anterior tear location.
7. Place sutures in a vertical mattress orientation where possible (biomechanically stronger, captures more circumferential fibers) spaced roughly 3–5 mm apart along the length of the tear, alternating from superior to inferior surfaces to achieve compression across the tear plane.
8. Assess tissue quality throughout: repair peripheral red-red/red-white tissue with good healing potential; if the tissue is degenerative white-white with poor healing potential, convert to limited debridement of the unstable flap rather than persisting with a repair doomed to fail.
9. On lateral posterior horn repairs, keep vertical-mattress/all-inside device placement away from the popliteal neurovascular bundle — know the posterolateral safe zone (roughly posterior to the lateral collateral ligament and biceps tendon, anterior to the popliteal vessels) and use a protective cannula/retractor for any device deployed in this region.
10. Tie sutures (inside-out) over the capsule through the accessory incision with the knee at approximately 20–30° of flexion, confirming firm apposition of the tear edges without over-constraining the joint through a full range of motion.
11. Perform a final probe test of the repair construct, cycling the knee through flexion-extension to confirm stability of the repair without gapping.
12. If performed with concurrent ACL reconstruction, sequence the meniscal repair either before or after tunnel drilling per surgeon preference, taking advantage of the biologically favorable marrow-venting/hemarthrosis environment of the ACLR case.
13. Close the accessory incision in layers if used, and close portal sites in standard fashion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Rasp or lightly abrade the tear edges and adjacent synovium before suturing — this small step meaningfully improves the local vascular healing response, particularly in red-white zone tissue.
- Use vertical mattress sutures preferentially over horizontal — they capture more circumferential collagen fibers and provide superior pull-out strength compared with horizontal sutures.
- For chronic bucket-handle tears, confirm reduction along the entire length of the fragment, not just at one point — a fragment can look reduced anteriorly while remaining foreshortened or rotated posteriorly.
- When placing an accessory incision for inside-out repair, use a dedicated protective retractor (curved cannula or spoon) between the capsule and the neurovascular bundle before passing any needle — never pass needles "blind" without this protection in place.
- On the lateral side, remember the popliteal tendon hiatus and the popliteus tendon itself are useful anatomic landmarks for staying anterior to the neurovascular structures during posterior horn work.
- Combine techniques when useful — for example, all-inside fixation for the mid-body/anterior segment of a long tear with inside-out fixation reserved for the posterior-most sutures where fixation strength matters most and accessory-incision exposure is already planned.
- Take advantage of concurrent ACLR biology: perform meniscal repair in the same setting whenever possible rather than staging it separately, given the documented improvement in healing rates.
- Always finish with a dynamic probe/range-of-motion check — a repair that looks solid with the knee static in extension can gap with flexion if suture tension or spacing was inadequate.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Incomplete reduction of a chronic bucket-handle fragment before fixation.
- Missing a concurrent ramp or root lesion by probing only the torn segment identified on MRI.
- Repairing degenerative white-white tissue with poor healing potential instead of debriding the unstable flap.
- Device or suture placement too close to the popliteal neurovascular bundle on lateral posterior horn repairs.
- Saphenous nerve (infrapatellar branch) injury with inside-out medial repair or outside-in techniques; peroneal nerve injury with inside-out lateral repair — meticulous posteromedial/posterolateral portal placement and protective retraction are essential.
- Reported re-tear/failure rates run roughly 10–20% for isolated repairs, generally lower with concurrent ACLR (favorable biologic environment from marrow-venting/hemarthrosis).

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weight-bearing**: historically restricted to partial/non-weight-bearing for 4–6 weeks; accelerated protocols (early weight-bearing as tolerated with a hinged brace locked or limited to 0–90°) are increasingly supported by recent systematic-review data showing comparable failure rates with faster return of function. Individualize to tear complexity/location and surgeon preference — the 2024–2025 literature trend favors earlier controlled loading over prolonged protection.
- **ROM**: typically limited to 0–90° for the first 4–6 weeks (avoid deep flexion, which increases posterior horn shear/hoop stress), progressing to full ROM by 6–8 weeks.
- Avoid pivoting, deep squatting, and kneeling until 3–4 months.
- Return to running at ~10–12 weeks; return to cutting/pivoting sport at 4–6 months with criteria-based clearance (quadriceps/hamstring strength symmetry ≥90%, hop-test symmetry ≥90%, no effusion, full painless ROM).
- Return-to-work: sedentary work typically resumes within 1–2 weeks (with brace/crutches as needed); physical/manual labor is deferred to match the return-to-sport milestones above.
- Rehabilitation should follow the 2024 formal EU-US Meniscus Rehabilitation Consensus (ESSKA-AOSSM-AASPT) framework, emphasizing criteria-based (not purely time-based) progression individualized to tear pattern/location, repair construct, and concurrent procedures.
