## Indications

Driven by the NOMS decision framework: high-grade epidural spinal cord compression (Bilsky 2–3) with reasonable surgical/systemic candidacy is the primary indication, **regardless of tumor radiosensitivity**. Mechanical instability (SINS 13–18) is a separate, independent indication for stabilization (open or percutaneous) regardless of ESCC grade or radiosensitivity. Low-grade ESCC with a radiosensitive tumor is generally treated with conventional EBRT alone; low-grade ESCC with a radioresistant tumor is generally treated with SBRT alone (dose escalation feasible without a cord-abutment constraint). Poor systemic status/short life expectancy favors radiation/systemic therapy/best supportive care and surgery should be avoided.

## Positioning & setup

The monograph emphasizes multidisciplinary tumor board discussion (surgery, radiation/medical oncology) before committing to the treatment pathway, and coordination with radiation oncology for postoperative SBRT timing; specific room/positioning detail is not given beyond the posterior approach implied by the technique.

## Steps

1. **Confirm indication via NOMS**: high-grade ESCC (Bilsky 2–3) and/or mechanical instability (SINS 13–18), with acceptable surgical/systemic candidacy.
2. **Perform posterior debulking of epidural tumor** to re-establish approximately **2–3mm of circumferential CSF space** around the thecal sac — this is deliberately not a gross-total-resection goal.
3. **Add stabilization (open or percutaneous)** if SINS scores as unstable, independent of the ESCC-driven decompression goal.
4. **Favor minimally invasive approaches** (percutaneous pedicle screw stabilization ± mini-open decompression) where feasible, to reduce wound complications and blood loss and allow earlier resumption of systemic therapy — an important consideration given limited life expectancy and radiation/chemotherapy-related wound-healing concerns.
5. **Sequence postoperative SBRT** typically within 2–3 weeks once the wound has healed, coordinated directly with radiation oncology.

## Pitfalls

- **Local recurrence** occurs in a minority of patients at one year but is meaningfully lower than with surgery or conventional radiation alone.
- **Wound complications** are elevated by systemic therapy and radiation effects — preoperative radiation is a particular risk factor, which is part of why SBRT is sequenced postoperatively rather than preoperatively.
- **Venous thromboembolism** risk is elevated at baseline in cancer patients.
- **Instrumentation failure** can occur with rapidly progressive disease.
- Real-world access barriers (scheduling conflicts, insurance denials, travel logistics) frequently delay timely postoperative SBRT delivery — a documented gap between the evidence-based NOMS paradigm and its reliable execution that should be actively managed, not assumed to resolve itself.
- A 2025 single-center comparison found separation surgery followed by SBRT produced superior local control and overall survival compared with separation surgery followed by conventional 3D-conformal radiotherapy — reinforcing SBRT, not 3D-CRT, as the preferred adjuvant modality.
- Carbon fiber-reinforced (CFR-PEEK) instrumentation has shown at least equivalent clinical efficacy to titanium while improving postoperative imaging quality (reduced artifact) for radiotherapy planning and recurrence surveillance — a reasonable instrumentation choice to consider, with the CARBOMETASPINE trial underway for prospective confirmation.

## Post-op protocol

- Early mobilization is a priority (goal: ambulate postoperative day 1), given the emphasis on preserving quality of life and function within a limited life expectancy.
- Coordinate postoperative SBRT timing (~2–3 weeks after surgery, once the wound is healed) directly with radiation oncology.
- Rehabilitation goals are functional/palliative (ADLs, pain control, safe mobility) rather than sport- or work-return-oriented; early involvement of palliative care and home health/hospice services as appropriate.
- Minimally invasive stabilization approaches (percutaneous pedicle screw fixation, vertebral augmentation, radiofrequency ablation) are supported by a 2024 systematic review of >3,000 metastatic spine disease patients showing good pain control and reduced perioperative morbidity versus open surgery.
