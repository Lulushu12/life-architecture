## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**, driven by the NOMS decision framework: high-grade epidural spinal cord compression (Bilsky 2–3) with reasonable surgical/systemic candidacy is the primary indication, **regardless of tumor radiosensitivity**. Mechanical instability (SINS 13–18) is a separate, independent indication for stabilization (open or percutaneous) regardless of ESCC grade or radiosensitivity. Low-grade ESCC with a radiosensitive tumor is generally treated with conventional EBRT alone; low-grade ESCC with a radioresistant tumor is generally treated with SBRT alone (dose escalation feasible without a cord-abutment constraint).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:
- Poor systemic status or short life expectancy where the expected surgical morbidity is disproportionate to expected benefit — favor radiation/systemic therapy/best supportive care instead.
- Complete, fixed paralysis present for a prolonged period (generally beyond ~24–48 hours) with low likelihood of neurologic recovery — decompression's neurologic-recovery goal is diminished, though stabilization for pain and mechanical support may still be reasonable.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or medical instability precluding safe anesthesia.
- Diffuse, multi-level circumferential epidural disease not amenable to a focal separation procedure — a different strategy (radiation-only, systemic therapy, or a more extensive open reconstruction) may be more appropriate.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging and staging**: whole-spine MRI to screen for occult/asymptomatic skip metastases (changes surgical and radiation planning); CT for bony architecture and SINS scoring; bone scan or PET for systemic staging.
- **Neurologic baseline**: document ASIA/Frankel grade and ambulatory status — ambulatory status is one of the strongest prognostic and decision-driving variables.
- **Multidisciplinary review**: tumor board discussion (surgery, radiation oncology, medical oncology) before committing to a treatment pathway.
- **Radiation coordination**: plan the postoperative SBRT timing and dosing target with radiation oncology up front, so the surgical wound-healing timeline and the radiation schedule are aligned from the outset.
- **Bleeding risk**: consider preoperative embolization for hypervascular primaries (renal cell carcinoma, thyroid, hepatocellular carcinoma) to reduce intraoperative blood loss.
- **Construct planning**: template percutaneous versus open instrumentation and construct length — typically 2 levels above and below the involved/unstable segment, more in osteoporotic or heavily tumor-involved bone.
- **General optimization**: correct coagulopathy, cross-match blood given the vascularity of many metastatic lesions.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia**; **prone positioning on a radiolucent frame** (Jackson table) for fluoroscopy/O-arm compatibility, with standard prone-spine padding (chest/iliac bolsters, abdomen free, arms <90°, face/eyes protected).
- Arterial line and large-bore IV access, particularly for anticipated blood loss with hypervascular metastases; Foley catheter.
- **Intraoperative neuromonitoring (MEP/SSEP)**, especially important when working immediately adjacent to a cord that is already compressed and potentially already functionally compromised.
- Fluoroscopy or O-arm draped into the field for percutaneous screw placement and level localization; navigation is increasingly used for minimally invasive separation surgery.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the NOMS indication** and finalize the instrumentation plan preoperatively (percutaneous vs open, levels to span).
2. **Place percutaneous or open pedicle screws**, typically 2 levels above and below the involved level(s), before performing the decompression — this establishes a stable frame before posterior element removal.
3. **Perform a laminectomy** at the involved level(s) for direct posterior decompression of the epidural space.
4. **Debulk epidural tumor circumferentially** through a posterolateral corridor around the thecal sac, using curettes, pituitary rongeurs, and bipolar cautery — deliberately targeting **~2–3mm of circumferential CSF space**, not gross-total tumor resection.
5. **Address residual ventral epidural disease**, if significant compression persists after posterolateral debulking, via a transpedicular or costotransversectomy corridor rather than a more aggressive posterior approach.
6. **Achieve hemostasis** — hypervascular metastases (renal cell, thyroid) can bleed heavily even after preoperative embolization; have bipolar cautery, hemostatic agents, and blood products ready.
7. **Add posterior segmental fixation** spanning the involved level(s) if SINS scores as unstable, connecting rods to the previously placed screws.
8. **Consider vertebral augmentation** (cement augmentation of instrumented or adjacent levels) in osteoporotic or tumor-weakened bone to reduce hardware pull-out risk.
9. **Close in layers**; consider a drain given tumor vascularity and the planned postoperative radiation.
10. **Assess wound healing** before proceeding to SBRT simulation/planning with radiation oncology.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Aim for 2–3mm of circumferential CSF space, not gross-total resection — pursuing more aggressive tumor removal increases blood loss and morbidity without improving the local control that SBRT will subsequently provide.
- Favor a minimally invasive (percutaneous screws plus mini-open decompression) approach whenever feasible — it reduces wound complications and blood loss, and shortens the interval before resuming systemic therapy, which matters given limited life expectancy.
- Have hemostatic control measures and blood products staged before starting decompression on hypervascular primaries (renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular), even after preoperative embolization, which reduces but does not eliminate bleeding risk.
- Instrument at least 2 levels above and below the decompressed/unstable segment in tumor-involved or osteoporotic bone to reduce hardware failure risk.
- Sequence surgery before radiation, not after — preoperative radiation is a major wound-healing risk factor, which is why SBRT is deliberately given postoperatively once the wound has healed (typically 2–3 weeks).
- Use intraoperative neuromonitoring when working immediately adjacent to a compressed cord — baseline function may already be abnormal, so a clear preoperative baseline is important for interpreting any intraoperative change.
- Communicate directly and early with radiation oncology about wound-healing status so SBRT is neither delayed unnecessarily nor started before adequate healing has occurred.
- Consider carbon fiber-reinforced (CFR-PEEK) instrumentation to minimize imaging artifact for postoperative SBRT planning and recurrence surveillance.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Local recurrence** occurs in a minority of patients at one year but is meaningfully lower than with surgery or conventional radiation alone.
- **Wound complications** are elevated by systemic therapy and radiation effects — preoperative radiation is a particular risk factor, which is part of why SBRT is sequenced postoperatively rather than preoperatively.
- **Venous thromboembolism** risk is elevated at baseline in cancer patients.
- **Instrumentation failure** can occur with rapidly progressive disease.
- Real-world access barriers (scheduling conflicts, insurance denials, travel logistics) frequently delay timely postoperative SBRT delivery — a documented gap between the evidence-based NOMS paradigm and its reliable execution that should be actively managed, not assumed to resolve itself.
- A 2025 single-center comparison found separation surgery followed by SBRT produced superior local control and overall survival compared with separation surgery followed by conventional 3D-conformal radiotherapy — reinforcing SBRT, not 3D-CRT, as the preferred adjuvant modality.
- Carbon fiber-reinforced (CFR-PEEK) instrumentation has shown at least equivalent clinical efficacy to titanium while improving postoperative imaging quality (reduced artifact) for radiotherapy planning and recurrence surveillance — a reasonable instrumentation choice to consider, with the CARBOMETASPINE trial underway for prospective confirmation.
- **Excessive intraoperative blood loss** from hypervascular primaries — mitigated but not eliminated by preoperative embolization; have hemostatic adjuncts ready regardless.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Early mobilization is a priority (goal: ambulate postoperative day 1), given the emphasis on preserving quality of life and function within a limited life expectancy.
- Coordinate postoperative SBRT timing (~2–3 weeks after surgery, once the wound is healed) directly with radiation oncology.
- Rehabilitation goals are functional/palliative (ADLs, pain control, safe mobility) rather than sport- or work-return-oriented; early involvement of palliative care and home health/hospice services as appropriate.
- Minimally invasive stabilization approaches (percutaneous pedicle screw fixation, vertebral augmentation, radiofrequency ablation) are supported by a 2024 systematic review of >3,000 metastatic spine disease patients showing good pain control and reduced perioperative morbidity versus open surgery.
