## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Vancouver B2 (stem loose, adequate proximal bone stock).
- Vancouver B3 (stem loose, poor proximal bone stock).
- Confirmed by intraoperative manual stress testing when preoperative imaging is equivocal.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active periprosthetic joint infection — a loose stem in the setting of infection needs a staged infection-eradication protocol (typically antibiotic spacer, then delayed reimplantation), not a single-stage aseptic revision.
- Medically unfit patients unable to tolerate the longer operative time and greater blood loss of revision arthroplasty compared with ORIF — in this subgroup, careful multidisciplinary discussion of goals of care, or in truly non-ambulatory/limited-life-expectancy patients, consideration of non-operative palliation, is appropriate.
- Extremely poor bone stock that will not support even a distal-fixation strategy (very rare, seen in massive segmental loss or multiply-revised femurs) may require staged reconstruction or, at the extreme, proximal femoral replacement as a primary salvage rather than an attempted biologic reconstruction.
- A truly well-fixed stem on intraoperative stress testing despite a preoperative B2 appearance — this converts the case to ORIF (see the companion B1/A/C technique) rather than revision.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** full-length femur radiographs are mandatory to plan implant overlap/bypass and to assess distal canal morphology and any pre-existing deformity or prior hardware. CT quantifies osteolysis and cortical bone loss more reliably than plain films — CT consistently reveals larger cavitary defects than radiographs suggest, and this should directly inform stem length and proximal reconstruction planning rather than being treated as confirmatory.
- **Implant/graft selection:** plan for a long, distally fixed tapered-fluted stem (cementless modular or monoblock titanium, or a long cemented stem in select lower-demand patients) sized to bypass the fracture by **≥2 cortical diameters**. Have both modular and monoblock tapered-fluted options available — modular stems allow independent intraoperative optimization of version, offset, and length; monoblock stems carry a lower risk of junction-related fracture/corrosion and are generally preferred when a very long distal fixation segment is needed. For B3 cases, have impaction grafting instrumentation, proximal femoral allograft, and a proximal femoral replacement (megaprosthesis) system all available, since the definitive proximal reconstruction strategy is frequently finalized only after the extent of bone loss is confirmed intraoperatively.
- **Equipment list:** extended trochanteric osteotomy (ETO) instrumentation (oscillating saw, high-speed pencil-tip burr, osteotomes), cerclage cable/wire and cable-plate systems (plan on 3–4 cables to secure the osteotomy), ultrasonic cement removal system if the retained implant was cemented, femoral canal reamers/broaches across the full size range, a full tapered-fluted stem system with trial components, metaphyseal cone/sleeve options for severe proximal deficiency, structural allograft, and a proximal femoral replacement system on standby.
- **Case-specific planning:** confirm the diaphyseal "scratch-fit" isthmus available distal to the fracture/defect — generally ≥4 cm of supportive diaphysis is the practical threshold favoring a standard-length tapered-fluted stem over a long-stem strategy bypassing the defect further.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or combined general/regional anesthesia; regional-only is usually insufficient given anticipated operative length, especially if an ETO and extensive extraction are required.
- Lateral decubitus position on a radiolucent table with secure pelvic fixation (peg-board or well-padded posts) — the position must remain absolutely stable through vigorous manipulation during stem extraction and impaction of the revision stem.
- Prep and drape the entire limb circumferentially from the iliac crest to below the knee to allow full-length fluoroscopic imaging, extensile exposure, and free intraoperative limb manipulation for trial reduction and length/offset assessment.
- Position a large C-arm to image the full femoral length without repositioning; periprosthetic revision is fluoroscopy-intensive both for extraction (confirming complete cement removal) and for final stem seating/bypass confirmation.
- Have blood-conservation measures set up before incision: cell salvage, tranexamic acid per protocol, and blood products immediately available given the substantially higher blood loss of revision compared to primary arthroplasty, particularly with ETO or extensive membrane debridement.
- Mark the prior incision and the planned ETO extent (typically 12–16 cm distal to the tip of the greater trochanter) on the skin using preoperative full-length films as a guide.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm stem looseness.** Reconfirm intraoperatively by direct manual stress testing after exposure, even when preoperative imaging already suggests a loose stem — this is the final gate before committing to revision.
2. **Approach and exposure.** Extend the prior incision proximally and distally as needed; elevate the vastus lateralis off the intermuscular septum, controlling perforating vessels by ligation (large vessels) or electrocautery (small vessels) as they are encountered.
3. **Extended trochanteric osteotomy (when needed for extraction).** Plan the posterior longitudinal limb of the osteotomy to approximately 12–16 cm distal to the tip of the greater trochanter. Complete the transverse distal cut and the posterior longitudinal cut with an oscillating saw, then complete the anterior longitudinal limb by controlled fracture of the remaining intervening bone rather than a full saw cut — this preserves vastus lateralis attachment and the periosteal/vascular supply to the osteotomy fragment, which is essential for the fragment's later healing back to the shaft. Round all osteotomy corners with a high-speed pencil-tip burr to avoid creating a new stress riser at the proximal or distal end of the osteotomy. Hinge the fragment anteriorly, maintaining abductor and vastus lateralis attachment to preserve vascularity throughout.
4. **Implant and cement removal.** With the canal now widely exposed via the ETO, remove the loose stem and all retained cement under direct vision, supplemented by ultrasonic cement-removal instruments and fluoroscopy to confirm a clean, debris-free canal before proceeding to reaming.
5. **Fracture-site debridement and provisional reduction.** Debride the fracture site of hematoma and devitalized tissue while preserving all viable periosteum; provisionally reduce major fragments with cerclage cables or temporary clamps to restore rough length, alignment, and rotation before proceeding to canal preparation.
6. **Canal preparation.** Sequentially ream (or broach, per system) the femoral canal by hand or power to solid endosteal engagement — ream until the reamer engages firmly, checking the reamer for bone shavings as feedback on how much bone is being removed at each pass. Prefer lower reaming velocity where the system allows: higher-speed reaming has been associated with greater micromotion at the implant bed, while lower-velocity preparation produces a more precise fit. Confirm canal preparation fluoroscopically for concentric reaming and to avoid perforation, particularly at any point of prior deformity or thinned cortex.
7. **Trial stem placement (B2, adequate proximal bone stock).** Insert a trial tapered-fluted stem sized to bypass the fracture by **≥2 cortical diameters**, seating it to solid distal diaphyseal scratch-fit fixation — axial and rotational stability come from this distal engagement, not proximal metaphyseal fit. Maximize stem-canal fill at the fixation zone; inadequate fill is a recognized risk factor for postoperative subsidence.
8. **Proximal reconstruction (B3, poor proximal bone stock).** After achieving distal fixation with a long tapered-fluted stem bypassing the defect, address the deficient proximal segment with one of: impaction bone grafting (morcellized allograft impacted circumferentially around a cemented stem, generally reserved for Type IV femurs or younger patients prioritizing bone stock restoration), a proximal femoral allograft-prosthetic composite (structural allograft fixed to host bone with a cerclage/plate construct and a stem cemented into the allograft-host junction), or — in low-demand/elderly patients, or where a durable biologic reconstruction is not achievable — a proximal femoral replacement (megaprosthesis), accepted as a salvage-type solution prioritizing immediate stable function over bone stock restoration.
9. **Reduce and fix the osteotomy/fracture fragments around the definitive stem.** Reapproximate the ETO fragment (and any additional fracture fragments) around the seated stem and secure with cerclage cables — typically 3–4 cables and/or a cable-plate construct — for compression and rotational control; cables have shown less proximal fragment migration than wires in comparative work, supporting cables as the preferred fixation medium over wires where equipment allows.
10. **Final stem and construct assessment.** With trial components (or final components, per system protocol) in place, check leg length and offset restoration, hip stability through a full range of motion, and confirm on fluoroscopy that the stem bypasses the fracture/defect adequately and that all cerclage fixation is secure without residual fracture displacement.
11. **Final implantation.** Impact the definitive stem (or cement the definitive components, per the chosen strategy) to final position; complete modular component assembly (proximal body onto distal stem, if modular) per manufacturer torque/impaction protocol.
12. **Closure.** Repair the vastus lateralis over the construct, confirm hemostasis (perforator vessels are a common source of missed bleeding), place a drain if extensive dissection occurred, and close in layers. Reconfirm neurovascular status distally before leaving the room.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- When performing the ETO, complete the anterior longitudinal limb by controlled fracture rather than a full saw cut through-and-through — this single technical detail preserves the vascularity of the osteotomy fragment and is one of the most consequential steps for reliable fragment healing.
- Round every osteotomy corner with a high-speed burr — sharp corners are a preventable stress-riser and a described mechanism of subsequent fracture through the osteotomy site itself.
- Ream or broach to solid endosteal engagement and check the instrument for bone debris at each pass as real-time feedback on how much bone is being removed — under-preparation risks a poorly seated stem and early subsidence, while reaming at excessive velocity has been associated with greater micromotion at the implant bed than slower, more controlled preparation.
- Maximize stem-canal fill at the diaphyseal fixation zone — this is a modifiable technical factor consistently associated with reduced subsidence risk for tapered-fluted stems.
- Expect and counsel patients that some early nonprogressive subsidence (commonly on the order of a few millimeters in the first six months) is a recognized, usually benign feature of tapered-fluted stem fixation rather than necessarily a sign of failure — distinguish this from progressive subsidence, which is not benign and warrants reassessment.
- Use cables rather than wires when securing the ETO fragment and fracture fixation around the stem — cables show less proximal migration in comparative biomechanical data — and plan on 3–4 cables for secure fixation without over-constraining or devascularizing the fragment.
- Choose modular stems when independent intraoperative optimization of version, offset, and length is anticipated to be needed (e.g., significant proximal deformity or leg-length discrepancy); choose monoblock stems when a very long distal fixation segment is required and junction-related fracture/corrosion risk should be minimized.
- Preserve abductor and vastus lateralis attachment to the ETO fragment throughout — this is the single most important maneuver for maintaining fragment vascularity and, downstream, reliable bony union of the osteotomy back to the shaft.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Underestimating the extent of osteolysis on plain films — CT consistently reveals larger cavitary defects than radiographs; undersizing the planned reconstruction intraoperatively is a common avoidable error.
- Reserve impaction bone grafting for Type IV femurs or younger patients where bone stock restoration is prioritized over the simplicity of a tapered-fluted stem.
- In frail elderly patients, weigh the survival benefit of early full weight-bearing achievable with a well-fixed, distally engaging stem against the slower-to-load construct of ORIF alone.
- Comparative data show tapered-fluted stems achieve reliable diaphyseal purchase with lower intraoperative fracture rates and better functional outcomes than extensively coated cylindrical designs in this setting; cemented long-stem revision around a previously cemented polished tapered stem has shown shorter fracture healing time and lower dislocation/re-loosening rates than uncemented revision in some series — selection should be patient- and bone-quality-specific rather than a universal default.
- Excessive stripping of the vastus lateralis from the ETO fragment, or completing the anterior limb with a full saw cut rather than a controlled fracture, devascularizes the fragment and risks nonunion of the osteotomy itself — a preventable, technique-dependent complication.
- Perforator vessel bleeding during vastus lateralis elevation, and during the extended exposure required for ETO/extraction, is a significant and underappreciated contributor to the substantially higher blood loss of this procedure compared with primary arthroplasty — anticipate and control proactively.
- Failure to achieve adequate distal scratch-fit fixation (inadequate canal fill, under-reaming, or seating in a zone of insufficient diaphyseal isthmus) risks early subsidence; long-term data on monoblock tapered-fluted stem revision report a cumulative 5.2% risk of subsequent periprosthetic fracture over extended follow-up (up to ~24 years), underscoring that this is a durable but not risk-free reconstruction.
- Dislocation, aseptic re-loosening, and periprosthetic joint infection are all elevated versus primary arthroplasty from the longer surgical time and more extensive dissection inherent to revision.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2:** a well-fixed tapered-fluted stem with solid diaphyseal scratch-fit generally allows **weight-bearing as tolerated** immediately — a key advantage over ORIF, particularly in frail elderly patients where prolonged restricted weight-bearing carries its own mortality risk. Impaction grafting or allograft-prosthetic composite reconstructions instead require **protected/partial weight-bearing for 6–12 weeks** to allow graft incorporation.
- **Weeks 2–12:** physiotherapy emphasizes early mobilization, fall-prevention strategy, and abductor/core strengthening; assistive device weaning guided by strength and balance testing.
- **Months 3–6:** functional recovery continues; anticipate a slower overall trajectory than primary arthroplasty given the reconstructive complexity, particularly after proximal femoral replacement or allograft-prosthetic composite.
- Complications to watch for: dislocation (highest immediately post-revision), aseptic re-loosening and periprosthetic joint infection (both elevated versus primary arthroplasty from longer surgical time and more extensive dissection), leg length discrepancy, nerve palsy (sciatic/femoral, particularly with limb lengthening), and hardware failure/recurrent periprosthetic fracture at the proximal end of a long stem/plate.
