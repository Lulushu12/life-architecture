## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- DDH presenting between roughly 6 weeks and 18 months of age (Pavlik-eligible age has passed, or Pavlik has failed) where a concentric, stable reduction can be achieved without excessive tension and without an interposed soft-tissue block.
- Pavlik harness failure — persistent instability/dislocation despite adequate wear, or a hip that reduces but re-dislocates on attempted weaning. Options to consider before proceeding to closed reduction include reapplication of the Pavlik harness for a further trial in carefully selected failures, or switching to a rigid abduction orthosis (Tübingen/Frejka).

**Contraindications / reasons to proceed directly to open reduction instead**
- A safe zone of abduction that remains narrow (<~20°) despite adductor tenotomy.
- Interposed soft tissue on arthrogram blocking concentric reduction — inverted labrum, hypertrophied ligamentum teres, pulvinar, or a constricting transverse acetabular ligament.
- Teratologic dislocation (arthrogrypotic, myelomeningocele-associated) — these are fixed in utero and respond poorly to closed methods; plan for primary open reduction instead.
- Presentation well beyond walking age (thresholds vary by center, generally >18–24 months) — closed reduction success falls and open reduction ± osteotomy is usually planned from the outset.

## Preoperative Planning

- Review the most recent ultrasound (Graf type) or AP pelvis radiograph (IHDI/Tönnis grade) to estimate the difficulty of reduction and anticipate the likely need for adductor tenotomy.
- Confirm the child has had an adequate trial of Pavlik (or documented reasons it was not appropriate) and discuss with family the possibility of proceeding to open reduction in the same anesthetic if closed reduction proves unsafe or unstable.
- Arrange for intraoperative fluoroscopy and arthrography (water-soluble contrast) — this is the core diagnostic tool of the procedure, not an optional adjunct.
- Have spica casting equipment and padding sized appropriately for the child, and arrange for a formal post-reduction cross-sectional imaging study (CT with dose-reduction protocol, or MRI where available/tolerated) to confirm concentricity before the child leaves the OR or shortly after.
- Consent should explicitly cover conversion to open reduction if closed methods fail intraoperatively.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia (endotracheal or LMA per anesthesia preference); a caudal or regional block is a reasonable adjunct for postoperative comfort in the cast.
- Supine on a radiolucent table with the perineum accessible for arthrogram needle placement and the pelvis square/level for symmetric fluoroscopic comparison of both hips.
- Prep both lower extremities and the perineum; the contralateral (normal) hip is a valuable intraoperative fluoroscopic reference.
- Position the C-arm for orthogonal AP views of the pelvis, and confirm equal, symmetric imaging of both hips before starting.
- Mark the planned arthrogram injection site (typically anteriorly, just lateral to the femoral pulse, or medially adjacent to the adductor longus depending on surgeon preference) and the site for percutaneous adductor tenotomy if it proves necessary.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Examine the hip under anesthesia first** — assess passive range of motion, and identify the angle of dislocation (on adduction) and any block to reduction before instrumenting the joint.
2. **Attempt gentle closed reduction** using flexion, abduction, and gentle traction/pressure over the greater trochanter (Ortolani-type maneuver) — never force a reduction; a hip that does not reduce easily on gentle manipulation is more likely to have a structural block.
3. **Perform intraoperative arthrography** — inject water-soluble contrast into the joint under fluoroscopic guidance to outline the cartilaginous femoral head, labrum, and any inverted or interposed structures, and to directly visualize concentricity of the reduced position.
4. **Assess the "safe zone" of abduction** — the arc between the angle of redislocation on adduction and the angle of subluxation/impingement on excessive abduction. A stable arc of at least ~20° is generally regarded as safe before accepting the reduction; a narrow safe zone predicts recurrent instability.
5. **Add percutaneous adductor longus tenotomy** liberally in tight hips to widen a narrow safe zone, rather than accepting it — reassess the arc of stability by re-examining and re-imaging under fluoroscopy after tenotomy.
6. **Re-confirm concentric reduction on arthrogram** after any tenotomy, checking the medial dye pool thickness (an excessively thick medial pool suggests persistent subluxation or a soft-tissue block) and symmetric joint space compared to the normal side.
7. **If reduction remains unstable, non-concentric, or requires an unsafe degree of abduction to maintain**, abandon closed reduction and proceed to open reduction in the same setting rather than accepting a marginal closed result.
8. **Apply a spica cast in the "human position"** — flexion around 90–100° and moderate abduction, deliberately avoiding the extremes of abduction (the frog-leg/forced-abduction position), which is the principal preventable cause of iatrogenic AVN in this pathway. Mold the cast carefully over the greater trochanters and iliac crests for rotational control while keeping it functional (allowing for perineal care and diaper access with a well-trimmed perineal cutout).
9. **Confirm concentric reduction with post-reduction CT or MRI** (not a static radiograph alone) before committing the family to a 10–14 week cast course.
10. **Plan for cast change under anesthesia at approximately 6 weeks**, with total spica duration approximately 12 weeks (range ~10–14 weeks depending on age and reduction stability); reassess stability and skin at each change.
11. **Transition to a removable abduction orthosis** after the spica period, weaned gradually.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Examine the hip under anesthesia before doing anything else — the anesthetized exam alone often changes the plan (some hips reduce far more easily asleep than awake, others reveal an unsuspected block).
- Use the contralateral, normal hip as a live fluoroscopic reference for what a concentric joint space should look like on arthrogram.
- Do not chase a marginal safe zone with increasingly extreme cast positioning — widen the arc with adductor tenotomy instead; a hip that only "stays reduced" in near-maximal abduction is not a safe closed reduction.
- Trim and pad the perineal region of the cast generously at the time of application — a poorly molded perineal cutout is a common source of skin breakdown and caregiver frustration over the following weeks.
- Use a well-padded, three-point mold (over the greater trochanters and pelvis) rather than relying on abduction alone to control rotation and prevent redislocation within the cast.
- Get the cross-sectional confirmatory imaging (CT/MRI) before the child leaves the recovery area whenever feasible — discovering a subtle subluxation days later after the family has gone home is far harder to manage.
- Keep the reduction position as close to "human position" (flexion, moderate — not maximal — abduction) as the safe zone allows; every degree of unnecessary abduction adds AVN risk without adding stability once the safe zone is adequate.
- Counsel the family at the time of casting that a cast change under anesthesia around 6 weeks is planned, not a sign anything went wrong — this reduces mid-treatment anxiety and improves follow-up compliance.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- A subtly subluxated "reduction" left uncorrected on a static radiograph is a common source of late residual dysplasia — always confirm concentricity with post-reduction CT or MRI before committing to a 6–12 week cast.
- Avoid the frog-leg/forced-abduction extreme when molding the cast — this is the single most preventable cause of iatrogenic AVN in this pathway.
- Do not accept a narrow safe zone (<~20°) without first performing percutaneous adductor tenotomy, a low-morbidity adjunct.
- Cast complications to actively monitor: skin breakdown (particularly over the sacrum, perineum, and bony prominences), compartment-type concerns with limb swelling in a snug cast, and soiling/hygiene problems from an inadequately trimmed perineal window.
- Redislocation or loss of reduction within the cast can occur silently — this is the specific reason for interval imaging in the cast, not just at cast change.
- A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of late-presentation DDH found comparable long-term acetabular remodeling outcomes between well-executed closed and open reduction when concentric reduction is achieved, reinforcing that reduction quality — not method alone — is the dominant driver of outcome.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: immobilization in the spica cast, no formal rehabilitation; monitor for cast complications (skin breakdown, compartment concerns with limb swelling); interval radiograph/CT or MRI in cast to confirm maintained reduction.
- **Weeks 6–12**: cast change under anesthesia if planned, continued spica immobilization; total spica duration ~10–14 weeks depending on age and reduction stability.
- **Post-cast (~week 12 onward)**: transition to a removable abduction orthosis, typically weaned over 2–3 months; gentle active range-of-motion play (formal physiotherapy usually not required at this age); interval ultrasound/radiograph surveillance for acetabular remodeling through at least age 2, with periodic follow-up radiographs through skeletal maturity given that a meaningful minority require secondary pelvic osteotomy years later even after successful initial reduction.
- **Return to normal activity**: unrestricted once the orthosis is weaned and reduction/containment is confirmed stable — most toddlers resume normal gait development on their own developmental timeline.
