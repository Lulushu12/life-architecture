## Indications

Gartland III/IV (completely displaced or multidirectionally unstable) extension-type supracondylar humerus fractures — urgent CRPP within 24 hours, emergent if vascular compromise. Also used for select Gartland II fractures with extension posterior fat pad/instability, rotation (IIA with rotation), or comminution, since many centers now pin most type II fractures given a measurable rate of loss of reduction with casting alone.

## Positioning & setup

Reduction is performed under fluoroscopy, restoring the **anterior humeral line** (should pass through the middle third of the capitellum) and **Baumann angle** (64–81°, side-to-side difference <5°) as the reduction targets. When open exploration of the brachial artery/median nerve is needed, a **medial approach** is generally preferred; an anterior approach is an alternative giving direct access to the neurovascular bundle.

## Steps

1. Closed reduction restoring the anterior humeral line and Baumann angle under fluoroscopy.
2. **Lateral-entry divergent pinning (2–3 pins)** is the default fixation when adequate pin spread and divergence at the fracture site can be achieved — this avoids iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury (a real risk with medial pin placement, historically reported up to 3–8%). Avoid parallel, non-divergent pins clustered in a single column, a common technical pitfall that reduces construct rigidity.
3. **Medial-lateral crossed pinning** for highly unstable, comminuted, or type IV fractures — providing superior biomechanical stability — when three well-spread lateral pins cannot be achieved or the fracture is markedly unstable in extension as well as flexion (the hallmark of Gartland IV). Place the medial pin via a mini-open medial approach or with the elbow in less flexion to move the ulnar nerve posteriorly, with direct palpation/protection of the nerve strongly recommended.
4. **Open reduction** for: failure to achieve an acceptable closed reduction, an irreducible fracture (button-holed brachialis/periosteum, the "pucker sign"), open fractures, and the pulseless-white hand requiring exploration.
5. **Pink pulseless hand algorithm** after reduction and pinning: if the hand is well-perfused (pink, warm, brisk capillary refill, normal function) but the radial pulse remains absent by palpation and Doppler, this represents vascular spasm, kinking, or entrapment with adequate collateral perfusion and can be observed with serial vascular checks in most cases rather than mandating immediate exploration. If the hand is poorly perfused/dysvascular (white, cool, delayed or absent capillary refill) despite reduction, this is a vascular emergency requiring immediate surgical exploration and, if needed, vessel repair/reconstruction — do not delay for imaging. Any hand that transitions from pink-pulseless to signs of poor perfusion, or that fails to improve after re-reduction of a malaligned fracture, mandates exploration.

## Pitfalls

- Iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury with medial pin placement (historically reported up to 3–8%) — direct palpation/protection or a mini-open approach reduces this risk.
- Parallel, non-divergent pins clustered in a single column reduce construct rigidity — ensure adequate spread and divergence.
- A single-shot regional nerve block placed before or immediately after fixation in a limb at risk for compartment syndrome is controversial, since a dense block can mask the earliest, most sensitive sign (pain out of proportion/pain with passive stretch); many centers avoid regional blockade in Gartland III/IV fractures with concerning presentation, or use catheter techniques with a defined, closely monitored dosing regimen instead.
- A substantial fraction of pink-pulseless arteries remain occluded on later imaging yet are clinically asymptomatic; immediate exploration in a well-perfused limb has not been shown to improve outcomes and adds surgical risk — but the threshold for exploring a persistently pulseless-but-perfused hand varies across centers and remains an area of active practice variation.
- Serial, structured perfusion reassessment (documented at set intervals) after initial fixation is the safety net in the "observe" pathway.

## Post-op protocol

- **0–3/4 weeks:** well-padded long-arm splint or cast; pins typically removed in clinic at 3–4 weeks once radiographic union/callus is present.
- **After pin/cast removal:** initiate active, unassisted ROM. Avoid passive stretching or formal physiotherapy in the first several weeks — children reliably regain motion on their own, and aggressive early passive therapy is associated with increased pain and heterotopic bone formation rather than faster recovery. Expect motion to lag 2–4 weeks behind the contralateral side initially.
- **By 3 months:** the great majority regain full or near-full motion. Residual mild flexion contracture (<10–15°) is common and usually resolves by 6–12 months without intervention.
- Formal physiotherapy is reserved for motion that plateaus well below expected milestones by 6–8 weeks post-cast-removal.
- **Return to sport:** contact sports/gymnastics/monkey bars typically permitted at 6–8 weeks once radiographic union is confirmed and functional strength/motion has returned.
