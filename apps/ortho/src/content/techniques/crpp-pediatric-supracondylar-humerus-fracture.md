## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Gartland III/IV (completely displaced or multidirectionally unstable) extension-type supracondylar humerus fractures — urgent CRPP within 24 hours, emergent if vascular compromise.
- Select Gartland II fractures with an extension posterior fat pad/instability on stress fluoroscopy, rotation (IIA with rotation), or comminution, since many centers now pin most type II fractures given a measurable rate of loss of reduction with casting alone.
- Any pattern with an associated neurovascular deficit, a floating elbow (ipsilateral forearm fracture), or skin puckering/dimpling anteromedially ("pucker sign," suggesting brachialis/periosteal entrapment).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Gartland I fractures — treat with a long-arm cast; pinning adds risk without benefit.
- Most Gartland II fractures with an intact posterior hinge, no rotational malalignment, and reliable follow-up — closed reduction and casting is appropriate, though the threshold for pinning has broadened (see above).
- A grossly contaminated open fracture requiring formal debridement — proceed to irrigation/debridement first; pin fixation can follow once the wound is addressed, often in the same anesthetic.
- Do not delay reduction of a genuinely dysvascular (white, pulseless) limb to optimize positioning/imaging — this is a surgical emergency that overrides routine sequencing.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: orthogonal (AP and true lateral) elbow radiographs to confirm Gartland grade, direction of displacement (posteromedial vs. posterolateral), and screen for a associated forearm fracture (floating elbow). A posterior fat pad sign with normal alignment should be treated as an occult (Gartland I-equivalent) fracture, not dismissed.
- **Neurovascular documentation**: a complete, documented NV exam (AIN, PIN, median sensory, ulnar motor/sensory, radial pulse, capillary refill) before any manipulation, compared to the contralateral side — this is both clinical care and medicolegal documentation, and must be repeated after reduction/pinning.
- **Implant selection**: 0.062" (1.6 mm) smooth Kirschner wires are standard for most school-age children; 0.045" (1.1 mm) wires may be used in very small/young patients, with a lower threshold to add a third pin for stability rather than relying on thinner wires alone.
- **Equipment list**: image intensifier (mini C-arm) positioned to allow both AP and lateral views without repositioning the arm, a radiolucent hand table or armboard, a wire driver, pin cutters, and a system for bending/capping pins if left percutaneous. Have equipment for a mini-open medial approach (small retractors, headlight if available) on standby in case medial pin placement is needed.
- **Anesthesia coordination**: discuss regional block strategy with the anesthesia team before the case — a dense single-shot block placed in a limb at risk for compartment syndrome or evolving NV compromise is controversial (see Pitfalls); many centers default to general anesthesia without a dense block, or use a catheter with a defined, low-concentration regimen instead.
- **Vascular surgery notification**: for markedly displaced/dysvascular presentations, alert vascular surgery or have a clear escalation pathway in place before starting, even if the plan is closed reduction — do not wait until intraoperative discovery of a non-improving pulse to make that call.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with a secured airway is standard given the need for fluoroscopic manipulation and precise pin placement; regional blockade for postoperative analgesia is used selectively (see Pitfalls regarding masking compartment syndrome).
- **Positioning**: supine, with the affected arm on a radiolucent hand table positioned to allow the C-arm to swing freely for both AP and lateral fluoroscopy without moving the fractured limb (rotate the C-arm around a stationary arm rather than rotating the arm around a stationary C-arm).
- **Table setup**: a small bump or folded towel under the distal humerus can help maintain the reduction position during pinning; some surgeons use a dedicated elbow/hand table attachment.
- **Tourniquet**: a sterile or non-sterile tourniquet is applied high on the arm but is usually not inflated for closed pinning; have it available and ready to inflate if conversion to open reduction is needed.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire arm circumferentially from above the tourniquet to the fingertips, allowing full manipulation of the elbow through flexion/extension and forearm pronation/supination during reduction, and allowing palpation of the medial epicondyle if a medial pin or mini-open approach becomes necessary.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the medial and lateral epicondyles and the olecranon before draping (the "elbow triangle") — this becomes difficult to palpate accurately once swelling obscures landmarks, and is essential for safe medial pin trajectory if needed.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: obtain a baseline AP and lateral of the contralateral, uninjured elbow if there is any question about normal Baumann angle or anterior humeral line in that particular patient — most surgeons do this only selectively, but it removes ambiguity in borderline cases.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Reduction maneuver**: with the forearm pronated (for the typical extension-type fracture with a posteromedial spike, pronation tensions the intact medial periosteal hinge and helps correct medial comminution/collapse — use supination instead if the fracture is posterolateral with an intact lateral hinge), apply longitudinal traction to the forearm with countertraction on the arm, correct any coronal (varus/valgus) and rotational malalignment first, then flex the elbow while applying an anteriorly-directed thumb pressure over the olecranon/distal fragment to reduce the posterior displacement.
2. **Confirm reduction on fluoroscopy**: obtain a true AP (with the elbow extended as much as reduction allows, or a "distal humerus AP" with the beam angled) and a true lateral. Confirm restoration of the anterior humeral line (should intersect the middle third of the capitellum) and Baumann angle (64–81°, side-to-side difference <5° compared with the contralateral elbow if available).
3. **Provisional pin placement**: with an assistant holding the reduction (typically with the elbow hyperflexed beyond 90° for extension-type fractures to keep the fragment reduced, taking care that hyperflexion does not compromise the radial pulse — check pulse/perfusion after positioning), place the first lateral-entry pin from the lateral condyle, starting just anterior to the midline of the distal fragment, aiming proximal and slightly posterior/medial to engage the medial column and far (opposite) cortex.
4. **Second and third lateral pins**: place a second lateral pin divergent from the first (not parallel), entering at a different point on the lateral condyle to maximize spread at the fracture site — the goal is pins that diverge proximally and are spread apart at their distal starting points, not a cluster of parallel pins in a single column, which is a common technical error that markedly reduces construct rigidity. Add a third lateral pin in comminuted, obese, or markedly unstable patterns, or whenever three well-spread lateral pins can be achieved — three lateral pins can match crossed-pin biomechanical stability in most series.
5. **Assess stability**: after 2–3 lateral pins, take the elbow through a gentle range of motion under live fluoroscopy (flexion/extension, and gentle varus/valgus stress) to confirm the fracture does not open or displace. If it does — particularly if it remains unstable in extension as well as flexion, the hallmark of a Gartland IV pattern — proceed to add a medial pin.
6. **Medial pin placement (when needed)**: extend the elbow to 45–60° of flexion (not fully flexed) to move the ulnar nerve posteriorly out of harm's way before placing the medial pin; palpate the medial epicondyle directly, and consider a small (5–10 mm) mini-open incision with blunt dissection down to bone and direct visualization/protection of the ulnar nerve with a hemostat or small retractor before passing the pin, rather than a purely percutaneous "poke" technique. Direct the medial pin proximally and slightly anteriorly to firmly engage the lateral cortex; this trajectory both improves fixation and reduces the risk of tenting/injuring the nerve, which most often is injured when the pin is directed too posteriorly or when placed with the elbow in full flexion (which brings the nerve anterior, into the pin's path).
7. **Final fluoroscopic and clinical check**: confirm pin position and divergence on AP and lateral views, confirm Baumann angle and anterior humeral line are maintained, and reassess the neurovascular exam (motor and, if age-appropriate, sensory) before leaving the OR.
8. **Pin management**: cut pins short and bend them outside the skin (left percutaneous for outpatient removal) in most cases; ensure pin ends are not under tension against the skin, which can cause pressure sores or pin-tract irritation.
9. **Open reduction (when indicated)**: proceed via a medial approach (preferred when brachial artery/median nerve exploration is anticipated) or an anterior approach; identify and protect the neurovascular bundle, extract any entrapped brachialis muscle or periosteum from the fracture site (the "buttonholing" that produces the pucker sign), reduce under direct vision, and pin as above once reduction is achieved. Explore and repair/reconstruct the brachial artery if it is disrupted or clearly non-patent after reduction.
10. **Closure and dressing**: a well-padded long-arm posterior splint or bivalved cast at approximately 70–90° of flexion (avoid hyperflexion in the postoperative splint given swelling and vascular risk over the following 24–48 hours) completes the case.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Reduce the coronal (varus/valgus) and rotational deformity first, before addressing the sagittal (flexion/extension) displacement — a fracture that looks reduced on the lateral view can still be malrotated or in varus, and coronal malalignment is the one that does not remodel and produces cubitus varus.
- Use a true lateral radiograph, not an oblique, to judge the anterior humeral line — a rotated lateral view is a common source of a falsely reassuring or falsely concerning anterior humeral line.
- When placing the first lateral pin, start slightly anterior to the midline of the lateral condyle — starting too posterior makes it difficult to achieve adequate divergence with subsequent pins.
- Check the pulse and hand perfusion immediately after moving the elbow into a hyperflexed reduction position — the brachial artery can kink with hyperflexion even in an otherwise well-reduced fracture, and this is easy to miss if you go straight to pinning.
- If three lateral pins can be spread widely across the fracture site with good divergence, this construct is biomechanically comparable to crossed pinning in most published series and avoids any ulnar nerve risk — do not reflexively add a medial pin "just to be safe" if lateral fixation is already solid.
- For the medial pin, hold the wire close to its sharp tip when passing it through soft tissue to the bone — this improves tactile control and reduces the chance of an errant trajectory through soft tissue near the nerve.
- Direct the C-arm around the stationary arm rather than moving the arm to the C-arm — repeatedly repositioning the arm to get fluoroscopic views risks losing the reduction between pins.
- If reduction is difficult to obtain or maintain, especially with a posteromedial spike, consider that the intact hinge itself may be blocking translation — a small amount of controlled hyperextension/traction, followed by re-flexion, can free a fracture that will not reduce with flexion-first technique alone.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury with medial pin placement (historically reported up to 3–8%) — direct palpation/protection or a mini-open approach reduces this risk; injury is most common when the medial pin is placed with the elbow in full flexion (which brings the nerve anterior into the pin's path) or is directed too posteriorly.
- Parallel, non-divergent pins clustered in a single column reduce construct rigidity — ensure adequate spread and divergence between pins.
- A single-shot regional nerve block placed before or immediately after fixation in a limb at risk for compartment syndrome is controversial, since a dense block can mask the earliest, most sensitive sign (pain out of proportion/pain with passive stretch); many centers avoid regional blockade in Gartland III/IV fractures with concerning presentation, or use catheter techniques with a defined, closely monitored dosing regimen instead.
- A substantial fraction of pink-pulseless arteries remain occluded on later imaging yet are clinically asymptomatic; immediate exploration in a well-perfused limb has not been shown to improve outcomes and adds surgical risk — but the threshold for exploring a persistently pulseless-but-perfused hand varies across centers and remains an area of active practice variation.
- Serial, structured perfusion reassessment (documented at set intervals) after initial fixation is the safety net in the "observe" pathway for a pink, pulseless hand.
- **Pink pulseless hand algorithm**: if the hand is well-perfused (pink, warm, brisk capillary refill, normal function) but the radial pulse remains absent by palpation and Doppler, this represents vascular spasm, kinking, or entrapment with adequate collateral perfusion and can generally be observed with serial vascular checks rather than mandating immediate exploration. If the hand is poorly perfused/dysvascular (white, cool, delayed or absent capillary refill) despite reduction, this is a vascular emergency requiring immediate surgical exploration and, if needed, vessel repair/reconstruction — do not delay for imaging. Any hand that transitions from pink-pulseless to poor perfusion, or that fails to improve after re-reduction of a malaligned fracture, mandates exploration.
- Losing the reduction while repositioning the C-arm between the AP and lateral view is a common, avoidable technical error — anticipate this and have the assistant maintain gentle reduction pressure throughout.
- Cubitus varus (the "gunstock deformity") results from inadequately corrected coronal-plane malunion and does not remodel — this is a prevention problem, not a "revise later" problem; get the Baumann angle right intraoperatively.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–3/4 weeks:** well-padded long-arm splint or cast; pins typically removed in clinic at 3–4 weeks once radiographic union/callus is present.
- **After pin/cast removal:** initiate active, unassisted ROM. Avoid passive stretching or formal physiotherapy in the first several weeks — children reliably regain motion on their own, and aggressive early passive therapy is associated with increased pain and heterotopic bone formation rather than faster recovery. Expect motion to lag 2–4 weeks behind the contralateral side initially.
- **By 3 months:** the great majority regain full or near-full motion. Residual mild flexion contracture (<10–15°) is common and usually resolves by 6–12 months without intervention.
- Formal physiotherapy is reserved for motion that plateaus well below expected milestones by 6–8 weeks post-cast-removal.
- **Return to sport:** contact sports/gymnastics/monkey bars typically permitted at 6–8 weeks once radiographic union is confirmed and functional strength/motion has returned.
