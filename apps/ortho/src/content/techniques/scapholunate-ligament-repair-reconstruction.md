## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- **Acute (<3–6 weeks), primary repairable tissue**: direct SLIL repair with dorsal capsulodesis augmentation, or arthroscopic-assisted reduction and pinning for dynamic instability without adequate tissue for open repair.
- **Chronic, static reducible/fixed without arthritis**: ligament reconstruction.
- **Static fixed with early arthrosis confined to the radial styloid**: radial styloidectomy combined with reconstruction may be considered; once midcarpal arthritis develops, proceed to the SLAC salvage ladder instead.

**Contraindications**
- **Absolute**: established midcarpal or panscaphoid arthrosis (SLAC pattern) — a reconstruction placed against arthritic joint surfaces will fail; treat with the salvage ladder instead. Active infection.
- **Relative**: chronic static-fixed deformity with poor-quality or absent native ligament remnant, where reconstruction success is less predictable and limited fusion may be a more reliable alternative; heavy manual laborers/high-demand athletes with static-fixed instability, who should be counseled about the real possibility of recurrent gapping even after a technically sound reconstruction; inability to comply with the multi-month protected motion protocol (K-wire care, activity restriction) required for healing.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/staging**: PA, lateral, clenched-fist AP, and radial/ulnar deviation stress radiographs, correlated with the Garcia-Elias staging (predynamic, dynamic, static reducible, static fixed, SLAC). Static films are frequently normal in predynamic/dynamic injury — plan for diagnostic wrist arthroscopy in these cases, graded by the Geissler classification (I–IV), as both the confirmatory test and potential first-line treatment.
- **Implant/graft planning**: suture anchors (dorsal ridge of the lunate and/or scaphoid) for direct repair and capsulodesis; FCR tendon harvest planned for modified Brunelli/three-ligament tenodesis; dorsal retinaculum flap planned if a bone-retinaculum-bone graft is chosen; suture-tape implant (internal brace) stocked if augmentation is planned; K-wires (0.045–0.062 in) for SL/scaphocapitate provisional and definitive fixation.
- **Equipment**: wrist arthroscopy tower and small-joint arthroscopic instruments (probe, shaver, thermal device availability though modern use is limited), suture anchor system, tendon harvesting instruments if autograft tenodesis planned, mini C-arm.
- **Case-specific planning**: confirm instability stage before committing to an operative plan, since acute repair, arthroscopic pinning, open tenodesis reconstruction, and radial styloidectomy each require different exposure and instrumentation — arthroscopy at the start of the case is often the deciding step.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Regional block (supraclavicular/axillary) or general anesthesia; supine with the arm on a hand table or dedicated arthroscopy traction tower if arthroscopy is planned first. For arthroscopy, apply finger-trap traction (typically 10–15 lb) through the index and long fingers with the wrist suspended and the elbow supported, allowing distraction of the radiocarpal and midcarpal joints for portal access. For open repair/reconstruction, position supine on a radiolucent hand table with the tourniquet inflated after exsanguination. Prep and drape circumferentially if a combined arthroscopic-then-open approach is planned, and prep the ipsilateral forearm proximally if FCR tendon harvest for tenodesis is anticipated. Mark the standard dorsal wrist arthroscopy portals (3-4, 4-5, 6R, midcarpal RMC/UMC) and the dorsal open-approach landmarks (Lister's tubercle, third extensor compartment) before incision/portal placement.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm instability stage** via stress radiographs and diagnostic wrist arthroscopy (Geissler grading) — this determines which of the following pathways applies and should be done even when the plan appears clear from imaging, since arthroscopy frequently changes the operative plan.

2. **Predynamic/dynamic instability, arthroscopic-first management.** Debride any frayed SLIL tissue with a shaver; for Geissler I–II lesions, arthroscopic debridement alone may suffice. For dynamic (Geissler III) instability with reducible malalignment, reduce the SL interval under arthroscopic and fluoroscopic control and place percutaneous K-wires across the SL joint (and scaphocapitate joint if additional stability is needed) to hold reduction while the ligament heals or scars down.

3. **Acute open repair (<3–6 weeks, repairable tissue).**
   a. Approach dorsally through a longitudinal incision centered over Lister's tubercle; transpose EPL radially, retract the second/fourth extensor compartments, and perform a ligament-sparing (Berger-type) capsulotomy.
   b. Identify the torn dorsal SLIL and place suture anchors into the dorsal ridge of the lunate and/or scaphoid at the ligament's native footprint.
   c. Reduce the SL interval (correcting scaphoid flexion/pronation and lunate extension) and secure the repair with the anchor sutures.
   d. Augment with a dorsal capsulodesis (Blatt technique): elevate a proximally based capsular flap and secure it to the dorsal scaphoid to reinforce the repair and limit scaphoid flexion.
   e. Place K-wires across the SL joint (and scaphocapitate joint as needed) to protect the repair during early healing.

4. **Arthroscopic-assisted alternative for dynamic instability with inadequate tissue for open repair.** Reduce the SL interval under arthroscopic/fluoroscopic guidance and fix with percutaneous SL and scaphocapitate K-wires alone, without formal open ligament repair, when the tissue quality does not support suture anchor fixation.

5. **Chronic static reducible/fixed reconstruction — select technique based on tissue quality, deformity, and surgeon experience.**
   a. **Modified Brunelli / three-ligament tenodesis (Garcia-Elias/Wolfe)**: harvest a strip of FCR tendon (leaving it attached distally), pass it through a bone tunnel in the scaphoid from volar to dorsal, then through/around the dorsal lunate and secure it to recreate both the SL ligament vector and the dorsal intercarpal ligament vector, tensioning with the SL interval held reduced.
   b. **Bone-retinaculum-bone (dorsal capsuloligamentous) autograft**: harvest a strip of extensor retinaculum with bone blocks at each end (typically from the radius and third metacarpal base region per the specific technique), and inset the bone blocks into prepared troughs in the scaphoid and lunate to recreate the dorsal SL ligament directly.
   c. **SL Internal Brace / suture-tape augmentation**: after primary repair or tenodesis reconstruction is completed, drill anchor points (commonly three tunnels — lunate, proximal scaphoid, distal scaphoid) and pass a high-strength nonabsorbable suture tape across the construct, tensioning it with the SL interval reduced to protect the biological repair/reconstruction during the healing period.
   d. **Scapholunate axis method (SLAM) / arthroscopic reduction and association of the scapholunate (ARSL) with suture-tape**: a less invasive arthroscopic-assisted variant placing suture-tape fixation across the reduced SL joint under scope guidance rather than through a full open dorsal exposure.

6. **Provisional and final fixation check.** With any repair/reconstruction tensioned, take the wrist through a passive range of motion intraoperatively to confirm the SL interval remains reduced without excessive tension that would restrict motion, then confirm SL gap and scapholunate angle on fluoroscopy before final tightening/tying of sutures.

7. **Static fixed instability with cartilage preserved.** Proceed with ligament reconstruction (three-ligament tenodesis or bone-retinaculum-bone graft) as above; if intraoperative assessment suggests the reconstruction is unlikely to hold (poor bone/tissue quality, inability to achieve reduction), convert to a limited fusion (e.g., scaphotrapeziotrapezoid or scapholunate fusion) instead.

8. **Radial styloid arthrosis present.** Combine a radial styloidectomy (resecting the distal radial styloid to decompress the radioscaphoid articulation) with the chosen reconstruction technique rather than performing reconstruction alone.

9. **Closure.** Repair the capsulotomy/retinaculum, close in layers, and apply a well-padded long-arm or short-arm thumb-spica splint/cast incorporating the wrist near neutral, protecting the K-wires.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Start with diagnostic arthroscopy even in cases that look clinically and radiographically clear-cut — Geissler grading frequently reveals a different severity than static films suggested and changes the operative plan (debridement alone vs pinning vs open repair).
- Use a ligament-sparing (Berger) capsulotomy for any open dorsal exposure — preserving the dorsal intercarpal and radiocarpal ligaments protects the secondary stabilizers the repair/reconstruction will depend on postoperatively.
- Take the wrist through a full passive range of motion intraoperatively after tensioning any repair or reconstruction, before final suture tying — this confirms the SL interval stays reduced without over-constraining motion.
- When harvesting FCR for tenodesis, leave the distal insertion intact and take only the needed proximal strip — this preserves wrist flexion strength and simplifies the harvest.
- Consider suture-tape internal-brace augmentation liberally in higher-demand patients or when native tissue quality is marginal — it protects the biological repair during the vulnerable early-healing window without replacing it.
- Confirm SL angle and gap on fluoroscopy before leaving the operating room, not just by palpation/visual assessment — subtle residual diastasis is easy to miss without imaging confirmation.
- Protect the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve and superficial radial nerve branches with careful subcutaneous dissection before deepening to the extensor retinaculum — both cross the standard dorsal SL exposure.
- If early radial styloid arthrosis is found intraoperatively but was not anticipated preoperatively, be prepared to add a limited styloidectomy to the reconstruction in the same setting rather than staging a second procedure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Recurrent SL gapping/loss of reduction after ligament reconstruction remains a persistent limitation of classic tenodesis techniques over long-term follow-up — this is the driving rationale behind Internal Brace/suture-tape augmentation, though comparative long-term re-gapping data against classic reconstruction are not yet mature.
- The dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve and superficial radial nerve branches are at risk with dorsal wrist approaches used for SL repair — protect these during exposure.
- Avoid resisted wrist extension/radial deviation loading (e.g., push-ups, heavy gripping in extension) until at least 3 months postoperatively — this loading pattern strains the healing SL construct.
- Once midcarpal arthritis develops, ligament reconstruction is no longer appropriate — transition to the Watson-staged SLAC salvage ladder rather than persisting with a reconstruction that will fail against arthritic joint surfaces.
- Over-tensioning a reconstruction to eliminate all SL motion can restrict overall wrist range of motion permanently — tension to reduce the interval, not to eliminate physiologic scapholunate motion entirely.
- K-wire migration or pin-tract infection can occur during the 8–10 week protection period; monitor pin sites and remove on schedule.
- Failure to perform a ligament-sparing capsulotomy risks sacrificing secondary stabilizing structures, compromising long-term stability even with a technically sound primary repair.

## Postoperative Protocol

K-wire protection typically maintained 8–10 weeks; wrist immobilized in a cast/removable brace during this period with wires in place. After wire removal: begin gentle AROM (weeks 8–10), dart-thrower's-motion pattern training and scapholunate proprioceptive/neuromuscular retraining (a hallmark of modern SL rehab, exploiting the plane of motion that minimizes SL joint strain), progressive strengthening from ~12 weeks, return to sport/heavy labor at 4–6 months. A removable protective orthosis/taping for an additional 4–6 weeks after return to high-risk sport is a reasonable transitional measure.
