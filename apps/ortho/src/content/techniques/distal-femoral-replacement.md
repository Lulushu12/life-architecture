## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Elderly patients (generally ≥65-75 years, individualized by physiologic age/bone quality) with severely comminuted, unreconstructable native distal femur fractures (33-C3), particularly with osteoporotic bone unlikely to hold fixed-angle implants reliably.
- Periprosthetic distal femur fractures around a failed or loose total knee arthroplasty where revision arthroplasty is indicated anyway (Su Type II/III with a loose or worn component).
- Salvage after failed ORIF/nonunion of a distal femur fracture in an elderly, low-demand patient.
- Massive distal femoral bone loss (from tumor resection, infection, or multiply-revised failed fixation) where reconstructable bone stock is insufficient for any internal fixation strategy.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Active or uncontrolled periprosthetic/local infection — treat infection first (debridement, staged antibiotic spacer) before proceeding to definitive DFR; implanting a large metal prosthesis into an infected field risks early catastrophic failure.
- Insufficient proximal femoral bone stock or diaphyseal integrity to accept a stemmed component with adequate fixation length.
- Absent or non-functional extensor mechanism without a reconstructive plan — DFR without a functioning extensor mechanism yields a poor functional result and instability risk.
- Young, high-demand patients with a reconstructable fracture — favor ORIF/combined-construct fixation over arthroplasty given the long-term revision burden of a megaprosthesis in a patient with a long remaining lifespan.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease or an already compromised limb where major reconstructive surgery carries prohibitive risk — consider amputation or non-operative palliation in shared decision-making with the patient/family.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP/lateral femur and dedicated knee films; CT with coronal/sagittal reconstruction to characterize the extent of comminution/bone loss and to assess remaining proximal femoral bone stock for stem fixation. For periprosthetic fractures, obtain prior operative records/implant details if available, and classify by the Su system (Type I: fracture proximal to the femoral component, treat as a standard distal femur fracture; Type II: fracture originating at or near the proximal edge of the component, most challenging for fixation given limited distal purchase; Type III: fracture at or distal to the component with the component itself loose/worn).
- **Implant/templating**: template stem length and diameter against the femoral canal on the contralateral limb if the injured side is too distorted; select a rotating-hinge distal femoral endoprosthesis (from the same implant families used in oncologic megaprosthesis reconstruction) sized to restore native joint line height and mechanical axis. Confirm availability of a range of femoral and (if needed) tibial component sizes, stem lengths (cemented vs press-fit/cementless options), and augment/sleeve options for additional bone loss.
- **Equipment**: standard total knee arthroplasty instrumentation plus the specific hinged-prosthesis system's dedicated cutting guides and trial components, cement and cementing instruments (pulsatile lavage, cement gun, pressurizer) if a cemented stem is planned, and a tourniquet (used selectively — see below) or hemostatic adjuncts (tranexamic acid) given the typically frail, anemia-prone patient population.
- **Case-specific planning**: involve orthogeriatrics/anesthesia early given the comorbidity burden typical of this population; optimize hemoglobin, anticoagulation status, and nutrition preoperatively where the clinical timeline allows, mirroring hip-fracture pathway principles. Favor primary DFR (performed as the index procedure) over ORIF-first with DFR reserved for salvage in appropriately selected frail elderly patients with unreconstructable fractures — comparative evidence shows primary DFR patients, despite being older and more comorbid, have significantly lower reoperation (12.2% vs 23.5%) and complication rates (15.7% vs 43.1%) than salvage DFR performed after failed ORIF.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or neuraxial (spinal/epidural) anesthesia, often combined with regional nerve blocks (femoral/adductor canal block) for postoperative analgesia — minimizing systemic opioid load is particularly important in this frail, delirium-prone population; involve anesthesia/orthogeriatrics in the anesthetic plan as for any hip-fracture-equivalent frail elderly patient.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard operating table with a tourniquet-capable thigh support if a tourniquet is planned, leg free-draped to allow full intraoperative flexion/extension for exposure and trialing, as in any hinged/revision knee arthroplasty. A padded bump or leg positioner can assist with maintaining flexion during component trialing.
- **Draping**: wide circumferential prep and drape from mid-thigh (well proximal to the anticipated stem length and any planned extended incision) to below the knee, keeping the full limb free for intraoperative length and rotational assessment.
- **Tourniquet**: use is individualized — many surgeons avoid a tourniquet or use it only for the initial exposure given the frail, often osteoporotic, vasculopathic population and the value of assessing perfusion/hemostasis directly during a long reconstructive case; tranexamic acid is a useful blood-loss-reduction adjunct in either approach.
- **Fluoroscopy/imaging**: available in the room for confirming stem seating, rotational alignment, and final limb alignment, particularly in periprosthetic or revision cases where anatomic landmarks are distorted.
- **Landmarks**: mark the standard anterior midline (or prior TKA) incision, the planned extensile proximal extension if needed for stem insertion, and palpable landmarks for the femoral epicondyles (rotational reference) before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incise and expose** through the standard anterior midline approach (or the prior TKA incision/scar if periprosthetic), typically with a medial parapatellar arthrotomy, extended proximally as needed to access the zone of comminution/bone loss and to allow safe stem preparation.
2. **Evert or subluxate the patella** and assess the extensor mechanism, remaining collateral ligament attachments, and the extent of bone loss directly.
3. **Remove all comminuted, devitalized, or (in periprosthetic cases) loose implant fragments and cement**, preserving as much of the remaining native metaphyseal/diaphyseal bone, collateral ligament origins, and extensor mechanism attachments as possible — these structures, where salvageable, contribute to soft-tissue tension and rotational stability around the hinge even though the implant's hinge mechanism itself does not depend on them for coronal/sagittal stability.
4. **Resect the distal femur** to a level of healthy bone proximal to the zone of comminution/loosening, using the implant system's cutting guides referenced off the remaining diaphysis (intramedullary referencing) since distal anatomic landmarks are typically destroyed.
5. **Ream and broach the femoral canal** to accept the selected stem, sizing conservatively to avoid further comminution of osteoporotic diaphyseal bone; assess canal fit and decide cemented versus press-fit stem fixation based on bone quality (cemented fixation is generally preferred in osteoporotic or previously instrumented bone).
6. **Trial the femoral and tibial (or tibial-sparing, if only the femoral side is being replaced in a native fracture without existing TKA) components**, assessing flexion-extension gap balance, patellar tracking, and overall limb alignment and length. Check that the patella engages the trochlear groove of the trial component without tilting or impingement across a full range of motion before committing to final components.
7. **Set rotation** of the femoral component using the same reference axes as primary/revision TKA (transepicondylar axis, posterior condylar axis referencing, and/or the Whiteside line) adapted to whatever native landmarks remain identifiable, since supracondylar comminution frequently destroys standard posterior-condylar reference points.
8. **Restore joint line height and limb length** by referencing the contralateral limb and any preserved proximal landmarks — a stemmed trial with adjustable length/offset options facilitates this restoration in the face of significant bone loss.
9. **Cement (or press-fit) the definitive femoral stem and component**, followed by the tibial component using standard technique, then assemble and lock the rotating-hinge mechanism per the specific implant system's instructions.
10. **Recheck patellar tracking, range of motion, and coronal/sagittal stability** through the hinge with the definitive components in place before final wound closure — the hinge is designed to neutralize varus/valgus and anteroposterior forces in the absence of native collateral ligaments, but gross malrotation or malalignment of the components will still produce a poorly functioning or unstable knee.
11. **Achieve meticulous hemostasis**, consider a drain per surgeon preference (frequently omitted or used briefly given TXA use and closure technique), and close in layers over the extensor mechanism repair if the arthrotomy required extensive proximal extension.
12. **Confirm final limb alignment and length** clinically and, if available, fluoroscopically before leaving the OR, comparing to the contralateral limb.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Preserve as much of the collateral ligament and extensor mechanism attachments as possible during exposure and resection — even though the rotating hinge does not require intact collaterals for coronal stability, preserved soft tissue improves overall knee kinematics, patellar tracking, and reduces the risk of extensor lag.
- Reference the femoral canal intramedullary (off the diaphysis) rather than off destroyed distal landmarks when determining resection level and rotation — distal anatomic references are frequently unreliable or absent in the comminuted or periprosthetic fracture setting.
- Always check patellar tracking and engagement in the trochlear groove of the trial components through a full arc of motion before cementing final components — malpositioned rotation is difficult and costly to correct once components are cemented.
- In periprosthetic (Su Type II/III) cases, have the prior implant's operative report or an implant-identification resource available, since removal technique and the anticipated remaining bone stock depend heavily on the existing component's fixation (cemented vs cementless) and design.
- Size the stem conservatively in osteoporotic diaphyseal bone; an oversized stem risks iatrogenic fracture during broaching in bone that is often already compromised.
- Address leg-length and rotational alignment intraoperatively as in any hinged knee arthroplasty, using trial components with adjustable length/offset before committing to final component selection.
- Involve orthogeriatrics/anesthesia early given the comorbidity burden typical of this population — the perioperative medical optimization matters at least as much as the technical reconstruction for this cohort's outcomes.
- Favor primary DFR over an ORIF-first strategy in appropriately selected frail elderly patients with truly unreconstructable fractures — the comparative reoperation and complication data favor earlier, decisive arthroplasty over a prolonged salvage pathway.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- DFR trades a shorter, higher-mortality-risk-adjusted recovery for a permanent large metal implant carrying its own long-term revision burden (aseptic loosening, infection, periprosthetic fracture around the stem) — counsel patients and families accordingly.
- A 2025 five-year district general hospital cohort (24 patients) reported DFR safely deliverable outside tertiary/oncologic centers, but with surgical site infection in 25%, 90-day mortality 8.3%, and 1-year mortality 16.7% — comparable to published tertiary-center data and to hip fracture mortality benchmarks, reinforcing that these patients need the same orthogeriatric, MDT-based perioperative pathway as fragility hip fractures.
- Periprosthetic fracture around the DFR/TKA stem itself is a long-term risk of any endoprosthetic reconstruction and requires the same vigilance and surgical planning as any periprosthetic fracture.
- Deep infection remains a recognized major late-failure mechanism of hinged distal femoral megaprostheses in long-term series and typically requires two-stage revision with an antibiotic spacer; reinfection risk after two-stage revision of a periprosthetic infection around a DFR is notably elevated compared with primary knee arthroplasty infection revision, underscoring the importance of getting the index procedure infection-free.
- Patellar maltracking or instability from malrotated femoral (or tibial) component placement — recheck tracking through full range of motion with trial components before final cementing, since correcting rotation after cementing is difficult.
- Extensor mechanism insufficiency (patellar tendon avulsion, quadriceps rupture) from either the original injury or extensile surgical exposure — plan a reconstructive strategy (allograft extensor mechanism, direct repair with augmentation) preoperatively if the extensor mechanism is compromised.
- Aseptic loosening of the stem over time, particularly with press-fit fixation in osteoporotic bone — cemented stem fixation is generally favored in this population for this reason.
- Iatrogenic diaphyseal fracture during canal preparation/broaching in osteoporotic bone from an oversized stem or aggressive reaming.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Immediate full weight bearing as tolerated is the principal advantage of DFR and should be encouraged from day 1-2 post-op as pain and medical status allow, following standard hinged total knee arthroplasty rehabilitation (early ROM, quadriceps activation, gait training with walker progressing to cane).
- Orthogeriatric/MDT perioperative pathway mirrors hip fracture care: same-day/next-day mobilization, delirium prevention, nutrition, bone-health/osteoporosis treatment initiation, and falls assessment, given the shared frail elderly population and comparable mortality risk profile.
- Lifetime surveillance for prosthetic problems (loosening, wear, late infection, periprosthetic fracture) is required, as with any megaprosthesis reconstruction — establish a long-term follow-up plan at the time of discharge rather than relying solely on symptom-driven re-presentation.
