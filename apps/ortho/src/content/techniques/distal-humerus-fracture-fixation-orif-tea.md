## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- **ORIF (dual-plate fixation):** AO/OTA 13B/C intra-articular distal humerus fractures where the articular block is reconstructable enough to permit a construct rigid enough for early active motion.
- **Non/minimally displaced AO 13A, stable pattern:** cast/splint with early motion may be sufficient, reserving surgery for displaced or unstable patterns.
- **Primary total elbow arthroplasty (TEA):** low-demand elderly patients (typically >65–70 years) with unreconstructable comminution (AO 13C3), severe osteoporosis, or when ORIF cannot achieve a construct stable enough for early motion.
- **Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty:** alternative to TEA in younger, higher-demand patients with unreconstructable fractures, where TEA's permanent lifting restrictions and long-term loosening risk are unattractive; preserves the option of later conversion.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Active local infection or severely compromised soft-tissue envelope over the posterior elbow — treat/stage soft tissues before definitive fixation or arthroplasty.
- TEA is relatively contraindicated in younger, high-demand patients who cannot accept permanent lifting restrictions — favor ORIF or hemiarthroplasty if the articular block is even marginally reconstructable.
- Active or recent deep infection anywhere in the extremity is an absolute contraindication to TEA implantation.
- Non-compliant patients unable to follow lifting restrictions after TEA — counsel carefully or favor ORIF/hemiarthroplasty when feasible.
- Severe medical comorbidity precluding a multi-hour reconstructive procedure — consider damage-control splinting/staged treatment.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP/lateral elbow radiographs plus **CT with 2D/3D reconstruction for essentially all intra-articular (AO 13B/C) fractures** — this is mandatory to characterize the coronal split pattern, quantify comminution, and decide ORIF vs. TEA before the patient is on the table; traction views can help but CT has replaced them for surgical planning in most centers.
- **Templating:** trace the medial and lateral columns and articular fragments on CT to plan screw trajectories that lag the articular block together before it is fixed to the shaft; identify whether a posterior/posterolateral coronal shear (capitellum/lateral trochlear ridge) component exists, since this changes screw direction (anterior-to-posterior lag screws) independent of the column plates.
- **Implant selection:** two 3.5mm precontoured locking distal humerus plates (medial and lateral column-specific sets exist from most major systems); decide orthogonal (90-90) vs. parallel plating preoperatively based on fracture pattern and column bone stock — parallel plating generally provides more distal fixation points and better resists metaphyseal comminution/varus-valgus load, while orthogonal (90-90) has an edge in torsional and AP bending stiffness. A 2024–2025 pooled systematic review comparing constructs found parallel plating trended toward a larger postoperative flexion arc and higher functional (MEPS) scores in some series, while union time was similar between the two configurations — use this to inform, not dictate, plate choice; either is an accepted contemporary standard when executed well.
- **TEA/hemiarthroplasty backup:** for any elderly patient with significant comminution on CT, have a TEA tray opened or immediately available in the room — the intraoperative decision to abandon ORIF for TEA should not require a delay for instrument availability.
- **Equipment checklist:** small fragment and mini fragment sets, headless/small cannulated screws for the articular block, dual distal humerus locking plate sets (both 90-90 and parallel options if uncertain preoperatively), K-wires for provisional fixation, olecranon osteotomy chevron-cut jig/oscillating saw and a pre-contoured olecranon plate or dual tension-band/screw construct for osteotomy fixation (if planned), TEA tray on standby for elderly comminuted patterns, radiolucent hand table, mini C-arm.
- **Case-specific planning:** review the patient's functional demand, bone quality (osteoporosis score/prior DEXA if available), and shoulder/wrist status on the same limb; discuss with the patient preoperatively that the definitive choice between ORIF and TEA may be made intraoperatively once the articular comminution is directly visualized, and consent for both procedures.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia, often combined with a regional block (supraclavicular or infraclavicular brachial plexus) for postoperative analgesia; a long case with tourniquet use is generally better tolerated under general anesthesia with a block as an adjunct rather than block alone.
- **Position:** lateral decubitus (operative arm draped over a padded bolster/arm holder) or prone with the arm flexed over a bolster — both give excellent posterior access for dual-plating and osteotomy; supine with the arm across the chest or on a hand table is an alternative for less comminuted patterns not requiring osteotomy. Lateral decubitus is generally preferred for its stable, reproducible exposure and ease of converting to TEA if needed.
- **Tourniquet:** pneumatic tourniquet on the proximal arm, exsanguinate, inflate to standard limb pressure; document tourniquet time and plan to deflate before final closure if the case is prolonged (dual-column ORIF with an osteotomy commonly runs 2–3+ hours).
- **Fluoroscopy:** mini C-arm brought in from the head or foot of the table (agree on side with the anesthesia team before draping) to confirm articular reduction, screw length, and plate position in AP and lateral/oblique planes, plus a final full flexion-extension check for hardware impingement.
- **Draping:** circumferential extremity prep from above the tourniquet to the fingertips; a sterile bump or bolster under the distal arm allows the elbow to be flexed to 90° or more for posterior exposure.
- **Landmarks marked:** palpate and mark the olecranon tip, both epicondyles, and the subcutaneous border of the ulna before incision — these define the posterior midline incision and the planned osteotomy apex (if used) roughly 2–3cm distal to the olecranon tip, with a "V" or chevron apex pointing proximally into the bare area of the trochlear notch.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a posterior midline incision curving slightly around the olecranon tip (avoid crossing directly over the point of the olecranon to reduce painful scar); raise full-thickness fasciocutaneous flaps medially and laterally.
2. Identify and protect the **ulnar nerve** first, before any bony work — mobilize it from the cubital tunnel proximally and distally between the two heads of FCU, and encircle with a vessel loop; plan for anterior subcutaneous transposition at the end of the case, especially if medial plating or preexisting ulnar symptoms are present.
3. **Choose the approach** based on fracture pattern and articular comminution:
   - **Posterior triceps-sparing (paratricipital/Bryan-Morrey)** or **triceps-splitting** — preserves the extensor mechanism, adequate for AO 13A/B patterns and less comminuted 13C fractures where the articular surface can be visualized/reduced without a full olecranon osteotomy.
   - **Olecranon osteotomy** — provides the best articular exposure for complex C-type fractures with comminution extending to the trochlea; make a chevron-shaped (apex-proximal) osteotomy through the bare area of the trochlear notch (devoid of articular cartilage) using an oscillating saw for most of the cut, finished with an osteotome to create an interdigitating surface that aids later reduction; reflect the olecranon fragment proximally with the triceps still attached. This adds a healing burden (osteotomy nonunion/hardware-irritation risk reported around 15–25% in some series) and violates the extensor mechanism that a later TEA would depend on — avoid in patients where conversion to TEA might eventually be needed, and prioritize fully addressing the distal humerus fracture over minimizing osteotomy-site morbidity, since residual articular incongruity carries greater long-term consequence than an osteotomy complication.
4. Expose and mobilize the medial and lateral columns; identify the radial and ulnar (medial) collateral ligament origins if epicondylar fragments are present, and preserve their soft-tissue attachments.
5. **Reconstruct the articular block first**, working from distal to proximal: reduce and provisionally pin the capitellum-to-trochlea relationship, then the coronal shear/posterior fragments if present, using pointed clamps and K-wires; place interfragmentary lag screws (headless/small cannulated, 2.4–3.5mm) directed from anterior-to-posterior or posterior-to-anterior as the fragment geometry dictates, buried beneath the articular cartilage or outside the weight-bearing arc.
6. Once the articular surface is a single reconstructed unit, reduce this block to the humeral shaft/columns; confirm rotational and coronal alignment under fluoroscopy before applying definitive plates.
7. **Apply the column plates.** Choose orthogonal (90-90: one plate posterolateral, one plate direct medial) or parallel (both plates placed more posteriorly/laterally-medially in the coronal plane, allowing more distal screws to cross and lock into the reconstructed articular block) configuration per preoperative plan and intraoperative bone quality — parallel constructs generally allow more screws to engage the distal articular fragments and are favored when metaphyseal/distal comminution is significant; orthogonal constructs may offer an edge in torsional stiffness. Contour each plate to sit flush on the respective column, place at minimum 3 (ideally more) locking or non-locking screws bicortically in the shaft proximal to the fracture, and angle distal screws to interdigitate with (not collide with) the opposite plate's distal screws and the articular lag screws already placed.
8. Confirm on fluoroscopy (AP, lateral, and oblique views) that no screw has penetrated the joint, that the articular reduction is anatomic, and that plate position does not impinge the olecranon fossa or coronoid fossa in extension/flexion.
9. If an osteotomy was performed, fix it with a pre-contoured olecranon plate, a tension-band construct, or an intramedullary screw with tension-band augmentation, restoring the trochlear notch anatomically — malreduction of the osteotomy itself can produce a new source of ulnohumeral incongruity and stiffness.
10. **Intraoperative decision point:** if, after direct visualization, the articular fragments are unreconstructable (extensive comminution, insufficient subchondral bone for screw purchase) or the patient's bone quality cannot support a rigid enough construct for early motion, **convert to primary TEA** (or hemiarthroplasty in a younger patient) rather than accepting a marginal fixation — this decision should be made early rather than after multiple failed reduction attempts have further devitalized fragments.
11. **If proceeding with TEA:** remove the comminuted articular fragments, prepare the humeral and ulnar canals per the implant system, cement both stems in appropriate flexion/rotation and with correct restoration of the flexion-extension axis, reduce and confirm stable, full-arc motion without impingement, and repair the triceps insertion/anconeus sleeve securely.
12. Take the elbow through a full passive range of motion under direct vision and fluoroscopy to confirm no hardware impingement in extension (olecranon fossa) or flexion (coronoid/radial fossae) and a stable, congruent articulation.
13. **Transpose the ulnar nerve anteriorly** (subcutaneous transposition) if medial plating was performed or preexisting ulnar symptoms were present; otherwise confirm the nerve lies free of hardware and glides without tension through the arc of motion.
14. Irrigate, achieve hemostasis (deflate tourniquet before final closure if not already done), repair the triceps/osteotomy fixation, close the fascia and subcutaneous layers, close skin, and apply a well-padded posterior splint with the elbow at 60–90° flexion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify and protect the ulnar nerve before any osteotomy or bony work — it is easy to injure incidentally during the initial posterior exposure if it is not isolated first.
- Reconstruct the articular block completely and provisionally pin it before attaching it to the shaft — do not try to fix column plates to a partially reduced articular surface, as this locks in malreduction.
- When choosing between orthogonal and parallel plating, let the fracture pattern decide: favor parallel plating when distal/metaphyseal comminution is extensive and more distal screw purchase across both columns is needed; either configuration is acceptable when executed with meticulous distal screw placement.
- Direct interfragmentary lag screws for the articular block outside the arc where the column plates will sit, so the plates do not have to be repositioned around previously placed screws.
- Chevron/apex-proximal osteotomy cuts through the bare (non-articular) area of the trochlear notch interdigitate better on reduction and are more forgiving to fix accurately than a transverse cut.
- Have a TEA tray open (or immediately available) for any elderly patient with significant comminution on preoperative CT — deciding to convert mid-case should never be delayed by instrument unavailability.
- Take the elbow through a full passive arc before final tightening of the plates to confirm no impingement in the olecranon or coronoid fossae — a plate placed slightly too proximal or too thick can block terminal extension or flexion.
- In parallel plating, angle distal screws to interdigitate with, not collide with, the opposite column's screws — plan trajectories on the preoperative CT/templating to avoid this intraoperatively.
- Minimize periosteal stripping of comminuted articular fragments wherever possible — preserving soft-tissue attachments protects blood supply and reduces the (rare but reported) risk of avascular necrosis of the trochlea/capitellum.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Failure to identify and protect the **ulnar nerve** (especially with medial plating) is a leading cause of iatrogenic neuropathy.
- Reserve olecranon osteotomy for cases where exposure is genuinely inadequate otherwise — literature reports osteotomy-site complications (symptomatic hardware requiring removal, nonunion, reoperation) in roughly **15–25%** of cases; avoid it if TEA might be needed later, since it violates the extensor mechanism a TEA depends on.
- Excessive periosteal stripping during ORIF increases the (rare but reported) risk of avascular necrosis of the trochlea/capitellum in high-energy fracture-dislocations — minimize stripping wherever possible.
- Biomechanical and pooled clinical comparisons show orthogonal plating has an edge in torsional/AP bending stiffness, while parallel plating trends toward a larger postoperative flexion arc and higher MEPS scores in some series, with similar union times (roughly 9 vs. 9.5 weeks) between configurations — neither is uniformly superior; match the construct to the fracture pattern and bone stock.
- Screw collision between the two column plates at the distal articular block is a recognized technical pitfall in parallel constructs — plan trajectories preoperatively and confirm with fluoroscopy before final tightening.
- TEA carries a lifelong lifting restriction (~2 kg / 5 lb repetitive, ~5 kg / 11 lb single lift); counsel patients pre-operatively, as this is a permanent trade-off against more reliable early function.
- Propensity-matched data show broadly similar short-term complication profiles between TEA and ORIF once patient factors are controlled, but TEA is associated with higher transfusion risk — relevant for informed consent.
- Persistent postoperative elbow stiffness is the dominant long-term complication of this fracture pattern regardless of fixation choice; a construct that is not rigid enough to permit early active motion has failed the primary technical goal even if radiographically "reduced."

## Postoperative Protocol

**ORIF (stable dual-plate construct):**
- **0–2 weeks:** posterior splint for comfort only if the construct is stable; begin gentle active-assisted ROM (AAROM) within days to 2 weeks.
- **2–6 weeks:** progress to AROM, gentle stretching into extension/flexion, avoid resisted loading.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive strengthening once radiographic union is trending (typically 8–12 weeks); light functional use.
- **3–6 months:** full strengthening; expect a residual extension deficit of 10–15° in many patients as a realistic outcome even with good technique.

**TEA:**
- Lifelong lifting restriction (~2 kg repetitive / 5 kg single lift).
- Earlier return to light ADLs (2–4 weeks) given implant stability.
- Avoid impact/repetitive loading permanently.
