## Indications

- **ORIF (dual-plate fixation):** AO/OTA 13B/C intra-articular distal humerus fractures where the articular block is reconstructable enough to permit a rigid enough construct for early active motion.
- **Non/minimally displaced AO 13A, stable pattern:** cast/splint with early motion may be sufficient, reserving surgery for displaced or unstable patterns.
- **Primary total elbow arthroplasty (TEA):** low-demand elderly patients (typically >65–70 years) with unreconstructable comminution (AO 13C3), severe osteoporosis, or when ORIF cannot achieve a construct stable enough for early motion.
- **Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty:** alternative to TEA in younger, higher-demand patients with unreconstructable fractures, where TEA's permanent lifting restrictions and long-term loosening risk are unattractive; preserves the option of later conversion.

## Positioning & setup

CT with 2D/3D reconstruction should be obtained preoperatively for essentially all intra-articular (13B/C) fractures to plan column-specific fixation and confirm reconstructability before committing to ORIF vs. TEA.

## Steps

1. **Approach selection:**
   - **Posterior triceps-sparing (paratricipital/Bryan-Morrey)** or **triceps-splitting** — preserves the extensor mechanism; use when articular exposure needs are moderate.
   - **Olecranon osteotomy** — provides the best articular exposure for complex C-type fractures with comminution extending to the trochlea, but adds a healing burden (osteotomy nonunion risk) and violates the extensor mechanism that a later TEA would depend on — avoid in patients where conversion to TEA might eventually be needed.
2. **Restore the articular block first** with interfragmentary screws before fixing it to the shaft.
3. **Dual-plate fixation:** apply either **orthogonal (90-90)** or **parallel** 3.5mm locking plate constructs on the medial and lateral columns, converging distally to capture as many articular fragments as biomechanically possible. The goal is a construct rigid enough to permit **early active motion**, since stiffness — not fixation failure — is the dominant long-term complication.
4. **Identify and protect the ulnar nerve** routinely; consider anterior transposition, especially with medial plating or pre-existing ulnar symptoms.
5. **If ORIF cannot achieve a stable-enough construct** (unreconstructable C3 comminution, severe osteoporosis) in a low-demand elderly patient, proceed to **primary TEA** rather than a marginal ORIF that risks later salvage conversion — primary TEA yields more reliable early functional recovery than a failed ORIF converted to TEA later.
6. **Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty** is an option in younger, higher-demand patients with unreconstructable fractures who wish to avoid TEA's lifting restrictions and long-term loosening risk, while preserving the option of later conversion to TEA.

## Pitfalls

- Failure to identify and protect the **ulnar nerve** (especially with medial plating) is a leading cause of iatrogenic neuropathy.
- Reserve olecranon osteotomy for cases where exposure is genuinely inadequate otherwise — it adds a nonunion/hardware-irritation risk and should generally be avoided if TEA might be needed later.
- Excessive periosteal stripping during ORIF increases the (rare but reported) risk of avascular necrosis of the trochlea/capitellum in high-energy fracture-dislocations — minimize stripping wherever possible.
- TEA carries a lifelong lifting restriction (~2 kg / 5 lb repetitive, ~5 kg / 11 lb single lift); counsel patients pre-operatively, as this is a permanent trade-off against more reliable early function.
- Propensity-matched data show broadly similar short-term complication profiles between TEA and ORIF once patient factors are controlled, but TEA is associated with higher transfusion risk — relevant for informed consent.

## Post-op protocol

**ORIF (stable dual-plate construct):**
- **0–2 weeks:** posterior splint for comfort only if the construct is stable; begin gentle active-assisted ROM (AAROM) within days to 2 weeks.
- **2–6 weeks:** progress to AROM, gentle stretching into extension/flexion, avoid resisted loading.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive strengthening once radiographic union is trending (typically 8–12 weeks); light functional use.
- **3–6 months:** full strengthening; expect a residual extension deficit of 10–15° in many patients as a realistic outcome even with good technique.

**TEA:**
- Lifelong lifting restriction (~2 kg repetitive / 5 kg single lift).
- Earlier return to light ADLs (2–4 weeks) given implant stability.
- Avoid impact/repetitive loading permanently.
