## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Symptomatic incomplete AFF (prodromal thigh/groin pain, lateral cortex "beaking"/radiolucent line on plain film or MRI marrow/periosteal edema) — the threshold for fixation is low given the high rate of progression to a complete, displaced fracture if managed non-operatively.
- Any incomplete AFF with a **complete transverse radiolucent line across the lateral cortex**, regardless of symptoms — this is the single strongest radiographic predictor of imminent completion and is a fixation indication on its own.
- Complete AFF (full-thickness, transverse, minimally comminuted fracture through the diaphysis, subtrochanteric-to-supracondylar span) — treated with IM nailing as standard of care.
- Bilateral disease — image and treat both femurs; contralateral progression to a complete fracture during unilateral treatment is a well-documented failure mode.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active local infection at the planned entry site or along the femur — treat/exclude infection first.
- Severe femoral bowing or deformity (post-traumatic, dysplastic, or pagetic) that would make a straight nail impossible to pass without a corrective osteotomy — plan for this pre-operatively rather than discovering it intraoperatively (see Preoperative Planning).
- An asymptomatic incomplete lesion with only mild lateral cortex thickening and no radiolucent line is a relative contraindication to immediate surgery — a protected weight-bearing trial with close radiographic follow-up (6–8 weekly intervals) is reasonable, but counsel the patient that surgery may still become necessary.
- Poor medical fitness for surgery/anesthesia may favor a non-operative trial even in a higher-risk incomplete lesion, accepting the completion risk.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP and lateral radiographs of **both** femurs (hip to knee, ideally on a single long cassette or stitched digital acquisition) with specific attention to the lateral cortex — unilateral imaging is inadequate given the high bilaterality rate. If plain films are normal but clinical suspicion is high (prodromal pain, long-term antiresorptive use), obtain MRI of the symptomatic femur to detect marrow/periosteal edema before a lucent line is radiographically visible.
- **Templating**: measure femoral length (tip of greater trochanter to intercondylar notch) and canal diameter at the isthmus to select nail length and diameter; a **full-length nail** is mandatory — the fracture's diaphyseal span (lesser trochanter to supracondylar flare) means a short cephalomedullary or short antegrade nail risks leaving an unprotected segment of femur that becomes a subsequent fracture site (stress-riser at the nail tip).
- **Assess coronal and sagittal bowing**: AFF patients — particularly with long-term bisphosphonate exposure — frequently have increased anterolateral femoral bowing. Measure the bow radius on the lateral film and compare to the nail's radius of curvature; a standard-curvature nail forced down an excessively bowed canal risks anterior cortex perforation distally or an iatrogenic fracture at the bow apex. For marked bowing (generally >10–15° or where templating shows the nail tip will impinge the anterior cortex), plan either a nail with a matched/reduced radius of curvature or a limited anterior cortical osteotomy/corticotomy to allow safe passage — decide this before draping, not after reaming.
- **Entry point plan**: a piriformis-fossa or trochanteric-tip entry nail system both work, but plan the starting point deliberately (see Step-by-Step) — a medialized trochanteric entry point with focused lateral endosteal reaming has been described specifically to counter the tendency toward varus malreduction in AFF nailing.
- **Implant/equipment**: full-length antegrade cephalomedullary nail (or equivalent long IM nail construct) with proximal interlocking options suited to the fracture location (subtrochanteric fractures need a strong proximal fixation option — cephalomedullary screw/blade — even for AFF given the metaphyseal-adjacent location of some fractures), radiolucent traction table or manual traction setup, large and small fragment reduction clamps, a femoral distractor as backup for a difficult incomplete-fracture reduction, and a full complement of reamers to the isthmus diameter plus 1 mm.
- **Biologic adjunct planning**: discuss teriparatide as an off-label healing adjunct with the treating physician/endocrinologist pre-operatively if the host antiresorptive-suppression biology is severe (long duration of bisphosphonate exposure) — this is a medical, not intraoperative, decision but should be arranged before the day of surgery so it can start promptly post-op.
- **Stop the causative antiresorptive** and correct vitamin D/calcium status before or immediately after surgery — flag this in the pre-op checklist since it is easy to overlook amid the surgical plan.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or neuraxial (spinal/epidural) anesthesia, per patient fitness; these are often older, osteoporotic-bone patients on long-term antiresorptive therapy, so standard fragility-patient anesthetic precautions apply (careful positioning, VTE prophylaxis, blood loss vigilance for reamed nailing).
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent (fracture) table, most surgeons using traction-table positioning for a complete or displaced fracture to aid reduction; for an incomplete, non-displaced lesion undergoing prophylactic nailing, a flat radiolucent table without traction is often sufficient since no reduction is required — decide based on fracture completeness.
- **Table setup**: perineal post well-padded if a traction table is used; the operative leg secured in a boot with countertraction, contralateral leg abducted and flexed out of the fluoroscopy path (or scissored/free-leg technique per surgeon preference) to allow unobstructed lateral fluoroscopy of the hip and full femur.
- **C-arm**: positioned to obtain true AP and lateral views of the proximal femur (entry point), the fracture site, and the distal femur (interlocking) without repositioning the patient — confirm this arc is achievable before draping.
- **Draping**: wide sterile prep and drape from the iliac crest to below the knee to allow full-length nail insertion and distal interlocking through the same field; keep the contralateral femur accessible (or plan a separate prep/drape) if bilateral nailing is being performed in the same anesthetic.
- **Tourniquet**: not used — this is a proximal femoral/diaphyseal procedure with no tourniquet-applicable anatomy.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the greater trochanter tip, the femoral shaft axis, and the planned entry portal incision (just proximal and slightly medial to the trochanteric tip for an antegrade start) before incision; mark the contralateral femur's exam findings on the whiteboard/consent if bilateral surgery is planned so the correct side sequencing is unambiguous.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm diagnosis and rule out mimics.** Re-verify the ASBMR AFF criteria (≥4 of 5 major features) and exclude a pathologic lesion (tumor, Paget disease, focal dysplastic lesion) on the available imaging before proceeding — surgery for AFF assumes suppressed-remodeling biology, not a destructive lesion, and the two are managed very differently.
2. **Position and prep** as above; obtain a baseline fluoroscopic image of the proximal femur to confirm C-arm arcs are adequate before incising.
3. **Establish the entry portal.** For an antegrade nail, make a small longitudinal incision proximal to the trochanteric tip, split the gluteus medius fibers in line, and palpate the trochanteric tip. Choose the entry point deliberately: a starting point at or just medial to the tip of the greater trochanter (rather than laterally displaced) is associated with a lower risk of varus malreduction in AFF nailing — a medialized entry with the guidewire directed slightly lateral-to-medial as it crosses the fracture helps engage the lateral cortex of the distal fragment and correct any tendency toward varus/apex-lateral deformity.
4. **Pass the guidewire** under biplanar fluoroscopy into the canal, confirming it crosses the fracture site centrally on both AP and lateral views before proceeding — do not ream over a malpositioned wire.
5. **Ream sequentially** to 1–1.5 mm over the planned nail diameter, working incrementally and reassessing resistance — AFF cortices are often diffusely thickened, and reaming can be noticeably harder than in a routine femoral shaft fracture. If marked anterolateral bowing was identified on templating, consider **preferential lateral/anterior endosteal reaming at the fracture site** (a focused reaming trick described for AFF nailing) to remove the endosteal "beak" that otherwise blocks full nail seating and promotes malalignment.
6. **Reduce the fracture** (complete fractures only). Use traction, manual manipulation, a percutaneous joystick (Schanz pin) in the proximal or distal fragment, and/or a femoral distractor to correct length, rotation, and — critically — coronal alignment before or during nail passage. Pay specific attention to avoiding **varus** malalignment at the fracture site; because the fracture plane is transverse and the surrounding bone is often bowed, these fractures have a particular tendency to settle into varus once the nail is seated if reduction is not actively maintained during passage.
7. **Pass the nail** slowly under fluoroscopic control across the fracture. If resistance is felt as the nail crosses an area of marked anterior bowing, stop — do not force it; either downsize, choose a nail with a smaller radius of curvature, or perform a limited anterior cortical corticotomy at the point of maximal bowing to allow safe passage without creating an iatrogenic fracture. Confirm on live fluoroscopy that the nail tip is not tenting or perforating the anterior cortex distally as it advances.
8. **Confirm reduction on both planes** once the nail has crossed the fracture — check coronal alignment (no varus/valgus), sagittal alignment (no procurvatum/recurvatum), and rotation (clinical assessment of the limb plus fluoroscopic cortical-step/lesser-trochanter-profile comparison to the contralateral, pre-injury films) before locking.
9. **Proximal interlocking**: place the proximal interlocking screw(s)/blade appropriate to the nail system, ensuring adequate purchase in the femoral head/neck if a cephalomedullary construct is used for a subtrochanteric-level fracture.
10. **Distal interlocking**: place at least two distal interlocking screws (static locking) via the free-hand or targeting-guide technique; static locking is standard given the biologically impaired healing potential of AFF bone — do not rely on friction fit alone.
11. **Final fluoroscopic check**: obtain true AP and lateral images of the entire construct (proximal and distal interlocks, fracture reduction, nail seating) before closure; compare the reduction to the contralateral femur's normal alignment if available.
12. **Closure**: layered closure of the trochanteric and distal incisions; standard wound dressing. No drain is routinely required for a closed nailing.
13. **Contralateral femur**: if bilateral disease meets fixation criteria, repeat the sequence on the contralateral side in the same anesthetic setting where feasible (reduces total anesthetic exposure and addresses the completion risk on both sides simultaneously) — reprep and redrape rather than attempting to work across a single unsterile field switch.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a **medialized (at or just medial to the trochanteric tip) entry point** rather than a laterally displaced start — this is the single most reproducible technical lever for avoiding varus malreduction in AFF nailing.
- When marked anterolateral bowing is present, **preferentially ream the lateral/anterior endosteal cortex** at the apex of the bow rather than reaming concentrically — this removes the endosteal "beak" that otherwise blocks nail passage and forces malalignment.
- Template the nail's radius of curvature against the patient's femoral bow radius pre-operatively; a mismatch is a predictable, avoidable cause of intraoperative iatrogenic fracture or failed passage.
- Keep a femoral distractor open on the back table even for a "simple" incomplete-lesion prophylactic nailing — if the lesion propagates intraoperatively (it can), you will want length/rotation control immediately available.
- Use **static distal interlocking** as the default, not dynamic — AFF bone heals slowly and unpredictably, and dynamization risks shortening/malalignment before adequate biology has had time to consolidate.
- Compare fluoroscopic rotational landmarks (lesser trochanter profile, distal condylar axis) against the contralateral femur's pre-operative films, since AFF patients frequently have symmetric baseline bowing that can be mistaken for malreduction if you only reference "normal" anatomy.
- For bilateral cases, sequence surgery so the more symptomatic/higher-risk side (complete radiolucent line, more pain) is nailed first in case of any intraoperative time constraint.
- If cortical reaming feels unusually resistant compared to a typical femoral shaft nailing, slow down and step reamers up gradually — AFF cortices are often diffusely thickened and can generate more thermal/mechanical stress during reaming than expected.
- Counsel the patient explicitly before surgery that healing will be slower than a standard femoral shaft fracture and that radiographic surveillance will be more frequent — this sets expectations that materially reduce mid-recovery anxiety and premature activity.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Varus malreduction** is the most common and consequential technical error in AFF nailing — it results from a laterally-displaced entry point and/or failure to actively control coronal alignment as the nail crosses the fracture; check alignment fluoroscopically before every interlock, not just at the end.
- **Iatrogenic fracture during nail passage** is a recognized risk in AFF with marked anterior/lateral bowing when nail curvature is mismatched to femoral bow or the nail is forced against resistance — template bowing pre-operatively and be prepared to downsize the nail or perform a corticotomy.
- Accept a **higher delayed-union/nonunion rate** than typical femoral shaft fractures, given the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology — do not equate "nail in good position at 6 weeks with no callus" with failure; continue surveillance before intervening.
- **Contralateral progression** to a complete fracture can occur during unilateral treatment if the other femur is not surveilled and treated — image and reassess the contralateral side at every follow-up visit, not only at the index presentation.
- Roughly **40% of AFFs are bilateral** or have a contralateral incomplete fracture at presentation — unilateral imaging alone is inadequate at diagnosis or during surveillance.
- **Dynamic (versus static) distal locking** risks progressive shortening or malalignment given slow, unpredictable healing biology — use static locking as the default.
- Postoperative **thigh/groin pain persisting or new pain at the contralateral hip** should prompt repeat imaging, not reassurance, given the completion risk described above.
- Do **not resume high-impact loading** or unrestricted return to sport until solid radiographic union is confirmed — generally not before 4–6 months and often longer; premature loading in incompletely healed AFF bone risks implant failure or peri-implant fracture.
- Watch for **peri-implant fracture** at the nail tip if a short nail construct was inappropriately chosen — this reinforces the mandate for a full-length nail spanning the entire diaphyseal/subtrochanteric-to-supracondylar risk zone.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Weeks 0–6:** Protected weight-bearing per fixation and fracture pattern; given the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology, expect a slower biological healing course than a typical traumatic femoral shaft fracture — radiographic surveillance is more frequent.

**Weeks 6–12+:** Progressive weight-bearing as callus/bridging is demonstrated; contralateral femur surveillance continues throughout.

**Beyond 12 weeks:** Delayed union is more common than in standard femoral shaft fractures; do not resume high-impact loading or unrestricted return to sport until solid radiographic union is confirmed, generally not before 4–6 months and often longer.
