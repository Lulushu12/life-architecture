## Indications

**Prophylactic nailing** is indicated for a symptomatic incomplete AFF or any complete radiolucent lateral cortex line, given the high risk of progression to a complete, often displaced fracture. **Complete AFF** is treated with IM nailing as standard. Both femurs should be assessed and nailed if bilateral disease is present, since contralateral progression during unilateral treatment is common.

## Positioning & setup

Full-length nail construct is required given the diaphyseal, subtrochanteric-to-supracondylar span of the fracture pattern. Obtain full-length radiographs of both femurs (lateral cortex focus) pre-operatively — bilaterality or a contralateral incomplete lesion is common enough that unilateral imaging alone is inadequate.

## Steps

1. Confirm the AFF diagnosis against the ASBMR criteria (≥4 of 5 major features) and exclude pathologic causes (tumor, Paget disease, other focal bone lesions).
2. Image the contralateral femur full-length with lateral cortex focus.
3. Discontinue the causative antiresorptive agent; correct vitamin D/calcium.
4. For an incomplete, non-displaced lateral cortex lesion without a full radiolucent line, a protected weight-bearing trial with close radiographic follow-up is reasonable — but the threshold for prophylactic fixation is low given the high rate of progression to complete fracture.
5. For a symptomatic incomplete AFF or a complete radiolucent lateral cortex line, proceed to prophylactic full-length intramedullary nailing.
6. For a complete AFF, perform full-length IM nailing spanning the diaphyseal/subtrochanteric-to-supracondylar extent of the pattern.
7. Assess and nail the contralateral femur if bilateral disease is present.
8. Some surgeons add teriparatide as an adjunct to promote healing — an off-label, biologically-rationalized rather than guideline-mandated practice.

## Pitfalls

- Accept a higher delayed-union/nonunion rate than typical femoral shaft fractures, given the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology.
- Contralateral progression to a complete fracture can occur during unilateral treatment if the other femur is not surveilled and treated.
- Do not resume high-impact loading or unrestricted return to sport until solid radiographic union is confirmed — generally not before 4–6 months and often longer.
- Roughly 40% of AFFs are bilateral or have a contralateral incomplete fracture at presentation — unilateral imaging alone is inadequate at diagnosis or during surveillance.

## Post-op protocol

**Weeks 0–6:** Protected weight-bearing per fixation and fracture pattern; given the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology, expect a slower biological healing course than a typical traumatic femoral shaft fracture — radiographic surveillance is more frequent.

**Weeks 6–12+:** Progressive weight-bearing as callus/bridging is demonstrated; contralateral femur surveillance continues throughout.

**Beyond 12 weeks:** Delayed union is more common than in standard femoral shaft fractures; do not resume high-impact loading or unrestricted return to sport until solid radiographic union is confirmed, generally not before 4–6 months and often longer.
