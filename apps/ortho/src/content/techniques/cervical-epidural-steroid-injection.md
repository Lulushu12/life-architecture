## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Cervical radiculopathy pain not controlled by initial conservative measures (activity modification, NSAIDs, structured PT) — used as a bridge to natural resolution or to avoid/delay surgery within the conventional 6–12 week conservative-care window.
- Diagnostic use (selective nerve root block) to confirm a symptomatic level when imaging is equivocal or multilevel disease is present.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active local or systemic infection, uncorrected coagulopathy or therapeutic anticoagulation not appropriately held.
- Allergy to contrast or injectate components.
- Progressive or severe motor deficit, or myelopathic signs — these are surgical, not injection, indications; do not use ESI to delay decompression once a compressive myelopathic syndrome is symptomatic at moderate/severe grade.
- Uncontrolled diabetes (transient hyperglycemia expected post-injection — counsel and coordinate with the patient's diabetes management).
- Anatomic distortion from prior surgery or radiation at the target level that precludes safe needle trajectory planning without cross-sectional imaging review first.

## Preoperative Planning

- Review recent MRI (and CT if available) before the procedure — specifically the axial T2 images at the target level — to identify the course of the vertebral artery, which can occasionally sit posteriorly within the neural foramen rather than in its typical anterolateral position; this preprocedural roadmap materially changes trajectory planning in atypical anatomy.
- Confirm the symptomatic level clinically and radiographically; in equivocal or multilevel disease, plan a diagnostic selective nerve root block first rather than proceeding directly to a therapeutic injection at a presumed level.
- Select approach (transforaminal vs. interlaminar) based on the desired risk trade-off for that patient's anatomy and pathology (see Anesthesia & Positioning).
- Select injectate: a non-particulate steroid is preferred by many practitioners for the transforaminal approach, given the catastrophic (though rare) consequence of intra-arterial particulate steroid embolization into the vertebral or a radicular feeder artery; particulate steroid is more commonly used interlaminar, where this specific risk is not present.
- Have resuscitation equipment and a documented emergency plan immediately available in the procedure suite given the (rare but reported) risk of spinal cord or brainstem infarction with transforaminal injection.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Performed under local anesthesia at the skin/needle tract, with the patient positioned prone (interlaminar) or prone/oblique (transforaminal) on a fluoroscopy-capable table; mild anxiolysis is reasonable but avoid heavy sedation, since patient-reported paresthesia during needle advancement is a valuable real-time safety signal that oversedation can mask.
- Fluoroscopy (or, in some practices, ultrasound for initial needle guidance with fluoroscopic confirmation) is mandatory for the cervical transforaminal approach given the small but serious risk profile — image guidance is not optional here the way it may be for some lumbar injections.
- Standard skin prep and sterile technique over the posterior or posterolateral neck at the target level; C-arm positioned to obtain a true oblique view of the neural foramen at the target level before the needle enters skin.
- For transforaminal injection, the conventional needle trajectory is roughly 50° oblique from midline, parallel to the lamina; a modified approach — oriented parallel to the ventral surface of the superior articular process, generally 66–72° oblique from midline — has been shown in imaging-based trajectory studies to reduce theoretical vertebral artery penetration risk essentially to zero versus 2–8% (level-dependent) with the conventional trajectory, and to substantially reduce theoretical carotid artery penetration risk as well; consider this modified trajectory, particularly when preprocedural imaging suggests atypical vertebral artery position.
- Regardless of trajectory chosen, target the needle tip at the dorsal (posterior) aspect of the intervertebral foramen — this is the single most important positioning principle for avoiding vertebral artery puncture, since the artery typically runs in the anterior/ventral part of the foramen.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the symptomatic level.** If imaging is equivocal or multilevel disease is present, perform a diagnostic selective nerve root block first; a positive block (concordant, substantial relief) confirms the level and is itself therapeutic.
2. **Position the patient and obtain a true oblique (transforaminal) or AP/lateral (interlaminar) fluoroscopic view** of the target level, opening up the neural foramen or interlaminar window clearly before proceeding.
3. **Anesthetize the skin and superficial soft tissue** with local anesthetic at the planned entry point.
4. **Advance the needle under intermittent fluoroscopic guidance**, using a tunnel-vision (transforaminal) or loss-of-resistance (interlaminar) technique as appropriate, checking AP and lateral views frequently to track depth and trajectory rather than relying on a single view.
5. **For transforaminal approach**: advance toward the dorsal/posterior aspect of the foramen (posterior to the mid-pedicular line on AP view, at or just anterior to the facet column on lateral), using the modified (steeper, superior-articular-process-parallel) trajectory when feasible to keep the needle tip away from the anterior/ventral foraminal zone where the vertebral artery runs.
6. **For interlaminar approach**: advance in the midline or slightly paramedian, using loss-of-resistance technique through the ligamentum flavum into the posterior epidural space, confirming needle depth against the lateral fluoroscopic view relative to the spinolaminar line.
7. **Aspirate before injecting** — check for blood (vascular puncture) or CSF (dural puncture) at the needle hub.
8. **Inject a small volume of contrast under live or digital-subtraction fluoroscopy** and observe the spread pattern in real time: an epidural (transforaminal: perineural/epidural along the nerve root sleeve; interlaminar: epidural filling defect pattern) spread pattern without vascular runoff confirms correct placement; any streaky, rapidly-clearing vascular pattern means reposition the needle before injecting further, even by only 1–2 mm, and re-test with contrast again.
9. **Inject the therapeutic solution** (non-particulate steroid preferred for transforaminal; local anesthetic ± steroid per protocol) slowly, watching for patient-reported symptom reproduction/change and for any new neurologic complaint, which should prompt immediate cessation and needle repositioning or abandonment.
10. **Withdraw the needle, apply a dressing**, and observe the patient briefly in a monitored setting for any immediate neurologic or vascular reaction before discharge.
11. **For facet-mediated axial spondylotic pain** — a distinct clinical problem from radicular pain — use a diagnostic medial branch block rather than an ESI, requiring ≥50–80% relief on at least one, ideally two, controlled blocks before proceeding to radiofrequency ablation (RFA).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Review axial T2 MRI at the target level before every case, not just complex ones — an aberrant, posteriorly-positioned vertebral artery within the foramen is uncommon but is exactly the scenario where a "routine" injection becomes dangerous, and it is only caught by looking beforehand.
- Consider the modified (steeper, ~66–72° oblique, superior-articular-process-parallel) needle trajectory instead of the traditional ~50°-oblique lamina-parallel trajectory — imaging-based trajectory studies report it reduces theoretical vertebral artery contact essentially to zero and meaningfully lowers carotid artery risk as well.
- Always keep the needle tip targeted at the dorsal/posterior foramen, never advance toward the ventral/anterior foramen where the vertebral artery runs, regardless of which trajectory convention is used.
- Use live or digital-subtraction contrast injection, not just a static single fluoroscopic frame, to catch transient vascular uptake that a still image can miss.
- Keep the patient responsive (avoid heavy sedation) so that a paresthesia, radicular pain reproduction, or new neurologic symptom during needle advancement or injection is reported immediately and acted on.
- Favor a non-particulate steroid for any transforaminal injection — the theoretical risk of catastrophic embolic cord/brainstem infarction from intra-arterial particulate steroid is the rationale, even though absolute event rates are low.
- If contrast spread looks vascular even after repositioning, abandon the transforaminal approach at that trajectory rather than persisting — consider an interlaminar approach or rescheduling with cross-sectional imaging review if there is any anatomic ambiguity.
- Document the fluoroscopic images (contrast spread pattern, needle position on AP/lateral) in the procedure note — this both confirms correct technique and is valuable if symptoms recur and repeat injection or surgery is later considered.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Small but serious risk profile with the transforaminal approach (vertebral/radicular artery injury, spinal cord infarction) — always use image guidance and live contrast injection; do not treat cervical transforaminal ESI as a routine, low-risk office procedure.
- Dural puncture and post-dural-puncture headache are the more common (though still uncommon) complication of the interlaminar approach, reflecting its different risk profile (lower vascular, higher dural puncture) compared to transforaminal.
- Evidence supports only short-term benefit: the 2023 AAN systematic review (published 2025), pooling cervical+lumbar radiculopathy data, found ESIs probably reduce short-term pain (NNT ≈ 4) and disability (NNT ≈ 6), with insufficient evidence for long-term pain benefit.
- Cervical-specific injection evidence remains sparser than lumbar — much of the evidence base is extrapolated from lumbar data; counsel patients accordingly rather than over-promising durable relief.
- No role in DCM/OPLL — do not use injections to delay decompression once a compressive myelopathic syndrome is symptomatic at moderate/severe grade; a new or progressive myelopathic exam finding should redirect the patient to surgical evaluation, not another injection.
- Transient post-injection hyperglycemia in diabetic patients — counsel and coordinate monitoring, particularly after a steroid dose.
- Facial flushing, vasovagal response, and transient increased pain (steroid flare) are common, self-limited, and should be distinguished from a true complication when counseling patients beforehand.

## Postoperative Protocol

- ESI is a bridge, not a long-term solution — use it to manage pain during the conventional 6–12 week conservative trial period (sooner reassessment if a progressive deficit develops), while awaiting natural resolution or as a bridge to a decision on surgery.
- Reassess symptom trajectory in the context of the overall conservative-care timeline: most acute radiculopathy from soft disc herniation improves substantially within 4–8 weeks regardless of intervention.
- Advise the patient to avoid strenuous activity for 24 hours and to monitor for any new weakness, worsening headache (dural puncture), fever, or increasing pain/swelling at the injection site, with a clear pathway to contact the practice urgently if these occur.
- For facet-mediated axial pain confirmed by diagnostic medial branch block, proceed to radiofrequency ablation rather than repeated ESI.
- If a diagnostic block was performed, document the concordance and percentage relief carefully — this becomes part of the surgical decision-making record if conservative measures ultimately fail.
