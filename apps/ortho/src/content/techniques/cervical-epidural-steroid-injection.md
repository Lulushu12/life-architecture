## Indications

Cervical transforaminal or interlaminar epidural steroid injection is a reasonable second-line option for cervical radiculopathy pain not controlled by initial conservative measures (activity modification, NSAIDs, structured PT). It is used as a bridge to natural resolution or to avoid/delay surgery. There is no meaningful injection-based adjunct for degenerative cervical myelopathy or OPLL — these are surgical decompressive diseases once symptomatic at moderate/severe grade, and injections have no role in altering the compressive/cord-injury pathophysiology.

## Positioning & setup

Perform under image guidance — fluoroscopy or ultrasound — given the small but serious risk profile (vertebral/radicular artery injury, spinal cord infarction) associated with the cervical transforaminal approach. A non-particulate steroid is preferred by many practitioners for the transforaminal approach; the interlaminar approach is an alternative with a different risk profile (lower vascular risk, higher dural puncture risk).

## Steps

1. **Confirm the symptomatic level**, using a selective nerve root block diagnostically when imaging is equivocal or multi-level disease is present; the same block is therapeutic if positive.
2. **Choose transforaminal vs. interlaminar approach** based on the desired risk trade-off — transforaminal carries vertebral/radicular artery injury and spinal cord infarction risk (mitigated with image guidance and non-particulate steroid); interlaminar carries lower vascular risk but higher dural puncture risk.
3. **Perform the injection under fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance.**
4. **For facet-mediated axial spondylotic pain** (a distinct problem from radicular pain), use a medial branch block as the diagnostic step, requiring ≥50–80% relief on at least one — ideally two — controlled blocks before proceeding to radiofrequency ablation (RFA) rather than ESI.

## Pitfalls

- Small but serious risk profile with the transforaminal approach (vertebral/radicular artery injury, spinal cord infarction) — always use image guidance.
- Evidence supports only short-term benefit: the 2023 AAN systematic review (published 2025), pooling cervical+lumbar radiculopathy data, found ESIs probably reduce short-term pain (NNT ≈ 4) and disability (NNT ≈ 6), with insufficient evidence for long-term pain benefit.
- Cervical-specific injection evidence remains sparser than lumbar — much of the evidence base is extrapolated from lumbar data.
- No role in DCM/OPLL — do not use injections to delay decompression once a compressive myelopathic syndrome is symptomatic at moderate/severe grade.

## Post-op protocol

- ESI is a bridge, not a long-term solution — use it to manage pain during the conventional 6–12 week conservative trial period (sooner reassessment if a progressive deficit develops), while awaiting natural resolution or as a bridge to a decision on surgery.
- Reassess symptom trajectory in the context of the overall conservative-care timeline: most acute radiculopathy from soft disc herniation improves substantially within 4–8 weeks regardless of intervention.
- For facet-mediated axial pain confirmed by diagnostic medial branch block, proceed to radiofrequency ablation rather than repeated ESI.
