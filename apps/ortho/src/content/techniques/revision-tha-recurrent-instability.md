## Indications

- Recurrent dislocation after THA, or a first-time dislocation with an identifiable major structural cause.
- Directed revision follows a work-up identifying the mechanistic cause (component malposition, soft-tissue deficiency, impingement, leg length/offset discrepancy, or hip-spine syndrome) — not empiric constraint without a diagnosis.

## Positioning & setup

- Standard revision hip setup; combined anteversion (cup + stem) assessed by CT pre-operatively.
- Exam under fluoroscopy if the impingement mechanism is unclear.

## Steps

1. Confirm the cause with a directed work-up: standing and supine radiographs, CT for combined anteversion, spinopelvic imaging (standing/sitting lateral radiographs or EOS) when spinopelvic stiffness/hip-spine syndrome is suspected, and exam under fluoroscopy if the impingement mechanism is unclear.
2. Reorient a malpositioned cup or stem — the single most common correctable cause.
3. Increase femoral head diameter and/or restore offset.
4. Convert to a **dual-mobility** construct — increasingly favored first-line for recurrent instability without a clearly correctable structural cause, and in revision settings generally, given effectiveness data and lower reported dislocation rates versus constrained liners in comparative series.
5. Reserve a **constrained liner** for cases with abductor deficiency/absence or recurrent instability after dual mobility/optimized component position has failed — constrained constructs carry a mechanical-failure/dissociation risk of their own and constrain native hip biomechanics.
6. Address abductor insufficiency directly (soft-tissue reconstruction, allograft/mesh augmentation, or acceptance of dual-mobility/constraint as compensation) when present.
7. In hip-spine syndrome (a fused or stiff lumbar spine reducing functional acetabular anteversion changes with position), favor increased combined anteversion tolerance (dual mobility) rather than attempting to "chase" an ideal fixed cup position that a stiff spine will not tolerate through its functional range.

## Pitfalls

- Revising an unstable THA without a clear mechanistic diagnosis — simply "upsizing the head" without addressing malposition or soft-tissue deficiency — has a materially higher failure rate than a directed revision.
- Roughly half of first-time dislocations recur without a structural correction — recurrent instability should never be attributed to "bad luck" and should always prompt a directed work-up.

## Post-op protocol

- Hip precautions (avoid the specific provocative position — posterior for posterior approach, anterior for anterior approach) strictly maintained for **6–12 weeks** minimum, longer if a constrained liner or major soft-tissue reconstruction was performed.
- Abductor strengthening progresses gradually starting around 6 weeks once soft-tissue healing allows.
- Return to unrestricted activity generally 3–4 months with a stable, well-positioned reconstruction.
- Dislocation risk is highest immediately post-revision, particularly with extensive soft-tissue release; aseptic re-loosening and periprosthetic joint infection are both elevated versus primary arthroplasty.
