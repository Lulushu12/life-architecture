## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Recurrent dislocation after THA, or a first-time dislocation with an identifiable major structural cause.
- Directed revision follows a work-up identifying the mechanistic cause (component malposition, soft-tissue deficiency, impingement, leg length/offset discrepancy, or hip-spine syndrome) — not empiric constraint without a diagnosis.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- A first-time, atraumatic dislocation without a clearly identifiable structural cause — trial non-operative management (abduction bracing, activity modification, targeted abductor strengthening) first; most such patients do not require revision.
- Active periprosthetic joint infection presenting as instability (loosening/malposition from septic osteolysis) — treat infection per the appropriate staged protocol rather than performing an isolated stability-directed revision.
- Severe, uncorrectable abductor absence in a patient who is a poor surgical candidate — in this narrow group, a constrained liner or dual-mobility construct combined with realistic counseling about residual dislocation risk (or, rarely, acceptance of a brace-dependent lifestyle) may be more appropriate than repeated revision surgery.
- Proceeding directly to a constrained liner without first attempting to correct a clearly malpositioned component — constraint should compensate for irreducible soft-tissue deficiency, not substitute for correctable component malposition.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** standing and supine AP pelvis radiographs, CT for combined anteversion (cup + stem) — this is the single most useful preoperative measurement for identifying a correctable component-position cause. Spinopelvic imaging (standing and sitting lateral radiographs, or EOS) when spinopelvic stiffness/hip-spine syndrome is suspected — a fused or degenerated, stiff lumbar spine changes functional (dynamic) cup anteversion far more than a mobile spine, and this must be anticipated in component selection, not just diagnosed.
- **Work-up:** exam under fluoroscopy if the impingement mechanism (bony versus prosthetic, anterior versus posterior) is unclear from static imaging alone. Assess abductor status clinically (Trendelenburg) and with MRI (fatty infiltration/atrophy of gluteus medius/minimus) when abductor insufficiency is suspected, since this predicts both dislocation risk and functional outcome after revision.
- **Implant/graft selection:** have both dual-mobility and constrained-liner options available intraoperatively regardless of the preoperative plan, since the final construct choice frequently depends on what is found (abductor quality, cup fixation status, degree of soft-tissue laxity) at surgery. If the existing acetabular shell is well-fixed and appropriately positioned, plan for the possibility of cementing a dual-mobility liner into the retained shell rather than removing a well-fixed component — this "double-socket" technique preserves host bone and minimizes the added morbidity of full acetabular revision, provided the shell's positioning is itself acceptable.
- **Equipment list:** full revision acetabular and femoral trays, dual-mobility system (both cementless modular options and a cementable liner/monoblock cup for the retained-shell scenario), constrained-liner system compatible with the existing or a new shell, femoral head/offset trial range, and abductor reconstruction material (allograft mesh or synthetic augmentation device) on standby if abductor deficiency is anticipated.
- **Case-specific planning:** if cementing a dual-mobility component into a retained shell, confirm compatibility of the liner's outer geometry with the shell's inner geometry and locking-mechanism status beforehand — this is not a universally interchangeable technique across all shell designs.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or combined general/regional (spinal + catheter) anesthesia; a nerve catheter is useful given the longer, more extensive dissection typical of instability revision.
- Lateral decubitus position on a radiolucent table with secure pelvic fixation, allowing free intraoperative manipulation of the limb through the full range of positions that provoke instability (deep flexion with internal rotation, extension with external rotation) for direct intraoperative stability testing.
- Prep and drape circumferentially from above the iliac crest to below the knee to allow full limb manipulation, leg-length assessment, and — if needed — extensile exposure for abductor reconstruction.
- Have fluoroscopy available for intraoperative confirmation of cup position (inclination/version) and, if a constrained or dual-mobility construct is placed into a retained shell, to confirm seating.
- Mark the prior incision(s) and approach; note the approach used at the index surgery, since posterior-approach hips without adequate capsular repair and anterior-approach hips have different characteristic instability mechanisms and inform intraoperative assessment priorities.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the mechanistic diagnosis intraoperatively.** Before committing to any specific revision, correlate the preoperative work-up (component position on CT, spinopelvic mobility, abductor status) with direct intraoperative findings — exposed component position, soft-tissue quality, and provocative range-of-motion testing for impingement.
2. **Approach and exposure.** Re-enter through the prior approach where feasible; assess capsular and abductor integrity directly during exposure — this is often the first concrete evidence of whether soft-tissue deficiency (versus pure malposition) is contributing to instability.
3. **Assess component position.** Expose the acetabular and femoral components sufficiently to assess fixation status (loose versus well-fixed) and position; correlate directly with preoperative CT-measured combined anteversion.
4. **Reorient a malpositioned component — the single most common correctable cause.** If the cup is loose or unacceptably positioned (outside the safe combined anteversion range), revise it to a well-fixed, well-positioned shell. If the cup is well-fixed but malpositioned and revision would sacrifice substantial host bone, consider whether an acceptable correction can be achieved with a liner change alone (e.g., a face-changing or lateralized liner) versus requiring full shell revision — this decision should weigh bone stock preservation against the degree of correction actually achievable by liner change alone.
5. **Correct femoral component malrotation or inadequate offset if present** — femoral-side malrotation is a less common but real contributor and is addressed by femoral revision when identified.
6. **Increase femoral head diameter and/or restore offset.** With the acetabular component acceptably positioned, increasing head size (within compatibility of the retained or new shell) and restoring native offset both increase the jump distance required for dislocation and improve soft-tissue tension.
7. **Convert to a dual-mobility construct** when no single correctable structural cause fully explains recurrent instability, or as the preferred first-line escalation in revision settings generally, given comparative effectiveness and dislocation-rate data favoring dual mobility over constrained liners. If the existing acetabular shell is well-fixed and appropriately positioned, a dual-mobility polyethylene liner (or, per system, a metal dual-mobility liner) can be cemented directly into the retained shell — the "double-socket" technique — preserving host bone while gaining the increased effective head size and jump distance dual mobility provides; confirm liner-shell compatibility and prepare both surfaces (roughening/cleaning the shell interior, drying thoroughly) per standard cementing technique before cement introduction, and cement with the liner held in the corrected, desired final orientation since cup version/inclination can be effectively adjusted at this step even when the underlying shell position is imperfect.
8. **Reserve a constrained liner** for cases with abductor deficiency/absence or recurrent instability after dual mobility/optimized component position has failed — constrained constructs carry a mechanical-failure/dissociation risk of their own and constrain native hip biomechanics; do not select constraint as a default first-line solution.
9. **Address abductor insufficiency directly** when present — soft-tissue reconstruction/repair, allograft or synthetic mesh augmentation, or explicit acceptance of dual-mobility/constraint as biomechanical compensation for an abductor deficiency that cannot be reconstructed.
10. **In hip-spine syndrome**, favor a construct with greater tolerance for combined anteversion variability (dual mobility) rather than attempting to chase a single "ideal" fixed cup position that a stiff spine will move the effective functional position away from through its sit-to-stand arc.
11. **Trial and intraoperative stability testing.** With trial components in the selected configuration, take the hip through provocative positions (deep flexion/internal rotation, extension/external rotation) and confirm stability without impingement before final implantation.
12. **Final implantation and closure.** Implant final components (or complete cementing of a liner into a retained shell, allowing full cement cure time per manufacturer protocol before final reduction). Repair the capsule and any released soft tissue meticulously — capsular repair, particularly in a posterior approach, is itself a modifiable contributor to postoperative stability. Close in layers over a drain if extensive dissection occurred.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- When the existing shell is well-fixed and reasonably positioned, seriously consider cementing a dual-mobility liner into the retained shell rather than removing it — this preserves host bone, is a technically reproducible technique, and effectively corrects functional cup version/inclination at the liner-cementing step even though the underlying shell position is not changed.
- Prepare the interior of the retained shell meticulously before cementing a liner into it (thorough roughening/cleaning and drying of the metal interior) — this is the single most important technical determinant of a durable cement-liner-shell bond.
- Use intraoperative provocative testing (deep flexion/internal rotation for posterior instability, extension/external rotation for anterior instability) with trial components before committing to final implants — this directly confirms whether the planned construct actually resolves the instability mechanism identified preoperatively.
- Correlate CT-measured combined anteversion with what is found intraoperatively — imaging and direct visual/goniometric assessment at surgery occasionally disagree, and the intraoperative finding should generally take precedence for final decision-making.
- In hip-spine syndrome, do not chase a single "ideal" fixed cup position — favor a construct (dual mobility) that tolerates the functional anteversion swing a stiff spine will still produce.
- Repair the capsule meticulously regardless of which component strategy is chosen — capsular repair quality is an independent, modifiable contributor to postoperative stability that is sometimes overlooked when attention is focused on component selection.
- Have both dual-mobility and constrained-liner systems open and available in the room — the intraoperative finding of abductor quality and soft-tissue tension, not the preoperative plan alone, should make the final call between these two options.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that dual-mobility constructs, while effective, carry their own specific late complication — intraprosthetic dislocation (dissociation of the polyethylene liner from the femoral head) — so continued follow-up is warranted even after a successful revision.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Revising an unstable THA without a clear mechanistic diagnosis — simply "upsizing the head" without addressing malposition or soft-tissue deficiency — has a materially higher failure rate than a directed revision.
- Roughly half of first-time dislocations recur without a structural correction — recurrent instability should never be attributed to "bad luck" and should always prompt a directed work-up.
- Cementing a liner into an incompatible shell design, or into a shell with an inadequately prepared interior surface, risks early liner-shell dissociation — confirm compatibility and follow cementing technique meticulously.
- Intraprosthetic dislocation is a specific, described late complication of dual-mobility constructs (dissociation of the polyethylene liner from the femoral head) — include it in the differential for new symptoms after an apparently successful dual-mobility revision, and counsel patients that it remains a risk even with a well-executed construct.
- Selecting a constrained liner as a default rather than reserving it for abductor deficiency or dual-mobility failure exposes the patient unnecessarily to the constrained-liner-specific mechanical failure/dissociation risk without the corresponding benefit.
- Dislocation risk is highest immediately post-revision, particularly with extensive soft-tissue release; aseptic re-loosening and periprosthetic joint infection are both elevated versus primary arthroplasty.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Hip precautions (avoid the specific provocative position — posterior for posterior approach, anterior for anterior approach) strictly maintained for **6–12 weeks** minimum, longer if a constrained liner or major soft-tissue reconstruction was performed.
- Abductor strengthening progresses gradually starting around 6 weeks once soft-tissue healing allows.
- Return to unrestricted activity generally 3–4 months with a stable, well-positioned reconstruction.
- Dislocation risk is highest immediately post-revision, particularly with extensive soft-tissue release; aseptic re-loosening and periprosthetic joint infection are both elevated versus primary arthroplasty.
