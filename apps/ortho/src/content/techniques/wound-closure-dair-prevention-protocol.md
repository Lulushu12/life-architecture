## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Closure and postoperative wound management for any orthopedic implant surgery, with intensified measures for high-risk wounds (obesity, revision surgery, prior wound complications, poor soft-tissue envelope, or high anticipated drainage).
- Goal: prevent the cascade of hematoma → persistent drainage → superficial then deep infection → need for DAIR or two-stage revision, rather than treating it after the fact.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications (to specific elements of the protocol, not to closure itself):**
- **Barbed suture:** relatively contraindicated (or used with caution) in tissue expected to be under high tension or in patients with a history of suture reaction, given case reports of suture extrusion/palpable knots; not a contraindication to layered closure generally, just to that specific suture choice.
- **Incisional NPWT:** avoid over exposed neurovascular structures, actively infected tissue (NPWT over frank infection is a different clinical scenario requiring source control first), or in patients with a known allergy to the dressing adhesive.
- **Silver-impregnated/prolonged occlusive dressings:** avoid in patients with known silver sensitivity; use clinical judgment in heavily draining wounds where prolonged non-inspection could delay recognition of a developing problem — the 5–7 day undisturbed interval assumes a wound that looks reassuring at the outset, not one already trending toward complication.
- **Prophylactic DAIR-style debridement** is not indicated for uncomplicated wounds — DAIR is a treatment for confirmed or highly suspected acute PJI, not a prophylactic maneuver.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Risk stratification:** identify high-risk wounds preoperatively — obesity (many centers use individualized BMI thresholds as relative contraindications to elective arthroplasty), revision surgery, prior wound complications at the same site, poor soft-tissue envelope (thin skin, prior radiation, multiple prior incisions), and anticipated high drainage (e.g., large dead space after tumor resection or complex revision) — so that closure technique and dressing/NPWT decisions are planned rather than improvised at closure.
- **Optimize modifiable SSI/PJI risk factors before the day of surgery:** poorly controlled diabetes (target perioperative glucose <200mg/dL regardless of diabetic status, since even non-diabetic hyperglycemia raises SSI risk), active smoking (cessation counseling, ideally weeks before elective surgery), malnutrition (albumin <3.5g/dL is a recognized independent risk marker — consider nutrition consult/supplementation if time allows), and modifiable immunosuppression (discuss holding/adjusting disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and biologics with rheumatology around the perioperative window).
- **Suture/closure material selection:** decide in advance whether a barbed running suture will be used for the capsular/subcutaneous layers (reduces closure time, comparable complication rates to conventional technique in the arthroplasty literature) and confirm availability; have conventional suture as backup for tissue where barbed suture is not ideal.
- **Dressing selection:** choose the dressing type (silver-impregnated, waterproof, or a transparent-window occlusive dressing) based on anticipated drainage and the planned undisturbed-wear duration; confirm availability before closure so the correct dressing is applied in the OR rather than substituted postoperatively.
- **NPWT equipment:** for identified high-risk wounds, confirm incisional NPWT device availability and appropriate sizing before the case.
- **Anticoagulation plan:** review the planned postoperative VTE prophylaxis regimen against bleeding/hematoma risk at the time of surgical planning, not as an afterthought at closure — this balance directly determines wound-complication risk downstream.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Not independently positioning- or anesthesia-dependent — wound closure and dressing technique are applied at the end of whatever approach and positioning the index procedure required.
- **Field/drape considerations relevant to closure:** maintain sterile technique through to dressing application; avoid contaminating the wound edges during final irrigation and closure, particularly after a long case where drape adhesion may have loosened.
- **Tourniquet interaction (if used):** where a tourniquet has been used, plan whether deflation will occur before or after final closure — deflating before skin closure allows a genuine hemostasis check on a reperfused field, catching bleeding points that a bloodless field would hide, at the cost of some additional time and a less pristine view for the final closure layers.
- **Landmarks:** no closure-specific skin marking; ensure planned incision/closure lines were marked appropriately at the start of the case per the index procedure's approach.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Irrigate the wound thoroughly** before closure (pulsatile lavage per surgeon preference/volume protocol) to clear debris, reduce bacterial load, and allow a clean view of any residual bleeding.
2. **Achieve meticulous hemostasis** before beginning closure — cauterize or ligate bleeding points systematically rather than relying on closure tension or dressing pressure to control ongoing ooze; this is the single highest-yield step in preventing the hematoma that seeds later infection.
3. **Close the deep/capsular layer first**, tension-free, using either conventional interrupted sutures or a running barbed suture technique. If using barbed suture for a running capsular closure, anchor proximally and close distally with a locking loop, maintaining uniform tension throughout the wound rather than let tension build unevenly toward the finishing end.
4. **Close the subcutaneous layer** in a similar fashion, reapproximating soft tissue to eliminate dead space (a major driver of postoperative hematoma/seroma) without strangulating tissue with overly tight sutures.
5. **Close the skin** using the surgeon's preferred technique (subcuticular running suture, staples, or adhesive strips/skin glue) — avoid excessive electrocautery near the skin edge during any final adjustments, since thermal injury at the wound margin impairs healing and raises dehiscence/infection risk.
6. **Reassess for any missed bleeding points**, particularly if a tourniquet was used and deflated only after closure began — a final check before dressing application catches problems that are far easier to fix now than postoperatively.
7. **Apply the planned dressing.** For standard-risk wounds: an occlusive dressing (often silver-impregnated or waterproof), applied to lie flat without wrinkling, left undisturbed for an extended initial period (commonly 5–7 days), with a transparent inspection window if the product allows.
8. **For identified high-risk wounds, apply incisional NPWT** instead of a standard dressing — obesity, revision surgery, prior wound complications, poor soft-tissue envelope, or high anticipated drainage are the indications; ensure a good adhesive seal around the entire wound margin, as an inadequate seal (common in skin folds or over irregular contours) undermines the negative-pressure mechanism and can itself cause skin irritation.
9. **Document the closure technique, suture materials, and dressing type used** in the operative note — this becomes relevant reference information if a wound complication develops later.
10. **Monitor for persistent drainage beyond postoperative day 5–7** — the single most consistently cited trigger for early surgical wound review and PJI workup. Address it proactively (wound review, consider incisional NPWT if not already in place, optimize anticoagulation intensity, prolonged elevation) rather than reassurance and waiting.
11. **If acute PJI is confirmed** (early postoperative, or acute hematogenous with less than approximately 3 weeks of symptoms and a stable, well-fixed implant), proceed to DAIR: thorough debridement of all visibly compromised tissue, exchange of modular components (liner/head, or equivalent), and pathogen-directed antibiotics with implant retention. Success depends heavily on speed — do not delay this step once acute PJI is confirmed while awaiting definitive culture speciation if the clinical picture is already clear.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- **Barbed running suture for the deep/capsular layer** meaningfully shortens closure time and, in the TKA-specific literature, produces comparable wound-complication rates and improved early recovery metrics versus conventional interrupted closure in some randomized comparisons — a genuinely useful default for standard cases, though conventional technique remains entirely reasonable.
- For proximal-wound exposure difficulty during closure (common at the proximal pole in knee incisions), **flex the joint and use a tenaculum to grasp and approximate the proximal tendon/capsule edge** before placing sutures — this dramatically eases visualization and suture placement compared to fighting extension-position tension.
- **Eliminate dead space deliberately** at the subcutaneous layer, not just the deep layer — dead space is where postoperative hematoma and seroma actually accumulate, and it is far easier to close out intraoperatively than to manage as a postoperative drainage problem.
- If deflating a tourniquet before final skin closure, **build the extra 5–10 minutes for a genuine post-reperfusion hemostasis check into your case timing** — it is one of the highest-value few minutes in the entire case for preventing a return trip to the OR for hematoma evacuation.
- **Match dressing choice to anticipated drainage, not habit** — a wound flagged preoperatively as high-risk should leave the OR already under incisional NPWT rather than a standard dressing with a plan to "step up if it looks bad," since the highest-risk period for hematoma/seroma formation is the first 24–72 hours, before a standard-dressing wound would typically be reassessed.
- **Avoid disturbing the dressing unnecessarily** during the 5–7 day window — every dressing change is a discrete opportunity for bacterial ingress, and transparent-window products let you confirm the wound looks appropriate without breaking the seal.
- When placing incisional NPWT, **take extra care with the adhesive seal over skin folds or irregular contours** (common in obese patients, who are also the population most likely to need NPWT) — an incomplete seal both defeats the negative-pressure mechanism and risks skin maceration/irritation under the leaking edge.
- **Treat anticoagulation dosing as a wound-care decision, not solely a VTE-prevention decision** — the same drug/dose choice that adequately prevents VTE can, if too aggressive for that specific patient's bleeding risk, directly cause the hematoma that starts the infection cascade; this is a balance to revisit explicitly rather than apply a single protocol blindly.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Success rates for DAIR fall sharply the longer symptom duration or biofilm maturity is allowed to progress** — this is the underlying rationale for aggressive early wound surveillance rather than a "wait and see" approach to drainage.
- **Prolonged wound drainage should not be escalated straight to wound review without first addressing anticoagulation intensity, dressing optimization, and prolonged elevation** — these are the first-line measures before wound review, and skipping them wastes the first, cheapest opportunity to resolve the problem.
- **Incisional NPWT's evidence base is stronger in trauma than in arthroplasty specifically:** a 2023 eClinicalMedicine meta-analysis found high-certainty evidence of SSI reduction with iNPWT broadly, and orthopedic-trauma-specific meta-analyses (2023, 2025) support benefit in that population, but arthroplasty-specific trial data remain more heterogeneous — treat the "high-risk-only" arthroplasty indication as an area of continued refinement rather than settled practice.
- **Do not use routine incisional NPWT in low-risk primary arthroplasty wounds** — benefit has not been demonstrated in that population, only in the higher-risk subgroup, and routine use adds cost and a foreign device without a clear offsetting benefit in this group.
- **Excessive electrocautery at the skin edge** during closure is an underrecognized contributor to wound-margin necrosis and subsequent dehiscence/infection risk — minimize thermal injury during final closure adjustments.
- **An inadequately sealed NPWT dressing** (common over skin folds) both fails to deliver the intended negative-pressure benefit and can cause skin maceration at the leak site — inspect the seal carefully at application and be willing to reapply rather than accept a partial seal.
- **Barbed suture extrusion or a palpable knot/suture end** is an occasional late complication reported with barbed suture use — counsel patients on what a small palpable subcutaneous nodule near the incision might represent rather than assuming it is a new mass or abscess.
- **Missed intraoperative bleeding points under a bloodless (tourniquet-inflated) field**, closed without a post-deflation check, is a preventable cause of postoperative hematoma — recognized postoperatively as an expanding, tense, painful thigh/knee with disproportionate drain output or swelling in the first 24–48 hours; if it occurs, have a low threshold to return to the OR for evacuation rather than observe an expanding hematoma, since hematoma is the direct precursor to the drainage → infection cascade this whole protocol exists to prevent.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Dressings left undisturbed for 5–7 days where feasible, with inspection through a transparent window as needed.
- Wound review triggered at postoperative day 5–7 if drainage persists, escalating to incisional NPWT consideration (if not already in place) or formal PJI workup.
- DAIR (debridement, antibiotics, implant retention) if acute PJI is confirmed within the early postoperative or acute hematogenous (<3 weeks symptoms, stable implant) window.
- Bone-health/secondary-fracture-prevention follow-up continues in parallel for fragility-fracture patients, independent of wound status.
