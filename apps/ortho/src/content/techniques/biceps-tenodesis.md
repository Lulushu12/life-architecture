## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Long head of biceps (LHB) tendon pathology (tendinopathy, subluxation/instability, partial tearing) whether isolated or concomitant with rotator cuff repair or SLAP-associated instability.
- Tenodesis is favored in younger, active, or cosmetically concerned patients who want to preserve the length-tension relationship and forearm supination/elbow flexion strength.
- Tenotomy is favored in older, lower-demand, sedentary patients where cosmesis and strength are lower priorities, or where operative time/complexity must be minimized.
- In SLAP tears specifically, tenodesis is increasingly favored over labral repair beyond age 35–40, in revision settings, or with concomitant biceps pathology, given comparable-or-better return-to-play/reoperation rates.
- Failed or symptomatic prior SLAP repair with persistent biceps-related pain.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active local infection at the planned incision site.
- Isolated LHB tendinopathy without instability or structural tear that has not yet had a trial of nonoperative management (activity modification, PT, sheath injection) — exhaust nonoperative options first in appropriate candidates.
- Severe osteoporosis or poor bone quality at the fixation site without a fixation strategy adapted for it (e.g., favor cortical button/bicortical fixation over interference screw alone).
- Patients unwilling to accept the sling/activity-restriction period required for tendon-to-bone healing — tenotomy may be a more realistic option in this group despite the cosmetic trade-off.
- Concomitant untreated subscapularis tear/pulley lesion driving biceps instability — address the subscapularis at the same setting, or tenodesis alone will not resolve the underlying problem.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI or MR arthrogram to characterize LHB pathology (tendinopathy, partial tear, subluxation) and to specifically evaluate the subscapularis and biceps pulley (coracohumeral ligament, superior glenohumeral ligament) for concomitant tears that co-drive instability.
- **Fixation site and construct selection**: decide subpectoral vs suprapectoral fixation preoperatively based on surgeon preference and whether concomitant arthroscopic work (cuff repair, SLAP) makes an all-arthroscopic suprapectoral technique more efficient, versus an open subpectoral approach for definitive groove clearance and lower residual groove pain.
- **Implant choice**: interference screw, suture anchor, or cortical button — no strong comparative evidence favors one method; select based on surgeon experience, bone quality, and whether biortical fixation is desired for maximal pull-out strength (relevant in higher-demand/athletic patients).
- **Equipment**: standard arthroscopy tower for the intra-articular/subacromial portion, plus open subpectoral instrumentation (retractors, drill/tap system matched to the chosen implant, sizing dilators for tendon diameter) if performing open subpectoral fixation.
- **Concomitant procedures**: confirm whether cuff repair, SLAP repair/debridement, or subscapularis repair will be performed at the same setting, and sequence the case so the biceps is addressed at a logical point (commonly after cuff/labral work, before final closure).
- **Patient counseling**: discuss the tenodesis-versus-tenotomy trade-off explicitly, including the ~30–50% Popeye deformity risk with tenotomy, and confirm patient priorities (cosmesis/strength vs procedural simplicity and faster initial recovery).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with an interscalene regional block, as for concomitant arthroscopic shoulder procedures; the block also provides analgesia for the open subpectoral incision if performed.
- **Position**: beach-chair position is generally preferred when a subpectoral approach is planned, since it allows direct access to the proximal humeral shaft/pectoralis major insertion with the arm adducted at the side; lateral decubitus is acceptable if the procedure is entirely arthroscopic (suprapectoral) and performed alongside other arthroscopic work already set up in that position.
- **Table setup**: standard shoulder positioner for beach-chair; arm free-draped to allow full adduction/extension and external rotation for subpectoral exposure — this arm position is a specific technical point (see Tips) that increases the safety margin from the musculocutaneous nerve.
- **Draping**: wide exposure from the base of the neck to the mid-humerus to permit both arthroscopic portal access and an open axillary/subpectoral incision if needed.
- **No tourniquet or fluoroscopy required** for standard biceps tenodesis.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: acromion, coracoid, deltopectoral interval, and the inferior border of the pectoralis major tendon insertion (the key landmark for subpectoral tunnel placement).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Perform diagnostic arthroscopy first** to confirm LHB pathology (fraying, partial tearing, subluxation/dislocation from the bicipital groove) and to inspect the subscapularis and pulley system specifically — do not proceed to tenodesis without confirming the biceps is truly the pain generator or structurally compromised.
2. **Release the LHB from its glenoid (labral) origin arthroscopically**, tagging the tendon stump with a traction suture so it can be retrieved at the fixation site and does not retract proximally out of reach.
3. **Choose the fixation site**: for **suprapectoral** tenodesis, proceed arthroscopically, tensioning and fixing the tendon just proximal to the pectoralis major tendon within or just above the bicipital groove; for **subpectoral** tenodesis, make a short (~3–4 cm) incision along the inferior border of the pectoralis major tendon insertion, dissect down through the deltopectoral interval to the humeral shaft just distal to the pectoralis major insertion, and deliver the tagged LHB tendon into the wound.
4. **Protect the musculocutaneous nerve during subpectoral exposure**: keep the arm in external rotation (this moves the nerve further from the field) and limit medial retraction — the nerve typically lies medial to the surgical field but comes measurably closer with internal rotation and aggressive medial retraction.
5. **Prepare the tendon**: whip-stitch or Krackow-stitch the distal ~2–3 cm of the tendon stump to create a secure suture construct for shuttling and tensioning, and debride any degenerated tendon beyond the healthy segment.
6. **Prepare the bone tunnel/socket**: drill orthogonal to the humeral shaft at the selected fixation point (for interference screw or cortical button techniques), sized to the tendon diameter; for subpectoral fixation, a drilling axis roughly 1 cm proximal to the inferior border of the pectoralis major tendon insertion keeps the far-cortex perforation an adequate distance from the radial nerve.
7. **Select and place the fixation construct**: interference screw (tendon and screw both seated into a single socket), suture anchor (tendon sutures tied over anchors placed in the humeral cortex), or cortical button (suture shuttled through a bicortical tunnel and secured over the far cortex) — no strong evidence favors one method; base the choice on surgeon experience and bone quality, favoring bicortical/button fixation for maximal pull-out strength in higher-demand patients or softer bone.
8. **Set tension with the elbow at ~20–30° flexion** to replicate native length-tension before final fixation — this reference position matters as much as the fixation method itself.
9. **Secure final fixation** (seat the interference screw, tie sutures over the anchor, or shuttle and tension the cortical button), then cycle the elbow through flexion-extension and forearm pronation-supination to confirm secure, non-impinging fixation.
10. **If performing tenotomy instead**, simply release the LHB from its origin arthroscopically without formal distal fixation, allowing the tendon to retract into the bicipital groove/upper arm — counsel the patient explicitly on the expected Popeye deformity before doing so.
11. **Address concomitant pathology at the same setting**: if performed alongside cuff repair or SLAP surgery, sequence the biceps tenodesis logically within the broader case rather than staging it separately.
12. **Close in layers**: repair the deltopectoral interval loosely if opened for subpectoral access, close subcutaneous tissue and skin, and apply a sterile dressing and sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Tag the LHB tendon with a secure traction suture at the moment of glenoid-origin release — a released, untagged tendon can retract out of easy reach and turn a straightforward tenodesis into a more difficult retrieval.
- Keep the arm in external rotation during subpectoral exposure — this measurably increases the working distance to the musculocutaneous nerve compared with internal rotation, and is one of the simplest, most effective ways to reduce nerve risk.
- Visualize the musculotendinous junction of the LHB directly before it is removed from the wound during subpectoral dissection, to avoid iatrogenic injury to adjacent neurovascular structures during blind tendon delivery.
- Reference a drilling axis roughly 1 cm proximal to the inferior border of the pectoralis major tendon insertion for the humeral tunnel — this keeps the far-cortex exit point at a safe distance from the radial nerve during bicortical fixation.
- Whip-stitch or Krackow-stitch a generous length (2–3 cm) of the tendon stump — a short stitch length risks suture pull-through under tension during final fixation.
- Set tension at 20–30° of elbow flexion consistently across cases — this single reference point does more to standardize outcomes than the specific implant chosen.
- When treating biceps instability, always inspect and, if needed, repair the subscapularis/pulley at the same setting — tenodesis alone will not resolve recurrent symptoms if an unaddressed pulley lesion persists.
- Limit medial retraction during subpectoral exposure to the minimum needed for visualization — aggressive retraction, not just arm position, is a modifiable contributor to musculocutaneous nerve risk.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Over-tensioning at fixation causes loss of elbow flexion/supination strength and groove pain; under-tensioning risks a residual Popeye-like bulge — set tension at ~20–30° elbow flexion to replicate native length-tension.
- Tenotomy carries a ~30–50% risk of a visible Popeye deformity and a small but real risk of cramping/fatigue discomfort — counsel patients explicitly if choosing this simpler option.
- Groove/residual biceps pain can occur after suprapectoral tenodesis; subpectoral fixation may reduce this.
- When biceps instability is present, always evaluate the subscapularis specifically — pulley lesions/subscapularis tears are a common co-driver of biceps instability that will not resolve with tenodesis alone if left unaddressed.
- No fixation method (interference screw, suture anchor, cortical button) has demonstrated clear superiority — do not over-invest in a specific construct at the expense of tensioning technique.
- The musculocutaneous nerve, radial nerve, and deep brachial (profunda) artery all course near the subpectoral operative field; overall complication rate for subpectoral tenodesis is reported around 2%, including infection, fixation failure, cosmetic deformity, cramping, humeral fracture, and nerve injury — internal rotation of the arm and aggressive medial retraction both measurably decrease the working margin to the musculocutaneous nerve and should be avoided.
- Humeral shaft fracture through an oversized or malpositioned drill hole is a rare but recognized complication, particularly in osteoporotic bone or with an overly large tunnel relative to shaft diameter — size the tunnel to the implant, not larger.
- Reaction to a biodegradable interference screw (localized inflammatory response) is a described, if uncommon, cause of persistent postoperative pain — consider this in the differential for unexplained pain after screw fixation that does not resolve with routine rehabilitation.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Sling 2–4 weeks.
- Avoid resisted elbow flexion/forearm supination for 6 weeks to protect fixation.
- Progressive strengthening from 6–8 weeks.
- Return to heavy lifting/sport at 3–4 months.
