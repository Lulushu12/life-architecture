## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- The definitive procedure for moderate-to-large ulnar-positive variance causing ulnocarpal impaction, whether or not a TFCC tear is present.
- Simultaneously tightens the TFCC/radioulnar ligaments (improving DRUJ stability) and tensions the interosseous membrane.
- Chronic lunotriquetral instability with ulnar-positive variance, where unloading the LT joint via USO often resolves symptoms even without direct ligament repair.

**Contraindications**
- **Absolute**: active infection; ulnar-neutral or ulnar-negative variance (shortening would create iatrogenic ulnar-negative impaction/radial-sided overload).
- **Relative**: mild ulnar-positive variance (typically ≤2–3 mm) with central impaction alone — an arthroscopic or open wafer procedure is preferred here, since it avoids osteotomy healing time; established DRUJ arthrosis (osteotomy will not address an arthritic joint — consider DRUJ arthroplasty/resection arthroplasty instead); skeletally immature patients with open distal ulnar physis; smoking or conditions impairing bone healing (raises nonunion risk at the diaphyseal osteotomy site); inability to comply with the protected forearm-rotation rehabilitation protocol.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: true neutral-rotation PA wrist/forearm radiograph (shoulder abducted 90°, elbow flexed 90°, forearm neutral) is mandatory for variance measurement and templating — pronated/supinated views falsely change apparent variance by several millimeters and must never be used for surgical planning. Bilateral comparison views can help confirm the patient's baseline "normal" variance if congenital asymmetry is suspected.
- **Implant selection/sizing**: dedicated ulnar shortening compression plate/cutting-guide jig system (transverse or step-cut/oblique design). Template the planned shortening (typically 2–4 mm) directly on the true PA radiograph to bring variance to neutral or 1–2 mm negative; account for saw kerf when calculating the resection. Step-cut/oblique designs increase bone contact area and may reduce nonunion risk relative to a simple transverse cut, though comparative data show both well-executed oblique-locking and transverse-nonlocking constructs achieve reliable union — construct choice is secondary to precise surgical execution.
- **Equipment**: pneumatic tourniquet, oscillating saw with irrigation, dedicated ulnar-shortening jig/guide with integrated compression plate, fluoroscopy, standard small-fragment screw set as backup.
- **Case-specific planning**: decide concurrently whether an associated peripheral TFCC tear needs separate arthroscopic repair, and whether chronic DRUJ instability is significant enough to warrant planning for possible concurrent or staged DRUJ ligament reconstruction.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Regional block (supraclavicular/axillary) or general anesthesia; supine with the arm abducted on a radiolucent hand table. Exsanguinate and inflate a pneumatic tourniquet on the proximal arm. Prep and drape the forearm circumferentially to allow free pronation/supination intraoperatively for confirming forearm rotation is unrestricted after plate application. Position the mini C-arm for AP and lateral imaging of the distal/mid ulnar shaft and the wrist (for final variance confirmation). Mark the subcutaneous border of the ulna along the planned incision before incision, and mark the DRUJ for reference.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm ulnar-positive variance** on the true neutral-rotation PA radiograph immediately before incision — reconfirm the templated shortening amount matches the intraoperative plan.

2. **Approach.** Make a longitudinal incision directly over the subcutaneous (dorsal-ulnar) border of the ulna, typically centered over the mid-to-distal diaphysis, staying between extensor carpi ulnaris and flexor carpi ulnaris.

3. **Expose the ulnar shaft subperiosteally** over a segment long enough to accommodate the shortening plate (typically 6–8 cm), protecting the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve, which crosses this field and is at risk with retraction or dissection that strays too far volar/dorsal from the subcutaneous border.

4. **Apply the cutting/compression jig to the bone before making either osteotomy cut.** This is the critical technical step — the jig guarantees the two saw cuts remain parallel (transverse design) or maintain the planned geometry (step-cut/oblique design), which is what allows reliable compression afterward.

5. **Perform the osteotomy.** With the jig secured, make the first cut, then the second cut at the jig-defined distance (typically 2–4 mm plus an allowance of roughly 1 mm for saw blade kerf) to shorten the ulna and bring variance to neutral or 1–2 mm negative. For a step-cut/oblique design, both the transverse and longitudinal limbs of the cut are made through the jig in sequence per the specific system's technique.

6. **Resect the intervening bone segment** and appose the two ulnar ends, confirming rotational alignment visually and by matching the bony geometry (the step-cut design self-indexes rotation; a transverse cut requires careful manual rotational control) before compression.

7. **Apply the compression plate construct**, using the jig's compression mechanism (eccentric screw holes or a dedicated compression device) to load the osteotomy site under axial compression while confirming apposition and rotation on fluoroscopy in two planes.

8. **Complete screw fixation** per the plate system's protocol, typically achieving at least three bicortical screws on each side of the osteotomy.

9. **Confirm final ulnar variance intraoperatively** on a true PA-equivalent fluoroscopic view — do not assume the templated resection was achieved exactly; measure it.

10. **Take the forearm through full pronation-supination** intraoperatively to confirm unrestricted rotation and no DRUJ impingement before closure — this is the direct check for overshortening/malrotation-related DRUJ incongruity.

11. **Address DRUJ instability if present and not resolved by the shortening itself.** If chronic DRUJ instability persists despite the tightening effect of shortening (and was not addressed by a separate TFCC repair), proceed with DRUJ ligament reconstruction (tendon graft) or arthroplasty for an arthritic DRUJ as a distinct procedure, staged or concurrent per preoperative planning.

12. **Closure.** Close periosteum where feasible, repair fascia, close skin in layers, and apply a well-padded long-arm or Muenster-type splint/cast incorporating forearm rotation control.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Apply the shortening jig to the bone **before** cutting, never after — this is the single technical step that determines whether the final compression is clean or a wedge/gap deformity results.
- Add roughly 1 mm to the templated resection to compensate for saw blade kerf — under-shortening is easy to correct with a repeat cut pass; over-shortening is not correctable in the same setting.
- Protect the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve early in the exposure — it crosses the standard subcutaneous-border approach and is easily injured with aggressive retraction.
- Take the forearm through full active/passive pronosupination intraoperatively after plate fixation, before closing — this is the most direct way to catch malrotation or DRUJ impingement while it can still be corrected on the table.
- Re-measure ulnar variance intraoperatively on fluoroscopy after fixation rather than trusting the preoperative template alone — small deviations from the planned shortening are common.
- Favor a step-cut/oblique osteotomy design when available — the increased bone contact area helps self-index rotation during apposition and may modestly reduce nonunion risk, though meticulous technique with a transverse cut and jig also achieves reliable union.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that symptomatic hardware requiring plate removal is a common (not rare) outcome — set expectations before surgery rather than at the postoperative visit when it arises.
- If a concomitant peripheral TFCC tear is present, address it (arthroscopic repair) in the same setting when feasible — USO alone may not fully resolve symptoms if a repairable peripheral tear is left untreated.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Overshortening or malrotation causes DRUJ incongruity/impingement pain; use the dedicated cutting/compression guide specifically to avoid this, and confirm full pronosupination intraoperatively before closure.
- Symptomatic hardware requiring plate removal is reported in up to 20–30% of cases in some series — discuss this with patients preoperatively as an expected trade-off.
- Step-cut/oblique osteotomy designs increase bone contact area and may reduce nonunion risk versus a simple transverse cut, but comparative outcome data show both oblique-locking-plate and transverse-nonlocking constructs can achieve reliable union with attention to technique — construct choice is less critical than surgical execution.
- Undercorrection leaves residual impaction symptoms — confirm final variance intraoperatively/postoperatively rather than assuming the planned shortening was achieved.
- Ulnar nerve dorsal sensory branch injury during exposure can cause numbness/dysesthesia over the dorsoulnar hand — careful subperiosteal dissection along the subcutaneous border minimizes this risk.
- Nonunion at the osteotomy site (reported in the low single digits up to roughly 5% depending on construct and technique) — reinforced by ensuring adequate bone contact, secure compression, and appropriate postoperative protection until bridging callus is confirmed.
- Failing to identify and treat a concurrent peripheral TFCC tear can leave the patient symptomatic despite a technically successful osteotomy and normalized variance.

## Postoperative Protocol

Splint/brace for 2 weeks, then removable brace. AROM of the wrist/forearm (pronosupination) is started early (within 1–2 weeks) to avoid stiffness — forearm rotation is the ROM domain most at risk and should be tracked specifically. Avoid axial loading/heavy gripping until the osteotomy shows bridging callus (typically 8–12 weeks). Full unrestricted use/sport by 3–4 months.
