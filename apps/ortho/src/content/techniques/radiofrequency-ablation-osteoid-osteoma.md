## Indications

- First-line procedural treatment for osteoid osteoma in an accessible location, after a trial of NSAIDs (which target the prostaglandin/COX-2-driven pain mechanism directly) has failed, is not tolerated, or the patient prefers definitive treatment over years of potential NSAID dependence.
- Nidus typically <1.5–2 cm by definition (larger lesions are termed osteoblastoma and are managed differently).
- Contraindicated or relatively contraindicated when the nidus is <1 cm from the skin or from a major neural structure (spinal cord, major peripheral nerve) — consider cryoablation or open excision instead in these locations.

## Positioning & setup

CT-guided percutaneous approach, performed under image guidance to localize and access the nidus precisely. Cryoablation is an alternative when the lesion is near skin or neural structures, offering better real-time monitoring of the ice ball on CT/MRI.

## Steps

1. Confirm the diagnosis and characterize the nidus with CT, which is superior to MRI for cortical detail and nidus characterization, and use it to guide percutaneous access.
2. Assess proximity of the nidus to skin and major neural structures (spinal cord, major peripheral nerves) — if <1 cm, consider cryoablation, hydro-/gas-dissection to displace the structure, or open excision instead of standard RFA.
3. Perform CT-guided percutaneous nidus ablation as an outpatient procedure, using temperature monitoring throughout.
4. When ablating near neurovascular structures but still attempting a percutaneous approach, use hydro- or gas-dissection to displace the adjacent nerve or vessel away from the ablation zone rather than abandoning the percutaneous approach.
5. For lesions abutting the spinal cord, major nerves, or where percutaneous access is not feasible, proceed instead to open surgical excision, optionally with intraoperative navigation or fluoroscopic/CT guidance to localize the nidus.
6. Retreat with repeat RFA for the minority of patients who fail a single treatment.

## Pitfalls

- Skin burn and nerve injury are recognized RFA-specific complications, particularly relevant for superficial peroneal/saphenous nerve territory lesions or spinal locations — plan the approach trajectory to avoid these structures.
- Fracture through the ablation cavity in weight-bearing bone can occur, rarely, if the ablation zone is oversized relative to remaining cortical bone stock.
- Do not attempt standard RFA when the nidus is <1 cm from skin or a major neural structure without additional protective measures (hydro-/gas-dissection) or an alternative modality (cryoablation, open excision).
- Intra-articular and posterior spinal element osteoid osteomas can cause painful scoliosis (concave-side lesion) — recognize this presentation, as the deformity may resolve after successful ablation/excision of the nidus.

## Post-op protocol

RFA is an outpatient percutaneous procedure; the monograph does not specify a phased weight-bearing/rehabilitation protocol distinct from routine post-procedural recovery. Reported single-treatment success is approximately 85–95%, with retreatment available for the minority who fail; a 2024 series reported 100% technical success with no complications and a 1.6% recurrence rate, successfully retreated with repeat RFA. A systematic review/meta-analysis of spinal osteoid osteoma confirms RFA is as safe and effective as surgical excision for that subset. Follow up with clinical reassessment of pain relief; recurrent or persistent pain warrants repeat imaging and consideration of retreatment.
