## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- First-line procedural treatment for osteoid osteoma in an accessible location, after a trial of NSAIDs (which target the prostaglandin/COX-2-driven pain mechanism directly) has failed, is not tolerated, or the patient prefers definitive treatment over years of potential NSAID dependence.
- Nidus typically <1.5–2 cm by definition (larger lesions are termed osteoblastoma and are managed differently).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Nidus <1 cm from the skin or from a major neural structure (spinal cord, major peripheral nerve) — standard monopolar RFA carries meaningfully higher risk of thermal skin/nerve injury here; consider cryoablation, hydro-/gas-dissection with a percutaneous approach, a bipolar/navigational electrode system, or open excision instead.
- Lesions directly abutting the spinal cord or where percutaneous access is not anatomically feasible — proceed to open surgical excision instead.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or active local/systemic infection at the planned access site.
- Inability of the patient to lie still/cooperate for a CT-guided procedure under the planned anesthesia (relevant mainly in young children, where general anesthesia is typically used instead of conscious sedation).
- Lesion not confidently characterized as osteoid osteoma (uncertain diagnosis, atypical imaging, nidus >2 cm suggesting osteoblastoma) — confirm diagnosis/consider biopsy before ablation rather than treating empirically.

## Preoperative Planning

- CT is superior to MRI for cortical detail and nidus characterization and is the primary planning study — use thin-slice CT to confirm nidus size, exact location, and its relationship to the cortex, adjacent joint, skin, and any neurovascular structures.
- Measure the shortest distance from the nidus to the skin and to any major neural structure on the planning CT; this single measurement drives the choice between standard percutaneous RFA, an adjunctive protective maneuver, cryoablation, or open excision.
- Select and confirm availability of the ablation system: standard monopolar RFA electrode with a grounding pad, or (for spinal/juxta-neural lesions) a bipolar/navigational electrode system with built-in thermocouples that allows precise real-time monitoring of ablation zone geometry without requiring a grounding pad.
- For lesions near the skin or a nerve, plan in advance for hydro-dissection (saline or D5W instillation) or gas-dissection (CO2) to displace the vulnerable structure away from the trajectory and ablation zone, and have the equipment for this available at the time of the procedure.
- Confirm anesthesia plan: general anesthesia or deep sedation is typical for CT-guided percutaneous ablation given the need for patient stillness and the discomfort of drilling into bone; discuss with anesthesia any relevant comorbidities.
- For posterior spinal element lesions associated with painful scoliosis, review imaging with attention to the concave-side location of the nidus and counsel the patient/family that the deformity may improve after successful ablation.
- Arrange same-day or next-day discharge planning, as this is typically an outpatient procedure.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia or deep sedation is standard, since the procedure requires the patient to remain motionless during CT-guided targeting and involves drilling/coring through cortical bone, which is painful without a general anesthetic depth.
- Position the patient on the CT table in whatever position provides the most direct, shortest percutaneous trajectory to the nidus while avoiding intervening neurovascular structures and joints — this varies by lesion site (prone for most posterior femoral/spinal lesions, supine or lateral for others) and should be planned from the preoperative CT.
- Perform a limited pre-procedure CT scan with the patient in the intended position to confirm the trajectory before prepping, adjusting position as needed.
- Prep and drape the skin entry site sterile, as for any percutaneous bone procedure.
- If using standard monopolar RFA, place the grounding pad on the patient per manufacturer instructions — a large-area adhesive gel grounding pad reduces current density at the dispersive electrode and lowers the risk of a thermal burn at that site; confirm good skin contact, as this is the most common technical cause of grounding-pad site burns. A bipolar/navigational electrode system with dual thermocouples eliminates the need for a grounding pad and the associated skin-injury risk entirely.
- Have the ablation generator, temperature monitoring display, and (if planned) hydro-/gas-dissection equipment set up and tested before starting the bone-access portion of the procedure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the diagnosis and characterize the nidus with CT, which is superior to MRI for cortical detail and nidus characterization, and use it to guide percutaneous access.
2. Assess proximity of the nidus to skin and major neural structures (spinal cord, major peripheral nerves) on the planning images — if <1 cm, plan for cryoablation, hydro-/gas-dissection to displace the structure, a bipolar/navigational electrode system, or open excision instead of standard monopolar RFA.
3. Position the patient for the shortest safe percutaneous trajectory and confirm with a limited CT acquisition before prepping and draping.
4. Advance a guide needle/trocar under intermittent CT guidance to the cortex overlying the nidus, confirming trajectory on sequential images to avoid the adjacent joint, growth plate (in children), and any neurovascular structure identified on planning imaging.
5. Drill or core through the cortex to the nidus using the system's bone-access instruments (coaxial drill/trocar system), advancing under CT guidance with repeated confirmatory images to avoid overshooting the nidus or breaching the far cortex unnecessarily.
6. Confirm final needle/probe tip position is centered within the nidus on CT before activating the ablation — the entire nidus, not just its center, must fall within the effective ablation zone.
7. When ablating near neurovascular structures but still attempting a percutaneous approach, perform hydro- or gas-dissection at this point to displace the adjacent nerve or vessel away from the ablation zone rather than abandoning the percutaneous approach; reconfirm the displaced structure's position on CT before proceeding.
8. Deploy/activate the RFA electrode and ablate per the generator's standard protocol (typically a target temperature around 90°C sustained for several minutes), using continuous or intermittent temperature monitoring throughout via the electrode's thermocouple(s).
9. Monitor temperature at the electrode and, when using a system with peripheral thermocouples (e.g., positioned a fixed distance from the ablation center), confirm the ablation zone geometry is tracking as expected and remains within the safety margin from the previously identified skin/neural structures.
10. On completion, withdraw the electrode/probe under CT guidance, confirming the ablation cavity position and checking for any unexpected finding (e.g., contrast/fluid tracking toward a joint or neural structure) before removing all instruments.
11. Obtain a final CT to document electrode/ablation zone position for the medical record and to screen for any immediate complication (hematoma, unexpected extension of the thermal zone).
12. Dress the small percutaneous entry site; the procedure concludes as an outpatient case with routine post-sedation/anesthesia recovery.
13. For lesions abutting the spinal cord, major nerves, or where percutaneous access is not feasible, proceed instead to open surgical excision, optionally with intraoperative navigation or fluoroscopic/CT guidance to localize the nidus.
14. Retreat with repeat RFA for the minority of patients who fail a single treatment, following the same workup and technique.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Take the extra few minutes to obtain a position-check CT before prepping — a suboptimal initial patient position that requires re-positioning mid-procedure costs far more time and sterility than confirming trajectory up front.
- Use intermittent, low-dose CT acquisitions to walk the needle/probe in stepwise rather than trying to advance to final position in a single pass — this is both safer and, cumulatively, no slower.
- Center the probe tip in the nidus, not simply "within the lesion" — ablation zones are roughly spherical around the electrode tip, so an off-center placement risks under-treating the far edge of the nidus while over-treating adjacent normal bone.
- For lesions close to skin or a nerve, actively displace the at-risk structure with hydro- or gas-dissection rather than simply reducing ablation time/temperature — under-treating the nidus to protect a nearby structure is a common cause of treatment failure, whereas displacing the structure allows a full, standard ablation.
- A large-area adhesive gel grounding pad with confirmed good skin contact meaningfully reduces the risk of a dispersive-electrode burn with monopolar systems; check pad contact before starting, especially in thin or edematous patients.
- Where available, a bipolar/navigational electrode system with dual active thermocouples (positioned at fixed distances from the ablation center) gives real-time confirmation of ablation zone size and eliminates grounding-pad-related burn risk altogether — worth prioritizing for spinal or other juxta-neural lesions.
- For intra-articular lesions, plan the trajectory to avoid crossing the joint capsule/cartilage where possible, since periarticular ablation and joint violation both carry distinct risk profiles from typical diaphyseal lesions.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that pain relief, while usually rapid, may take days to fully manifest as post-ablation inflammation settles — this avoids mistaking normal early post-procedure discomfort for treatment failure.
- Document the ablation zone geometry and final probe position on the completion CT — this becomes the reference study if the patient re-presents with recurrent or persistent pain and repeat imaging is needed to distinguish recurrence from an incompletely treated margin.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Skin burn and nerve injury are recognized RFA-specific complications, particularly relevant for superficial peroneal/saphenous nerve territory lesions or spinal locations — plan the approach trajectory to avoid these structures, and consider a bipolar electrode system (which avoids grounding-pad placement and its associated skin-injury risk) for higher-risk locations.
- Fracture through the ablation cavity in weight-bearing bone can occur, rarely, if the ablation zone is oversized relative to remaining cortical bone stock.
- Do not attempt standard monopolar RFA when the nidus is <1 cm from skin or a major neural structure without additional protective measures (hydro-/gas-dissection, bipolar electrode system) or an alternative modality (cryoablation, open excision).
- Off-center probe placement relative to the true nidus (rather than the visually apparent lesion boundary) is a correctable technical cause of incomplete ablation and treatment failure — recentering on the final pre-ablation CT check before activating the generator prevents this.
- Poor grounding-pad skin contact is the most common preventable cause of a dispersive-electrode thermal burn with monopolar systems — confirm contact before starting.
- Intra-articular and posterior spinal element osteoid osteomas can cause painful scoliosis (concave-side lesion) — recognize this presentation, as the deformity may resolve after successful ablation/excision of the nidus.

## Postoperative Protocol

RFA is an outpatient percutaneous procedure; the monograph does not specify a phased weight-bearing/rehabilitation protocol distinct from routine post-procedural recovery. Reported single-treatment success is approximately 85–95%, with retreatment available for the minority who fail; a 2024 series reported 100% technical success with no complications and a 1.6% recurrence rate, successfully retreated with repeat RFA. A systematic review/meta-analysis of spinal osteoid osteoma confirms RFA is as safe and effective as surgical excision for that subset. Follow up with clinical reassessment of pain relief; recurrent or persistent pain warrants repeat imaging and consideration of retreatment.
