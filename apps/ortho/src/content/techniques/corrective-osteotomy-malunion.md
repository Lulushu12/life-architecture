## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Symptomatic deformity beyond functional tolerance — general orthopaedic teaching accepts up to ~5° varus/valgus and ~10° antero-posterior angulation in the tibial diaphysis, and up to ~10–15° rotational malalignment in the femur before symptomatic dysfunction, though thresholds are lower near joints (articular malunion has essentially no tolerance and mandates surgical correction).
- Progressive adjacent-joint arthrosis attributable to malalignment (documented on serial imaging or examination).
- Unacceptable cosmesis or functionally significant limb-length discrepancy.
- Gait dysfunction, recurrent instability, or compensatory back/hip/knee pain traceable to the malunion.
- Combined bone-loss malunions (shortened, malaligned, with a segmental deficit) requiring simultaneous deformity correction and defect reconstruction.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Asymptomatic, functionally tolerated malunion in a low-demand patient — observe with activity modification rather than operate.
- Active infection at the prior fracture/fixation site — treat infection first (this may itself require a staged Masquelet/bone-transport approach rather than a simple corrective osteotomy).
- Poor soft-tissue envelope or compromised vascularity that would not tolerate an acute correction or the surgical exposure required — favor gradual, minimally invasive frame-based correction, or address the soft-tissue problem first.
- Uncontrolled medical comorbidities that make an elective reconstructive procedure and its prolonged rehabilitation unsafe.
- Patient unable or unwilling to comply with a gradual-correction frame protocol (frequent clinic visits, self-directed strut/hinge adjustments, pin-site care) if gradual correction is the indicated strategy — this shifts consideration toward acute correction with internal fixation where feasible, or toward optimizing the support structure around the patient.
- Very longstanding deformity with chronically contracted neurovascular structures is a relative contraindication to acute correction specifically (not to correction in general) — plan gradual correction instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Deformity analysis**: obtain long-leg standing (mechanical axis) radiographs and, when rotational malunion is suspected, a CT rotational profile comparing the affected limb's femoral/tibial torsion to the contralateral side. Fully characterize the deformity in every plane — magnitude, apex level, translation, rotation, and any associated length discrepancy — before selecting a correction strategy.
- **CORA determination**: identify the center of rotation of angulation on orthogonal views. Whenever anatomically feasible, plan the osteotomy at or near the CORA; an osteotomy performed away from the CORA corrects the angular deformity but introduces a new translational deformity at the osteotomy site (a classic and avoidable planning error).
- **Correction strategy selection**: single-plane deformities correctable in one maneuver are candidates for acute correction with internal fixation (plate or IM nail); multiplanar deformity, combined length discrepancy, or a compromised soft-tissue/neurovascular envelope favor gradual correction with a circular or hexapod external fixator (e.g., Taylor Spatial Frame).
- **Implant/frame planning**: for acute correction, template the planned wedge (opening, closing, or dome osteotomy) and preselect plate length/working length or nail diameter, including any blocking-screw positions needed to control the correction during nail passage. For gradual correction, size the frame (ring diameter with adequate soft-tissue clearance), plan strut/hinge configuration, and pre-calculate correction parameters (magnitude, rate, and any deliberate overcorrection) using the manufacturer's software or classic Ilizarov reference method.
- **Neurovascular risk assessment**: for any correction involving valgus angulation, lengthening, or derotation near the fibular neck, specifically assess peroneal nerve risk preoperatively and counsel the patient; consider a lower acute-correction magnitude or staged/gradual correction if the deformity is longstanding.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: oscillating saw and osteotomes, image intensifier, appropriate plate/nail system or full circular/hexapod frame set, and — if a bone-loss component coexists — the same equipment list required for Masquelet or bone-transport reconstruction (see those technique guides).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, often combined with regional/neuraxial block for postoperative analgesia; avoid a dense block that would mask a developing neurovascular complication in the immediate postoperative period if acute correction near a nerve at risk (e.g., peroneal) was performed.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table for most femoral/tibial osteotomies, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for tibial cases to neutralize rotation; lateral or prone positioning is occasionally used for specific femoral or hindfoot deformity corrections. A well-padded thigh tourniquet is typically used for the osteotomy itself.
- **Table/imaging setup**: image intensifier positioned to allow unobstructed orthogonal views throughout — deformity correction surgery relies heavily on intraoperative fluoroscopic confirmation of the mechanical axis, so plan C-arm position and screen placement before draping.
- **Draping**: circumferential prep and drape of the entire limb, particularly for gradual/frame-based correction, since the limb must be freely manipulable in multiple planes for frame assembly and intraoperative correction checks.
- **Landmarks**: mark the mechanical axis, the planned osteotomy/CORA level, and (for frame cases) the planned ring/wire safe-corridor levels on the skin before the first incision; a preoperative alignment check with a cautery cord or radiopaque marker down the mechanical axis under fluoroscopy is a useful confirmation step before committing to the osteotomy level.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the deformity analysis and CORA** intraoperatively under fluoroscopy before making any bone cut — re-verify magnitude, plane, and level against the preoperative plan.
2. **Expose the planned osteotomy level** through a standard approach for the bone involved, protecting neurovascular structures at risk for that level and elevating only the periosteum necessary to place the saw/osteotome safely.

**Acute correction (single-stage, internal fixation)**

3. Perform the planned osteotomy (transverse, oblique, opening-wedge, closing-wedge, or dome osteotomy depending on the deformity pattern) at or near the CORA, using controlled, low-heat technique (multiple drill holes plus osteotome completion, or thin oscillating saw with irrigation) to avoid thermal injury and to allow a controlled, reproducible correction.
4. Correct the deformity acutely under direct vision and fluoroscopic confirmation of the mechanical axis in both planes — restore the mechanical axis, rotational profile (checking against preoperative CT rotational-profile targets or intraoperative clinical landmarks), and length simultaneously where all three are involved.
5. Provisionally stabilize the correction with K-wires or a temporary clamp and reconfirm alignment on fluoroscopy before committing to definitive fixation.
6. Apply definitive internal fixation (plate with or without a structural wedge graft at an opening-wedge site, or an IM nail with blocking screws to control the correction during and after nail passage) — bone graft any residual opening-wedge gap with autograft, allograft, or a graft substitute as needed for defect size.
7. Recheck final alignment, rotation, and length on fluoroscopy in both planes and against clinical landmarks before closure; perform a gentle neurovascular exam under anesthesia (pulses, and if feasible, tone/response to stimulation of at-risk nerves) before leaving theatre.

**Gradual correction (frame-based)**

3. Apply the circular/hexapod frame reference rings proximal and distal to the planned osteotomy level, using standard safe-corridor wire/half-pin placement, before performing the osteotomy itself.
4. Perform a low-energy corticotomy/osteotomy at the CORA (technique as described for bone transport — multiple drill perforations plus controlled osteotome completion, preserving periosteal and medullary blood supply) rather than a full high-energy osteotomy.
5. Complete frame assembly (struts or threaded rods connecting the reference rings) and confirm the frame's mechanical axis and hinge point (for hinged/monolateral constructs) match the CORA before leaving theatre.
6. Allow a latency period (typically 5–7 days, similar to bone transport) before beginning correction, to allow early healing response at the osteotomy site.
7. Begin gradual correction per the preoperative plan — typically 0.5–1 mm/day equivalent rate for translation/lengthening, or a comparable slow angular rate for pure angular/rotational correction — with the patient or a caregiver executing the programmed schedule (manual strut chart for Taylor Spatial Frame–type constructs, or manual hinge/rod adjustment for classic Ilizarov constructs).
8. See the patient at regular intervals (typically weekly) during correction for neurovascular checks, pin-site assessment, and radiographic confirmation that correction is tracking the plan; adjust the schedule if radiographs show the correction diverging from target.
9. Deliberately plan overcorrection for rotational deformities in some cases, to offset expected settling once the frame is removed and normal loading resumes.
10. Once the target correction is achieved, hold the frame in the neutral/final position through the consolidation phase, monitoring for radiographic bridging at the osteotomy/corticotomy site before removal (as for bone transport).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always re-verify the CORA under live fluoroscopy immediately before cutting bone — preoperative imaging measurements can shift subtly with limb positioning on the table, and a small error in osteotomy level translates directly into a residual translational deformity.
- For opening-wedge acute corrections, slightly under-correct the medial or lateral cortex hinge (leave it intact as a "greenstick" hinge) when the correction magnitude allows — an intact bone hinge adds inherent stability to the acute correction and reduces reliance on the fixation construct alone.
- Use intraoperative long-alignment tools (a cautery cord, radiopaque rod, or dedicated alignment guide) run from the femoral head to the ankle center under fluoroscopy to directly visualize the mechanical axis before final fixation, rather than relying solely on the appearance of the bone ends.
- When correcting combined angular and rotational deformity acutely, correct rotation first under direct vision (using a reproducible clinical landmark such as the second web-space/patella alignment or a pre-placed rotational reference pin) before finalizing angular reduction — sequencing the correction in this order reduces compounding error.
- For gradual correction, build in a small planned overcorrection (a few degrees) for rotational and some angular deformities, since a modest amount of settling toward the pre-correction position is expected once the frame is removed and normal muscular forces resume.
- In frame-based correction near the fibular neck, place a temporary marker or reference stitch over the peroneal nerve course and specifically re-examine active dorsiflexion/EHL function at each early follow-up visit — early detection of a stretch neurapraxia allows the correction rate to be slowed or reversed before permanent injury occurs.
- When a segmental bone-loss component coexists with the malunion, plan the corrective osteotomy and the transport/Masquelet reconstruction as a single integrated frame construct from the outset rather than as sequential, uncoordinated procedures — this avoids a second major frame application.
- Photograph or fluoroscopically document the final corrected alignment (mechanical axis, rotational profile) at the end of the case as a clear baseline for postoperative comparison, especially important in gradual-correction cases where subtle settling needs to be distinguished from true loss of correction later.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Acute correction of a longstanding deformity risks iatrogenic neurovascular injury** from acute stretch of chronically contracted soft tissue around nerves/vessels — the peroneal nerve is particularly at risk with acute valgus/lengthening correction. When the deformity is longstanding, prefer gradual correction of rotation and length rather than acute correction.
- **Failing to plan the osteotomy at or near the CORA** introduces a secondary translational deformity even when the angular correction itself is accurate — this is one of the most common technical planning errors in corrective osteotomy.
- **Articular malunion has essentially no tolerance** for non-anatomic position and should not be managed with the same functional-tolerance thresholds used for diaphyseal malunion; intra-articular malalignment mandates anatomic correction regardless of the small angular thresholds that are acceptable in the diaphysis.
- **Loss of correction after acute fixation** — inadequate provisional stabilization before definitive fixation, or an under-sized construct for the correction achieved, can allow the correction to drift before union; confirm alignment on fluoroscopy immediately before and after definitive fixation, not just before.
- **Delayed union/nonunion at the osteotomy site**, particularly with large opening-wedge corrections left without structural graft support, or in gradual-correction cases where the corticotomy technique was too aggressive (full osteotomy rather than a preserved-periosteum corticotomy).
- **Pin-site infection and frame-related complications** in gradual/frame-based correction, as with bone transport — proactive pin-site care and prompt treatment of early infection prevent progression to septic loosening.
- **Under- or over-correction from planning error or execution drift** — regular radiographic check-ins during a gradual correction protocol catch divergence from plan early enough to adjust the schedule before consolidation locks in a residual deformity.
- **Neurovascular stretch injury during gradual correction** if the rate is too fast for a chronically contracted nerve or vessel — slow or temporarily reverse the correction rate at the first sign of a new sensory or motor deficit, then resume more slowly once symptoms resolve.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: protected weight-bearing per fixation method (acute plate/nail fixation may allow earlier progressive loading than a frame under active correction); frame pin-site care if applicable; ROM of adjacent joints maintained throughout.
- **Correction phase (if gradual/frame-based)**: typically 0.5–1 mm/day distraction or angular correction rate, with neurovascular checks at each clinic visit; overcorrection is sometimes intentionally planned for rotational deformities to offset expected settling.
- **Consolidation phase**: frame retained or fixation left in place until radiographic union at the osteotomy site, generally 3–5 months depending on bone quality and correction magnitude; progressive weight-bearing as consolidation index improves.
- **Return to sport/heavy work** typically 6–12 months after corrective osteotomy, contingent on solid union and restored strength/proprioception.
