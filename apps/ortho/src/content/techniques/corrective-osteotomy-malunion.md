## Indications

Symptomatic deformity beyond functional tolerance — general orthopaedic teaching accepts up to ~5° varus/valgus and ~10° antero-posterior angulation in the tibial diaphysis, and up to ~10–15° rotational malalignment in the femur before symptomatic dysfunction, though thresholds are lower near joints (articular malunion has essentially no tolerance and mandates surgical correction). Also indicated for progressive adjacent-joint arthrosis, unacceptable cosmesis/limb-length discrepancy, or gait dysfunction.

## Positioning & setup

Preoperative planning is mandatory before any osteotomy: full deformity analysis (magnitude, plane, level, translation, rotation) using long-leg standing (mechanical axis) films and/or CT rotational profile comparing femoral/tibial torsion to the contralateral limb. Identify the center of rotation of angulation (CORA) and plan the osteotomy at or near that level whenever possible to avoid secondary translational deformity.

## Steps

1. Complete deformity analysis and CORA determination before committing to a correction strategy.
2. For single-plane deformities correctable in one stage: acute correction with osteotomy and internal fixation (plate or IM nail).
3. For multiplanar deformity, concurrent limb-length discrepancy, or when soft-tissue/neurovascular structures cannot tolerate acute correction: gradual correction with a circular/hexapod external fixator (e.g., Taylor Spatial Frame).
4. Plan the osteotomy at or near the CORA to avoid introducing a secondary translational deformity.
5. For combined bone-loss malunions (shortened, malaligned, with a segmental deficit), manage with the same Masquelet/bone-transport framework used for segmental bone loss, often combined with deformity correction in the same frame construct.

## Pitfalls

- Acute correction of a longstanding deformity risks iatrogenic neurovascular injury from acute stretch of chronically contracted soft tissue around nerves/vessels — the peroneal nerve is particularly at risk with acute valgus/lengthening correction. When the deformity is longstanding, prefer gradual correction of rotation and length.
- Failing to plan the osteotomy at or near the CORA introduces a secondary translational deformity even when the angular correction itself is accurate.
- Articular malunion has essentially no tolerance for non-anatomic position and should not be managed with the same functional-tolerance thresholds used for diaphyseal malunion.

## Post-op protocol

**Malunion post-corrective osteotomy**
- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing per fixation method (acute plate/nail fixation may allow earlier progressive loading than a frame under active correction); frame pin-site care if applicable; ROM of adjacent joints maintained throughout.
- Correction phase (if gradual/frame-based): typically 0.5–1 mm/day distraction or angular correction rate, with neurovascular checks at each clinic visit; overcorrection is sometimes intentionally planned for rotational deformities to offset expected settling.
- Consolidation phase: frame retained or fixation left in place until radiographic union at the osteotomy site, generally 3–5 months depending on bone quality and correction magnitude; progressive weight-bearing as consolidation index improves.
- Return to sport/heavy work typically 6–12 months after corrective osteotomy, contingent on solid union and restored strength/proprioception.
