## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Revision TKA of any cause (aseptic loosening, instability, periprosthetic fracture, second-stage reimplantation after infection) requiring intraoperative selection of the appropriate level of prosthetic constraint based on the residual soft-tissue/ligamentous envelope and bone stock found at surgery.
- The ladder runs, in order of increasing constraint: posterior-stabilized (PS) primary-style articulation → varus-valgus constrained (VVC)/condylar-constrained knee (CCK) → rotating-hinge → distal femoral replacement (segmental/tumor-style, hinged).
- Constraint is added only to the degree required by the deficiency confirmed intraoperatively — never selected preoperatively from radiographs alone, because true collateral competence can only be judged after component trialing and gap balancing.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active or unresolved periprosthetic joint infection — treat infection first (single- or two-stage protocol); do not build a high-constraint construct into an infected bed.
- Insufficient extensor mechanism or soft-tissue coverage to close over a bulkier hinge/segmental implant — plan a gastrocnemius flap or other soft-tissue coverage in advance rather than discovering the problem at closure.
- Poor bone stock inadequate even for a segmental implant (rare, but relevant in massive infected non-unions or multiply-revised cases) may require a two-stage bone-reconstruction strategy or, at the extreme, arthrodesis or amputation discussion with the patient preoperatively.
- Young, high-demand patients: escalate constraint reluctantly — a rotating-hinge or segmental replacement is a durability trade-off that should be reserved for when lower-constraint options are mechanically insufficient, not chosen for convenience.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** long-leg mechanical-axis radiographs, AP/lateral knee films for component position and osteolysis, and CT for rotational alignment of femoral and tibial components (malrotation is a common, correctable contributor to instability that plain films miss) and for AORI defect grading.
- **Templating:** grade the femur and tibia independently on AORI criteria — asymmetric defects (e.g., AORI 1 tibia with AORI 3 femur) are common; template each side to its own severity rather than matching to the worse side by default.
- **Implant/graft selection:** have the full constraint ladder available in the room (PS insert, VVC/CCK insert, rotating-hinge system) — the final construct is frequently decided only after trial reduction, so do not commit to ordering a single-constraint system in advance for a complex revision.
- **Template metaphyseal cone/sleeve sizing from preoperative CT** before committing to a size intraoperatively; oversizing wastes host bone, undersizing risks incomplete seating.
- **Stem planning:** decide in advance whether cemented, cementless press-fit, or tapered-fluted diaphyseal-engaging stems are anticipated for each side based on projected bone loss; have offset stem options available since metaphyseal deficiency often requires eccentric reaming that changes mechanical axis unless offset is corrected.
- **Equipment list:** full revision TKA tray, extraction instruments (osteotomes, Gigli saw, ultrasonic cement removal if a cemented primary is being revised), metaphyseal cone/sleeve trial and impaction set, tapered-fluted stem trials in multiple lengths/diameters, structural allograft availability on standby for AORI 3 defects, and a rotating-hinge/segmental system available even if not the primary plan.
- **Blood management:** plan tranexamic acid and consider cell salvage — revision constructs, especially those requiring extensive membrane debridement or stem removal, carry substantially higher blood loss than primary TKA.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or combined general/regional (spinal plus catheter, or adductor canal/femoral nerve block) anesthesia, per institutional revision-arthroplasty protocol; a nerve catheter is particularly useful given the longer, more painful postoperative course of high-constraint reconstructions.
- Supine on a standard or radiolucent table; a tourniquet is optional and often omitted or used only for initial exposure in revision cases with extensive prior scarring, since a bloodless field can be less important than early identification of neurovascular structures displaced by scar.
- A well-padded thigh support or lateral post allows the knee to be flexed to 90–110° for exposure and extended for final trialing; ensure the leg can be freely manipulated through a full arc for gap balancing.
- Prep and drape widely — from mid-thigh to well below the tibial tubercle — because extensile exposures (quadriceps snip, V-Y turndown, tibial tubercle osteotomy) may be needed and must be available without re-draping.
- Have fluoroscopy available if extraction of a well-fixed stem or assessment of cone/sleeve seating is anticipated to be difficult.
- Mark the prior incision(s) and plan to incorporate the most lateral previous longitudinal incision if multiple exist, to protect skin viability.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Exposure.** Re-open the prior incision (using the most lateral if multiple exist). Perform a thorough medial and lateral parapatellar arthrotomy release, excise the suprapatellar pouch and gutters of scar, and mobilize the extensor mechanism before attempting patellar eversion — forcing eversion in a stiff, scarred knee risks patellar tendon avulsion. Have a low threshold for a quadriceps snip if the patella will not evert or the knee will not flex to 90° without excessive tension.
2. **Component removal.** Remove failed/loose components with thin, curved osteotomes or a reciprocating micro-saw at the bone-cement or cement-implant interface, preserving as much host bone as possible. Remove all cement with hand instruments, ultrasonic cement-removal tools, and fluoroscopic confirmation of a clean canal if stems are being revised.
3. **Debridement and defect assessment.** Debride all fibrous membrane, granulation tissue, and necrotic bone. Send tissue for culture/histology if infection has not already been excluded. Assess and grade the femoral and tibial defects (AORI) directly, now that bone is visualized — this often differs from preoperative imaging.
4. **Assess soft-tissue/ligamentous competence.** With trial components in place (or after minimal bone re-cuts), examine coronal-plane stability at 0° and 30–90° flexion, and sagittal stability with an anterior/posterior drawer. This examination — not preoperative imaging — is what determines the constraint level.
5. **Select the construct on the ladder:**
   - **PS primary-style articulation** — use when both collaterals are competent through the full arc and only PCL substitution is needed (most routine revisions for isolated wear or loosening with preserved collaterals).
   - **VVC/CCK** — step up when there is partial collateral incompetence or coronal laxity a PS post cannot control; a taller, more conforming tibial post engages a deeper femoral box to resist varus-valgus stress while still permitting some native rotation. This is now supported as a durable first-line constraint choice for first-time aseptic revision by contemporary registry data.
   - **Rotating-hinge** — reserve for global ligamentous instability (both collaterals deficient/absent), extensor mechanism compromise requiring a linked construct, severe flexion-extension gap mismatch that soft-tissue balancing cannot correct, or salvage after a failed lower-constraint revision. Confirm the femoral and tibial components are truly captured/linked before trusting the construct to control the knee without collateral support.
   - **Distal femoral replacement (segmental)** — the final rung, reserved for unreconstructable bone loss (AORI 3 with a metaphysis that will not support a cone/sleeve) or a failed hinge revision; prioritizes immediate stable function over further bone-preserving attempts.
6. **Manage metaphyseal bone loss (AORI 2B/3).** Prepare the defect with sequential reamers/broaches/burrs in a stepwise fashion, contouring just enough bone to accept the cone or sleeve as a tight circumferential press-fit — over-reaming sacrifices host bone unnecessarily and risks a construct with no biologic fixation surface; under-preparing risks incomplete seating and rocking. Trial the cone/sleeve and confirm full seating (no visible gap circumferentially, no rock on manual stress) before final impaction. Fill any small residual gaps between cone/sleeve and host bone with cancellous allograft chips or bone-graft substitute — do not rely on cement to fill a large gap, as unsupported cement mantle creep is a described mechanism of later failure. Note that reamer position can be used deliberately: pulling the reamer toward the lateral cortex during femoral preparation shifts final component alignment toward valgus, while a more medial reaming path shifts it varus — useful when correcting a pre-existing deformity during reconstruction.
7. **Stem the components.** Once the metaphysis is reconstructed (cone/sleeve) or found adequate without one, place diaphyseal-engaging stems (cemented or press-fit tapered-fluted) for load-sharing across the reconstructed zone — this is essentially mandatory whenever a cone, sleeve, or augment is used, since an unstemmed component over reconstructed metaphyseal bone risks toggling at the cone-implant interface.
8. **Trial and balance.** With trial components and the selected constraint level in place, recheck coronal and sagittal stability through full range of motion, confirm appropriate flexion-extension gap symmetry, restore the joint line (use the fibular head, epicondyles, and the patella's prior tracking position as landmarks when native anatomy is distorted), and check patellar tracking.
9. **Cement/press-fit final components.** Cement the tibial and femoral components (or press-fit if using fully porous stems in a well-prepared canal) per implant-specific protocol; cement mantle should not be relied upon to fill large voids — bone graft or augments should have already addressed those.
10. **Final stability and patellar tracking check.** Take the knee through a full range of motion under direct vision to confirm no impingement, correct patellar tracking without excessive lateral force, and full coronal/sagittal stability with the final constrained construct.
11. **Closure.** Close in layers over a drain if extensive dissection or bleeding surfaces are present; assess extensor mechanism tension and consider medial/lateral retinacular reinforcement if extensive scar release was required. Plan soft-tissue coverage (e.g., gastrocnemius flap) proactively if skin/soft-tissue is thin over a bulky hinge construct.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Do not decide the final constraint level until after trialing — many knees that look grossly unstable on the table stabilize adequately with a well-balanced CCK once appropriate bone cuts and soft-tissue tensioning are achieved.
- Use the fibular head and epicondylar axis as reliable landmarks for restoring rotation and joint line when native landmarks are destroyed by prior surgery or bone loss.
- When reaming for a cone or sleeve, direct the reamer path deliberately (lateral for valgus correction, medial for varus correction) rather than reaming purely concentrically — this is a useful, underused tool for correcting pre-existing coronal deformity at the time of reconstruction.
- Always stem a component seated on a metaphyseal cone or sleeve — an unstemmed component risks toggling at the cone-implant junction even when the cone itself is well-fixed.
- Keep the full constraint ladder open in the room (PS through hinge) rather than committing to a single system preoperatively; the intraoperative soft-tissue exam, not the preoperative plan, should drive the final choice.
- For partial collateral incompetence, trial a CCK before reflexively escalating to a hinge — meta-analytic data show CCK and rotating-hinge achieve broadly similar mid-term survivorship, so match constraint to the deficiency rather than assuming the more constrained option is inherently safer.
- When extensive membrane and granulation tissue debridement is anticipated, plan for tranexamic acid and cell salvage up front — revision blood loss is meaningfully higher than primary TKA.
- If the extensor mechanism is tight on trial closure after a bulkier hinge/segmental implant, address it proactively (partial patellar tendon release of scar, or planned soft-tissue coverage) rather than forcing closure under tension.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Over-constraining transmits more stress to the bone-implant interface, accelerating aseptic loosening; under-constraining leaves the knee unstable — match constraint precisely to the deficiency found intraoperatively rather than defaulting upward "to be safe."
- Under-reaming/under-preparing the metaphyseal defect for a cone risks incomplete seating and early loosening; over-reaming sacrifices host bone unnecessarily — template from preoperative CT and confirm seating with trials before final impaction.
- Choose between CCK and rotating-hinge based on the degree of ligamentous deficiency present, not an assumed durability advantage of either design — meta-analytic pooling shows both achieve broadly similar ~90% 5-year survivorship.
- Mechanical failure at the constraint mechanism itself (bushing wear, post fracture, dissociation) is specific to constrained/hinge constructs and should be part of the differential for late pain or instability after a constrained revision.
- Cone/sleeve constructs have excellent early-to-mid-term survivorship in isolation (implant-specific revision rates often <5% at 5–10 years), even though overall reoperation-for-any-reason rates in the broader complex reconstruction remain meaningfully higher, reflecting the elevated baseline risk of infection, instability, or extensor mechanism complications in these cases — counsel patients on this distinction preoperatively.
- Failing to stem a component over a cone/sleeve, or under-filling residual gaps with graft/substitute (relying instead on cement to bridge a large void), is a recognized avoidable mechanism of early failure.
- A quadriceps snip or more extensile exposure performed too late (after already forcing eversion) risks an uncontrolled patellar tendon avulsion — have a low threshold to extend the exposure proactively rather than reactively.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated if stemmed components achieve solid diaphyseal fixation; ROM progression targeting 90° flexion by 6 weeks; full recovery trajectory 6–12 months, following standard revision TKA principles.
- Watch for mechanical failure at the constraint mechanism itself (bushing wear, post fracture, dissociation) and higher aseptic loosening rates transmitted to the bone-implant interface from constrained kinematics — the fundamental trade-off of adding constraint.
- Cone/sleeve constructs have excellent early-to-mid-term survivorship in isolation (implant-specific revision rates often <5% at 5–10 years), even though overall reoperation-for-any-reason rates in the broader complex reconstruction remain meaningfully higher, reflecting the elevated baseline risk of infection, instability, or extensor mechanism complications in these cases.
