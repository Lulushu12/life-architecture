## Indications

- Revision TKA requiring implant constraint selection based on residual soft-tissue/ligamentous deficiency confirmed intraoperatively — from routine revision for isolated component wear/loosening with preserved collaterals through to global ligamentous instability or unreconstructable bone loss.
- Constraint is added only to the degree required by the deficiency found: over-constraining transmits more stress to the bone-implant interface and accelerates aseptic loosening, while under-constraining leaves the knee unstable.

## Positioning & setup

- Standard revision TKA setup with intraoperative assessment of collateral ligament competence and coronal/flexion-extension balance driving the construct choice.
- Template metaphyseal cone/sleeve sizing from preoperative CT before committing to a size.

## Steps

1. Assess residual ligamentous and soft-tissue deficiency intraoperatively before selecting a construct.
2. **Posterior-stabilized (PS) primary-style articulation:** sufficient when both collateral ligaments are competent and only PCL substitution is needed (most routine revisions for isolated component wear/loosening with preserved collaterals).
3. **Varus-valgus constrained (VVC) / condylar-constrained knee (CCK):** step up when there is partial collateral incompetence or coronal-plane laxity a PS poly cannot control; a deeper, more conforming tibial post/femoral box resists varus-valgus stress while still permitting some native rotation. Registry data supports CCK as the default constraint step before hinge in first-time aseptic revision.
4. **Rotating-hinge:** reserve for global ligamentous instability (both collaterals deficient/absent), extensor mechanism compromise requiring a linked construct, severe flexion-extension gap mismatch unmanageable by soft-tissue balancing, or salvage after failed lower-constraint revision. A rotating (rather than fixed) hinge articulation reduces transmitted torsional stress at the bone-implant interface compared with older fixed-hinge designs.
5. **Distal femoral replacement (segmental/tumor-style):** the final rung, for unreconstructable bone loss (AORI Type 3 with unsalvageable metaphysis) or failed hinge revision, prioritizing immediate stable function over further bone-preserving reconstruction attempts.
6. For associated metaphyseal bone loss (AORI Type 2B/3), use a metaphyseal cone or sleeve (porous tantalum or highly porous titanium) as a biologic scaffold for osseointegration onto which the stemmed revision component is then cemented or press-fit — cones for the most severe defects (± structural allograft), sleeves for moderate, more standardized defects.
7. Use tapered-fluted stems for diaphyseal load-sharing fixation alongside augments in Type 2A/2B reconstructions.

## Pitfalls

- Over-constraining transmits more stress to the bone-implant interface, accelerating aseptic loosening; under-constraining leaves the knee unstable — match constraint precisely to the deficiency found intraoperatively rather than defaulting upward "to be safe."
- Under-reaming/under-preparing the metaphyseal defect for a cone risks incomplete seating and early loosening; over-reaming sacrifices host bone unnecessarily — template from preoperative CT.
- Choose between CCK and rotating-hinge based on the degree of ligamentous deficiency present, not an assumed durability advantage of either design — meta-analytic pooling shows both achieve broadly similar ~90% 5-year survivorship.

## Post-op protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated if stemmed components achieve solid diaphyseal fixation; ROM progression targeting 90° flexion by 6 weeks; full recovery trajectory 6–12 months, following standard revision TKA principles.
- Watch for mechanical failure at the constraint mechanism itself (bushing wear, post fracture, dissociation) and higher aseptic loosening rates transmitted to the bone-implant interface from constrained kinematics — the fundamental trade-off of adding constraint.
- Cone/sleeve constructs have excellent early-to-mid-term survivorship in isolation (implant-specific revision rates often <5% at 5–10 years), even though overall reoperation-for-any-reason rates in the broader complex reconstruction remain meaningfully higher, reflecting the elevated baseline risk of infection, instability, or extensor mechanism complications in these cases.
