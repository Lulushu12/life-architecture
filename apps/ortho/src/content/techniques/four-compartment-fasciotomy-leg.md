## Indications

Emergent decompression of the leg for acute compartment syndrome (ACS) — high clinical suspicion with pain out of proportion/pain on passive stretch (especially where a regional block may be masking symptoms), or a ΔP (diastolic BP − intracompartmental pressure) < 30 mmHg on continuous monitoring in an obtunded, sedated, intubated, or regionally-blocked patient. In the clinically obvious case this is a "no delay for imaging" emergency — there is no role for pressure monitoring once irreversible neuromuscular/vascular damage is already evident.

## Positioning & setup

Performed emergently, without waiting for imaging, once the diagnosis is clinically established or supported by ΔP < 30 mmHg monitoring. All circumferential dressings/casts are removed first and the limb kept at heart level (not elevated above it, which reduces arterial perfusion pressure) as an immediate temporizing step before the patient reaches theatre.

## Steps

1. Confirm the diagnosis clinically (pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, paresthesia as the earliest reliable signs — pallor and pulselessness are late and unreliable) or via continuous intracompartmental pressure monitoring (ΔP < 30 mmHg) in the obtunded/blocked patient.
2. Perform emergent four-compartment fasciotomy via the standard two-incision (medial + lateral) technique.
3. Release all four compartments explicitly: anterior, lateral, superficial posterior, and deep posterior.
4. Leave the wounds open and dress; do not attempt primary closure at the index procedure.
5. Plan delayed primary closure or split-thickness skin graft at 5–10 days once swelling subsides, using a staged closure technique (shoelace, vessel-loop, or gradual approximation).

## Pitfalls

- **Incomplete fasciotomy** — missing the deep posterior compartment via the medial incision is a common technical error. The deep posterior compartment (behind the soleus, containing tibialis posterior/FDL/FHL) is the most frequently under-released and re-explored compartment; explicitly confirm and document release of all four compartments.
- Time from onset to fasciotomy is the dominant prognostic factor: fasciotomy within 6 h of onset gives the best functional outcomes, while risk of myonecrosis, contracture, and need for delayed debridement/amputation rises sharply beyond 8–12 h.
- Regional anesthesia/neuraxial blocks blunt the cardinal pain symptom and are a recognized risk factor for delayed diagnosis — maintain a lower threshold for pressure monitoring when a block is in place in an at-risk limb.
- Do not delay fasciotomy for imaging or further workup once the clinical picture is obvious — this is a time-critical surgical emergency, not a diagnosis to be confirmed at leisure.

## Post-op protocol

**Post-fasciotomy**
- Early (days 0–5): dressing changes, staged wound closure (shoelace technique or serial VAC-assisted closure), active/passive ankle and toe ROM to limit contracture, elevation.
- Weeks 1–3: definitive wound closure or split-thickness skin graft once edema resolves; progressive weight-bearing as underlying fracture fixation allows; desensitization and scar management once healed.
- Months 1–3+: strength and gait rehabilitation; residual sensory deficit or mild motor weakness is common and should be addressed with targeted physiotherapy and, if persistent beyond 3–6 months, nerve conduction studies.
- Delayed or incomplete decompression risks Volkmann's ischemic contracture, permanent nerve palsy (foot/wrist drop), myonecrosis requiring debridement or amputation, and rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury.
