## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Emergent decompression of the leg for acute compartment syndrome (ACS) — high clinical suspicion with pain out of proportion/pain on passive stretch (especially where a regional block may be masking symptoms), or a ΔP (diastolic BP − intracompartmental pressure) < 30 mmHg on continuous monitoring in an obtunded, sedated, intubated, or regionally-blocked patient.
- In the clinically obvious case this is a "no delay for imaging" emergency — there is no role for pressure monitoring once irreversible neuromuscular/vascular damage is already evident; proceed directly to fasciotomy.
- Prophylactic fasciotomy in select high-risk scenarios (prolonged tourniquet time, revascularization after prolonged ischemia, severe crush injury with impending compartment syndrome) even before a diagnostic threshold is definitively met, when clinical judgment favors decompression over watchful waiting.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Established, irreversible muscle necrosis with a nonviable limb, where fasciotomy will not restore function and definitive amputation may be the more appropriate emergent procedure — a shared decision made with the patient/family and senior surgical input where time allows.
- There is no absolute medical contraindication to fasciotomy once ACS is diagnosed — this is a limb-threatening emergency, and even a physiologically unstable polytrauma patient should have decompression performed rather than deferred, since delay converts a reversible injury into an irreversible one. Physiologic instability changes anesthetic management, not the indication itself.
- Chronic exertional compartment syndrome (a distinct, non-emergent diagnosis) does not share this indication — that entity is managed electively and is outside the scope of this acute technique.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: none required or indicated to confirm ACS in the clinically obvious case — do not delay surgery for imaging. Plain radiographs of the leg are reasonable if not already obtained (to characterize an underlying fracture) but should not delay the fasciotomy itself.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: standard soft-tissue set, long straight scissors (Metzenbaum or similar) for fascial release, a marking pen for incision planning, and material for the planned staged-closure technique (vessel loops and skin staples for a shoelace technique, or negative pressure wound therapy dressing supplies) should be available in the room from the outset, since the closure strategy is planned at the time of the index procedure.
- **Concurrent fracture fixation planning**: if an underlying fracture requires stabilization, plan whether fixation will be performed at the same anesthetic (common and often preferred, since it protects the fasciotomy wounds from further disruption) — have the relevant fixation implants and fluoroscopy available in the same setting.
- **Team preparation**: this is a "no delay" emergency — mobilize theatre staff and anesthesia as soon as the diagnosis is clinically established; do not wait for a full elective-case preoperative workup.
- **Documentation before incision**: record baseline neurovascular exam (specific named nerve motor/sensory function, pulses, capillary refill) so that any postoperative change can be accurately compared against a documented starting point.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is preferred over dense regional/neuraxial block for the index fasciotomy in a limb where compartment syndrome is still evolving, since a dense block would mask any residual or recurrent compartment symptoms in the immediate postoperative period; once decompression is confirmed adequate, regional analgesia can be added for postoperative pain control.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard or radiolucent table (radiolucent if concurrent fracture fixation is planned), with the leg free-draped to allow access to both the medial and lateral aspects of the leg.
- **Tourniquet**: a thigh tourniquet may be applied for hemostasis during exposure but should be used cautiously and released before final assessment of muscle viability, since compartment pressure and the very physiology being treated make prolonged additional ischemia undesirable — many surgeons avoid tourniquet use altogether for this procedure, or use it only briefly with early release once the fascia is opened.
- **Table/imaging setup**: image intensifier available and positioned if concurrent fracture reduction/fixation is planned in the same setting.
- **Draping**: circumferential or wide free-drape of the entire leg from above the knee to below the ankle, since both incisions (anterolateral and posteromedial) must be accessible, along with room to extend either incision proximally or distally if needed.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: palpate and mark the fibular shaft, the anterior tibial crest, and the posteromedial border of the tibia. Plan the anterolateral incision roughly 2 cm anterior to the fibula, and the posteromedial (two-compartment) incision roughly 1–2 cm posterior to the palpable posteromedial border of the tibia — mark both before the first cut to keep the skin bridge between them wide enough to protect its blood supply.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the diagnosis clinically** (pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, paresthesia as the earliest reliable signs — pallor and pulselessness are late and unreliable) or via continuous intracompartmental pressure monitoring (ΔP < 30 mmHg) in the obtunded/blocked patient, then proceed directly to the operating room without further delay.
2. **Make the anterolateral incision** longitudinally, roughly 2 cm anterior to the fibula, extending from just below the fibular head to just above the ankle; identify and carefully protect the superficial peroneal nerve as it becomes subcutaneous in the distal third of the leg — this is the nerve most at risk of iatrogenic injury in this approach.
3. **Incise the fascia overlying the anterior compartment** longitudinally, then identify the intermuscular septum between the anterior and lateral compartments; open the fascia over the lateral compartment through the same skin incision, staying 1–2 cm anterior and posterior to the septum respectively to avoid injuring the septal perforating vessels and the peroneal nerve as it passes near the fibular neck proximally.
4. **Extend each fascial incision the full length of the compartment**, from the fibular head proximally to the ankle distally, using long scissors with tips partially opened, pushed subcutaneously along the fascia in both directions — a common technical error is a fascial release that is too short, leaving proximal or distal fascial bands that still constrict the muscle belly.
5. **Make the posteromedial incision** longitudinally, roughly 1–2 cm posterior to the palpable posteromedial border of the tibia, taking care to preserve a skin bridge of adequate width (generally at least 6–8 cm) between this and the anterolateral incision to protect skin viability.
6. **Identify and protect the saphenous vein and saphenous nerve** as they course subcutaneously along the medial leg — retract them anteriorly with the skin flap rather than working directly over them.
7. **Release the superficial posterior compartment fascia** longitudinally, exposing the gastrocnemius-soleus complex.
8. **Release the deep posterior compartment**: this is the step most commonly incomplete. Retract the soleus posteriorly (detaching its origin from the tibia along the soleal bridge/arch where needed) to expose the deep posterior compartment fascia overlying tibialis posterior, flexor digitorum longus, and flexor hallucis longus, then incise this fascia along its full length. Explicitly release the soleal arch/bridge itself, since a residual intact soleal bridge is a well-recognized cause of incomplete deep posterior decompression even when the fascia distal to it has been opened.
9. **Protect the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle**, which lies on the posterior aspect of tibialis posterior and FDL, medial to flexor hallucis longus, and becomes more superficial near the medial ankle — identify it directly before working in that plane distally.
10. **Assess muscle viability in each compartment** directly (color, consistency, contractility to stimulation, capacity to bleed — the "four C's") once all four compartments are open; excise any frankly necrotic muscle at this stage rather than deferring to a second look if necrosis is unambiguous.
11. **Leave all wounds open** — do not attempt primary skin closure at the index procedure, since the whole point of the release is to allow the swollen compartment contents room to expand.
12. **Dress the open wounds** (simple non-adherent dressing, NPWT, or the first stage of a planned staged-closure technique such as a shoelace/vessel-loop construct placed at the index procedure to allow progressive tightening at subsequent visits) and elevate the limb.
13. **Plan and document a second-look/staged-closure schedule**: return to theatre for repeat debridement if needed, and progressive wound closure (shoelace technique, vessel-loop technique, or gradual approximation) or split-thickness skin grafting once swelling has resolved, typically at 5–10 days.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the two-incision (medial + lateral) technique as the default for trauma-related four-compartment leg fasciotomy — it reliably decompresses all four compartments and preserves a wide skin bridge, and is generally preferred over single-incision perifibular techniques in the acute trauma setting.
- Open the fascia with the scissor-push technique (partially opened scissor tips advanced subcutaneously along the fascial plane in both directions from a small initial fascial incision) rather than sharply incising the full length under direct vision alone — this is faster and reduces the risk of an inadvertently short release.
- Explicitly release the soleal bridge/arch when decompressing the deep posterior compartment — this single step is the most common reason for a technically "complete-looking" but functionally incomplete deep posterior release.
- Keep the skin bridge between the two incisions wide (roughly a third of the leg's circumference, commonly cited as at least 6–8 cm) to protect its blood supply and reduce the risk of skin-bridge necrosis, particularly in a already-swollen, high-energy injured limb.
- Identify the superficial peroneal nerve early in the anterolateral dissection — mark it directly or with a vessel loop once seen, since ongoing retraction and swelling in the field can make it easy to lose track of and injure later in the case.
- If a tourniquet was used for exposure, release it and inspect all four compartments for active bleeding and muscle color/contractility before leaving theatre — a compartment that still looks pale/nonviable under tourniquet-induced ischemia may recover once perfused; conversely, do not close over frankly dead muscle.
- When an underlying fracture needs fixation, do it in the same anesthetic once the fasciotomy is complete — this avoids a second trip to theatre and protects the open fasciotomy wounds from the additional soft-tissue disruption of a delayed, separate fixation procedure.
- Place the initial elements of a planned staged-closure system (vessel loops for a shoelace technique) at the index procedure rather than at the first return trip — this allows progressive gentle tightening to begin the moment swelling starts to resolve, rather than waiting for a second full setup.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Incomplete fasciotomy** — missing the deep posterior compartment via the medial incision, and specifically failing to release the soleal bridge/arch, is a common technical error. The deep posterior compartment (behind the soleus, containing tibialis posterior/FDL/FHL) is the most frequently under-released and re-explored compartment; explicitly confirm and document release of all four compartments, including the soleal arch.
- **Nerve injury** — the superficial peroneal nerve (subcutaneous in the distal third of the leg) is at risk during the anterolateral incision, and the common peroneal nerve is at risk proximally where it wraps the fibular neck; the saphenous nerve and vein are at risk during the medial incision if dissection strays posterior to the tibial border without direct identification.
- **Posterior tibial neurovascular bundle injury** during deep posterior compartment release, particularly distally near the medial ankle where the bundle becomes more superficial — work under direct vision in this zone rather than by feel.
- **Skin-bridge necrosis** from placing the two incisions too close together, particularly in an already-swollen or high-energy injured limb — plan incision spacing generously at the outset.
- **Time from onset to fasciotomy is the dominant prognostic factor**: fasciotomy within 6 h of onset gives the best functional outcomes, while risk of myonecrosis, contracture, and need for delayed debridement/amputation rises sharply beyond 8–12 h.
- **Regional anesthesia/neuraxial blocks blunt the cardinal pain symptom** and are a recognized risk factor for delayed diagnosis — maintain a lower threshold for pressure monitoring when a block is in place in an at-risk limb.
- **Do not delay fasciotomy for imaging or further workup** once the clinical picture is obvious — this is a time-critical surgical emergency, not a diagnosis to be confirmed at leisure.
- **Delayed or incomplete decompression risks** Volkmann's ischemic contracture, permanent nerve palsy (foot/wrist drop), myonecrosis requiring debridement or amputation, and rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury.
- **Wound closure complications**: attempting definitive closure too early under tension can recreate a compartment-syndrome-like picture in the still-swollen limb or lead to wound dehiscence/marginal skin necrosis; a staged approach with progressive tightening (shoelace/vessel-loop technique) or split-thickness skin grafting once true swelling resolution is confirmed avoids this.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Early (days 0–5)**: dressing changes, staged wound closure (shoelace technique or serial VAC-assisted closure), active/passive ankle and toe ROM to limit contracture, elevation.
- **Weeks 1–3**: definitive wound closure or split-thickness skin graft once edema resolves; progressive weight-bearing as underlying fracture fixation allows; desensitization and scar management once healed.
- **Months 1–3+**: strength and gait rehabilitation; residual sensory deficit or mild motor weakness is common and should be addressed with targeted physiotherapy and, if persistent beyond 3–6 months, nerve conduction studies.
- Delayed or incomplete decompression risks Volkmann's ischemic contracture, permanent nerve palsy (foot/wrist drop), myonecrosis requiring debridement or amputation, and rhabdomyolysis with acute kidney injury.
