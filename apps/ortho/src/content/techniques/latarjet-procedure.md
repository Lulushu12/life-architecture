## Indications

Glenoid bone loss ≥20–25%, off-track lesions with bone loss beyond remplissage's reliable range, and revision instability surgery. Increasingly used as a primary option in high-risk contact/collision athletes even with subcritical bone loss, given lower recurrence in this subgroup. Revision Latarjet after a failed arthroscopic Bankart repair has outcomes comparable to primary Latarjet with low recurrence, supporting its role as the workhorse revision procedure.

## Positioning & setup

Performed open or arthroscopically; arthroscopic technique is technically demanding with a steep learning curve (measurable improvement in operative time/complications across the first ~40–171 cases in published series). Surgeon experience is the dominant variable in complication and recurrence rates, and arthroscopic technique carries a possibly higher screw-related complication rate than open in some systematic reviews.

## Steps

1. Coracoid osteotomy to harvest the coracoid process with the conjoint tendon attached.
2. Transfer the coracoid graft through, or in the "congruent-arc" variant below, a split in the subscapularis.
3. Fix the graft flush with the glenoid face at the 3–5 o'clock position (right shoulder) using 2 screws (or cortical button constructs).
4. Avoid lateral graft overhang (>2–3 mm is associated with later arthropathy) or medialization (under-corrects instability).
5. The stabilizing mechanism is a "triple effect": bony increase of the glenoid arc, a dynamic sling effect from the conjoint tendon through the split subscapularis, and capsular reinforcement/repair.
6. **When the coracoid is unsuitable for transfer** (prior fracture, insufficient size) or in a revision setting after a failed Latarjet: use an alternative free bone-block graft — iliac crest autograft classically, or distal tibial/glenoid allograft, which is increasingly favored because its native articular cartilage cap can restore a more congruent articular surface — fixed to the anteroinferior glenoid to restore the glenoid arc. Iliac crest remains more widely available and familiar, while distal tibial allograft is gaining favor in revision algorithms.

## Pitfalls

- Lateral screw/graft overhang >2–3 mm is associated with later glenohumeral arthropathy (Samilson-Prieto progression); medialized graft placement under-corrects instability.
- Suprascapular and axillary nerves are at risk during coracoid mobilization and inferior screw placement; musculocutaneous nerve is at risk during medial coracoid dissection.
- Nonunion/fibrous union occurs in ~5–10%; graft osteolysis and screw-related pain/loosening/removal occur in 0–18% (higher after arthroscopic technique in some series).
- Intra-articular hardware prominence increases long-term arthropathy risk — anatomic, extra-articular hardware placement is intended to mitigate this.
- Subscapularis dysfunction can follow open subscapularis takedown/repair (mitigated by the split technique in modern Latarjet) or aggressive arthroscopic subscapularis-splitting portals.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: sling for comfort; pendulum and passive ROM within a protected arc; avoid resisted internal/external rotation.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive passive-to-active ROM under a subscapularis-protective protocol — limit early resisted internal rotation and forced external rotation stretching to protect the subscapularis split/repair (a key technical difference from isolated Bankart rehab).
- **Weeks 6–12**: active strengthening once radiographic evidence of early graft healing (or clinical criteria per surgeon protocol) supports loading; progress scapular and rotator cuff strengthening.
- **3 months**: confirm bony union/incorporation before considering full-contact clearance; light sport-specific training may begin with surgeon clearance.
- **Return to contact/collision sport**: typically 4–6 months, gated by radiographic graft healing (union rates generally >90–95% in modern series), full strength recovery, and functional testing.
- Reported recurrent instability rate 2–8%; reoperation 5–15% (screw removal a major driver); nonunion 5–10%.
