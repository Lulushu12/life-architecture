## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Glenoid bone loss ≥20–25%, off-track lesions with bone loss beyond remplissage's reliable range, and revision instability surgery.
- Increasingly used as a **primary** option in high-risk contact/collision athletes even with subcritical bone loss, given lower recurrence in this subgroup.
- Revision Latarjet after a failed arthroscopic Bankart repair has outcomes comparable to primary Latarjet with low recurrence, supporting its role as the workhorse revision procedure.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active glenohumeral infection.
- Coracoid process unsuitable for transfer — prior coracoid fracture, insufficient length/bulk, or prior coracoid-based surgery (e.g., prior failed Latarjet at the same site) — favors an alternative free bone-block graft instead.
- Established, at least moderate glenohumeral arthropathy (Samilson-Prieto II–III) — a bone-block stabilization procedure does not address arthritic change and may be a poor use of a first (or revision) surgical opportunity in this setting; arthroplasty planning should be considered instead.
- Patients unable to comply with the subscapularis-protective and union-gated rehabilitation timeline (important given the reliance on bony union and tendon healing for the "triple effect" mechanism).
- Relative: very young patients with substantial remaining growth, where bone-block hardware placement near the glenoid should be planned with awareness of any residual physeal considerations (uncommon at the ages typically presenting with bone loss requiring Latarjet, but worth explicit note).

## Preoperative Planning

- **CT with 3D reconstruction and digital humeral subtraction** to quantify glenoid bone loss (best-fit-circle/PICO method) and confirm off-track status; also assess coracoid morphology/length to confirm it is an adequate graft source (length, width, presence of prior fracture).
- MR arthrogram as needed to assess concomitant labral/rotator cuff pathology and confirm no missed HAGL, since the capsular repair component of the Latarjet "triple effect" still depends on adequate soft tissue.
- **Implant planning**: 2 cannulated screws (partially threaded, typically 3.5–4.5 mm) or a cortical button/suture-tensioner construct (for the arthroscopic technique) — confirm the specific guide/jig system if performing the arthroscopic approach, since precise, reproducible graft positioning without a guide is difficult given arthroscopic lens distortion.
- If the coracoid is unsuitable (prior fracture, insufficient size) or this is a revision after a failed Latarjet, plan for an **alternative free bone-block graft**: iliac crest autograft (classic, widely available) or distal tibial/glenoid allograft (increasingly favored in revision algorithms because its native articular cartilage cap can restore a more congruent articular surface); confirm allograft availability preoperatively if that is the chosen source.
- Decide **open vs arthroscopic** technique based on surgeon experience — arthroscopic Latarjet has a defined, substantial learning curve (measurable improvement in operative time and complication rate across roughly the first 40–170 cases in published series), so surgeons early in that curve should weigh this against open technique's more established safety profile.
- Consent should explicitly cover the specific complication profile of this procedure: nonunion/fibrous union, screw-related complications requiring possible removal, and nerve injury (axillary, musculocutaneous, suprascapular) in addition to standard instability-surgery risks.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically with an interscalene regional block, though some surgeons limit or time the block to allow an early postoperative musculocutaneous/axillary nerve check given the nerve-risk profile of this procedure.
- **Positioning**: **beach chair**, arm free-draped to allow positioning through abduction/external rotation (to expose and release the coracoacromial ligament) and adduction/internal rotation (to release pectoralis minor and access the subscapularis split) during different stages of the case.
- Pad all bony prominences; secure the head with neutral cervical alignment.
- Prep and drape widely to include the coracoid, full anterior shoulder, and enough of the arm to allow free intraoperative repositioning through rotation.
- Mark the coracoid, clavicle, acromion, and planned deltopectoral incision (from the coracoid tip extending distally along the deltopectoral interval) before incision.
- **Fluoroscopy**: have available and typically draped into the field, particularly for the arthroscopic technique or when confirming screw trajectory/length and final graft position flush with the glenoid face.
- Perform an exam under anesthesia to confirm the instability pattern before starting.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Deltopectoral approach.** Incise from the coracoid tip distally along the deltopectoral interval; identify and protect (retract, do not routinely ligate) the cephalic vein.
2. **Expose and harvest the coracoid graft.** Abduct and externally rotate the arm to place the coracoacromial ligament under tension, then release it approximately 1 cm from the coracoid (preserving a cuff of ligament on the coracoid for later use in some techniques, or excising it per preference). Adduct and internally rotate the arm to expose and release the pectoralis minor insertion from the medial coracoid. Osteotomize the coracoid at its curve (angled osteotomy, typically with an oscillating saw), harvesting a graft at least ~20 mm in length.
3. **Prepare the graft.** Flatten and decorticate the deep (undersurface) of the coracoid graft with a saw or burr to create a flat surface that will sit flush against the prepared glenoid. Drill two parallel holes through the graft for later screw or button passage, sized and spaced according to the fixation system being used.
4. **Develop the subscapularis interval.** Identify the junction of the upper two-thirds and lower one-third of the subscapularis (the interval used for the split); a **subscapularis split** (rather than tenotomy) is favored in modern technique for adequate exposure with less risk to subscapularis function.
5. **Split the subscapularis and expose the capsule/glenoid.** Split the subscapularis in line with its fibers at the chosen interval, then incise the underlying capsule (vertically or in a manner that allows later repair/imbrication) to expose the anteroinferior glenoid.
6. **Prepare the anterior glenoid neck.** Decorticate the anterior glenoid neck (from roughly the 2–3 o'clock to 5–6 o'clock position, right shoulder) with a burr or rasp to create a flat, bleeding bony bed flush with the native articular surface — inadequate flattening is a common cause of an improperly seated, non-flush graft.
7. **Transfer the coracoid graft.** Pass the graft, with the conjoint tendon still attached, through the subscapularis split so the tendon sling passes beneath (deep to) the subscapularis.
8. **Position the graft.** Place the graft flush with the glenoid articular surface (not proud, not medialized) and in a **subequatorial position** — centered at roughly the 3–5 o'clock position (right shoulder), spanning the glenoid defect so its lateral edge does not overhang the articular surface by more than ~2–3 mm.
9. **Provisional fixation.** Provisionally pin or hold the graft in position, then confirm flush, subequatorial placement visually and (if using the arthroscopic technique or when in doubt open) with fluoroscopy before final fixation.
10. **Final fixation.** Place 2 screws (or a cortical button/suture-tensioner construct in the arthroscopic technique) through the pre-drilled graft holes into the glenoid neck, directed slightly medial-to-lateral and angled to avoid the articular surface and the posterior glenoid cortex; tighten to compress the graft against the glenoid, using a suture tensioner/tensiometer if using a button construct.
11. **Confirm the "triple effect" is achieved.** Check that the graft provides a bony extension of the glenoid arc, the conjoint tendon sling creates a dynamic inferior/anterior buttress with the arm in abduction-external rotation, and the capsule can be repaired/imbricated over or lateral to the graft for the soft-tissue component of stabilization.
12. **Capsular repair.** Repair or imbricate the capsule to the native glenoid rim or over the graft, per surgeon preference, restoring capsular tension without over-constraining the joint.
13. **Confirm range of motion.** Take the shoulder through a full ROM arc (external rotation to tolerance, abduction) to confirm no impingement of hardware, no restriction from the subscapularis split, and no residual apprehension.
14. **Close the subscapularis split** anatomically with side-to-side sutures (this is a split, not a full takedown, so closure is more limited than after a tenotomy/peel).
15. **Alternative graft (unsuitable coracoid or revision after failed Latarjet).** If proceeding with iliac crest autograft or distal tibial/glenoid allograft instead: harvest or prepare the graft, contour it to match the glenoid defect, decorticate the anterior glenoid neck bed as above, position the graft flush and subequatorial, and fix with screws in the same manner, without the conjoint-tendon sling component (so the "dynamic sling" element of the triple effect is not present with a free bone graft — stabilization relies on the bony and capsular components alone).
16. **Closure.** Layered closure of the deltopectoral interval and skin. Place the arm in a sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the arm position actively during coracoid harvest: abduct/externally rotate to tension and release the coracoacromial ligament, then adduct/internally rotate to expose and release pectoralis minor — this sequence materially eases an otherwise fiddly exposure.
- Favor a subscapularis split over a tenotomy in the modern technique — it preserves more native subscapularis function and simplifies closure, and is the currently favored approach in most published series.
- Flatten the coracoid undersurface deliberately with a saw or burr rather than relying on the native curved shape — a flat graft-to-glenoid interface is what allows a truly flush, subequatorial position.
- Use a dedicated positioning guide system for the arthroscopic technique if available — precise flush, subequatorial graft placement is genuinely difficult to judge by arthroscopic view alone because of lens distortion, and a guide reduces this source of malposition.
- Confirm graft position with fluoroscopy (or direct visualization/palpation in the open technique) before final screw tightening — this is the last easy opportunity to correct a proud or medialized graft before it is committed.
- Direct screws with a slight lateral-to-medial and inferior angulation to maximize purchase in the glenoid neck while staying well clear of both the articular surface and the posterior glenoid cortex/suprascapular notch region.
- Take the shoulder through a full ROM arc intraoperatively after fixation, before final closure — this both confirms no hardware impingement and gives an early check that the subscapularis split has not been overtightened.
- When converting to a free bone graft (iliac crest or distal tibial allograft) for a revision or unsuitable-coracoid case, remember that the dynamic conjoint-tendon sling is absent — the graft and capsular repair are doing more of the stabilizing work, so precise flush positioning and secure fixation matter even more than in a standard coracoid transfer.
- Counsel and plan explicitly for the arthroscopic technique's learning curve — published series show measurable improvement in operative time and complication rate over the first several dozen cases; surgeons transitioning from open technique should consider a structured mentorship/proctoring period.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Lateral screw/graft overhang >2–3 mm is associated with later glenohumeral arthropathy (Samilson-Prieto progression); medialized graft placement under-corrects instability.
- Suprascapular and axillary nerves are at risk during coracoid mobilization and inferior screw placement; musculocutaneous nerve is at risk during medial coracoid dissection (it typically enters the conjoint tendon complex several centimeters distal to the coracoid tip, but this distance is variable and dissection should stay close to the coracoid/conjoint tendon rather than wandering medially).
- Nonunion/fibrous union occurs in ~5–10%; graft osteolysis and screw-related pain/loosening/removal occur in 0–18% (higher after arthroscopic technique in some series — reported screw-removal/complication rates up to 18% arthroscopic vs 7.3% open in some systematic reviews).
- Intra-articular hardware prominence increases long-term arthropathy risk — anatomic, extra-articular hardware placement is intended to mitigate this.
- Subscapularis dysfunction can follow open subscapularis takedown/repair (mitigated by the split technique in modern Latarjet) or aggressive arthroscopic subscapularis-splitting portals.
- An insufficiently flattened glenoid neck bed or coracoid undersurface is a common technical cause of a non-flush graft even when position looks acceptable on a single fluoroscopic view — check flushness circumferentially, not just on one projection.
- Inadequate decortication of the glenoid neck bed, like inadequate coracoid-side preparation, predisposes to nonunion even with technically sound screw fixation.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: sling for comfort; pendulum and passive ROM within a protected arc; avoid resisted internal/external rotation.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive passive-to-active ROM under a subscapularis-protective protocol — limit early resisted internal rotation and forced external rotation stretching to protect the subscapularis split/repair (a key technical difference from isolated Bankart rehab).
- **Weeks 6–12**: active strengthening once radiographic evidence of early graft healing (or clinical criteria per surgeon protocol) supports loading; progress scapular and rotator cuff strengthening.
- **3 months**: confirm bony union/incorporation before considering full-contact clearance; light sport-specific training may begin with surgeon clearance.
- **Return to contact/collision sport**: typically 4–6 months, gated by radiographic graft healing (union rates generally >90–95% in modern series), full strength recovery, and functional testing.
- Reported recurrent instability rate 2–8%; reoperation 5–15% (screw removal a major driver); nonunion 5–10%.
