## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Incarcerated fragment within the joint after an elbow dislocation-reduction — the fragment can be trapped in the joint (mistaken for a loose body if the CRITOE ossification order is not accounted for) and requires extraction.
- Open fracture.
- Ulnar nerve dysfunction in the presence of a displaced fragment.
- Gross valgus instability of the elbow.
- Displaced fragments in adolescent throwing/overhead athletes and gymnasts, where a growing body of evidence (see Pitfalls) shows a markedly higher nonunion rate with cast treatment and raises real concern for long-term valgus instability and reduced throwing performance — many centers are lowering the threshold for fixation in this group specifically.
- Displacement thresholds historically cited (2 mm, 5 mm, 10–15 mm) have never been well-validated and vary by author — use clinical judgment regarding instability, incarceration, and patient activity demands rather than anchoring rigidly to a single millimeter cutoff.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Minimally displaced (<2 mm) fragments with no incarceration and no elbow instability in a low-demand (non-throwing) patient — brief immobilization and early motion remains a reasonable option, acknowledging the evolving evidence discussed above.
- Extremely comminuted/fragmented pieces with inadequate bone stock for screw purchase — plan suture fixation instead rather than forcing screw fixation.
- Chronic, longstanding ununited fragments with substantial retraction may require a different reconstructive discussion (e.g., excision with medial collateral ligament repair/reconstruction in a symptomatic, skeletally mature patient) rather than acute fixation technique.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP and lateral elbow radiographs, with a contralateral comparison view considered selectively if there is uncertainty about whether the medial epicondyle ossification center is truly displaced versus simply not yet fused (know the CRITOE sequence — the medial epicondyle typically ossifies at 5–7 years). If an incarcerated fragment is suspected after a reduced elbow dislocation, dedicated views or CT can help confirm the fragment's location within the joint.
- **Implant selection**: a single partially-threaded cannulated screw (most common technique) sized to the fragment — typically a 3.5–4.0 mm screw in adolescents, smaller diameters in younger children; have heavy nonabsorbable suture (for a suture-through-drill-tunnel technique) available as a backup for small or fragmented pieces not amenable to screw purchase.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: image intensifier, cannulated screw system with guide wires and washers, a small curved elevator/probe for fragment extraction if incarcerated, and suture-passing instruments/drill for tunnel fixation as a backup technique.
- **Neurologic baseline**: document a detailed preoperative ulnar nerve exam (sensory to the little finger, motor to the intrinsics/FDP to the small finger) so any change can be accurately attributed to surgery versus the injury itself.
- **Case-specific planning**: for a fragment incarcerated in the joint after a dislocation, plan the sequence — some surgeons attempt closed extraction with supination/valgus stress and direct pressure under fluoroscopy before committing to open extraction, while others proceed directly to open exposure given the generally low success rate of closed extraction maneuvers.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard.
- **Positioning**: supine with the arm abducted and externally rotated on a hand table (or a bump under the shoulder), or supine with the arm across the chest, to expose the medial elbow — choose the position that gives comfortable access to the medial epicondyle without excessive external rotation stress on the shoulder.
- **Table setup**: ensure the C-arm can obtain AP and lateral elbow views with the arm in the chosen position without needing to reposition the limb mid-case.
- **Tourniquet**: a pneumatic tourniquet applied high on the arm is standard.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire arm circumferentially to allow full elbow flexion/extension and valgus stress testing intraoperatively (both before and after fixation, and to assess for ulnar nerve subluxation with flexion).
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the medial epicondyle, the course of the ulnar nerve (palpable in the cubital tunnel groove posterior to the medial epicondyle), and the planned incision before prepping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach**: make a curvilinear incision centered over the medial epicondyle, curving slightly posteriorly to allow ulnar nerve identification if needed, and develop the subcutaneous plane.
2. **Identify and protect the ulnar nerve**: locate the ulnar nerve in the cubital tunnel posterior to the medial epicondyle before any bony work — gently mobilize it with a vessel loop if it lies close to the operative field, and specifically check whether it subluxates anteriorly over the epicondyle with elbow flexion, which (if present) is an indication to formally transpose it.
3. **Extract an incarcerated fragment (if present)**: if the fragment is trapped within the joint following a dislocation-reduction, apply gentle valgus stress and supination to open the medial joint space, and use a small elevator or probe to tease the fragment out from the joint under direct vision and fluoroscopic guidance; avoid aggressive prying that could fracture the fragment further or injure adjacent articular cartilage.
4. **Expose and prepare the fracture bed**: clear hematoma and any interposed soft tissue from the donor site on the distal humerus and from the undersurface of the fragment, without stripping the flexor-pronator origin more than necessary from the fragment itself (the muscle origin provides some residual blood supply and soft-tissue continuity useful for orientation).
5. **Reduce the fragment**: reposition the fragment anatomically against its donor bed, correcting any rotation, and hold provisionally with a pointed reduction clamp or a temporary K-wire.
6. **Screw fixation**: place a guide wire from the fragment into the distal humeral metaphysis under fluoroscopic control, confirm position on AP and lateral views, then place a partially-threaded cannulated screw (with a washer if the fragment is thin or at risk of countersinking) achieving interfragmentary compression; avoid over-tightening on a small or thin fragment, which can crack it.
7. **Suture fixation (for small/fragmented pieces)**: drill two small parallel tunnels through the donor bed on the distal humerus, pass heavy nonabsorbable suture through/around the fragment (capturing the periosteum and flexor-pronator origin substance) and through the tunnels, and tie over a bone bridge with the fragment held reduced.
8. **Assess ulnar nerve position and stability**: take the elbow through a full flexion-extension arc and check the ulnar nerve does not subluxate over the medial epicondyle or fixation hardware; if it does, or if there was preoperative ulnar nerve dysfunction, perform a formal anterior subcutaneous (or submuscular) transposition.
9. **Stress test the fixation**: apply gentle valgus stress under fluoroscopy to confirm the fixation resists displacement and the joint is congruent, and cycle the elbow through a range of motion to confirm no mechanical block.
10. **Closure**: close the fascia and skin in layers, taking care not to constrict the ulnar nerve if it has been transposed.
11. **Splinting**: apply a well-padded long-arm splint or cast at approximately 90° flexion before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always identify the ulnar nerve early in the exposure, before any bony manipulation — the nerve's proximity to the fragment and the frequency of transposition decisions make this a "find it first" structure rather than one to protect incidentally.
- Specifically test for ulnar nerve subluxation with elbow flexion intraoperatively, even if the preoperative exam was normal — a subluxating nerve that will ride directly over your hardware is an indication to transpose it at the index surgery rather than risk a symptomatic revisit.
- For an incarcerated fragment, gentle valgus stress combined with supination is the maneuver most likely to open the joint enough to deliver the fragment — resist the temptation to lever forcefully, which risks fracturing the fragment or damaging the trochlear cartilage.
- Preserve as much of the flexor-pronator muscle attachment to the fragment as is reasonable during exposure — beyond blood supply considerations, it helps you orient a small fragment correctly during reduction.
- When using screw fixation on a thin or immature fragment, under-tighten slightly relative to what feels appropriate in an adult bone — pediatric epicondylar fragments are more prone to fracturing under compression than their size alone would suggest.
- If suture fixation is chosen, take a generous soft-tissue bite (periosteum plus flexor-pronator origin) rather than suturing bone-to-bone alone — the added soft-tissue purchase meaningfully increases construct strength in a fragmented piece.
- Confirm reduction and joint congruity with a valgus stress fluoroscopic view before leaving the OR — this both validates the fixation and gives you a baseline to compare against at follow-up if instability is a later concern.
- Counsel throwing athletes and their families before surgery, not just postoperatively, that the rehabilitation and return-to-throwing timeline (3–4 months with a structured progression) is longer than for other elbow-region fractures, given the valgus-stress environment the healed fixation must tolerate.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Historically cited displacement thresholds for surgery (2 mm, 5 mm, 10–15 mm) have never been well-validated and vary by author — do not treat any single number as definitive.
- The traditional teaching that medial epicondyle nonunion is a benign, asymptomatic radiographic curiosity (given the extrasynovial location) is being challenged: a 2025 RCT reported nonunion in only 1/37 (2.7%) surgically-fixed fractures versus 24/35 (68.6%) cast-treated fractures, raising real questions about long-term valgus stability and throwing performance in the nonoperative group.
- A follow-on UK multicenter RCT protocol (SCIENCE trial, results expected 2025–2027) is formally testing surgery vs. cast for displaced fractures and may further shift the displacement threshold for ORIF — treat current thresholds as evolving.
- Iatrogenic ulnar nerve injury or irritation is the principal structure-at-risk complication of this exposure — most commonly from retraction injury or an unrecognized subluxating nerve riding over hardware; a formal transposition at the index procedure, when indicated, is protective.
- Fragment fracture/comminution during extraction of an incarcerated piece, or during over-aggressive screw compression, converts a straightforward screw case into a more difficult suture-fixation case — anticipate this possibility and have suture fixation instruments open and available even when planning a screw.
- Loss of terminal elbow extension is a recognized, generally mild postoperative finding after medial-sided elbow surgery in this population — usually resolves with the standard active-motion protocol below, but warrants monitoring if it persists beyond expected milestones.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Immobilize 3–4 weeks, consistent with other elbow-region fractures; active ROM begins after cast removal, with formal physiotherapy reserved for motion that plateaus well below expected milestones.
- Return to sport generally at 6–8 weeks once union and function have returned.
- **Return to competitive throwing** is delayed to **3–4 months** with a structured, gradual throwing progression, given the valgus-stress environment of the healed fixation.
