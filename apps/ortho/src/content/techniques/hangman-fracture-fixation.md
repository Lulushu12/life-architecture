## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Levine-Edwards Type II hangman fracture that fails or is unsuitable for halo vest treatment (persistent instability, unacceptable angulation/translation, or inability to tolerate a halo).
- Type IIA hangman fracture — traction is contraindicated in this pattern, so surgical fixation is favored over prolonged halo attempts when reduction with extension/compression alone is unsatisfactory.
- Type III hangman fracture (bilateral pars fracture with facet dislocation) — surgery is mandatory regardless of the pars fracture's own displacement, because the facet dislocation component is inherently unstable.
- Significant C2-3 disc/ALL disruption with translation, where an anterior construct can directly restore the segment.
- Failure of nonoperative treatment (progressive angulation/translation on interval imaging, nonunion) in an initially Type I or II injury.

**Contraindications**
- **Type I hangman fracture** (<3mm translation, no angulation, intact C2-3 disc/ALL) — stable, treated with a rigid collar; surgery is not indicated.
- **Active local infection** at the planned surgical site.
- **Prohibitive medical comorbidity** where the risk of anesthesia/surgery outweighs the marginal benefit over a halo trial, particularly in frail patients — individualized discussion is warranted.
- **Anterior approach specifically contraindicated** by prior anterior neck surgery/scarring, or by anatomy precluding safe exposure (relative) — favor posterior fixation in these cases.
- **Posterior approach specifically limited** by posterior element comminution precluding safe pars/pedicle screw purchase — favor anterior ACDF or a combined approach instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- **CT (thin-cut, sagittal/coronal reconstructions)** through C2-C3 to characterize the pars fracture pattern, translation, angulation, and facet status — this differentiates Levine-Edwards types and is the primary driver of surgical planning.
- **MRI** to assess C2-3 disc/ALL/PLL integrity, cord status, and to identify any epidural hematoma or occult ligamentous injury not apparent on CT — particularly important when the CT findings are borderline between Type II and IIA.
- **CT angiography of the neck** given the proximity of the fracture to the transverse foramen/vertebral artery — assess for vertebral artery injury or anomalous course before planning posterior C2 screw fixation.
- **Decide approach preoperatively based on dominant pathology**: anterior C2-3 ACDF when disc/ALL disruption and translation predominate; posterior C2 pars/pedicle fixation (direct pars screw fixation of the fracture itself, or C1-C3 posterior instrumented fusion) when facet dislocation or posterior element integrity favors direct posterior fixation while preserving C2-3 motion where possible.
- **Implant selection**: for ACDF, a low-profile anterior cervical plate and interbody graft/cage sized to the C2-3 disc space; for posterior fixation, a screw-rod system capable of C1-C3 instrumentation if extension beyond C2-3 is needed, plus dedicated pars/pedicle screws for direct fracture fixation where anatomy allows.
- **Plan for halo vest as a bridge or bail-out**: even in surgical candidates, an initial trial of reduction in a halo (except in Type IIA, where axial traction is contraindicated) may be attempted first in select Type II injuries; have a halo available on standby if intraoperative findings suggest a staged approach is safer.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia with awake fiberoptic intubation** (or careful in-line stabilization) is preferred given the unstable C2-3 segment; avoid neck extension/rotation during induction.
- **For anterior ACDF**: supine positioning on a radiolucent table with a small shoulder roll and the head in a neutral-to-slightly-extended position in a horseshoe headrest or gel doughnut, similar to a standard subaxial ACDF setup; shoulders taped caudally for lower-level fluoroscopic visualization if needed, though C2-3 is usually accessible without aggressive shoulder retraction.
- **For posterior fixation**: prone positioning with the head rigidly fixed in a Mayfield three-pin clamp (preferred for precise C2 screw placement) or, in select cases, a well-padded horseshoe headrest if a clamp is contraindicated; neutral cervical alignment confirmed under lateral fluoroscopy before prepping, with gentle traction/positioning used to reduce translation/angulation as far as safely possible before final head fixation.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: true lateral and AP views of C2-3 (and C1-C2 if instrumentation will extend there) obtainable without repositioning the head clamp or table — confirm this before draping in both anterior and posterior approaches.
- **Prep and drape**: for anterior approach, a standard low-to-mid anterior cervical field (chin to sternal notch); for posterior approach, a posterior midline field extending from occiput/C1 (in case of extension to C1-C3 fixation) to at least C4-C5.
- Mark planned incision levels under fluoroscopy before incising in either approach.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the injury pattern on CT and MRI immediately before proceeding** — reassess C2-3 disc/ALL disruption, translation, and angulation to confirm the Levine-Edwards type and the planned approach remain appropriate.
2. **If attempting reduction first (Type II, non-IIA)**: apply gentle extension and axial traction under fluoroscopy to reduce translation/angulation before committing to the definitive approach; reassess alignment on lateral fluoroscopy. **Do not apply axial traction in Type IIA** — extension and gentle compression are used instead, since the anteriorly-opened disc space in this hinge-injury pattern makes traction likely to worsen distraction.

### Anterior C2-3 ACDF pathway
3. **Approach**: standard left- or right-sided Smith-Robinson anterior cervical exposure to the C2-3 disc space, developing the interval between the carotid sheath and trachea/esophagus, with cephalad extension of dissection sufficient to visualize the inferior body of C2 and superior body of C3.
4. **Perform discectomy** at C2-3, removing disrupted disc material and any retropulsed fragments, and directly visualize/assess the posterior longitudinal ligament and any residual canal compromise.
5. **Restore alignment** by gentle distraction/manipulation across the disc space under fluoroscopy, correcting residual translation/angulation of the traumatic spondylolisthesis before graft placement.
6. **Place a structural interbody graft or cage** sized to restore disc height and segmental lordosis, then apply a low-profile anterior cervical plate spanning C2-3, securing bicortical or unicortical screws per plate system design into the C2 and C3 bodies.
7. **Confirm final alignment and hardware position** on AP and lateral fluoroscopy before closure.

### Posterior C2 pars/pedicle fixation pathway
8. **Approach**: posterior midline exposure with subperiosteal dissection to the posterior elements of C1 (if extension planned), C2, and C3, taking care around the C2 pars/lamina where the fracture lies.
9. **Assess the pars fracture directly** and, for direct pars screw fixation, identify a trajectory that crosses the fracture line into the C2 vertebral body under fluoroscopic guidance — this is feasible when the fracture pattern and bone stock allow safe screw purchase across the fracture itself.
10. **For Type III injuries or when direct pars fixation is not feasible**, proceed to C1-C3 (or C2-3) posterior instrumented fusion: place C1 lateral mass screws (if extending to C1), C2 pedicle/pars/translaminar screws, and C3 lateral mass screws as needed, with attention to vertebral artery anatomy at C2 as in Goel-Harms-type constructs.
11. **Reduce the facet dislocation** (Type III) directly under vision and fluoroscopy before final rod placement and screw tightening — direct posterior access is a key advantage of this approach for facet dislocation injuries.
12. **Contour and secure rods**, decorticate the posterior elements across the instrumented levels, and pack autograft/allograft to promote fusion.
13. **Confirm alignment and hardware position on final AP/lateral fluoroscopy.**

### Closure (either approach)
14. **Irrigate, achieve hemostasis**, and close in anatomic layers; a drain may be used for the posterior approach given the potential for more extensive dissection and epidural venous bleeding.
15. **Obtain postoperative CT** before mobilization to confirm hardware position and alignment.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Reassess the fracture type (II vs IIA vs III) immediately before positioning, not just from the initial trauma-bay imaging — interval displacement can change the surgical plan.
- In Type IIA specifically, use extension and gentle compression rather than any axial traction during intraoperative reduction attempts — the biomechanics of this hinge-type injury make traction actively harmful.
- For the anterior approach, take the discectomy dissection cephalad enough to fully visualize the inferior C2 body — under-visualizing this margin risks incomplete decompression/reduction of the traumatic spondylolisthesis.
- For direct pars screw fixation, confirm the fracture pattern truly allows a trajectory across the fracture into solid C2 body bone on preoperative CT — attempting this in a pattern better suited to segmental fixation risks inadequate purchase.
- When extending posterior fixation to C1, apply the same vertebral-artery-conscious dissection and screw-trajectory principles used in Goel-Harms C1-C2 fixation, since the pars fracture sits close to the vertebral artery's course through the C2 transverse foramen.
- Reduce facet dislocations under direct posterior vision before final rod tightening — attempting reduction purely through rod-based compression after screws are locked is less controlled and risks screw/bone interface failure.
- Keep a halo vest available and consider a staged approach (halo trial first, surgery reserved for failure) in Type II injuries where reduction and stability are achieved without traction — not every Type II fracture that reaches the surgical discussion ultimately needs surgery.
- In combined injuries with both anterior disc disruption and posterior facet involvement, do not force a single-approach solution — a combined anterior-posterior construct may be safer than an aggressive single-approach fixation attempting to address both columns.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Do not apply axial traction to Type IIA injuries** — the anteriorly-opened disc space and flexion-distraction mechanism make traction likely to worsen distraction rather than reduce the fracture; treat with extension and compression in a halo, or go to primary surgical fixation.
- **Type III's facet dislocation component is what mandates surgery** — reduction and fixation (posterior C2-3, or combined) is required regardless of how displaced the pars fracture itself appears.
- **Choose ACDF when the pathology is primarily anterior** (disc/ALL disruption with translation) **and posterior C2 pars/pedicle fixation when posterior element integrity allows direct fracture fixation while preserving C2-3 motion** — approach selection should follow the dominant injury pattern rather than surgeon preference alone.
- **Vertebral artery injury** is a risk during posterior C2 screw placement given the fracture's proximity to the transverse foramen — preoperative CTA and careful trajectory planning are essential mitigations.
- **Esophageal/tracheal retraction injury** is a risk of the anterior approach, as with any anterior cervical exposure — use padded retractors and periodic release during longer cases.
- **Incomplete reduction of translation/angulation** with either approach if distraction/manipulation is not adequately achieved before graft or hardware placement — reassess alignment on fluoroscopy at each stage rather than only at the end.
- **Nonunion or hardware failure** if construct choice does not match the dominant instability pattern (e.g., an anterior-only construct in a predominantly posterior/facet injury, or vice versa).
- **Postoperative dysphagia** after anterior exposure, usually transient.

## Postoperative Protocol

Post-op management follows the general upper cervical fracture rehabilitation pathway: wound care and pain control acutely (0–2 weeks); formal ROM restrictions until early fusion consolidation (~6–8 weeks) in the subacute phase (2–12 weeks), with interval imaging at 6–12 weeks; progressive loading and brace weaning once radiographic fusion is confirmed (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable fixation; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months. Return-to-sport/contact-activity clearance requires solid radiographic fusion (≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, and normalized strength, and remains individualized and conservative for cervical spine injuries.
