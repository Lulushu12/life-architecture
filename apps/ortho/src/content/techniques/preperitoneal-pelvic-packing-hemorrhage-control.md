## Indications

For the hemodynamically unstable, FAST-negative patient in whom the pelvis is identified as the hemorrhage source, when instability persists despite binder placement and 1–2 L crystalloid resuscitation. Transfusion trigger for escalation: ongoing instability after 2 U PRBC or persistent base deficit >-6 despite binder. Performed for the exsanguinating patient in the OR — not the angio suite.

## Positioning & setup

Performed in the operating room, not interventional radiology, so that packing and mechanical stabilization can be delivered together for the exsanguinating patient. A pelvic binder or sheet is placed at the level of the greater trochanters (not the iliac crests) immediately on clinical suspicion, before imaging. REBOA (femoral access, Zone III, below the renal arteries) may be used concurrently as an extremis bridge, and is combined with — not a substitute for — mechanical stabilization and packing.

## Steps

1. Apply binder/sheet at the greater trochanters immediately on suspicion of unstable pelvis, before further workup.
2. Primary survey: if hemodynamically unstable despite binder + 1–2 L crystalloid, activate the massive transfusion protocol, obtain a pelvic AP film, and perform FAST.
3. FAST positive (intraperitoneal blood) → proceed to laparotomy first; address the pelvis intra- or post-operatively.
4. FAST negative, pelvis identified as the source, patient unstable → proceed to preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) ± REBOA Zone III as a bridge, performed concurrently with external fixation or a pelvic C-clamp for mechanical stability, together in the OR.
5. Make a 6–8 cm lower midline incision and pack lap sponges into the true pelvis along the pelvic brim, typically 3 per side.
6. Place external fixation or a pelvic C-clamp concurrently to provide mechanical stability and restore tamponade by reducing pelvic volume.
7. If REBOA is used, keep Zone III inflation time under 60 minutes where possible given sharply rising distal ischemia risk beyond this point.
8. In a patient who is hemodynamically stable, or responds transiently, with contrast blush on CT, proceed to angioembolization (non-selective internal iliac trunk or selective named-branch) in the interventional suite — as an initial strategy in the stable-enough patient, or as a staged next step after packing if bleeding persists.
9. Stage definitive posterior/anterior internal fixation once physiology normalizes (lactate clearing, base deficit corrected, coagulopathy resolved) — typically 2–10 days post-injury (damage-control orthopaedics).

## Pitfalls

- Angioembolization targets arterial sources (superior gluteal, obturator, internal pudendal, lateral sacral branches of the internal iliac) but is often ineffective for the presacral venous plexus and cancellous bone bleeding, which is the major volume source in most pelvic hemorrhage — this is why packing/mechanical stabilization and embolization are complementary, not substitutive.
- Repeated manual pelvic "spring testing" for stability risks dislodging clot and precipitating hemorrhage; a single gentle manual assessment is acceptable only once, early, before the binder is applied.
- Open pelvic fracture (perineal, vaginal, or rectal wound, degloving) carries mortality up to 25–50% and mandates consideration of diverting colostomy, aggressive debridement, and external fixation in addition to hemorrhage control.
- Urethral/bladder injury (blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal ecchymosis) requires a retrograde urethrogram before Foley placement — do not overlook this while focused on hemorrhage control.
- Ring disruption can more than double pelvic volume in an "open-book" pattern; binder/mechanical stabilization is what restores the tamponade effect and must not be delayed for imaging in a clinically unstable pelvis.

## Post-op protocol

Weight-bearing and mobilization follow the pelvic ring definitive-fixation pathway once staged internal fixation is complete: touch-down/toe-touch weight-bearing at 0–6 weeks if posterior fixation is percutaneous with good bone quality and anatomic reduction (non-weight-bearing if fixation is tenuous), continued DVT prophylaxis, and bed mobility/transfer training in the acute phase. Progressive weight-bearing at 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic healing, with full weight-bearing typically by 12 weeks for percutaneous posterior fixation in good bone (longer for lumbopelvic constructs or severe comminution).
