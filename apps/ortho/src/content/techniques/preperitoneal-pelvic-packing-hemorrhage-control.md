## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- The hemodynamically unstable, FAST-negative patient in whom the pelvis is identified as the hemorrhage source, when instability persists despite binder placement and 1–2 L crystalloid resuscitation.
- Transfusion trigger for escalation: ongoing instability after 2 U PRBC or persistent base deficit >-6 despite binder.
- Performed for the exsanguinating patient in the OR — not the angio suite — where packing and mechanical stabilization (external fixation or pelvic C-clamp) can be delivered together.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- FAST-positive patient with intraperitoneal hemorrhage — proceed to laparotomy first; the pelvis is addressed intra- or post-operatively once the intraperitoneal source is controlled, not before.
- Hemodynamically stable patient, or one who responds and remains stable after initial resuscitation, with a contrast blush identified on CT — proceed to angioembolization in the interventional suite rather than an OR-based packing procedure, since this patient is stable enough to tolerate transport and a more targeted arterial intervention.
- Do not delay binder application or mechanical stabilization to obtain imaging in a clinically unstable pelvis — imaging (including CT) should follow, not precede, initial mechanical stabilization in the truly unstable patient.
- Packing does not substitute for arterial control — a patient with an identified arterial blush who remains unstable after packing still needs angioembolization as a staged next step; do not treat packing as definitive hemorrhage control on its own.

## Preoperative Planning

- **This is a damage-control, time-critical procedure** — "preoperative planning" is compressed into the primary survey and resuscitation bay, not a scheduled workup. The key planning task is recognizing the indication quickly and mobilizing the right team and equipment without delay.
- **Team activation**: activate the massive transfusion protocol (MTP) early — do not wait for packing to begin before requesting blood products. Mobilize orthopaedic trauma (for concurrent external fixation/C-clamp) and general/trauma surgery (for the packing incision and laparotomy decision) simultaneously; this is inherently a multidisciplinary procedure performed in the same setting, not sequential handoffs.
- **Equipment**: have laparotomy pads immediately available in sufficient number (typically 3 per side, up to 9 total in more severe cases), a Cobb elevator or ringed (sponge) forceps for pack placement, a pelvic external fixation set or pelvic C-clamp with fluoroscopy, and a standard laparotomy tray on standby in case the procedure is combined with or converted to laparotomy.
- **Imaging planning**: a pelvic AP film is obtained as part of the primary survey before OR transfer when time allows; CT angiography is reserved for the patient stable enough to acquire it — do not plan to obtain CT in the truly exsanguinating patient before proceeding to packing.
- **Blood bank/hematology coordination**: anticipate large-volume transfusion and early correction of coagulopathy (calcium, fibrinogen repletion, balanced ratio transfusion) as part of the same resuscitative sequence, not as an afterthought once packing is underway.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with rapid-sequence induction is standard, recognizing that induction itself can precipitate hemodynamic collapse in a marginally compensated patient from loss of sympathetic tone — have vasopressors and blood products immediately available at induction, and consider having the surgical team ready to begin incision immediately after the airway is secured rather than waiting for full anesthetic stabilization.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent operating table capable of accommodating fluoroscopy for concurrent external fixation/C-clamp placement and, if needed, pelvic or long-bone imaging.
- **Binder/mechanical stabilization first**: a pelvic binder or sheet is placed at the level of the greater trochanters (not the iliac crests — placement at the crests fails to adequately compress the pelvic ring and can worsen certain fracture patterns) immediately on clinical suspicion of an unstable pelvis, before imaging and before OR transfer if possible. This is not replaced but supplemented by definitive external fixation or C-clamp placement in the OR.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the lower abdomen/pelvis for the midline preperitoneal incision, and simultaneously prep the iliac crests/lateral thighs if external fixation pins or a C-clamp will be placed in the same setting — plan the field to allow both without re-prepping.
- **REBOA access**: if REBOA is anticipated as an extremis bridge, prep femoral access (typically common femoral artery, ultrasound-guided or cutdown) as part of the same setup; REBOA (Zone III, below the renal arteries) is used concurrently with — not as a substitute for — mechanical stabilization and packing.
- **Fluoroscopy**: position the C-arm to allow pelvic AP imaging for external fixation/C-clamp placement without interfering with the packing incision field.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Apply a binder/sheet at the greater trochanters** immediately on suspicion of an unstable pelvis, before further workup — this single step restores tamponade by reducing pelvic volume and should never be delayed for imaging in a clinically unstable patient.
2. **Primary survey**: if hemodynamically unstable despite the binder and 1–2 L crystalloid, activate the massive transfusion protocol, obtain a pelvic AP film, and perform FAST.
3. **FAST positive** (intraperitoneal blood) → proceed to laparotomy first; address the pelvis intra- or post-operatively once the intraperitoneal source is controlled.
4. **FAST negative, pelvis identified as the source, patient unstable** → proceed to preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP) ± REBOA Zone III as a bridge, performed concurrently with external fixation or a pelvic C-clamp for mechanical stability, together in the OR.
5. **Make a 6–8 cm lower midline incision** from just above the pubic symphysis cephalad, carrying dissection through skin and subcutaneous tissue to the fascia, and incise the fascia in the midline (linea alba) — stay extraperitoneal throughout; do not enter the peritoneal cavity.
6. **Enter the preperitoneal space**: once opened, hematoma is typically encountered and egresses — this confirms the correct plane. The preperitoneal space forms an inverted-U shape around the bladder, comprising the preperitoneal, paravesical, and presacral spaces bilaterally.
7. **Retract the bladder to the contralateral side** to allow access to the true pelvis on the side being packed, then work one side at a time.
8. **Pack the true pelvis along the pelvic brim**: using a blunt instrument (Cobb elevator or ringed forceps), push the first laparotomy pad down onto the sacrum as far posteriorly and inferiorly as possible — this deepest pack is the most important for tamponading the presacral venous plexus. Place additional packs sequentially anterior to the first, building toward the pelvic brim. Typically 3 pads per side (6 total) achieve adequate tamponade; up to 9 total may be needed in more severe hemorrhage.
9. **Repeat on the contralateral side** using the same technique.
10. **Place external fixation or a pelvic C-clamp concurrently** (by the orthopaedic team, simultaneously or immediately following packing) to provide mechanical stability and restore tamponade by reducing pelvic volume — packing without mechanical stabilization, or vice versa, is incomplete treatment for the exsanguinating unstable pelvis.
11. **Close the midline fascia** over the packs, leaving the packs in place — this is a temporary closure anticipating planned re-exploration, not a definitive closure.
12. **If REBOA is used**, keep Zone III inflation time under 60 minutes where possible, given sharply rising distal ischemia risk beyond this point; deflate as soon as source control and adequate resuscitation allow.
13. **In a patient who becomes hemodynamically stable, or responds transiently**, with a contrast blush on CT, proceed to angioembolization (non-selective internal iliac trunk or selective named-branch) in the interventional suite — either as an initial strategy in the stable-enough patient, or as a staged next step after packing if bleeding persists despite packing and mechanical stabilization.
14. **Plan re-exploration and pack removal** at 24–48 hours once resuscitation parameters (lactate clearance, base deficit correction, coagulopathy resolution) support it. At re-exploration, remove the packs, inspect for ongoing bleeding, and proceed to definitive closure with subperitoneal drainage if hemostasis is adequate; concurrent definitive bone fixation or urologic exploration can be performed at the same setting if physiology allows.
15. **Stage definitive posterior/anterior internal fixation** once physiology normalizes fully (lactate clearing, base deficit corrected, coagulopathy resolved) — typically 2–10 days post-injury (damage-control orthopaedics), separate from the initial packing/pack-removal procedures.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Place the deepest, most posterior pack directly onto the sacrum first, using a blunt instrument to push it as far into the presacral space as possible — this pack does the most work tamponading the presacral venous plexus, which is the dominant volume source in most pelvic hemorrhage.
- Work systematically one side at a time with a consistent pack count (typically 3 per side) rather than packing haphazardly — this makes pack retrieval at re-exploration reliable and reduces the risk of a retained pack.
- Retract the bladder fully to the contralateral side before packing — inadequate bladder retraction both limits access to the true pelvis and risks bladder injury during pack placement.
- Perform packing and external fixation/C-clamp placement concurrently rather than sequentially when staffing allows — the two interventions are complementary (mechanical stability plus tamponade) and this shortens total time to hemorrhage control.
- Do not attempt to achieve arterial control through packing alone — if a CTA-confirmed arterial blush exists and the patient stabilizes enough to reach the angio suite, proceed to angioembolization rather than assuming packing has addressed the arterial source.
- Keep the initial midline incision limited (6–8 cm) — this is a damage-control exposure, not a definitive laparotomy, and a smaller incision is faster to make and close while still providing adequate access to the preperitoneal space bilaterally.
- Communicate explicitly with the receiving ICU/floor team about the planned 24–48 hour re-exploration window at the time of the index procedure — this is a staged plan, not an open-ended decision, and clear communication prevents both premature and delayed pack removal.
- Screen for venous thromboembolism proactively in patients who have undergone PPP — duplex screening is reasonable given this population's combination of pelvic trauma, immobility, and major surgery, all independent VTE risk factors.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Angioembolization targets arterial sources** (superior gluteal, obturator, internal pudendal, lateral sacral branches of the internal iliac) but is often ineffective for the presacral venous plexus and cancellous bone bleeding, which is the major volume source in most pelvic hemorrhage — this is why packing/mechanical stabilization and embolization are complementary, not substitutive, and why persistent instability after successful embolization does not rule out ongoing venous/cancellous bleeding requiring packing.
- **Repeated manual pelvic "spring testing"** for stability risks dislodging clot and precipitating hemorrhage; a single gentle manual assessment is acceptable only once, early, before the binder is applied — do not repeat this test to "confirm" instability once a binder is already in place.
- **Open pelvic fracture** (perineal, vaginal, or rectal wound, degloving) carries mortality up to 25–50% and mandates consideration of diverting colostomy, aggressive debridement, and external fixation in addition to hemorrhage control — inspect the perineum, rectum, and vagina (log-roll, rectal/vaginal exam) as part of the same evaluation, since this changes the management plan (contamination control) independent of the hemorrhage-control pathway.
- **Urethral/bladder injury** (blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal ecchymosis) requires a retrograde urethrogram before Foley placement — do not overlook this while focused on hemorrhage control, and do not place a Foley blindly in a patient with these findings.
- **Ring disruption can more than double pelvic volume** in an "open-book" pattern; binder/mechanical stabilization is what restores the tamponade effect and must not be delayed for imaging in a clinically unstable pelvis.
- **Bladder injury during pack placement** from inadequate retraction — retract the bladder fully to the contralateral side before advancing packs.
- **Retained pack** at re-exploration — maintain a consistent, documented pack count per side at the index procedure so nothing is missed at removal.
- **Persistent bleeding despite packing** should prompt escalation (angioembolization if an arterial source is identified and the patient can tolerate transport, or repacking/laparotomy) rather than assuming packing alone will eventually control ongoing arterial hemorrhage.
- **VTE risk** is notably elevated in patients undergoing PPP — this reflects both the severity of the underlying injury and the procedure itself; maintain a structured screening and prophylaxis protocol once bleeding risk allows initiation of chemoprophylaxis.
- **Surgical site infection** is a recognized risk given the packed, re-explored wound — risk factors include prolonged pack dwell time and the severity of associated injuries; minimize dwell time to the planned 24–48 hour window where physiology allows.

## Postoperative Protocol

Weight-bearing and mobilization follow the pelvic ring definitive-fixation pathway once staged internal fixation is complete: touch-down/toe-touch weight-bearing at 0–6 weeks if posterior fixation is percutaneous with good bone quality and anatomic reduction (non-weight-bearing if fixation is tenuous), continued DVT prophylaxis, and bed mobility/transfer training in the acute phase. Progressive weight-bearing at 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic healing, with full weight-bearing typically by 12 weeks for percutaneous posterior fixation in good bone (longer for lumbopelvic constructs or severe comminution).
