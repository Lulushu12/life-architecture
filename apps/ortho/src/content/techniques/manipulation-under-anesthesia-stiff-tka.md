## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Stiffness after TKA — commonly defined as flexion <90° and/or a flexion contracture >15° beyond the expected postoperative timeline (generally by 6–12 weeks) — that has not responded to aggressive supervised physiotherapy and splinting.
- Best performed in the **6-week to 3-month** window post-primary TKA; efficacy drops substantially beyond 3 months and MUA is largely ineffective beyond ~1 year, at which point mature fibrosis no longer yields to closed manipulation.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active or suspected periprosthetic joint infection — exclude infection in every case before attributing stiffness to arthrofibrosis alone; manipulating an infected knee delays diagnosis and risks bacteremic seeding.
- A correctable structural cause identified on work-up (component malposition/oversizing, elevated joint line, tibial slope error, patella baja, flexion/extension gap mismatch) — MUA does not correct a mechanical problem and should not substitute for revision when one is present.
- Osteoporotic or otherwise fragile bone where the force required for manipulation carries an unacceptable periprosthetic fracture risk — consider arthroscopic or open lysis instead.
- Presentation well beyond the 1-year mark, where mature dense fibrosis is unlikely to yield to closed manipulation and the risk-benefit tips toward lysis of adhesions or revision.
- Extensor mechanism compromise (partial patellar tendon or quadriceps injury) — manipulation under load through a compromised extensor mechanism risks completing a rupture.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm timing: calculate weeks since index TKA and place the patient in the optimal 6-week to 3-month window if possible; document why intervention is being pursued earlier or later if outside that window.
- Imaging: current AP/lateral knee radiographs to assess component position, patella height (patella baja), and to serve as a baseline for comparison should a periprosthetic fracture be suspected after manipulation. CT for femoral/tibial component rotation if malrotation is a possible driver of stiffness — this changes the plan from MUA to revision.
- Laboratory: ESR/CRP (± aspiration) to exclude infection in every case before proceeding.
- Confirm no contraindication exists (see above) and discuss with the patient that failure to gain/maintain ROM by the 2-week mark will prompt consideration of arthroscopic lysis of adhesions (aLOA) rather than repeated manipulation.
- No special implants are needed; have the original operative report available to confirm implant design (cruciate-retaining designs are associated with higher MUA failure rates and should raise the index of suspicion for structural causes) and box/insert geometry.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Regional (spinal or dense femoral/adductor canal block sufficient to abolish protective quadriceps guarding) or general anesthesia — adequate muscle relaxation is essential; attempting manipulation under inadequate anesthesia both fails to achieve full ROM and risks applying uncontrolled force against a guarding quadriceps.
- Supine position on a standard table with the hip flexed to roughly 90° and the operative leg free to be flexed over the end of the table or with the surgeon supporting the thigh — position varies by surgeon preference, but the key requirement is a short, controlled lever arm at the proximal tibia, not a long lever arm through the ankle/foot.
- No tourniquet is required; sterile technique/draping is minimal since no incision is made, but standard aseptic skin prep is still appropriate given the joint is being manipulated.
- Have fluoroscopy available in the room (not always used, but useful if a periprosthetic fracture is suspected intraoperatively or if the surgeon wants to confirm component tracking through the manipulated arc).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the patient is anesthetized to a level that abolishes quadriceps guarding — test with a gentle initial flexion attempt before applying any real force.
2. Document baseline passive ROM (flexion and any extension deficit) under anesthesia before manipulation for comparison afterward.
3. Support the distal thigh with one hand close to the joint line and apply a slow, steady, controlled flexion force through the proximal tibia — a short lever arm at the tibia, not a long lever arm through the ankle or foot, which multiplies force uncontrollably and raises fracture/extensor injury risk.
4. Feel for the release of adhesions as a gradual "give" rather than forcing through a hard end point; a hard, sudden stop suggests bony or mechanical block (e.g., patellar impingement, component conflict) rather than soft-tissue restriction, and should prompt reassessment rather than escalating force.
5. Progress flexion incrementally, pausing to reassess rather than applying maximal force in one continuous motion; work the knee through several cycles of flexion-extension once initial adhesions release to consolidate the gain.
6. If a significant extension deficit/flexion contracture is also present, apply gentle, controlled extension force as well — both directions of restriction may need to be addressed.
7. Reassess and document final ROM achieved under anesthesia; compare to baseline.
8. Begin an immediate continuous passive motion (CPM) or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM program the same day as the manipulation, before the patient leaves the recovery area if feasible — ROM gains achieved intraoperatively are lost rapidly without immediate reinforcement.
9. Consider adjuvant anti-inflammatory strategies around MUA to improve sustained ROM gains, per recent multicenter randomized data.
10. Arrange formal physiotherapy to begin within 24–48 hours and schedule the 2-week ROM checkpoint before the patient leaves.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm true quadriceps relaxation before applying force — a partially guarding quadriceps under inadequate anesthesia is the most common reason for both under-treatment (inadequate ROM gained) and injury (uncontrolled force against resistance).
- Use a short lever arm at the proximal tibia rather than levering through the foot/ankle — this concentrates controlled force at the knee and avoids the mechanical amplification that causes fracture or extensor injury.
- Apply steady, sustained pressure rather than rapid thrusts; feel for a gradual give characteristic of adhesion release rather than forcing through a hard bony end point.
- If a hard end point is reached well short of expected ROM, stop and consider that a mechanical block (retained cement, component malposition, patellar baja with impingement) rather than pure fibrosis is limiting motion — this patient likely needs imaging/revision rather than repeat manipulation.
- Work the knee through several cycles once initial motion is regained rather than stopping at first release — this helps consolidate the gain before reinforcement begins.
- Start CPM or an aggressive ROM program the same day, ideally before leaving the recovery area — the single biggest determinant of durable success is preventing the knee from re-stiffening in the first 24–48 hours.
- Set the patient's expectations before the procedure: the 2-week ROM checkpoint will determine whether further intervention (aLOA) is needed, so early, consistent engagement with physiotherapy in that window is critical.
- Have a low threshold to abandon repeat MUA in favor of arthroscopic or open lysis, or a structural work-up, if a first MUA does not hold — repeating a failed MUA without a plan change has a poor track record.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Excessive force through a long lever arm risks periprosthetic fracture (particularly in osteoporotic bone) or extensor mechanism injury (patellar tendon avulsion, quadriceps rupture) — the most feared complication of overly forceful manipulation.
- Older age, cruciate-retaining implant design, and lower ROM achieved at the 2-week post-MUA check are independent predictors of MUA failure.
- A history of MUA and/or lysis of adhesions is itself associated with a higher risk of subsequent revision — a marker of a more complex knee trajectory rather than a fully benign, isolated procedure. Identify and correct a structural cause when one is present rather than repeating closed interventions indefinitely.
- Arthroscopic lysis of adhesions (aLOA) is the next step for patients who failed a prior MUA, particularly for anterior interval/suprapatellar scarring; open surgical lysis is reserved for extensive or posterior capsular scarring not accessible arthroscopically.
- Revision TKA is indicated when component malposition/malrotation, oversizing, joint-line elevation, or flexion/extension gap mismatch is identified as the structural driver — revising a well-positioned implant purely for "idiopathic" arthrofibrosis without correcting an underlying technical error yields inconsistent results.
- Failing to exclude infection before manipulation risks masking or delaying diagnosis of a septic knee presenting as stiffness.
- Losing ROM gains in the immediate postoperative period from delayed or inadequate physiotherapy engagement is a common, preventable cause of MUA failure — reinforce with the patient and care team that the first 24–48 hours are critical.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Day 0:** immediate CPM or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM program starting the same day as manipulation.
- **Weeks 0–2:** formal physiotherapy 3–5 times weekly, static/dynamic splinting overnight to hold end-range extension/flexion.
- **Week 2 checkpoint:** ROM at this visit is a validated predictor of ultimate success; patients failing to maintain gains are candidates for repeat intervention (aLOA) rather than prolonged observation.
- **Weeks 2–6:** continued intensive physiotherapy, transition to home program with periodic supervised reassessment.
