## Indications

- Stiffness after TKA — commonly defined as flexion <90° and/or a flexion contracture >15° beyond the expected postoperative timeline (generally by 6–12 weeks) — that has not responded to aggressive supervised physiotherapy and splinting.
- Best performed in the **6-week to 3-month** window post-primary TKA; efficacy drops substantially beyond 3 months and MUA is largely ineffective beyond ~1 year, at which point mature fibrosis no longer yields to closed manipulation.
- Exclude infection and a correctable structural cause (component malposition/oversizing, elevated joint line, tibial slope error, patella baja, flexion/extension gap mismatch) before attributing stiffness to arthrofibrosis alone.

## Positioning & setup

- Regional or general anesthesia.
- Controlled, gentle supracondylar-lever technique to avoid periprosthetic fracture or extensor mechanism rupture.

## Steps

1. Confirm the patient is within the optimal timing window (6 weeks–3 months post-primary TKA).
2. Perform controlled, gentle supracondylar-lever manipulation under regional or general anesthesia.
3. Begin an immediate continuous passive motion (CPM) or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM program the same day as the manipulation — ROM gains achieved intraoperatively are lost rapidly without immediate reinforcement.
4. Consider adjuvant anti-inflammatory strategies around MUA to improve sustained ROM gains, per recent multicenter randomized data.
5. Reassess ROM at the 2-week checkpoint — a validated predictor of ultimate success. Patients failing to maintain gains by 2 weeks are candidates for repeat intervention (arthroscopic lysis of adhesions) rather than prolonged observation.

## Pitfalls

- Excessive force through a long lever arm risks periprosthetic fracture (particularly in osteoporotic bone) or extensor mechanism injury (patellar tendon avulsion, quadriceps rupture) — the most feared complication of overly forceful manipulation.
- Older age, cruciate-retaining implant design, and lower ROM achieved at the 2-week post-MUA check are independent predictors of MUA failure.
- A history of MUA and/or lysis of adhesions is itself associated with a higher risk of subsequent revision — a marker of a more complex knee trajectory rather than a fully benign, isolated procedure. Identify and correct a structural cause when one is present rather than repeating closed interventions indefinitely.
- Arthroscopic lysis of adhesions (aLOA) is the next step for patients who failed a prior MUA, particularly for anterior interval/suprapatellar scarring; open surgical lysis is reserved for extensive or posterior capsular scarring not accessible arthroscopically.
- Revision TKA is indicated when component malposition/malrotation, oversizing, joint-line elevation, or flexion/extension gap mismatch is identified as the structural driver — revising a well-positioned implant purely for "idiopathic" arthrofibrosis without correcting an underlying technical error yields inconsistent results.

## Post-op protocol

- **Day 0:** immediate CPM or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM program starting the same day as manipulation.
- **Weeks 0–2:** formal physiotherapy 3–5 times weekly, static/dynamic splinting overnight to hold end-range extension/flexion.
- **Week 2 checkpoint:** ROM at this visit is a validated predictor of ultimate success; patients failing to maintain gains are candidates for repeat intervention (aLOA) rather than prolonged observation.
- **Weeks 2–6:** continued intensive physiotherapy, transition to home program with periodic supervised reassessment.
