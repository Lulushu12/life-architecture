## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Failure of an adequate physical therapy trial for congenital muscular torticollis (CMT) — persistent head tilt/rotational deficit (>15–30°) beyond roughly age 12–18 months to 3 years.
- Late presentation (>1 year of age) with a fixed contracture, where a meaningful non-operative trial is less likely to succeed and delay only worsens secondary craniofacial asymmetry.
- Progressive facial or cranial asymmetry despite an adequate PT trial, even if the raw rotational deficit is borderline, is a relative indication given the cosmetic and developmental consequences of continued asymmetric growth.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Torticollis with an atypical clinical picture — painful onset, older child at first presentation, no palpable SCM band/mass, or associated neurologic findings — should prompt imaging workup (cervical spine radiographs/MRI) to exclude atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation, Klippel-Feil syndrome, or a posterior fossa/spinal cord tumor before any surgical release is considered; operating on a non-muscular cause of head tilt would be a fundamental misdiagnosis.
- An inadequate trial of physical therapy (either too short in duration or genuinely not attempted, e.g., due to access barriers) should be corrected with a proper PT course before surgery is offered, since success rates with early, adequately delivered PT exceed 90–95% in mild-moderate cases.
- Active skin infection over the planned incision site.
- Very young infants (well under 12 months) with a mild, still-improving deficit should generally continue PT rather than proceed to surgery — the natural history and PT response window has not yet been exhausted.
- Undiagnosed developmental dysplasia of the hip should be actively screened for (given the shared intrauterine positional mechanism) and, where relevant, addressed as part of the overall care plan rather than overlooked while focus is on the neck.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Confirm diagnosis and rule out mimics:** document head tilt toward, and chin rotation away from, the affected side, passive ROM deficit on exam, and a palpable SCM band/mass. Reserve cervical spine imaging for atypical presentations as above.
- **Document baseline deformity objectively:** measure and record passive cervical rotation and lateral flexion deficits (goniometer or clinical estimate in degrees) at the preoperative visit, both to guide the surgical decision and to have a clear baseline against which postoperative improvement is tracked.
- **Assess craniofacial asymmetry:** note any plagiocephaly or facial asymmetry, which informs family counseling on expected cosmetic outcome — surgical release corrects the underlying muscular contracture but established craniofacial remodeling changes may only partially resolve, especially in later-presenting or long-standing cases.
- **Technique selection:** decide preoperatively (while remaining open to intraoperative modification) among unipolar (distal) release, bipolar release, or Z-plasty/endoscopic release, based on the severity of contracture, presence of a discrete fibrotic band versus diffuse muscle involvement, and surgeon preference/experience — more severe or longstanding contractures with a thick fibrotic cord more often need a bipolar approach to achieve full correction.
- **Associated condition screening:** confirm hip ultrasound has been obtained (or obtain preoperatively if not already done) given the DDH association, and address plagiocephaly management (helmet therapy referral if indicated) as part of the overall treatment plan, not solely the SCM release itself.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list:** standard soft-tissue set, nerve stimulator not typically required but useful in revision cases or where anatomy is distorted, and standard skin closure supplies suited for a cosmetically sensitive pediatric neck incision (fine absorbable suture, consideration of a subcuticular closure).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation; a laryngeal mask airway is generally avoided given the need for neck extension and manipulation intraoperatively, which can dislodge an LMA.
- **Positioning:** supine with a shoulder roll placed transversely beneath the scapulae to extend the neck and rotate the head to the contralateral side, maximizing exposure of the SCM and putting the contracted muscle/band under tension for easier identification and release. Secure the head in a neutral, stable position (foam donut or gel headrest) to prevent movement during the case.
- **Draping:** prep and drape widely enough to expose the entire anterior and lateral neck from the mandible to the clavicle on the affected side, with the contralateral side accessible for comparison if needed.
- **Landmarks marked before incision:** the mastoid process, the clavicular and sternal heads of the SCM at their origin, and the planned incision line — typically a short transverse incision in a natural skin crease approximately 1–2 cm above the clavicle for a distal (unipolar) release, with a second incision near the mastoid tip if a bipolar release is planned.
- **Additional setup:** no tourniquet or dedicated fluoroscopy is needed for this soft-tissue procedure; have bipolar cautery available for meticulous hemostasis in this vascular, cosmetically sensitive region.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### A. Confirm exposure and identify structures

1. With the neck extended and rotated away from the operative side per positioning above, palpate and mark the taut SCM band/mass before incision — this is often more prominent under anesthesia-induced muscle relaxation than in clinic.
2. Make the planned transverse incision in a skin crease for cosmesis; incise through skin and platysma, then carefully develop the plane down to the SCM, using blunt dissection and bipolar cautery for hemostasis.
3. Identify and protect the structures at risk in this field before proceeding with any tenotomy: the external jugular vein (crosses superficially in this region), the great auricular nerve (crosses the SCM more superiorly, relevant mainly for a proximal/mastoid-level dissection), the spinal accessory nerve (enters the SCM's deep surface and continues to the trapezius — at risk with overly deep or posteriorly directed dissection, particularly near the muscle's upper third), and the carotid sheath contents (deep and medial to the SCM — stay superficial to the deep cervical fascia and avoid deep dissection medially).

### B. Unipolar (distal) release

1. With the SCM's clavicular and sternal heads clearly identified and isolated at their origin, pass a right-angle clamp deep to each tendinous head in turn.
2. Divide each head sharply under direct vision, close to its clavicular/sternal origin, leaving the muscle belly and mastoid insertion intact.
3. Confirm the release has adequately lengthened the contracted structure by passively rotating and laterally flexing the neck through a full corrective range on the table — persistent tightness suggests an incompletely released head or a residual fibrous band that needs further division.
4. Achieve meticulous hemostasis with bipolar cautery before closure, given the vascularity of this region.

### C. Bipolar release

1. Perform the distal (clavicular/sternal) release as in section B first.
2. Through a second incision near the mastoid tip, expose and identify the SCM's insertion, taking care to identify and protect the great auricular nerve and the spinal accessory nerve as they course through this more proximal field.
3. Divide the mastoid insertion (or perform a controlled partial release/lengthening at this level, depending on surgeon technique) under direct vision.
4. Re-confirm full passive correction of head tilt and rotation on the table after both releases are complete.
5. Close both incisions in layers with meticulous hemostasis.

### D. Z-plasty / endoscopic release (cosmesis-focused alternative)

1. For a Z-plasty release, plan and mark the Z-shaped incision/tenotomy pattern preoperatively over the distal SCM to allow the tendon to be lengthened (rather than simply divided) while distributing the resulting scar in a less linear, more camouflaged pattern.
2. Perform the tenotomy/lengthening according to the marked Z pattern, then transpose and re-approximate the limbs of the Z to achieve both lengthening and a favorable scar orientation.
3. For an endoscopic approach, use a small axillary or remote incision with an endoscope and specialized instruments to divide the SCM origin without a visible anterior neck scar — this technique requires specific training and equipment and is used selectively by surgeons experienced with it, generally for a well-defined distal (unipolar-equivalent) release rather than a full bipolar release.
4. Confirm correction and hemostasis as in the open techniques before closing.

### E. Closure and immediate postoperative positioning

1. Close platysma and skin in layers, typically with a subcuticular absorbable suture for the best cosmetic result in this visible location.
2. Apply a light dressing; a soft collar or gentle positioning aid may be placed at the end of the case per surgeon preference to protect the repair briefly while swelling settles, though it is not universally used.
3. Begin gentle passive stretching guidance for the family before discharge, since the structured postoperative stretching program that follows is what ultimately determines durability of the correction.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Position with a true transverse shoulder roll and full contralateral head rotation before draping — under-extending the neck is the most common reason for a cramped, harder-to-dissect exposure.
- Palpate the SCM band again after induction, once muscle relaxation has set in — the contracture is often easier to define under anesthesia than in the awake clinic exam, and this re-confirmation helps plan the incision precisely.
- Work from superficial to deep in a stepwise fashion and use bipolar cautery liberally in this region — the neck is vascular, and troublesome oozing obscures the nerve and vessel landmarks that must be protected.
- When performing a bipolar release, deliberately look for and protect the spinal accessory nerve and great auricular nerve before dividing the mastoid insertion — do not rely on "staying superficial" alone as a substitute for direct identification in a reoperative or anatomically distorted field.
- Test the corrected range of motion intraoperatively before closing, not just at the end of the case — if rotation/lateral flexion is still restricted after a unipolar release, consider whether a residual fibrous band or the need for a bipolar release was under-appreciated preoperatively.
- Favor a transverse incision in a natural skin crease for the distal release — this consistently produces the most cosmetically favorable scar in a highly visible anatomic location.
- Set expectations with families preoperatively that established facial/cranial asymmetry may only partially improve with the release itself — surgery corrects the muscular contracture, but remodeling of longstanding secondary craniofacial changes is a separate, slower process (or may not fully resolve), particularly in later-presenting children.
- Start the family on gentle stretching instructions before leaving the recovery area — early engagement with the postoperative program, rather than a delayed start once the incision has fully healed, appears to support better maintenance of the surgical correction.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Recurrent contracture** can result if postoperative stretching is inadequate — the surgical release alone does not guarantee a durable result without the follow-on rehabilitation program.
- **Residual head tilt/facial asymmetry** can persist if treatment (non-operative or surgical) is delayed — this reinforces prompt referral for surgery once an adequate PT trial has genuinely failed rather than continued observation past the effective window.
- **Hypertrophic/keloid scarring** is a recognized complication of the surgical incision — discuss this risk (including any personal/family history of keloid formation) at consent, and favor incision placement in natural skin creases to minimize visibility if scarring does occur.
- **Spinal accessory nerve injury** during a bipolar or proximal-field dissection can cause trapezius weakness/winging — direct nerve identification during proximal dissection is the key preventive step.
- **Great auricular nerve injury** during proximal dissection causes numbness over the ear/lower cheek — a generally minor but avoidable morbidity with careful dissection.
- **Incomplete release** (missing a residual fibrous band, or performing only a unipolar release when the contracture in fact required a bipolar release) leaves persistent restriction — always test full passive range of motion intraoperatively before closing.
- **Screen for hip dysplasia** (developmental dysplasia of the hip) given the shared intrauterine positioning mechanism with CMT — hip ultrasound is recommended regardless of surgical timing.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Stretching program and cervical range-of-motion exercises begin within days of surgery.
- A soft collar or positioning device may be used briefly.
- Home stretching continues for several months to prevent recurrent contracture.
- Full activity typically by 6–8 weeks.
