## Indications

Complete patellar tendon rupture is a surgical diagnosis. Non-operative management is reserved for partial tears with intact extensor mechanism function (preserved active straight-leg raise, <50% tendon width torn on MRI); a low threshold to convert to surgery applies if extensor lag develops or is found on serial exam.

## Positioning & setup

Standard supine open approach to the extensor mechanism; intraoperative fluoroscopy or direct comparison to the contralateral knee is used to confirm correct patellar height before final fixation tensioning.

## Steps

1. Time surgery ideally within **2–3 weeks** of injury (acute repair); delayed repair (>3–6 weeks) requires additional techniques for retraction.
2. Choose fixation technique:
   - **Transosseous tunnel repair** — non-absorbable suture through longitudinal patellar tunnels, tied over the proximal pole; the traditional gold standard.
   - **Suture anchor repair** — an increasingly favored modern alternative, biomechanically comparable-to-superior in failure load with less patellar bone disruption (avoids stress risers/longitudinal fracture risk from tunnel drilling). Recent systematic reviews (2024–2025) show similar re-rupture rates and PROMs between the two techniques, so selection is largely surgeon preference/cost-driven.
3. Consider **suture tape (internal brace) augmentation** to protect the primary repair and enable earlier, more aggressive rehabilitation — biomechanical/early clinical data support decreased gap formation without adverse effect on posterior tendon tension or contact mechanics.
4. For delayed/retracted ruptures, add retraction-management techniques: V-Y quadricepsplasty, semitendinosus/allograft augmentation, or a Codivilla-type turndown flap.
5. Re-approximate partial-thickness tears in the tendon substance primarily; repair peripatellar retinacular tears concurrently.
6. Before final fixation tensioning, restore correct patellar height by comparing to the contralateral Caton-Deschamps index intraoperatively/fluoroscopically.

## Pitfalls

- Overtensioning causes patella baja (painful, restricted flexion); undertensioning causes patella alta and extensor weakness — check height against the contralateral side before finishing.
- Anchor pull-out is a risk in osteoporotic bone; consider transosseous augmentation in this setting.
- Delayed repair (>3–6 weeks) carries worse outcomes and requires additional retraction-management techniques.
- Re-rupture occurs in **2–6%**, higher with delayed repair.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: hinged brace **locked in full extension** for ambulation and at rest; isometric quad sets and ankle pumps immediately; no active resisted extension.
- **Weeks 2–6**: begin protected, gradually increasing passive/active-assisted ROM per surgeon protocol — commonly progressing ~0–30° at week 2, advancing ~30° every 2 weeks toward full flexion by week 6–8; internal brace/augmented repairs often permit earlier, faster ROM progression than isolated suture repair.
- **Weeks 6–12**: brace unlocked for ambulation once active SLR without lag is demonstrated (often ~week 6); progressive closed-chain strengthening; stationary bike; full weight-bearing without brace by ~week 8–12 if quad control is adequate.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive resistance strengthening and proprioception; light jogging permitted around **16–24 weeks** once quadriceps strength deficit is <30% and cleared by surgeon.
- **Return to sport/heavy labor**: typically **6–9 months**; strength deficits (quadriceps torque) frequently persist beyond 12 months even in good outcomes. Return to sport union/strength criteria: >90% expected return to sport, RTS ~5–9 months, though strength deficits can persist >1 year.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often at 2–4 weeks (brace-dependent mobility); physical/manual labor requiring kneeling, squatting, or ladder work typically delayed to 4–6 months pending strength and functional milestones.
