## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Complete patellar tendon rupture — a surgical diagnosis in essentially all cases; the goal is to restore active knee extension.
- Partial tears with a demonstrated extensor lag or that fail to maintain an active straight-leg raise against gravity.
- Chronic/missed ruptures once diagnosed, with the understanding that delayed repair requires additional retraction-management techniques and carries a worse prognosis than acute repair.
- Re-rupture after prior repair — surgical revision is indicated; augmentation (allograft, autograft, or synthetic) is typically required given tissue deficiency at revision.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Non-operative management is appropriate for **partial tears with intact extensor mechanism function** — preserved active SLR, <50% tendon width torn on MRI — managed with a hinged brace locked in extension and progressive protocol; maintain a low threshold to convert to surgery if extensor lag develops on serial exam.
- Active local infection or severe skin compromise over the anterior knee — treat/optimize the soft-tissue envelope first.
- Poor medical optimization for surgery (uncontrolled diabetes, active systemic infection) — correct where the injury pattern allows a short delay; complete ruptures generally still warrant surgery within the acute window once medically fit.
- Very low-demand, non-ambulatory patients with a partial tear and preserved function may reasonably be managed non-operatively after shared decision-making.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: lateral radiograph to confirm patella alta (Caton-Deschamps or Insall-Salvati index) and look for an avulsed bony fragment; MRI to define tear location (proximal pole avulsion vs. mid-substance vs. distal/tibial tubercle avulsion), retraction gap, and the condition of the remaining tendon stump and peripatellar retinaculum; ultrasound is a reasonable bedside alternative to confirm a gap when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated.
- **Timing**: plan for repair ideally within **2–3 weeks** of injury. Beyond 3–6 weeks, tendon retraction and quadriceps contracture make primary tension-free repair progressively harder — flag these cases preoperatively for augmentation techniques (semitendinosus/gracilis autograft, allograft, or a Codivilla-type turndown flap) and counsel the patient accordingly.
- **Fixation construct planning**: decide transosseous tunnel vs. suture anchor technique preoperatively based on bone quality, cost/availability, and surgeon experience — outcomes are comparable, so this is not a case-defining decision, but anchor count/size and suture material (heavy non-absorbable braided suture, ± suture tape for internal-brace augmentation) should be selected and available before incision.
- **Equipment**: heavy non-absorbable suture (No. 2 or 5) for the core Krackow/locking stitch, drill for transosseous tunnels if selected, suture anchors (typically 3.5–5.5 mm, 2–3 per side of the repair for a double-row-style construct) if selected, suture tape for internal-brace augmentation if planned, and a means of intraoperatively confirming patellar height (fluoroscopy or direct comparison to a preoperative contralateral lateral radiograph).
- **Case-specific**: for retinacular disruption, plan concurrent repair of the medial/lateral retinaculum. For an avulsed bony fragment large enough to fix, plan for fragment-specific fixation (screws or suture through drill holes) rather than excision when it preserves tendon length and quality.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or spinal/regional anesthesia; a femoral or adductor canal nerve block is a reasonable adjunct for postoperative analgesia.
- Supine on a standard table with a bump or bolster available to flex the knee for exposure and for the intraoperative ROM check after fixation.
- Non-sterile thigh tourniquet, elevated and exsanguinated before inflation; the leg is prepped and draped free from mid-thigh to foot to allow full ROM assessment.
- Fluoroscopy or a preoperative lateral radiograph of the contralateral, uninjured knee should be available in the room for intraoperative comparison of patellar height.
- Mark the incision (longitudinal, midline anterior, extending from the inferior pole of the patella to just past the tibial tubercle) and confirm the tourniquet time budget before starting, since restoring tension accurately benefits from an unhurried final assessment.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a midline longitudinal anterior incision extending from the inferior patellar pole to the tibial tubercle; develop full-thickness skin flaps to expose the paratenon.
2. Open the paratenon longitudinally and preserve it as a separate layer for closure — paratenon repair contributes vascularity and a gliding layer over the tendon repair.
3. Débride the ruptured tendon ends back to healthy, bleeding tissue; evacuate the hematoma and inspect the medial and lateral retinaculum for concurrent tears.
4. Place a locking Krackow (or similar whip) stitch of heavy non-absorbable suture into each side of the tendon, running at least 2–3 locking loops per side to distribute load across the repair and resist suture pull-through.
5. Choose the patellar-side fixation construct:
   - **Transosseous tunnel repair** — drill 2–3 longitudinal parallel tunnels from the inferior pole to the superior pole of the patella, pass the Krackow suture limbs through the tunnels, and tie them over the proximal pole. The traditional gold standard; avoid placing tunnels too close together or too close to the anterior cortex to reduce longitudinal fracture risk.
   - **Suture anchor repair** — place 2–3 anchors (medial, central, lateral) into the inferior pole footprint, load each with the tendon-side Krackow sutures, and tension. Biomechanically comparable-to-superior in failure load with less patellar bone disruption than tunnel drilling; a double-row style construct (anchors placed slightly proximal to the footprint, tensioned to compress the tendon stump against the inferior pole) improves footprint contact. Recent systematic reviews (2024–2025) show similar re-rupture rates and PROMs between the two techniques, so selection is largely surgeon preference/cost-driven.
6. Consider **suture tape (internal brace) augmentation** run in parallel to the core repair and fixed proximally and distally (e.g., anchor-to-anchor or anchor-to-transosseous tunnel) to protect the primary repair and permit earlier, more aggressive rehabilitation — biomechanical/early clinical data support decreased gap formation without adverse effect on posterior tendon tension or contact mechanics.
7. For delayed/retracted ruptures, add a retraction-management technique before final tensioning: V-Y quadricepsplasty for proximal lengthening, semitendinosus or allograft augmentation woven through the repair, or a Codivilla-type turndown flap from the quadriceps tendon.
8. Re-approximate any partial-thickness intratendinous tears primarily, and repair peripatellar retinacular tears concurrently — these restore secondary stability and protect the primary repair from shear.
9. Before final tensioning, restore correct patellar height: compare the reduced construct to the contralateral Caton-Deschamps index intraoperatively (fluoroscopy or a reference lateral film in the room), and set tension with the knee at approximately 20–30° flexion — tying/tensioning in full extension risks a falsely short (baja) construct.
10. Tie down the core sutures, then the augmentation suture tape if used, checking patellar height and tracking after each stage.
11. Take the knee through a gentle ROM arc intraoperatively (typically to 40–60°, or per the strength of the repair and surgeon judgment) to confirm the repair holds without gapping and that no bony impingement or catching occurs.
12. Repair the paratenon over the tendon repair as a separate layer, then close the subcutaneous tissue and skin in standard fashion.
13. Apply a hinged knee brace locked in full extension before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a locking (Krackow) stitch with at least 2–3 loops per side rather than a simple running stitch — locking loops resist suture pull-through under cyclic load far better and are the standard for extensor tendon repair.
- When using suture anchors, distribute them across the tendon footprint (medial, central, lateral) for even load-sharing rather than clustering centrally — this more closely restores the native broad tendon insertion and reduces focal stress concentration.
- Always confirm patellar height against the contralateral side before final tensioning — do this by eye/palpation and, when available, fluoroscopically; do not rely on "how the knee looks" with the tourniquet still inflated and the patient anesthetized, since resting tension is easy to misjudge.
- Tension the repair with the knee flexed roughly 20–30°, not in extension — extension-tensioned repairs tend to end up shortened (baja) once the knee is allowed to flex postoperatively.
- Preserve and repair the paratenon as its own layer — it is a distinct vascular and gliding structure, and paratenon repair measurably improves the local healing environment and postoperative gliding.
- In an avulsion with a viable bony fragment, fix the fragment rather than excising it when feasible — preserving native tendon length avoids the tensioning uncertainty of a pure soft-tissue repair.
- Test repair integrity intraoperatively with a gentle ROM arc before closing — identifying gapping or excessive tension at this stage, while still open, is far preferable to discovering it postoperatively.
- In a chronic/retracted case, release the quadriceps mechanism proximally (mobilize adhesions, consider partial V-Y lengthening) before forcing distal advancement of the tendon — forcing an overtensioned primary repair in a chronic case is a common cause of early failure.
- Consider suture tape augmentation liberally in higher-demand or delayed-presentation patients — the marginal cost is low relative to the value of enabling earlier, safer motion.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Overtensioning** causes patella baja (painful, restricted flexion); **undertensioning** causes patella alta and extensor weakness — check height against the contralateral side before finishing, at 20–30° flexion, not in extension.
- **Anchor pull-out** is a risk in osteoporotic bone; consider transosseous augmentation or a hybrid construct in this setting.
- Placing transosseous tunnels too close together or too close to the anterior cortex risks **longitudinal patellar fracture** — space tunnels adequately and angle them to avoid cortical breach.
- **Delayed repair** (>3–6 weeks) carries worse outcomes and requires additional retraction-management techniques (V-Y quadricepsplasty, augmentation graft, turndown flap) — flag these cases early rather than attempting a forced primary closure.
- **Re-rupture** occurs in **2–6%**, higher with delayed repair; recognized clinically by a new extensor lag, palpable gap, or inability to perform SLR — confirm with ultrasound or MRI and plan revision with augmentation given tissue deficiency.
- **Infection** and **arthrofibrosis** are recognized complications; arthrofibrosis risk is mitigated by internal-brace/augmented repairs that permit earlier protected motion compared with isolated suture repair requiring prolonged immobilization.
- **Suture/anchor irritation** can produce localized anterior knee discomfort requiring later hardware removal.
- Missing a concurrent retinacular tear leaves the repair without secondary support — inspect and repair the retinaculum at the time of primary repair rather than treating it as optional.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: hinged brace **locked in full extension** for ambulation and at rest; isometric quad sets and ankle pumps immediately; no active resisted extension.
- **Weeks 2–6**: begin protected, gradually increasing passive/active-assisted ROM per surgeon protocol — commonly progressing ~0–30° at week 2, advancing ~30° every 2 weeks toward full flexion by week 6–8; internal brace/augmented repairs often permit earlier, faster ROM progression than isolated suture repair.
- **Weeks 6–12**: brace unlocked for ambulation once active SLR without lag is demonstrated (often ~week 6); progressive closed-chain strengthening; stationary bike; full weight-bearing without brace by ~week 8–12 if quad control is adequate.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive resistance strengthening and proprioception; light jogging permitted around **16–24 weeks** once quadriceps strength deficit is <30% and cleared by surgeon.
- **Return to sport/heavy labor**: typically **6–9 months**; strength deficits (quadriceps torque) frequently persist beyond 12 months even in good outcomes. Return to sport union/strength criteria: >90% expected return to sport, RTS ~5–9 months, though strength deficits can persist >1 year.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often at 2–4 weeks (brace-dependent mobility); physical/manual labor requiring kneeling, squatting, or ladder work typically delayed to 4–6 months pending strength and functional milestones.
