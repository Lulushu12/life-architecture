## Indications

**Non-vascularized grafting**: scaphoid nonunion with a viable proximal pole (no AVN on MRI/intraoperative bleeding) and minimal deformity.

**Vascularized grafting**: AVN of the proximal pole, failed prior grafting, or long-standing (>1 year) nonunion. Recent comparative literature continues to support reserving vascularized grafting for these AVN/proximal-pole/revision scenarios rather than routine use in all nonunions, given added morbidity (donor site, operative time) without proven benefit when the proximal pole is viable.

## Positioning & setup

Preoperative MRI (or reliance on intraoperative punctate bleeding) is used to assess proximal fragment vascularity, since imaging sensitivity/specificity for AVN prediction is imperfect and many surgeons still use intraoperative bleeding as the practical determinant of viability. Free vascularized medial femoral condyle (MFC) grafting requires microsurgical setup for anastomosis to the radial artery or a branch.

## Steps

1. Confirm proximal pole viability (MRI with gadolinium or intraoperative bleeding) to select between non-vascularized and vascularized grafting.
2. **Non-vascularized**: Russe inlay/Matti-Russe technique, or a corticocancellous wedge from the iliac crest/distal radius for humpback correction, combined with screw fixation. Reported union rates ~85–95% in this favorable (viable proximal pole) subgroup.
3. **Vascularized, pedicled**: 1,2-intercompartmental supraretinacular artery (1,2-ICSRA) graft harvested from the distal radius — workhorse for nonunion with preserved but compromised vascularity; reported union rates ~80–90%.
4. **Vascularized, free**: medial femoral condyle (MFC) graft, preferred for established AVN or failed pedicled graft — provides a larger corticocancellous block with a robust independent pedicle (descending genicular artery) anastomosed to the radial artery or a branch; union rates in modern series commonly cited in the 85–100% range even in AVN nonunion, though this requires microsurgical expertise and added operative time.
5. Correct humpback deformity intraoperatively — restore scaphoid length/angulation with a corticocancellous wedge graft before fixation; this step is essential regardless of graft type.
6. Fix with a headless screw (standard) or, occasionally, a volar locking plate for unstable comminuted nonunion needing structural support (evidence for plating is limited to small series).

## Pitfalls

- Failure to correct humpback deformity perpetuates DISI and midcarpal malalignment even after the fracture eventually unites — length/angulation restoration is not optional.
- Vascularized grafting adds donor-site morbidity and operative time without proven benefit when the proximal pole is viable — reserve it for AVN, failed prior grafting, or long-standing (>1 year) nonunion rather than using it routinely.
- MRI/intraoperative-bleeding assessment of proximal fragment vascularity is imperfect; do not treat a negative or ambiguous vascularity study as fully ruling out AVN — many surgeons still weight intraoperative bleeding as the practical determinant.
- Free MFC grafting requires microsurgical expertise for the anastomosis; this should factor into case selection and surgeon/team readiness, not just the biological indication.

## Post-op protocol

Thumb-spica immobilization 6–8 weeks (longer, 10–12 weeks, for vascularized MFC grafts with a structural block). Serial CT to confirm union before advancing loading — union is commonly assessed at 10–12 weeks postoperatively but may take up to 4–6 months. AROM and progressive strengthening follow confirmed union; return to unrestricted heavy use only after union is confirmed, often 4–6 months total.
