## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

Knee OA (intra-articular and periarticular ligament/insertion targets), lateral epicondylitis, chronic low back pain (sacroiliac/interspinous ligaments — weak evidence), Achilles/patellar tendinopathy (adjunct). Best characterized as a reasonable, low-cost, low-risk option in a shared-decision-making context rather than a guideline-driven default — it has not been formally endorsed by AAOS, NICE, or OARSI, though multiple small-to-moderate RCTs (2023–2025) show dextrose prolotherapy outperforms saline/sham for knee OA pain and function at 6–12 months, and a 2025 network meta-analysis places it among the effective semi-invasive knee OA options alongside PRP and HA.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Active local or systemic infection at the intended injection site.
- Suspected septic arthritis or an unexplained effusion — aspirate and work up before considering any injectate, including prolotherapy solution.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or supratherapeutic anticoagulation for periarticular targets near named vessels/nerves (e.g., sacroiliac region).
- True mechanical joint instability requiring surgical correction — prolotherapy's fibrotic response is not a substitute for ligament reconstruction or other definitive stabilization; using it as a workaround in an unstable joint risks masking a problem that needs surgery.
- Known allergy to dextrose or the specific solution components used (rare).
- Pregnancy — limited safety data; defer elective use.
- Unrealistic patient expectations of disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating benefit, or expectation of a guideline-endorsed "standard of care" therapy — recalibrate before starting a multi-session series, since trial quality remains modest and no major society has formally endorsed it.
- Poor tolerance of injection-site discomfort — the expected post-injection soreness is part of the proposed mechanism and a multi-session series is a poor fit for a patient unwilling to tolerate repeated discomfort.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the diagnosis and target structures clinically (and with imaging where relevant) before starting a series — as with PRP, prolotherapy is time-intensive across multiple sessions and should not substitute for pursuing a missed alternate diagnosis.
- Select and prepare the solution in advance: most commonly hypertonic dextrose 10–25%, sometimes combined with lidocaine; confirm concentration and any local anesthetic component match the intended protocol and the patient's allergy history.
- Plan the full injection map before the first session — specific ligament/tendon entheses and/or intra-articular targets — so the series is consistent visit-to-visit rather than improvised each time.
- Plan the series length and interval with the patient up front: typically 3–6 sessions spaced 2–6 weeks apart; set this expectation (and the associated visit burden/cost) before starting rather than after session one.
- Equipment needs are minimal — no processing equipment is required (unlike PRP/BMAC), which is a practical advantage in resource-limited settings; ensure an adequate supply of small-gauge needles suited to multiple small-volume injections per visit.
- For spinal (sacroiliac/interspinous) targets, plan for image guidance (fluoroscopy) if precision at deeper ligamentous targets is needed, particularly for less experienced injectors.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- No sedation is required; the injections themselves are typically tolerated with only the mild local anesthetic effect of the lidocaine component (if included in the solution) or a brief topical/skin anesthetic at each entry point.
- Position is target-specific:
  - **Knee (IA and periarticular ligament entheses):** supine, knee extended for IA targets (same superolateral or anteromedial/anterolateral approach as corticosteroid/PRP knee injection); periarticular entheseal targets (medial/lateral collateral ligament origins/insertions, pes anserine, patellar tendon origin/insertion) accessed with the knee in slight flexion to palpate the relevant bony landmarks.
  - **Lateral epicondyle:** seated, elbow flexed and forearm pronated, point of maximal tenderness at the common extensor origin palpated and marked.
  - **Sacroiliac/interspinous ligament targets:** prone, with bony landmarks (posterior superior iliac spine, sacral sulcus, interspinous spaces) palpated and marked; fluoroscopic guidance improves precision for deeper SI ligamentous targets.
  - **Achilles/patellar tendon entheses:** prone (Achilles) or supine/seated with knee flexed (patellar), tendon insertion palpated and marked, ultrasound guidance optional but helpful for precise entheseal targeting.
- Standard sterile skin prep at each injection site; no tourniquet used.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Identify target sites: ligament/tendon insertions (entheses) and/or intra-articular space, depending on the diagnosis, and mark each planned entry point.
2. Prep the skin with antiseptic at each site.
3. Inject multiple small volumes (0.5–1 mL per site) at the identified entheses and/or intra-articularly, advancing the needle to bone/periosteum at entheseal targets before depositing the solution, since the ligament/tendon insertion onto bone is the intended target.
4. Withdraw and redirect the needle slightly between adjacent entheseal sites where a single skin entry can reach multiple nearby targets, minimizing the total number of skin punctures.
5. Repeat as a series: 3–6 sessions spaced 2–6 weeks apart, reassessing pain/function at each visit before proceeding to the next session.
6. Counsel on mechanism at each visit: the irritant solution is intended to incite a local inflammatory cascade and subsequent fibroblastic repair/proliferation ("proliferative therapy") — injection site pain is expected and part of the mechanism, not a complication.
7. Document the specific sites injected, solution/concentration used, and volume per site at each session so the series can be reproduced or adjusted consistently visit-to-visit.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Advance to bone/periosteum at true entheseal targets before injecting — depositing solution short of the actual ligament/tendon-bone junction dilutes the intended local effect.
- Map out the full set of injection sites before the first session and keep a simple diagram or checklist in the chart — a multi-session series is much easier to reproduce consistently with a documented map than from memory.
- Use the smallest practical needle gauge that still allows solution to be delivered without excessive resistance — smaller gauge reduces incidental soft-tissue trauma across a series of repeated visits.
- Where a single skin entry can reach two or three adjacent entheseal targets by redirecting rather than fully withdrawing, use that approach to minimize total skin punctures per visit.
- Set the expectation of post-injection soreness explicitly at every session, not just the first — patients who understand the soreness is mechanism-consistent are far less likely to call between visits or discontinue prematurely.
- Reassess pain/function before each subsequent session rather than running a fixed series on autopilot — if there is no discernible trend by session 3–4, revisit the diagnosis before continuing.
- For low back (sacroiliac/interspinous) targets, use bony landmark palpation carefully and consider fluoroscopic guidance for less experienced injectors — the evidence base here is weaker than for knee OA, and technique precision matters more when the underlying data is thinner.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Overall trial quality remains modest (small samples, variable blinding, heterogeneous protocols) — set expectations accordingly and frame as shared-decision-making rather than a guideline-endorsed standard.
- Rare nerve injury can occur with periarticular injections.
- Do not use prolotherapy's fibrotic response as a substitute for surgically correcting true mechanical joint instability — theoretical risk of overuse if misapplied this way.
- Do not promise prolotherapy as disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy — evidence supports symptomatic benefit in appropriately selected patients, not structural disease reversal.
- A series that is not reassessed between sessions risks continuing an ineffective treatment course purely on protocol momentum — build in a clinical checkpoint (typically by session 3–4) to confirm a trend toward improvement before committing to the full planned series.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 1 (Day 0–3):** Relative rest of the treated region for 24–48 hours. Ice for the expected inflammatory flare, which is part of the intended mechanism and typically resolves in 48–72 hours. Avoid NSAIDs for 3–7 days if feasible (theoretical blunting of the intended inflammatory cascade) — this differs from corticosteroid injection, where NSAID use is not restricted.
- **Phase 2 (Week 1–2):** Resume gentle active ROM and low-load ADLs; begin or resume formal physiotherapy targeting the underlying mechanical driver once acute post-injection soreness settles.
- **Phase 3 (Week 2–6):** Progress strengthening per underlying diagnosis in parallel with the ongoing injection series.
- **Manual labor/heavy lifting:** 1–2 weeks post-injection to allow the expected inflammatory phase to settle before heavy loading.
