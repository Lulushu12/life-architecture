## Indications

Knee OA (intra-articular and periarticular ligament/insertion targets), lateral epicondylitis, chronic low back pain (sacroiliac/interspinous ligaments — weak evidence), Achilles/patellar tendinopathy (adjunct). Best characterized as a reasonable, low-cost, low-risk option in a shared-decision-making context rather than a guideline-driven default — it has not been formally endorsed by AAOS, NICE, or OARSI, though multiple small-to-moderate RCTs (2023–2025) show dextrose prolotherapy outperforms saline/sham for knee OA pain and function at 6–12 months, and a 2025 network meta-analysis places it among the effective semi-invasive knee OA options alongside PRP and HA.

## Positioning & setup

Lower cost than PRP/BMAC since no processing equipment is required — a practical draw in resource-limited settings. Solution is most commonly hypertonic dextrose 10–25%, sometimes combined with lidocaine.

## Steps

1. **Identify target sites**: ligament/tendon insertions (entheses) and/or intra-articular space, depending on the diagnosis.
2. **Inject multiple small volumes** (0.5–1 mL per site) at the identified entheses and/or intra-articularly.
3. **Repeat as a series**: 3–6 sessions spaced 2–6 weeks apart.
4. **Counsel on mechanism**: the irritant solution is intended to incite a local inflammatory cascade and subsequent fibroblastic repair/proliferation ("proliferative therapy") — injection site pain is expected and part of the mechanism, not a complication.

## Pitfalls

- Overall trial quality remains modest (small samples, variable blinding, heterogeneous protocols) — set expectations accordingly and frame as shared-decision-making rather than a guideline-endorsed standard.
- Rare nerve injury can occur with periarticular injections.
- Do not use prolotherapy's fibrotic response as a substitute for surgically correcting true mechanical joint instability — theoretical risk of overuse if misapplied this way.
- Do not promise prolotherapy as disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy — evidence supports symptomatic benefit in appropriately selected patients, not structural disease reversal.

## Post-op protocol

- **Phase 1 (Day 0–3):** Relative rest of the treated region for 24–48 hours. Ice for the expected inflammatory flare, which is part of the intended mechanism and typically resolves in 48–72 hours. Avoid NSAIDs for 3–7 days if feasible (theoretical blunting of the intended inflammatory cascade) — this differs from corticosteroid injection, where NSAID use is not restricted.
- **Phase 2 (Week 1–2):** Resume gentle active ROM and low-load ADLs; begin or resume formal physiotherapy targeting the underlying mechanical driver once acute post-injection soreness settles.
- **Phase 3 (Week 2–6):** Progress strengthening per underlying diagnosis in parallel with the ongoing injection series.
- **Manual labor/heavy lifting:** 1–2 weeks post-injection to allow the expected inflammatory phase to settle before heavy loading.
