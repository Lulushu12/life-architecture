## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Essentially all orthopedic surgical procedures with meaningful surgical site infection (SSI) risk, particularly arthroplasty and instrumented trauma fixation.
- Goal: administer effective antibiotic coverage timed to peak at incision, then stop promptly to avoid resistance pressure without sacrificing efficacy.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- **True cefazolin/beta-lactam anaphylaxis** (urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension within minutes of exposure), Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN, or other severe immediate-type reaction — the only history that should redirect away from cefazolin to vancomycin or clindamycin. A vague reported rash, GI upset, or unknown-mechanism reaction is **not** a contraindication to cefazolin and should not trigger diversion.
- **Vancomycin as a routine default** is relatively contraindicated by its own risk profile (nephrotoxicity, red-man reaction with rapid infusion, inferior gram-positive/gram-negative coverage relative to cefazolin for standard SSI prophylaxis) — it should be reserved for documented beta-lactam anaphylaxis or MRSA-adjunct indications, not used as a blanket "safe" substitute.
- **Extended gram-negative coverage or routine vancomycin adjunct** is not indicated in the absence of documented colonization or a specific high-risk profile — routine broad-spectrum escalation is a stewardship failure, not a safety margin.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Allergy history review, ideally through a formal delabeling pathway:** at pre-admission assessment (not day-of-surgery), review any reported penicillin/cephalosporin allergy using a validated risk-stratification tool such as PEN-FAST; low-risk patients can be considered for direct oral amoxicillin challenge or referral for penicillin skin testing well before the operative date, so the decision is made with time and information rather than under time pressure in the preoperative holding area.
- **MRSA screening:** for elective arthroplasty, arrange preoperative nasal MRSA screening where the institutional protocol supports it, so vancomycin-adjunct dosing and/or decolonization (nasal mupirocin + chlorhexidine bathing) can be planned in advance for colonized patients rather than decided reactively.
- **Weight-based dosing confirmation:** confirm patient weight is documented and current so the correct cefazolin dose (2g standard, 3g if ≥120kg) is ordered — a stale or missing weight is a common preventable source of underdosing in larger patients.
- **Renal function review:** check renal function where vancomycin is anticipated (beta-lactam anaphylaxis patients), since vancomycin dosing and infusion planning are renal-function-dependent.
- **Timing coordination with anesthesia/OR scheduling:** because vancomycin requires a substantially longer infusion window (120 minutes) than cefazolin (60 minutes), confirm with anesthesia/preoperative nursing that any patient routed to vancomycin has the antibiotic started early enough — this requires active coordination, not just an order in the chart, since it is a frequent source of inadequate pre-incision antibiotic levels if the case starts on time but the infusion was started late.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Not applicable in the surgical-positioning sense — this is a preoperative and intraoperative pharmacologic protocol layered onto whatever anesthesia and positioning the index procedure requires. The relevant "setup" is administrative and pharmacologic rather than physical:

- **IV access timing:** ensure IV access adequate for antibiotic infusion is established early enough in the preoperative sequence that the chosen agent's infusion window (60 minutes for cefazolin, 120 minutes for vancomycin) can be completed before incision without delaying the start of surgery.
- **Documented "time-out" verification:** antibiotic administration and completion time should be explicitly confirmed as part of the surgical time-out, with the actual infusion-complete time (not just "ordered" or "started") checked against the incision time.
- **Redosing triggers built into the case:** communicate to the circulating nurse/anesthesia team the case's anticipated duration and expected blood loss at the outset, so the 4-hour intraoperative redosing interval and the >1500mL blood-loss redosing trigger are tracked proactively rather than missed in a long or high-blood-loss case.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the allergy history and dosing weight** at the final preoperative check, drawing on the pre-admission delabeling review where one was performed rather than re-litigating the allergy question from scratch on the day of surgery.
2. **Administer cefazolin 2g IV** (3g if body weight ≥120kg) as the standard regimen, with infusion completed within **60 minutes before incision**.
3. **Verify infusion-complete time against planned incision time** at the surgical time-out; if the infusion will not be complete in time, communicate this explicitly to the team rather than proceeding with incomplete coverage.
4. **Evaluate reported penicillin allergy against true cross-reactivity risk before diverting away from cefazolin.** True cross-reactivity is mediated by shared R1 side chains, not the shared beta-lactam ring, and cefazolin has a unique side-chain structure not shared with any penicillin — making cross-reactivity with cefazolin essentially negligible even in patients with a well-documented penicillin allergy. Reserve avoidance for a history of true anaphylaxis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN, or other severe immediate-type reaction; a vague reported rash, GI upset, or unknown-mechanism reaction should not trigger diversion.
5. **For true beta-lactam anaphylaxis:** use vancomycin or clindamycin, with infusion started within **120 minutes before incision** given the slower required infusion rate for vancomycin — this requires starting the infusion well before the patient leaves the preoperative holding area in most workflows. Do not use vancomycin as a default "safe" substitute outside this indication — routine unnecessary vancomycin use is associated with higher SSI rates in some cohorts (inferior gram-negative and staphylococcal coverage relative to cefazolin, plus red-man reaction/nephrotoxicity risk) and drives resistance pressure.
6. **In MRSA-colonized or high-risk patients**, add a single dose of vancomycin as an adjunct to (not a replacement for) cefazolin, informed by preoperative nasal MRSA screening; where decolonization was arranged preoperatively (nasal mupirocin + chlorhexidine bathing), confirm it was actually completed rather than assuming it occurred.
7. **Extend to gram-negative coverage only if documented colonization or high MRSA-negative gram-negative risk**, as an escalation step rather than routine practice — this decision should generally be made with infectious disease input rather than as an intraoperative reflex.
8. **Track intraoperative time and blood loss actively** and **redose every 4 hours**, or after **>1500mL blood loss**, whichever comes first — do not rely on memory alone in a long or complicated case; a visible case clock or a nursing prompt is a reasonable safeguard.
9. **Document the antibiotic given, dose, timing, and any redosing** in the operative record.
10. **Discontinue prophylaxis within 24 hours of surgery** — prolonged courses do not reduce SSI rates and increase resistant-organism and *C. difficile* risk. Write the discontinuation into the postoperative order set at the time of the initial dose so it does not require an active decision later to stop.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- **Front-load the allergy-delabeling conversation to the pre-admission clinic**, not the day of surgery — patients evaluated with PEN-FAST and, where appropriate, an amoxicillin challenge or skin testing well before their operative date arrive already cleared for cefazolin, avoiding a rushed and often overly conservative decision made under time pressure in the preoperative bay.
- **Treat the vancomycin infusion window as a scheduling problem, not just a pharmacy order** — because it needs 120 minutes versus cefazolin's 60, a vancomycin patient needs their antibiotic started notably earlier relative to case start; build this into the preoperative nursing workflow explicitly for any patient routed to vancomycin.
- **Use a visible case-time/blood-loss tracker** to trigger the 4-hour or >1500mL redosing threshold — this is a simple, high-value practice in long revision cases or high-blood-loss trauma where the redose is easy to forget mid-case.
- **Confirm dosing weight is current, not historical**, especially in patients who have had recent significant weight change — the 2g/3g threshold at 120kg is a real dosing decision, not a rounding preference.
- **Verify MRSA decolonization was actually completed**, not just ordered — a mupirocin/chlorhexidine protocol ordered at the pre-admission visit but not confirmed compliant by the day of surgery provides false reassurance.
- **Write the 24-hour discontinuation order at the time of the first dose**, not as a task to remember postoperatively — this closes the most common gap where prophylaxis silently continues beyond the evidence-based window.
- **Default to cefazolin and require an active, documented reason to deviate** — framing the decision this way (rather than treating vancomycin as an equally reasonable default option) is the single most effective stewardship habit for this protocol.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The historically taught ~10% penicillin-cephalosporin cross-reactivity figure is a substantial overestimate driven by older, impure cephalosporin preparations and shared testing methodology flaws — do not use it to justify routine avoidance of cefazolin in reported penicillin-allergic patients.
- Formal preoperative allergy delabeling pathways (e.g. using validated risk-stratification tools such as PEN-FAST, followed by direct oral amoxicillin challenge or referral for penicillin skin testing) measurably shift patients back onto cefazolin, reducing vancomycin/clindamycin utilization, operative-day delays, and associated SSI risk — this is increasingly built into pre-admission joint-replacement clinics rather than left to day-of-surgery clarification.
- Vancomycin's slower infusion requirement (120 minutes vs. cefazolin's 60) means it must be started earlier relative to incision — a common source of inadequate pre-incision coverage if not anticipated; this is the single most frequent avoidable timing error in this protocol.
- Contemporary arthroplasty-specific data directly support cefazolin use even in patients with a documented penicillin anaphylaxis history; a 2023 cohort study reported a cefazolin reaction rate of only 1.3% in patients with a penicillin allergy label (mostly mild, self-limited rash).
- **Missed intraoperative redosing** in a long case or after major blood loss is a preventable gap in coverage; recognized (in retrospect) when an SSI develops in a case where redosing should have occurred at the 4-hour mark or after >1500mL loss but did not — a case-time/blood-loss tracker prevents this.
- **Routine, non-indicated vancomycin adjunct use** drives resistance pressure and adds nephrotoxicity/infusion-reaction risk without a clear offsetting SSI benefit in patients without documented MRSA colonization or high-risk status — this should be recognized as overtreatment, not extra safety margin.
- **Prophylaxis continued beyond 24 hours** (commonly because a drain or catheter is still in place, or simply because no one actively stopped it) does not reduce SSI rates and raises resistant-organism and *C. difficile* risk — this should be treated as a protocol deviation to actively prevent via the discharge/discontinuation order, not a benign extra precaution.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Stop prophylaxis within 24 hours of surgery regardless of drains or catheters still in place.
- Perioperative glucose target **<200mg/dL** regardless of diabetic status to reduce SSI risk.
- No prophylaxis-specific follow-up beyond standard wound surveillance; persistent wound drainage beyond postoperative day 5–7 should trigger wound review and PJI workup rather than continued or restarted antibiotics.
