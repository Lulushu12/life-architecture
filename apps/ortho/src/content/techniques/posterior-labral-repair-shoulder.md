## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Recurrent or persistent posterior shoulder instability that has failed 3–6 months of structured rehabilitation (activity/technique modification and posterior capsule/rotator cuff strengthening), especially in collision athletes (e.g., American football linemen) and overhead/throwing athletes with symptomatic posterior labral tears.
- A discrete posterior labral tear (reverse Bankart) with or without posterior capsular redundancy confirmed on exam (jerk test, Kim test) and imaging.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- An inadequate or unconfirmed nonoperative trial — as with anterior and multidirectional instability, surgery should follow, not substitute for, a genuine structured rehabilitation program in most patients.
- Significant posterior glenoid bone loss, dysplasia, or pathologic retroversion as the dominant driver of instability — isolated soft-tissue repair is unlikely to be durable in this setting and bone augmentation/osteotomy should be planned instead of (or in addition to) labral repair.
- A large reverse Hill-Sachs lesion causing engagement — analogous to the anterior on-/off-track concept, isolated posterior capsulolabral repair may be insufficient and bone-block or remplissage-equivalent augmentation should be considered.
- Voluntary/psychogenic posterior instability (some patients can demonstrate posterior subluxation at will) — screen for this before committing to surgery, since outcomes are poor.
- Active infection or compromised posterior soft-tissue envelope (rare).

## Preoperative Planning

- CT to assess posterior glenoid bone loss/retroversion and reverse Hill-Sachs size/location, particularly in revision cases or when bone-deficiency is suspected clinically.
- MR arthrogram to characterize the posterior labral tear pattern, capsular redundancy, and to exclude a concomitant anterior or superior (SLAP) labral extension.
- Rule out missed posterior fracture-dislocation on prior imaging in any patient with a seizure or high-energy electrical-injury mechanism — review old films specifically for the "lightbulb sign," rim sign, or trough sign if not already excluded.
- **Implant planning**: low-profile suture anchors sized for the tight posterior glenohumeral working space — **all-suture anchors** (1.4–1.8 mm) are an effective and increasingly favored technical evolution for this confined space and are associated with good outcomes in contact-athlete cohorts; have a standard biocomposite anchor set as a backup/alternative.
- Plan for possible bone augmentation instruments (posterior bone block/allograft set, or an osteotomy plan) only if preoperative imaging suggests genuine bone deficiency or pathologic retroversion — do not open augmentation trays routinely for a straightforward primary repair.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically with an interscalene regional block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning**: **lateral decubitus with balanced traction** is generally preferred for posterior labral work, as it opens the posterior joint space more reliably than beach chair and facilitates the specific portal trajectories needed for low posterior/posteroinferior anchor placement. Some surgeons use beach chair successfully with a dedicated posterior portal strategy; choose based on comfort and case-specific anatomy.
- Pad all bony prominences (peroneal nerve at the fibular head, axillary roll) carefully in lateral decubitus.
- Prep and drape to allow full posterior and posteroinferior portal access, and to allow conversion to an open posterior approach if bone augmentation becomes necessary.
- Mark the standard posterior viewing portal and, critically, the **accessory posterolateral ("7 o'clock") portal**, created at roughly a 45° angle off the posterolateral corner of the acromion under direct arthroscopic visualization using an outside-in spinal-needle localization technique before final cannula placement — this portal's trajectory is what allows low, inferior anchor placement at the glenoid-labral junction.
- Fluoroscopy is not routinely required unless bone augmentation or osteotomy is planned.
- Perform an exam under anesthesia (jerk test, Kim test, posterior load-and-shift) to confirm the direction and degree of instability before starting.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy.** Establish the standard posterior portal (slightly higher/more lateral than the classic anterior-instability posterior portal is sometimes favored to preserve room for the 7 o'clock portal below it) and an anterosuperolateral working/viewing portal. Perform a circumferential survey, including the axillary pouch and anterior labrum, to exclude combined anterior-posterior pathology and confirm the diagnosis.
2. **Assess bone quality.** Directly inspect the posterior glenoid rim and humeral head for a reverse Hill-Sachs lesion or posterior glenoid bone loss/erosion; correlate with preoperative CT. If bone deficiency is more significant than anticipated, be prepared to modify the plan toward augmentation.
3. **Establish the 7 o'clock (posterolateral) accessory portal.** Localize with a spinal needle under direct visualization at roughly a 45° angle off the posterolateral acromion, confirming the trajectory allows access to the inferior glenoid-labral junction before committing to the skin incision; place an 8 mm cannula after sequential dilation, or proceed percutaneously without a cannula per surgeon preference (percutaneous access is commonly used for this portal specifically to preserve working room in the tight posterior space).
4. **Mobilize the posterior labrum and capsule.** Release the torn posterior labrum and redundant posterior capsule off the glenoid neck with an elevator or shaver, working from inferior to superior, until the tissue can be mobilized adequately onto the glenoid face.
5. **Prepare the glenoid rim.** Lightly decorticate the posterior glenoid face to a bleeding bony bed.
6. **Place the first (most inferior) anchor via the 7 o'clock portal.** This portal's angle is specifically what enables placement of the lowest anchor at the true inferior glenoid-labral junction, which is otherwise very difficult to reach from a standard posterior portal; use a curved anchor guide/flexible drill if available to further facilitate access and control at this inferior position.
7. **Place sequential anchors working superiorly.** Continue placing anchors from inferior to superior up the posterior glenoid rim (typically 2–4 total, depending on tear extent), using all-suture anchors for a low-profile construct that minimizes hardware crowding in the confined posterior space.
8. **Pass sutures and plicate the capsule.** For each anchor, pass sutures capturing labrum and a generous bite of the redundant posterior capsule, then tie to advance the capsulolabral tissue superiorly/laterally onto the glenoid face — this simultaneously repairs the labral tear and reduces capsular redundancy (posterior capsular plication).
9. **Reassess stability after each level.** Check capsular tension and joint translation with a probe as anchors are sequentially tied, working inferior to superior, to avoid asymmetric over- or under-tensioning.
10. **Address bone deficiency only if confirmed significant.** In bone-deficient or revision cases, proceed to posterior bone block/allograft augmentation or glenoid osteotomy for pathologic retroversion as a planned, separate component of the procedure — this is not part of the standard primary repair and should be reserved for cases where imaging and direct inspection confirm the need.
11. **Final inspection.** Confirm restored posterior labral height/bumper, adequate capsular tension without excessive tightness (test internal rotation and cross-body adduction to confirm no gross restriction), and no anchor prominence.
12. **Closure.** Evacuate the joint, remove instruments, and close portals — explicitly remember to close the 7 o'clock portal site, which is sometimes overlooked given its percutaneous, off-axis creation. Place the arm in a sling in a neutral (not abducted) position.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Localize the 7 o'clock portal with a spinal needle under direct arthroscopic visualization before committing to the skin incision — its trajectory is unforgiving, and a poorly placed portal makes inferior anchor placement genuinely difficult.
- Use a curved anchor guide or flexible drill for the most inferior anchor — this materially eases controlled placement at the true glenoid-labral junction, which is otherwise the hardest position to reach in the posterior working space.
- Favor all-suture (low-profile) anchors in the posterior space specifically — the tight working corridor and thin posterior capsule make hardware crowding a real practical problem with standard-diameter anchors.
- Percutaneous (cannula-free) placement of the 7 o'clock portal preserves working room and is commonly used specifically for this portal even when other portals are cannulated.
- Work strictly inferior to superior when placing and tying both anchors and plication sutures — this is the same tensioning logic as anterior repair and prevents an asymmetrically tight superior repair with a residual inferior gap.
- Test internal rotation and cross-body adduction intraoperatively after tying — these motions specifically stress a posterior repair and reveal overtightening before the patient leaves the OR.
- Do not open a bone-augmentation tray by default — reserve it for cases where preoperative CT or direct intraoperative inspection confirms genuine posterior bone deficiency or pathologic retroversion.
- Explicitly confirm portal closure at the end of the case, particularly the percutaneous 7 o'clock portal, which is easy to overlook.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Reserve bone augmentation or osteotomy for genuinely bone-deficient or revision cases — it is not part of the standard primary repair.
- Posterior instability is frequently missed on an AP radiograph alone ("lightbulb sign," rim sign, trough sign); maintain a low index of suspicion after seizure, electrocution, or high-energy trauma, and obtain axillary views bilaterally in epileptic/electrical-injury dislocations given the risk of bilateral posterior fracture-dislocation.
- Reoperation and slightly slower return-to-sport timeline than anterior repair are recognized trade-offs (5–15% reoperation reported).
- A poorly localized 7 o'clock portal can make inferior anchor placement unreliable or force compromise on anchor position, undermining the repair at its most biomechanically important point.
- Overtightening posterior capsular plication risks restricted internal rotation and cross-body adduction — test these motions before final closure.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–4**: sling with abduction pillow avoided (neutral position); passive ROM with internal rotation and cross-body adduction restricted to protect the posterior repair, while elevation/external rotation are generally allowed earlier than in anterior repair rehab (mirror-image logic).
- **Weeks 4–8**: progressive active ROM; begin scapular/rotator cuff isometrics.
- **Weeks 8–16**: progressive resistance strengthening, closed-chain and posterior-chain (posterior deltoid, cuff external rotators) emphasis.
- **Return to collision sport** (e.g., football linemen): typically 5–7 months — later than anterior stabilization given higher posterior-chain loading demands of the position and slower confidence in posterior capsule strength.
- Outcomes: recent systematic reviews/meta-analyses report ~90–92% return to sport/work and favorable patient-reported outcomes at short-to-midterm follow-up, including in collision-sport cohorts; minimum-10-year data show durable outcomes for isolated posterior capsulolabral repair.
