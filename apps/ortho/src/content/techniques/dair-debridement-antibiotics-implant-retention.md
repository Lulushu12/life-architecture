## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications — all of the following should generally be met:**
- Acute infection by timing classification — early postoperative (<4 weeks) or acute hematogenous with symptom duration <3 weeks.
- Implant well-fixed/stable on imaging and intraoperative assessment.
- Intact, closable soft-tissue envelope (no established sinus tract or extensive abscess undermining the skin).
- Organism identified or presumed susceptible to an oral, anti-biofilm-active regimen — rifampin-susceptible staphylococcus is the favorable scenario.
- Host and local grade favorable enough that retention is a reasonable goal (McPherson A/B preferred).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Loose or unstable implant on stress radiographs, intraoperative palpation, or a positive "screwdriver test" of a well-fixed component — this is an absolute contraindication irrespective of symptom timing.
- Established sinus tract, gross undermining of the skin envelope, or extensive abscess that cannot be closed primarily.
- Symptom duration >3–4 weeks even within a nominally "acute" timing window — biofilm is very likely mature and DAIR success drops sharply.
- Difficult-to-treat organism (rifampin-resistant staphylococci, fungi, multidrug-resistant Gram-negatives) in a host who cannot tolerate a failed attempt.
- McPherson host C with no realistic biofilm-active oral regimen available — consider suppression-only or staged exchange instead of subjecting a frail host to a low-probability salvage attempt.
- Culture-negative status is not itself a contraindication but weakens confidence in postoperative rifampin combination therapy, since biofilm-active dosing depends on knowing susceptibility.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** Plain radiographs to confirm implant position and rule out obvious loosening (progressive radiolucent lines, migration, cement mantle fracture); review serial films if available to detect subtle interval change. CT/MRI is rarely needed for DAIR planning itself but is useful if an occult abscess or sinus tract is suspected.
- **Confirm eligibility on paper before consenting the patient** — walk through timing classification, implant stability on the most recent films, soft-tissue envelope, and McPherson host/local grade; this is the single highest-yield planning step because it is the point at which many cases are correctly redirected to staged exchange instead.
- **Implant/instrumentation compatibility:** identify the exact modular components in situ (manufacturer, size, head/liner options) before surgery — order a full range of trial and definitive femoral heads, acetabular/tibial liners/inserts, and the specific extraction instruments for that system (liner extraction hooks, threaded extractors, osteotomes) so the team is not searching mid-case.
- **Multidisciplinary input:** involve infectious disease early — ideally before surgery — to plan empiric coverage, anticipate rifampin timing, and pre-approve the antibiotic plan with the treating team so there is no delay starting therapy after cultures are drawn.
- **Culture and antibiotic timing:** confirm the patient has been off antibiotics for ≥2 weeks preoperatively when clinically safe, to maximize intraoperative culture yield; if the patient is septic and cannot wait, accept lower culture sensitivity and plan for broader empiric coverage.
- **Equipment list:** pulsatile lavage system, large-volume sterile irrigation (9+ liters is a reasonable working target for a large joint), dilute povidone-iodine or other antiseptic lavage adjunct per institutional protocol, full modular component tray, and separate sterile instrument sets for tissue sampling (a fresh instrument/blade per culture site to avoid cross-contamination between specimens).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** General or neuraxial (spinal/epidural) anesthesia per standard revision arthroplasty practice for the joint involved; regional catheter for postoperative analgesia is reasonable since the wound will need aggressive early mobilization.
- **Positioning:** Standard revision-arthroplasty position for the joint — supine with a bump/lateral positioner for hip approaches (direct anterior, anterolateral, or posterior depending on the index approach and surgeon preference; re-using the prior incision/approach is generally preferred to avoid creating a second scar and skin bridge), supine with a leg holder or tourniquet-mounted positioner for the knee.
- **Draping and prep:** Wide sterile prep extending well beyond the planned incision to allow for possible extensile exposure if unexpected findings (abscess tracking proximally/distally, unrecognized loosening) are encountered. Use the prior surgical incision whenever feasible — excise any chronically draining sinus tract in continuity with the deep dissection rather than leaving it as a separate wound.
- **Tourniquet:** For the knee, a tourniquet may be used for the exposure but should be released before final lavage and closure if there is any concern about tissue viability or vascularity of the wound edges; many surgeons avoid tourniquet use altogether for infection cases to preserve normal reactive hyperemia and avoid tourniquet-related soft-tissue compromise in already-marginal tissue.
- **Fluoroscopy:** Not routinely required for DAIR itself; have it available if there is any preoperative uncertainty about component position or a possible occult periprosthetic fracture.
- **Landmarks:** Mark the prior incision, any sinus tract orifice (for planned excision), and bony landmarks (patella, tibial tubercle for knee; greater trochanter, ASIS for hip) before draping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Reconfirm eligibility in the room** — implant stability, timing, and soft-tissue envelope — before proceeding; this is the last checkpoint before committing to retention.
2. **Incise through or excise the prior scar/sinus tract.** If a sinus tract is present, excise it en bloc with the deep dissection down to the joint rather than leaving it as a separate defect; do not simply curette it superficially.
3. **Expose the joint via full arthrotomy.** Arthroscopic access is inadequate for adequate debridement in DAIR — it cannot achieve complete synovectomy or reach posterior/deep recesses, and multiple large series show inferior results with arthroscopic DAIR compared with open arthrotomy.
4. **Obtain deep tissue cultures first, before any irrigation or antibiotic exposure.** Take a minimum of 3, ideally 5–6, specimens from distinct sites (capsule, periprosthetic membrane, any purulent collection) using a separate, unused instrument/blade for each sample to avoid cross-contamination; send for extended culture (≥14 days) plus histology if there is any diagnostic uncertainty remaining.
5. **Perform a complete synovectomy.** Excise all inflamed/necrotic synovium and any organized biofilm-laden membrane circumferentially, including posterior and difficult-to-access recesses — this is the single most important technical determinant of DAIR success and the step most often done incompletely.
6. **Remove all modular components.** In the knee, removing the polyethylene insert first materially improves visualization of the posterior compartment and allows a far more thorough posterior synovectomy than is possible with the insert in place. In the hip, remove the femoral head and the acetabular liner — use a threaded liner extractor or an osteotome at the liner-cup junction to lever the liner out; if no purpose-made extractor is available, a large cancellous screw threaded into the liner can serve as a handle for controlled extraction. Inspect the trunnion and locking mechanism for damage that would preclude simple re-seating of a new modular part.
7. **Debride any accessible cement mantle irregularities, loose fibrous membrane, or necrotic bone** without disturbing well-fixed components — the goal is mechanical debulking of the bacterial load, not implant removal.
8. **Perform thorough pulsatile lavage.** Large volume (a reasonable working target is 6–9+ liters for a large joint), with or without a dilute antiseptic adjunct (e.g., dilute povidone-iodine) per institutional protocol, directed into all recesses exposed during synovectomy.
9. **Inspect the extensor mechanism (knee) or abductors/capsule (hip)** for any compromise from chronic infection before proceeding — this affects both the reconstruction and the postoperative protocol.
10. **Reassemble with new, sterile modular components** — new polyethylene liner/insert and, for the hip, a new femoral head (upsizing head diameter modestly is reasonable if it improves stability without compromising fit) — even though the fixed (stem/cup/femoral and tibial trays) components are retained.
11. **Check stability and range of motion** through a full functional arc before closure, confirming there is no impingement or instability introduced by the new modular components.
12. **Consider a drain** per surgeon preference, though many infection surgeons avoid drains given the risk of a retrograde conduit for reinfection; if used, remove early.
13. **Close in layers** with attention to a tension-free, well-vascularized closure — this is not the time for a marginal wound closure, and plastic surgery input should be sought preoperatively if the soft-tissue envelope is borderline.
14. **Start empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics only after all cultures are secured** (typically vancomycin ± a Gram-negative agent), narrowing once culture and sensitivities return.
15. **Add rifampin combination therapy for confirmed rifampin-susceptible staphylococcal biofilm** once the wound has stabilized/is closed — never as monotherapy (rapid resistance emergence), always paired with a companion agent (commonly a fluoroquinolone or an alternative per susceptibility).
16. **Frame expected success with KLIC (early postoperative DAIR) and CRIME80 (late acute/hematogenous DAIR)** as decision-support for the postoperative discussion — not as an absolute cutoff, given inconsistent external validation.
17. **Plan for a minimum 12-week total systemic antibiotic duration**; consider indefinite suppressive therapy in McPherson host C, difficult-to-treat organisms, or incomplete source control.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Remove the polyethylene/liner **before** doing the posterior synovectomy, not after — it converts an awkward, limited exposure into a genuinely complete one, particularly posteriorly in the knee.
- Excise a chronic sinus tract in continuity with the deep dissection as a single specimen when possible, rather than treating it as a separate superficial procedure — this avoids leaving a residual biofilm-laden tract.
- Use a fresh blade/instrument for each culture specimen and take cultures before any lavage or antibiotic exposure — contaminated or antibiotic-suppressed cultures are the most common reason for a "culture-negative" DAIR that later fails empiric therapy.
- If a threaded polyethylene liner extractor is not available for the hip, a 6.5 mm cancellous screw threaded into the liner face makes a serviceable extraction handle in a pinch.
- Extend the exposure proximally/distally without hesitation if an abscess is found tracking beyond the visible wound — under-exposure to "save" incision length is a false economy in an infection case.
- Involve infectious disease before the wound is closed, not after, so empiric coverage and the rifampin-timing plan are already agreed before the patient leaves the OR.
- Consider modestly upsizing the femoral head (hip) at the time of modular exchange if it improves stability margin — dislocation after DAIR compounds an already difficult recovery.
- Release the tourniquet before final lavage/closure in marginal soft tissue so the surgeon can directly assess perfusion of the wound edges before committing to closure.
- Document implant stability findings explicitly in the operative note (manual stress testing, "screwdriver test" on well-fixed vs loose components) — this becomes important if a repeat DAIR or conversion to staged exchange is later debated.
- Repeated DAIR for a technical failure (rather than true reinfection) can still succeed and avoid implant removal in a meaningful proportion of carefully selected cases — but only when the failure was clearly technical (incomplete debridement, missed modular exchange) and the host remains favorable.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Symptom duration >3–4 weeks in an otherwise "acute" presentation suggests a mature biofilm — DAIR success drops sharply (often <50%) even when the timing category nominally looks acute; favor staged exchange instead.
- An unstable/loose implant or exposed cement mantle contraindicates DAIR irrespective of timing.
- Arthroscopic debridement is inadequate for DAIR — incomplete synovectomy and inability to exchange modular components make it inferior to open arthrotomy; do not substitute it for open debridement even in an ostensibly "less morbid" early presentation.
- Difficult-to-treat organisms (rifampin-resistant staphylococci, fungi, multidrug-resistant Gram-negatives, quinolone-resistant Gram-negatives when the biofilm-active regimen relies on a quinolone) lower DAIR success — consider two-stage or suppression-only in frail hosts.
- KLIC and CRIME80 scores have shown inconsistent external validation across cohorts (some Northern European cohorts found no predictive value for KLIC; neither score reliably predicted failure at repeat DAIR in at least one series) — use them as one input among several, not a strict gatekeeper.
- Repeat DAIR only if there is a clear technical failure and a favorable host; otherwise convert to staged exchange rather than repeating a failing strategy.
- Success falls sharply with S. aureus infection, symptom duration >3–4 weeks, and McPherson host C patients — overall reported success is highly variable across the literature (roughly 26–92% depending on cohort/case-mix), with strict-indication series clustering around 60–90% at 2 years.
- Incomplete synovectomy and failure to exchange all modular components are the two most common technical causes of DAIR failure — both are directly under the surgeon's control and should be treated as non-negotiable steps.
- Postoperative dislocation or instability (hip) can follow modular head/liner exchange if sizing is not carefully checked intraoperatively — confirm stability through a full range of motion before closing.
- Persistent or recurrent drainage beyond 5–7 days postoperatively should prompt urgent reassessment and a low threshold to return to theatre rather than a prolonged trial of oral antibiotics alone.
- Rifampin has significant drug interactions (reduces efficacy of oral contraceptives, warfarin, and many CYP3A4-metabolized drugs) — coordinate dosing and counsel the patient before starting combination therapy.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Weeks 0–2:** Weight-bearing as tolerated for hip/knee unless a concurrent structural procedure limits it. Focus on wound protection, edema control, gentle active-assisted ROM, and early mobilization — the joint has not been reconstructed, only debrided, so formal arthroplasty precautions from the index surgery still apply.

**Weeks 2–6:** Progressive strengthening as antibiotics continue (often still IV/early oral phase). Monitor CRP trend at each follow-up as a surrogate for treatment response. Therapy focuses on regaining the pre-DAIR functional baseline.

**Weeks 6–12:** Continue targeted antibiotics to the 12-week minimum. Functional milestones mirror standard post-arthroplasty rehabilitation (independent ambulation, stair negotiation, return to low-impact ADLs).

**Return to work:** Sedentary work 2–4 weeks; physical/manual work typically deferred until antibiotic course completion and confirmed clinical/serologic response (often 3 months).

**Return to sport/high-demand activity:** Generally deferred until antibiotics are complete and sustained clinical remission is confirmed (commonly ≥6 months from DAIR), with shared decision-making given residual reinfection risk.
