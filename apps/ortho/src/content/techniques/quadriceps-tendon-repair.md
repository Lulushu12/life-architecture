## Indications

Complete quadriceps tendon rupture is a surgical diagnosis, typically occurring in patients >40 years, often with degenerative/metabolic tendon disease (chronic kidney disease on dialysis, diabetes, gout, hyperparathyroidism, SLE, fluoroquinolone or statin exposure) via eccentric quadriceps contraction against a flexed knee (e.g., stumbling). Non-operative management is reserved for partial tears with intact extensor mechanism function (preserved active straight-leg raise, <50% tendon width torn on MRI); a low threshold to convert to surgery applies if extensor lag develops.

## Positioning & setup

Standard supine open approach to the extensor mechanism; the earlier repair is performed after injury, the better the outcome.

## Steps

1. Choose fixation technique — both are well-supported, with a 2025 systematic review finding similar re-rupture rates and PROMs between them:
   - **Suture anchor repair** — less invasive (smaller incision, no patellar tunnel drilling) with biomechanically more consistent failure loads; many high-volume centers have shifted to this as the preferred modern technique for primary repair.
   - **Transosseous tunnel repair** — remains a valid, lower-cost option with equivalent clinical outcomes.
2. For chronic/retracted ruptures (>4–6 weeks, retraction precluding tension-free primary repair), use augmentation: semitendinosus/gracilis autograft, Achilles allograft, or synthetic mesh, sometimes combined with V-Y quadricepsplasty for length.
3. Before final fixation tensioning, restore correct patellar height by comparing to the contralateral Caton-Deschamps index intraoperatively/fluoroscopically.
4. Post-repair, address underlying metabolic/systemic risk factors (CKD, hyperparathyroidism, gout) — contralateral rupture risk is elevated in these populations and warrants counseling/surveillance.

## Pitfalls

- Overtensioning causes patella baja (painful, restricted flexion); undertensioning causes patella alta and extensor weakness.
- Chronic/retracted ruptures (>4–6 weeks) require augmentation and carry worse outcomes than acute repair.
- Re-rupture occurs in **2–6%**, higher in delayed repair or comorbid tendon disease.
- Bilateral atraumatic quadriceps tendon rupture should prompt screening for chronic kidney disease, hyperparathyroidism, gout, fluoroquinolone use, diabetes, SLE, or steroid/statin exposure.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: hinged brace **locked in full extension** for ambulation and at rest; isometric quad sets and ankle pumps immediately; no active resisted extension.
- **Weeks 2–6**: begin protected, gradually increasing passive/active-assisted ROM per surgeon protocol — commonly progressing ~0–30° at week 2, advancing ~30° every 2 weeks toward full flexion by week 6–8; internal brace/augmented repairs often permit earlier, faster ROM progression.
- **Weeks 6–12**: brace unlocked for ambulation once active SLR without lag is demonstrated (often ~week 6); progressive closed-chain strengthening; stationary bike; full weight-bearing without brace by ~week 8–12 if quad control is adequate.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive resistance strengthening and proprioception; light jogging permitted around **16–24 weeks** once quadriceps strength deficit is <30% and cleared by surgeon.
- **Return to sport/heavy labor**: typically **6–9 months**, with quadriceps strength deficits frequently persisting beyond 12 months even in good outcomes.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often at 2–4 weeks (brace-dependent mobility); physical/manual labor requiring kneeling, squatting, or ladder work typically delayed to 4–6 months pending strength and functional milestones.
