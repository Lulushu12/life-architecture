## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Complete quadriceps tendon rupture — a surgical diagnosis in essentially all cases; typically occurs in patients >40 years, often with degenerative/metabolic tendon disease (chronic kidney disease on dialysis, diabetes, gout, hyperparathyroidism, SLE, fluoroquinolone or statin exposure) via eccentric quadriceps contraction against a flexed knee (e.g., stumbling).
- Partial tears with a demonstrated extensor lag or inability to maintain an active straight-leg raise against gravity.
- Chronic/missed ruptures once diagnosed — surgery remains indicated, with the caveat that retraction and quadriceps contracture make repair progressively more complex.
- Re-rupture after prior repair — revision with augmentation, given tissue deficiency at the repair site.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Non-operative management is reserved for **partial tears with intact extensor mechanism function** — preserved active SLR, <50% tendon width torn on MRI — managed with a hinged brace locked in extension and a progressive protocol; low threshold to convert to surgery if extensor lag develops on serial exam.
- Active local infection or compromised overlying skin — optimize the soft-tissue envelope before proceeding.
- Poor surgical candidacy from uncontrolled medical comorbidity — many quadriceps rupture patients have the exact metabolic comorbidities (CKD, dialysis-dependence) that also raise perioperative risk; involve medicine/nephrology early for optimization rather than delaying surgery indefinitely, since complete ruptures still generally warrant timely repair.
- Very low-demand, non-ambulatory patients with a partial tear and preserved function may reasonably be managed non-operatively after shared decision-making.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: lateral radiograph looking for patella baja (unopposed patellar tendon pull after quadriceps rupture) and any avulsed bony fragment from the superior pole; MRI defines tear location (most commonly at the osteotendinous junction on the superior patellar pole), retraction gap, and the quality/thickness of the remaining tendon layers (rectus femoris superficial, vasti deep) — this trilaminar detail matters because superficial-layer-only tears may be amenable to a less extensive repair than full-thickness ruptures.
- **Timing**: earlier repair is associated with better outcomes — plan for surgery as soon as the patient is medically optimized; ruptures repaired beyond 4–6 weeks should be flagged preoperatively as likely to need augmentation (semitendinosus/gracilis autograft, Achilles allograft, or synthetic mesh) and possibly V-Y quadricepsplasty for length.
- **Fixation construct planning**: decide suture anchor vs. transosseous tunnel technique based on bone quality, cost/availability, and surgeon experience — both are well supported with comparable outcomes; anchor count (2–3 per side, double-row constructs in higher-demand patients) and suture material should be selected before incision.
- **Metabolic work-up**: in patients without an obvious traumatic mechanism, or with bilateral presentation, initiate screening for chronic kidney disease, hyperparathyroidism, gout, diabetes, SLE, and review medication history (fluoroquinolones, statins) — this affects both surgical planning (tissue quality expectations) and postoperative counseling.
- **Equipment**: heavy non-absorbable suture (No. 2 or 5) for the core Krackow/locking stitch, suture anchors (typically 4.5–5.5 mm) if selected, drill for transosseous tunnels if selected, suture tape for augmentation if planned, and a means of intraoperatively confirming patellar height (fluoroscopy or a reference contralateral lateral radiograph).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or spinal/regional anesthesia; adductor canal or femoral nerve block is a reasonable adjunct for postoperative analgesia.
- Supine on a standard table, non-sterile thigh tourniquet elevated and exsanguinated before inflation, leg prepped and draped free from mid-thigh to foot to allow full ROM assessment intraoperatively.
- A bump or bolster should be available to flex the knee for the post-fixation ROM check.
- Fluoroscopy or a preoperative lateral radiograph of the contralateral, uninjured knee available in the room for intraoperative comparison of patellar height.
- Mark the incision (longitudinal, midline anterior, centered over the superior pole of the patella and extending proximally into the distal thigh as needed for exposure of a retracted tendon).
- In patients with significant medical comorbidity (CKD/dialysis), coordinate perioperative timing with medicine/nephrology (e.g., dialysis scheduling) as part of the positioning/setup plan for the day of surgery.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a midline longitudinal anterior incision centered over the superior patellar pole, extending proximally as needed to expose retracted tendon; develop full-thickness skin flaps.
2. Evacuate the hematoma, identify the tear plane (osteotendinous junction at the superior pole in the majority of cases), and débride frayed tendon edges back to healthy tissue while preserving as much tendon length as possible.
3. Assess the trilaminar structure (superficial rectus femoris, deep vasti) — if only the superficial layer is torn with the deep layer intact and functional, this may permit a more limited repair; full-thickness tears require a complete repair construct.
4. Place a locking Krackow (or similar whip) stitch of heavy non-absorbable suture into the medial and lateral aspects of the torn tendon, with additional central sutures for a broad tear — at least 2–3 locking loops per limb to resist suture pull-through.
5. Choose the patellar-side fixation construct:
   - **Suture anchor repair** — place 2–3 (or a double-row construct of 4) anchors into the superior pole footprint, load each with the tendon-side Krackow sutures, and tension to reduce the tendon to bone. Less invasive (smaller incision, no patellar tunnel drilling) with biomechanically more consistent failure loads; many high-volume centers have shifted to this as the preferred modern technique for primary repair.
   - **Transosseous tunnel repair** — drill 2–3 longitudinal parallel tunnels from the superior pole distally through the patella, pass the Krackow suture limbs through the tunnels, and tie them over the distal/inferior pole. Remains a valid, lower-cost option with equivalent clinical outcomes; a 2025 systematic review found similar re-rupture rates and PROMs between the two techniques.
6. For chronic/retracted ruptures (>4–6 weeks, retraction precluding tension-free primary repair), add an augmentation technique before final tensioning: semitendinosus/gracilis autograft or Achilles allograft woven through the repair, synthetic mesh reinforcement, or V-Y quadricepsplasty for additional length.
7. Repair any associated retinacular tears concurrently to restore secondary stability.
8. Before final tensioning, restore correct patellar height: compare the reduced construct to the contralateral Caton-Deschamps index intraoperatively (fluoroscopy or a reference lateral film), and set tension with the knee at approximately 20–30° flexion.
9. Tie down the core sutures, then any augmentation construct, checking patellar height and tracking after each stage.
10. Take the knee through a gentle ROM arc intraoperatively (typically to 40–60°, per repair strength and surgeon judgment) to confirm the repair holds without gapping.
11. Close in layers — repair the quadriceps expansion/retinaculum over the primary repair, then subcutaneous tissue and skin.
12. Apply a hinged knee brace locked in full extension before the patient leaves the OR.
13. Post-repair, formally document and address underlying metabolic/systemic risk factors (CKD, hyperparathyroidism, gout) before discharge — contralateral rupture risk is elevated in these populations and warrants counseling and surveillance for early symptoms in the other knee.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a locking (Krackow) stitch with at least 2–3 loops per limb rather than a simple running stitch — locking configurations resist suture pull-through under cyclic load far better.
- When using a suture anchor construct, distribute anchors across the full width of the footprint (medial, central, lateral, or a double-row pattern) rather than clustering them centrally — this better restores the native broad tendon insertion.
- Always confirm patellar height against the contralateral side before final tensioning, at 20–30° flexion, not in extension — resting tension is easy to misjudge with the tourniquet inflated and the patient anesthetized.
- Assess the trilaminar tendon structure explicitly — a partial-thickness (rectus-only) tear with an intact, functional deep vasti layer may not require the same extensive repair construct as a full-thickness rupture; matching repair extent to actual injury pattern avoids over-treating.
- In a chronic/retracted case, mobilize the quadriceps mechanism proximally (release adhesions, consider partial V-Y lengthening) before forcing distal advancement — forcing an overtensioned repair is a common cause of early failure in delayed presentations.
- Test repair integrity intraoperatively with a gentle ROM arc before closing — identify gapping or excessive tension while still open rather than discovering it postoperatively.
- Screen proactively for the metabolic drivers of tendinopathy (CKD, gout, hyperparathyroidism, statin/fluoroquinolone exposure) even in an apparently traumatic single-sided rupture — a substantial minority have an underlying contributor, and identifying it changes both healing expectations and contralateral-side surveillance.
- Counsel patients with an identified metabolic risk factor about contralateral rupture risk explicitly at the first postoperative visit, not just at final follow-up — bilateral atraumatic rupture is a recognized pattern in this population and early recognition of contralateral symptoms changes outcomes.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Overtensioning** causes patella baja (painful, restricted flexion); **undertensioning** causes patella alta and extensor weakness.
- Chronic/retracted ruptures (>4–6 weeks) require augmentation and carry worse outcomes than acute repair — recognize these preoperatively so the augmentation plan (graft, mesh, quadricepsplasty) is ready rather than improvised.
- **Re-rupture** occurs in **2–6%**, higher in delayed repair or comorbid tendon disease — recognized by a new extensor lag, palpable gap, or inability to perform SLR; confirm with ultrasound or MRI and plan revision with augmentation given tissue deficiency at the repair site.
- **Bilateral atraumatic quadriceps tendon rupture** should prompt screening for chronic kidney disease, hyperparathyroidism, gout, fluoroquinolone use, diabetes, SLE, or steroid/statin exposure.
- **Anchor pull-out** is a risk in osteoporotic bone; consider transosseous augmentation or a hybrid construct in this setting.
- **Infection** and **arthrofibrosis** are recognized complications; arthrofibrosis risk is mitigated by earlier, protected motion when repair strength (augmented constructs) allows it.
- Missing a concurrent retinacular tear leaves the repair without secondary support — inspect and repair concurrently rather than treating it as optional.
- Underestimating retraction in a delayed presentation and attempting a purely primary repair without a mobilization/augmentation plan is a common technical error that leads to an overtensioned, high-failure-risk construct.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: hinged brace **locked in full extension** for ambulation and at rest; isometric quad sets and ankle pumps immediately; no active resisted extension.
- **Weeks 2–6**: begin protected, gradually increasing passive/active-assisted ROM per surgeon protocol — commonly progressing ~0–30° at week 2, advancing ~30° every 2 weeks toward full flexion by week 6–8; internal brace/augmented repairs often permit earlier, faster ROM progression.
- **Weeks 6–12**: brace unlocked for ambulation once active SLR without lag is demonstrated (often ~week 6); progressive closed-chain strengthening; stationary bike; full weight-bearing without brace by ~week 8–12 if quad control is adequate.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive resistance strengthening and proprioception; light jogging permitted around **16–24 weeks** once quadriceps strength deficit is <30% and cleared by surgeon.
- **Return to sport/heavy labor**: typically **6–9 months**, with quadriceps strength deficits frequently persisting beyond 12 months even in good outcomes.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often at 2–4 weeks (brace-dependent mobility); physical/manual labor requiring kneeling, squatting, or ladder work typically delayed to 4–6 months pending strength and functional milestones.
