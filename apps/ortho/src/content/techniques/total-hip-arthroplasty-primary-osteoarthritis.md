## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:** radiographically confirmed hip joint destruction (Tönnis ≥2, generally Kellgren-Lawrence ≥3) concordant with clinically significant pain and functional limitation that has failed an adequate trial of the non-operative ladder (education, exercise, weight management, NSAIDs, assistive devices). THA is not indicated on imaging alone, and is not withheld purely for age — the decision is symptom- and function-driven, shared with the patient. For inflammatory hip arthritis (RA, axial spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis), THA is indicated once mechanical destruction is present, with surgical timing coordinated around immunosuppression management.

**Absolute contraindications:**
- Active local or systemic infection (septic arthritis of the hip, active bacteremia, untreated osteomyelitis in the operative field) — must be excluded, not merely treated empirically, before proceeding.
- Medically unfit for anesthesia/surgery without a realistic path to optimization.
- Absent or non-functional abductor musculature/severe neuromuscular deficit such that no bearing construct can be expected to remain stable (relative in the dual-mobility era, but still a discussion point in extreme cases).

**Relative contraindications / factors requiring specific mitigation, not automatic exclusion:**
- Morbid obesity (many centers use BMI ~40 as a threshold for a dedicated risk discussion rather than a hard cutoff) — elevated wound complication, infection, and dislocation risk.
- Poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c ≥8%) — proceed only after an attempt at optimization given elevated PJI risk.
- Active smoking — counsel cessation; elevated wound-healing and infection risk.
- Recent intra-articular injection (within ~3 months) — recognized, debated-magnitude PJI risk factor; most arthroplasty surgeons use this as a practical delay window.
- Charcot (neuropathic) arthropathy of the hip — technically feasible but with markedly elevated loosening/instability risk; counsel accordingly.
- Young, high-demand patient with preserved cartilage and correctable structural deformity (FAI, dysplasia, Tönnis 0–1) — a joint-preservation candidate, not a THA candidate; Tönnis ≥2 removes this option regardless of age.
- Uncontrolled inflammatory disease activity or an unaddressed immunosuppression plan — defer until rheumatology coordination is in place.

## Preoperative Planning

**Imaging and templating:** standing AP pelvis plus a frog-leg or Dunn lateral of the symptomatic hip, calibrated with a radiographic marker (magnification-corrected digital templating is standard of care — freehand estimation is a recognized source of leg-length/offset error). Template both the acetabular cup size/position (restoring the anatomic center of rotation) and the femoral component (canal fit, offset, neck resection level, anticipated leg-length change). Assess Dorr type on the templating film (canal-flare index) to pre-commit to a cemented-versus-uncemented femoral strategy before the day of surgery. Obtain standing and sitting lateral spinopelvic radiographs (or dedicated spinopelvic imaging) preoperatively in any patient with known spine pathology, prior spinal fusion, or clinical spinal stiffness on exam — this changes the cup anteversion target and the threshold for dual mobility, and is far easier to plan for than to react to intraoperatively.

**Implant selection to finalize before the case:**
- Femoral fixation (cemented vs uncemented) per Dorr type/age/bone quality.
- Acetabular cup size and augmentation needs (screw fixation plan; anticipate augments only in revision, not typical primary, settings).
- Bearing surface (ceramic-on-highly-crosslinked polyethylene as the default; consider ceramic-on-ceramic or dual mobility per the discussion below) and head size (36 mm+ where anatomy allows).
- Dual mobility construct availability on the shelf for any patient meeting a high-risk-instability profile (abductor deficiency, neuromuscular disease, femoral neck fracture indication, Hip-Spine group 2B).
- Stem length/offset options (standard, high-offset, lateralized) — have at least one offset option beyond standard available in the room.
- For inflammatory arthritis with anticipated protrusio: have medial wall bone graft (femoral head autograft if available) or a protrusio ring/cage available.

**Equipment/instrumentation checklist:** standard THA tray (broaches, reamers, trial components, cup impactor, femoral stem inserter), approach-specific retractor set, C-arm/fluoroscopy if used for intraoperative cup position or leg-length checks (routine with most DAA setups, selective with posterior/lateral), electrocautery, pulse lavage, cell salvage or tranexamic acid protocol per blood-management pathway, cerclage cable/wire set immediately available (not just "in the building") given a reported intraoperative periprosthetic femur fracture incidence of roughly 1.7–5.1%, higher with cementless stems and with compaction broaching techniques compared with standard broaching. Confirm robotic/navigation platform availability and register the pin/array setup in advance if that technology is planned.

**Preoperative optimization:** HbA1c <8% (ideally <7%) in diabetics, smoking cessation counseling, BMI optimization, anemia correction (iron repletion or erythropoietin if time allows), dental clearance if indicated. For inflammatory arthritis patients, a documented immunosuppression hold plan coordinated with rheumatology: methotrexate is generally continued through surgery per current consensus; most biologics are held for one dosing interval preoperatively; corticosteroids are continued with stress dosing rather than abruptly stopped.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

**Anesthesia:** spinal or general anesthesia are both acceptable; spinal (with or without a light general/sedation adjunct) is favored in many contemporary ERAS pathways for reduced blood loss, lower VTE risk, and faster early mobilization. A periarticular local infiltration analgesia injection and/or a regional block (fascia iliaca, PENG block) is typically added as part of multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia regardless of the primary anesthetic.

**Positioning by approach:**
- **Posterior approach:** lateral decubitus position with the operative side up, secured with a peg board or bean bag; anterior and posterior pelvic supports (or a bean bag molded around the pelvis) to keep the pelvis perpendicular to the floor — pelvic tilt is one of the most common sources of cup malposition, so re-check and secure pelvic orientation immediately before draping and again after final positioning adjustments. A padded axillary roll protects the brachial plexus on the down side.
- **Direct anterior (DAA):** either a dedicated hip/traction table (allows controlled leg extension, adduction, and external rotation via a boot attachment for femoral exposure) or a standard radiolucent table with the leg free-draped and a bump or slight table break under the lumbosacral area/ASIS to assist femoral elevation. Supine position; both legs prepped into the field if a fracture table with a contralateral well leg is used. Confirm fluoroscopy access under the table before draping — C-arm position is set up and test-fired at this stage, not discovered mid-case.
- **Direct lateral (Hardinge):** lateral decubitus, similar setup to posterior approach but exposure and retraction are centered anterolaterally over the greater trochanter and abductor mass.

**Draping/landmarks:** mark the anterior superior iliac spine, greater trochanter, and planned incision line before prep. For DAA, mark the ASIS-to-lateral-patella line to help judge intraoperative leg positioning and confirm the incision starts roughly 2 cm lateral and 2 cm distal to the ASIS. For posterior approach, mark the posterior border of the greater trochanter and a line extending proximally toward the PSIS. Circumferential or three-quarter draping with an adhesive iodine-impregnated barrier drape is standard; isolate the perineum carefully in the lateral position.

**Fluoroscopy/imaging setup:** C-arm draped and positioned for cross-table or AP pelvis shots as needed by approach; confirm a true AP pelvis reference image is obtainable before incision if fluoroscopic cup-position or leg-length checks are planned, since a rotated or tilted reference image invalidates intraoperative comparisons.

**Tourniquet:** not routinely used for THA (no distal extremity exsanguination target as in knee arthroplasty); blood loss is instead managed with tranexamic acid (IV and/or topical), cell salvage where indicated, and meticulous hemostasis during exposure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm approach selection** based on patient anatomy, dislocation-risk profile, and surgeon experience/volume — posterior (extensile workhorse, best for most body habitus/deformity and easiest revision conversion), direct anterior (intermuscular/internervous, lower early dislocation risk and faster early recovery, best suited to thinner patients and high-volume DAA surgeons), or direct lateral/Hardinge (reliable low-dislocation exposure at the cost of some abductor mechanism violation, reserved for very high dislocation-risk patients or select revisions). Comparative evidence (2024–2025 meta-analyses, including a 5-year RCT) shows DAA carries modest early advantages (lower day-1 pain, shorter length of stay in some series) at the cost of roughly 14 minutes longer operative time, with no difference in functional, radiographic, or survivorship outcomes at mid-term.

2. **Incision and superficial exposure.** Posterior: curvilinear incision centered over the greater trochanter, extending proximal-posterior toward the PSIS and distal along the femoral shaft axis; incise fascia lata/gluteus maximus fascia in line with the skin incision, split the gluteus maximus fibers bluntly. DAA: incision roughly 8–10 cm starting ~2 cm lateral and 2 cm distal to the ASIS, developing the interval between tensor fasciae latae (laterally) and sartorius/rectus femoris (medially) — the internervous plane (superior gluteal nerve laterally, femoral nerve medially) that defines the approach. Direct lateral: incision centered over the greater trochanter, splitting gluteus medius/minimus anteriorly and detaching the anterior portion of the abductor mass with a cuff of tissue for later repair, taking care not to violate the superior gluteal nerve more than ~3–5 cm proximal to the trochanter tip.

3. **Deep exposure and capsulotomy.** Posterior: identify and protect the sciatic nerve by internally rotating the leg before releasing the short external rotators (piriformis, obturator internus/externus, gemelli) as a single cuff off the femur, tagged with heavy sutures for later repair; perform a posterior capsulotomy (T-shaped or trapezoidal flap) preserving tissue for repair. DAA: develop the plane deep to the fascia, ligate/cauterize the ascending branch of the lateral femoral circumflex vessels (a reliable, expected step — failure to control this is a common source of unexpected blood loss), retract the rectus femoris medially, and perform an anterior capsulotomy or capsulectomy exposing the femoral neck.

4. **Dislocate the hip and perform the femoral neck osteotomy** per the preoperative template — typically a two-cut technique with the initial cut guided by a neck-resection jig/template referencing the lesser trochanter, adjusted intraoperatively for the planned implant collar/neck geometry. Remove the femoral head with a corkscrew extractor; measure the native head diameter as a cross-check against templated cup size.

5. **Acetabular preparation.** Circumferentially release the labrum and any remaining capsule to fully visualize the acetabular rim (transverse acetabular ligament is a useful landmark for medial depth and version). Sequentially ream in small increments starting under-reamed relative to template, checking depth and orientation against anatomic landmarks (transverse acetabular ligament, cotyloid fossa) until a bleeding, evenly reamed hemispherical bed is achieved without over-medializing through the medial wall.

6. **Trial and impact the acetabular component.** Impact the trial or final press-fit cup to the templated inclination (~40°) and anteversion (~15–20° as a starting reference, adjusted for spinopelvic risk category — see Tips), confirming a stable press-fit ("hoop stress" click/resistance on the impactor) before committing; place supplemental screws through peripheral holes into the posterosuperior quadrant (safest zone, avoiding the obturator vessels/nerve inferiorly and external iliac vessels anteriorly) if screw augmentation is templated or press-fit stability is marginal. Confirm final seating with a direct visual/tactile check of rim contact circumferentially — a gap at the rim is the most common sign of incomplete seating or reaming mismatch.

7. **Insert the liner** (polyethylene, ceramic, or dual-mobility per plan) with a firm, audible/tactile seat — a ceramic liner in particular must seat flush and axially aligned or it risks chipping and later dissociation.

8. **Femoral preparation.** Open the canal with a box osteotome/awl at the templated starting point (typically piriformis fossa or slightly lateral to it depending on stem philosophy), then sequentially broach (or ream, for fully coated cylindrical designs) up in size, following the templated trajectory and checking anteversion visually against the transepicondylar axis of the knee (typically targeting the femoral component in roughly 10–15° of anteversion, adjusted so combined anteversion with the cup falls in the target zone). Stop broaching at the size that achieves a stable rotational and axial press-fit without excessive hoop stress in dense (Dorr A) bone — forcing an oversized broach into a tight canal is a recognized mechanism for intraoperative fracture.

9. **Trial reduction.** Assemble trial neck/head onto the trial stem (or use a calcar trial for cemented stems), reduce the hip, and assess: (a) stability through a full range of motion including the position(s) of highest risk for the approach used (posterior approach: flexion-adduction-internal rotation; anterior-based approaches: extension-external rotation); (b) leg length and offset by direct comparison to a fixed reference mark placed on the ilium and femur before dislocation, or by fluoroscopic overlay/transverse-rod technique when available — freehand visual estimation alone is the least accurate method and should be cross-checked; (c) soft tissue tension (a "shuck test" — gentle distraction with the hip in neutral should demonstrate only a few millimeters of laxity before a palpable/visible reduction, confirming adequate but not excessive tension); (d) impingement-free motion arc without bony or component-on-component contact.

10. **Adjust and finalize component selection** based on trial findings — change head length/offset, neck version, or stem size as needed; this is the last practical opportunity to correct leg length or instability before final implantation, so do not rush the trial-reduction step.

11. **Implant the final femoral component.** For uncemented stems, impact to the templated depth confirming rotational stability. For cemented stems, prepare the canal with a distal cement restrictor, pulse-lavage and dry the canal, retrograde-fill with cement via a cement gun to achieve a complete mantle, then insert the stem with a centralizer to the templated depth and version before cement cures, holding position until polymerization is complete.

12. **Assemble the final head onto the trunnion** with a clean, dry taper (blood or debris on the trunnion is a recognized contributor to corrosion/fretting) and a firm axial impaction strike.

13. **Final reduction and stability/leg-length recheck**, repeating the range-of-motion and length/offset checks from the trial step to confirm nothing changed with final component seating.

14. **Layered closure with soft tissue repair specific to the approach** (see Step 15 below) — hemostasis is confirmed first, with particular attention to the medial circumflex vessels and gluteal vessels.

15. **Approach-specific soft tissue repair — the single most important technical step for dislocation prevention:**
    - **Posterior:** repair the short external rotators and posterior capsule as a combined flap back to the greater trochanter through drill holes or suture anchors. A side-to-side (capsule-to-capsule) repair or a "capsular noose" technique reduces early dislocation rates compared with repair anchored solely to bone at the trochanter — comparative series report roughly 5% dislocation with classic trochanteric-anchor repair versus well under 1% with side-to-side/noose repair, and obturator externus repair specifically has also been shown to lower dislocation risk when added to the standard short-external-rotator repair.
    - **DAA:** repair the anterior capsule if tissue quality allows; no muscle repair is required given the intermuscular/internervous exposure.
    - **Direct lateral:** securely reattach the anterior one-third to one-half of gluteus medius (and minimus) to the greater trochanter through transosseous sutures or suture anchors — this repair, not the skin closure, is the technical determinant of postoperative abductor strength and limp risk.

16. **Close fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin** in layers; a running subcuticular or barbed suture with skin adhesive/dressing is standard, with drain use individualized (routine closed suction drains are not standard-of-care in most contemporary ERAS pathways).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use fluoroscopy or a fixed intraoperative reference (pin, mark, transverse-rod technique) to check leg length and offset at trial reduction rather than relying on freehand visual estimation alone — freehand technique is consistently the least accurate method in comparative studies of intraoperative leg-length assessment.
- Ream the acetabulum incrementally, starting one size under template, and stop as soon as a full hemispherical bleeding bed is achieved circumferentially — chasing "perfect" reaming by over-reaming risks medial wall violation and loss of press-fit purchase.
- In DAA, controlled, planned release of the conjoint tendon insertion (rather than a reactive "release-on-demand" strategy only after exposure proves difficult) has been shown to reduce greater trochanteric fracture rates to near zero in comparative series, versus a measurable fracture rate with the release-on-demand approach — build a deliberate soft-tissue release sequence into the femoral exposure rather than fighting a tight exposure with retractor force.
- In DAA on a standard table, a bump placed under the lumbosacral spine/centered under the ASIS assists hip extension for femoral elevation without needing a traction table; keep firm downward pressure on the stem inserter handle during broaching and stem insertion to prevent the femur from riding into extension and inadvertently increasing broach/stem anteversion.
- Adjust cup anteversion upward from the classic Lewinnek target in patients with a stiff or fused spine (reduced posterior pelvic tilt on sitting), and have a low threshold to open dual mobility in this same group — functional, spinopelvic-adjusted cup targets have superseded a single fixed "safe zone" for higher-risk patients.
- Keep the femoral trunnion completely dry and debris-free before final head impaction — a single firm axial strike on a clean taper is preferable to multiple strikes, which risk taper damage.
- In dense Dorr A bone, resist the urge to upsize the broach purely to achieve a maximal rotational fit; a well-seated one-size-smaller broach with excellent metaphyseal fill is safer than an oversized broach that produces visible or audible cortical strain.
- For cemented stems, a complete, even cement mantle (not stem-to-cortex contact) is the single strongest predictor of long-term fixation — use a distal centralizer and a cement restrictor, and pressurize before insertion rather than relying on the stem itself to distribute cement.
- Confirm posterosuperior quadrant screw placement for supplemental cup fixation — it is the safest quadrant, avoiding the obturator neurovascular bundle inferiorly and external iliac vessels anteriorly.
- Book cerclage cable/wire as an immediately-opened or immediately-available option (not merely "available somewhere in the department") for any uncemented femoral case, given a reported 1.7–5.1% intraoperative periprosthetic fracture incidence that rises further with compaction (versus broach) techniques — recognizing and treating a non-displaced calcar crack with a prophylactic cerclage cable at the time of broaching is far simpler than managing a displaced fracture after final stem impaction.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Dislocation:** historically the most common early complication, particularly with posterior approach and inadequate soft tissue repair; contemporary rates with modern technique, larger heads, and appropriate bearing/approach selection are well under 1–2% in most series. Failure to repair the posterior capsule/short external rotators is the strongest modifiable driver of early dislocation risk with the posterior approach; side-to-side or "capsular noose" repair techniques outperform repair anchored solely to the trochanter. Other risk factors: abductor insufficiency, cognitive impairment/non-adherence to precautions, adverse spinopelvic mobility, prior lumbar fusion, and malpositioned components.
- **DAA-specific:** forced femoral exposure (rather than a planned, staged soft tissue release or early conversion to an extended approach) is the principal driver of the approach's higher intraoperative femur and greater trochanter fracture risk; expect a defined complication bump early in a surgeon's learning curve. Watch for excessive medial retractor pressure on the femoral neurovascular bundle. Counsel patients preoperatively about expected anterolateral thigh numbness from lateral femoral cutaneous nerve traction (usually sensory-only, often improves but can be persistent). A shorter ASIS-to-greater-trochanter distance and osteoporotic bone are independent risk factors for femoral fracture in DAA.
- **Intraoperative periprosthetic femur fracture:** overall incidence roughly 1.7–5.1%, more common with cementless stems than cemented, and more common with compaction broaching than sequential broaching. Most intraoperative fractures are Vancouver-type calcar/proximal fractures managed with cerclage cable/wire fixation (with or without screw augmentation); modern cerclage constructs achieve durable fixation, with reported femoral component survival essentially unaffected at long-term follow-up when the fracture is recognized and treated intraoperatively rather than missed.
- **Direct lateral:** inadequate gluteus medius/minimus repair or postoperative avulsion produces a persistent abductor lurch — the approach's principal downside relative to intermuscular approaches.
- **Combined anteversion:** femoral plus cup anteversion exceeding roughly 25–50° (combined anteversion, Widmer/Ranawat concept) increases dislocation risk regardless of approach.
- **Nerve injury** patterns differ by approach: sciatic nerve (posterior approach, more common with lengthening or retraction), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (DAA), femoral nerve (anterior-based approaches, less common).
- **Heterotopic ossification** is more common with direct lateral and posterior approaches (greater soft tissue trauma); risk factors include ankylosing spondylitis, male sex, and prior heterotopic ossification — consider NSAID or selective radiotherapy prophylaxis in high-risk patients.
- **Wound complications/prolonged drainage** are more common in obese patients, those on chronic corticosteroids, and with DAA exposure in higher-BMI patients where the incision sits in a skin fold; drainage beyond 5–7 days warrants a low threshold for surgical evaluation given its association with subsequent periprosthetic joint infection.
- **Leg length/offset error:** under-restored offset increases joint reactive force on the abductors (contributing to persistent limp); under-restored length can predispose to instability by reducing soft tissue tension; over-lengthening is a common source of patient dissatisfaction even when technically within acceptable limits. Magnification-calibrated templating plus an objective (not purely freehand) intraoperative length/offset check are the standard mitigations.
- **Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI):** the most feared complication; risk elevated by inflammatory arthritis/immunosuppression, diabetes, obesity, smoking, prior hip surgery, and recent intra-articular injection close to the surgical date.
- **Aseptic loosening/osteolysis:** long-term failure mode, substantially reduced with modern highly-crosslinked polyethylene and ceramic bearings compared to legacy conventional polyethylene; remains the leading indication for late revision.
- **Squeaking and ceramic component fracture:** specific to ceramic-on-ceramic bearings; squeaking is patient-bothersome but rarely clinically significant, while fracture, though rare with modern fourth-generation ceramics, requires immediate revision.
- **Robotic assistance** adds meaningful operative time (roughly 30–35 minutes) without a demonstrated functional-outcome advantage in standard-risk primary THA — weigh the time/technology cost against case complexity rather than applying it routinely.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Phase 1 — Acute (Day 0–2):** weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is standard for cementless and cemented THA through all approaches (no formal restriction unless intraoperative fracture, cortical perforation, or a specific fixation concern dictates protected weight-bearing). Early mobilization same day or POD1 within ERAS pathways; hip precautions education (posterior approach: avoid combined flexion/adduction/internal rotation in the early weeks; anterior/lateral approach precautions are typically less restrictive given the preserved posterior capsule/short external rotators). Multimodal analgesia (regional block/periarticular injection, acetaminophen, NSAID if not contraindicated, minimized opioids). Most ERAS pathways target discharge POD0–2.

**Phase 2 — Early rehabilitation (Weeks 1–6):** outpatient/home PT for gait normalization (weaning assistive device, typically cane by 2–4 weeks), active-assisted then active hip ROM, progressive isometric-to-isotonic strengthening of abductors, extensors, and core stabilizers. Precautions (if posterior approach without robust capsular repair) typically maintained through 6 weeks. Driving typically resumes 2–4 weeks (left THA sooner, right THA/manual transmission later), per surgeon criteria and local regulation. Wound check and radiograph at 2 and 6 weeks.

**Phase 3 — Progressive strengthening (Weeks 6–12):** discontinue formal hip precautions (surgeon-dependent, typically 6–12 weeks). Progress to resistance training, stationary cycling, pool therapy, balance/proprioception work. Return to sedentary/light-duty work: 2–6 weeks; physically demanding work: 3–6 months, individualized. Return to low-impact sport (golf, swimming, cycling, doubles tennis): 6–12 weeks. Return to higher-impact recreational activity (singles tennis, hiking, skiing): 3–6 months, contingent on strength symmetry and surgeon clearance; high-impact/pivoting sport (running, contact sport) is generally discouraged long-term given theoretical wear/loosening concerns, as an individualized shared-decision discussion.

**Phase 4 — Long-term maintenance (3+ months):** indefinite maintenance strengthening; annual or biennial clinical/radiographic surveillance (more frequent if symptomatic or high-risk bearing/fixation construct). Antibiotic prophylaxis for dental/invasive procedures per current (more restrictive) AAOS/ADA guidance — routine prophylaxis is no longer recommended for most low-risk dental procedures without specific immunocompromise or prior PJI history.

**Longitudinal surveillance:** clinical/radiographic review at 6 weeks, 1 year, then every 2–5 years thereafter (more frequent, e.g. annually, for legacy metal-on-metal bearings, known problematic implant designs under recall/registry surveillance, or symptomatic patients). New-onset pain after a prior asymptomatic interval, groin/thigh pain with weight-bearing, or a new limp should prompt earlier re-evaluation for loosening, infection, or bearing-specific failure.

**Inflammatory arthritis note:** coordinate the rehabilitation timeline with rheumatology, since disease flares can transiently limit PT participation; polyarticular involvement (contralateral hip, knees, shoulders) may slow expected milestones and requires individualized goal-setting; osteopenic bone quality reinforces adherence to any surgeon-specific loading precautions in the first 6 weeks even when WBAT is otherwise permitted.
