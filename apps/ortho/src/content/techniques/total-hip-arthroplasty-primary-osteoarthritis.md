## Indications

Radiographically confirmed hip joint destruction (Tönnis ≥2, generally Kellgren-Lawrence ≥3) concordant with clinically significant pain and functional limitation that has failed an adequate trial of the non-operative ladder (education, exercise, weight management, NSAIDs, assistive devices). THA is not indicated on imaging alone, and is not withheld purely for age — the decision is symptom- and function-driven, shared with the patient. For inflammatory hip arthritis (RA, axial spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis), THA is indicated once mechanical destruction is present, with surgical timing coordinated around immunosuppression management.

## Positioning & setup

**Preoperative optimization:** HbA1c <8% (ideally <7%) in diabetics, smoking cessation counseling, BMI optimization (many centers use BMI 40 as a relative threshold for additional risk discussion, not an absolute cutoff), anemia correction, dental clearance if indicated. For inflammatory arthritis patients, a documented immunosuppression hold plan coordinated with rheumatology: methotrexate is generally continued through surgery per current consensus; most biologics are held for one dosing interval preoperatively; corticosteroids are continued with stress dosing rather than abruptly stopped.

**Approach-specific setup:** posterior approach uses a standard lateral decubitus table. Direct anterior (DAA) uses a dedicated traction table, or a standard table with careful hip hyperextension/external rotation for femoral exposure. Direct lateral (Hardinge) splits the abductors anteriorly to the greater trochanter.

## Steps

1. **Select surgical approach** based on patient anatomy, dislocation risk, and surgeon experience/volume:
   - **Posterior** — the default workhorse approach: extensile, familiar, excellent acetabular/femoral exposure for essentially any body habitus or deformity, and easiest for revision conversion.
   - **Direct anterior (DAA)** — intermuscular/internervous with lower early dislocation risk and faster early functional recovery; best suited to thinner-build patients and surgeons with dedicated fellowship training/high volume in the approach.
   - **Direct lateral (Hardinge)** — reliable low-dislocation-risk exposure at the cost of some abductor mechanism violation; reserved for patients at very high dislocation risk or select revision scenarios.
   - Comparative evidence (2024–2025 meta-analyses, including a 5-year RCT) shows DAA carries modest early advantages (lower day-1 pain, shorter length of stay in some series) at the cost of ~14 minutes longer operative time, but no difference in functional, radiographic, or survivorship outcomes at mid-term — approach selection should track surgeon experience/volume and patient anatomy, not a presumption of DAA superiority.
2. **Posterior approach — capsule/short external rotator repair.** Repair the posterior capsule and short external rotators (piriformis, obturator internus/externus, gemelli) back to the greater trochanter through drill holes or suture anchors — this single technical step is the strongest modifiable determinant of early dislocation risk with this approach. Avoid excessive femoral component anteversion combined with cup anteversion that together exceed a combined anteversion target of roughly 25–50° (Widmer/Ranawat concept).
3. **DAA — femoral exposure.** Use hip hyperextension/external rotation (traction table or standard table) for femoral exposure. Watch for excessive medial retractor pressure on the femoral neuromuscular bundle. Convert early to an extended approach or accept a lower stem insertion trajectory rather than forcing exposure.
4. **Direct lateral — abductor repair.** Reattach the anterior one-third to one-half of gluteus medius (and minimus) securely to the greater trochanter.
5. **General exposure check.** Use intraoperative fluoroscopy or navigation-assisted checks of cup inclination/anteversion and leg length/offset regardless of approach chosen — increasingly used even by high-volume surgeons as a final safety check rather than a substitute for technique.
6. **Select femoral fixation.** Uncemented (press-fit) fixation for adequate bone stock (Dorr A/B) — avoids cement-related cardiopulmonary events and is faster. Cemented femoral fixation for older patients (typically >70–75 years) with osteoporotic or Dorr type C bone, or for a femoral neck fracture indication — significantly reduces periprosthetic femur fracture risk in patients ≥65. Apply the same low-bone-quality logic to inflammatory arthritis with corticosteroid-related osteopenia, independent of chronologic age. The acetabular cup is essentially always uncemented (porous-coated hemispherical, with screw augmentation as needed) in primary THA.
7. **Select bearing surface.** Ceramic-on-highly-crosslinked-polyethylene (CoP) is the current default — excellent wear characteristics, no ceramic-on-ceramic squeak/fracture risk, comparable long-term survivorship (~96–99% at 8–10 years) to ceramic-on-ceramic (CoC). CoC remains reasonable in younger/high-demand patients with disclosed squeak/fracture risk. Metal-on-highly-crosslinked-polyethylene is acceptable and cost-effective in older/lower-demand patients but has measurably higher wear than ceramic-headed bearings. Metal-on-metal is essentially abandoned for primary THA. Use larger femoral heads (36 mm and above where anatomy allows) — increases jump distance and is tolerated by modern crosslinked liners.
8. **Assess dislocation-risk profile and consider dual mobility.** Dual mobility (a mobile polyethylene liner articulating with a fixed metal shell around a standard femoral head) increases functional jump distance and substantially reduces dislocation risk. Consider for abductor deficiency, neuromuscular disease (Parkinson's, prior stroke, cognitive impairment affecting precautions compliance), femoral neck fracture as the surgical indication, and adverse spinopelvic mobility.
9. **Screen and plan for spinopelvic mobility.** Obtain preoperative standing and sitting lateral radiographs (or dedicated spinopelvic imaging) in patients with known spine pathology, prior spinal fusion, or clinical stiffness on exam. Stratify by the Hip-Spine Classification (spinal deformity vs normal alignment; spinal mobility vs stiff spine, <10° sacral slope change standing-to-sitting) — group 2B (deformity plus stiff spine) carries the highest dislocation risk. High-risk patients benefit from adjusted (typically increased) cup anteversion targets to compensate for reduced posterior pelvic tilt when seated, larger femoral heads, and a lower threshold for dual mobility.
10. **Inflammatory arthritis-specific reconstruction.** Anticipate protrusio acetabuli and plan medial wall bone grafting (femoral head autograft when available) or a protrusio ring/cage if severe. Use a cemented femoral stem preferentially given near-universal osteopenia in this population, with a lower threshold for a collared stem to offload axial subsidence risk. Coordinate perioperative immunosuppression management explicitly with rheumatology in advance.
11. **Consider robotic/navigation assistance selectively.** Improves radiographic accuracy (center of rotation, offset, cup inclination/anteversion) with lower variance than manual technique, but shows no significant difference in patient-reported functional outcomes and adds ~30–35 minutes of operative time. Reasonable in complex deformity or high-risk-instability cases where precise component targeting matters most; not evidence-justified as a routine requirement for standard-risk primary THA.

## Pitfalls

- Posterior approach: failure to repair the posterior capsule/short external rotators is the strongest modifiable driver of early dislocation risk with this approach.
- DAA: forced femoral exposure (rather than converting early or accepting a lower insertion trajectory) is the principal driver of the approach's higher intraoperative femur fracture risk; expect a defined complication bump early in a surgeon's learning curve; counsel patients preoperatively about expected anterolateral thigh numbness from lateral femoral cutaneous nerve traction (usually sensory-only, often improves but can be persistent).
- Direct lateral: inadequate gluteus medius/minimus repair or postoperative avulsion produces a persistent abductor lurch — the approach's principal downside relative to intermuscular approaches.
- Combined femoral + cup anteversion exceeding roughly 25–50° (combined anteversion) increases dislocation risk regardless of approach.
- Nerve injury patterns differ by approach: sciatic nerve (posterior approach, more common with lengthening or retraction), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (DAA), femoral nerve (anterior-based approaches, less common).
- Heterotopic ossification is more common with direct lateral and posterior approaches (greater soft tissue trauma); risk factors include ankylosing spondylitis, male sex, and prior heterotopic ossification — consider NSAID or selective radiotherapy prophylaxis in high-risk patients.
- Wound complications/prolonged drainage are more common in obese patients, those on chronic corticosteroids, and with DAA exposure in higher-BMI patients where the incision sits in a skin fold; drainage beyond 5–7 days warrants a low threshold for surgical evaluation given its association with subsequent periprosthetic joint infection.
- Under-restored offset increases joint reactive force on the abductors (contributing to persistent limp); under-restored length can predispose to instability — magnification-calibrated templating and intraoperative length/offset checks are standard mitigation.
- Robotic assistance adds meaningful operative time without a demonstrated functional-outcome advantage in standard-risk primary THA — weigh the time/technology cost against the case complexity.

## Post-op protocol

**Phase 1 — Acute (Day 0–2):** weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is standard for cementless and cemented THA through all approaches (no formal restriction unless intraoperative fracture, cortical perforation, or a specific fixation concern dictates protected weight-bearing). Early mobilization same day or POD1 within ERAS pathways; hip precautions education (posterior approach: avoid combined flexion/adduction/internal rotation in the early weeks; anterior/lateral approach precautions are typically less restrictive given the preserved posterior capsule/short external rotators). Multimodal analgesia (regional block/periarticular injection, acetaminophen, NSAID if not contraindicated, minimized opioids). Most ERAS pathways target discharge POD0–2.

**Phase 2 — Early rehabilitation (Weeks 1–6):** outpatient/home PT for gait normalization (weaning assistive device, typically cane by 2–4 weeks), active-assisted then active hip ROM, progressive isometric-to-isotonic strengthening of abductors, extensors, and core stabilizers. Precautions (if posterior approach without robust capsular repair) typically maintained through 6 weeks. Driving typically resumes 2–4 weeks (left THA sooner, right THA/manual transmission later), per surgeon criteria and local regulation. Wound check and radiograph at 2 and 6 weeks.

**Phase 3 — Progressive strengthening (Weeks 6–12):** discontinue formal hip precautions (surgeon-dependent, typically 6–12 weeks). Progress to resistance training, stationary cycling, pool therapy, balance/proprioception work. Return to sedentary/light-duty work: 2–6 weeks; physically demanding work: 3–6 months, individualized. Return to low-impact sport (golf, swimming, cycling, doubles tennis): 6–12 weeks. Return to higher-impact recreational activity (singles tennis, hiking, skiing): 3–6 months, contingent on strength symmetry and surgeon clearance; high-impact/pivoting sport (running, contact sport) is generally discouraged long-term given theoretical wear/loosening concerns, as an individualized shared-decision discussion.

**Phase 4 — Long-term maintenance (3+ months):** indefinite maintenance strengthening; annual or biennial clinical/radiographic surveillance (more frequent if symptomatic or high-risk bearing/fixation construct). Antibiotic prophylaxis for dental/invasive procedures per current (more restrictive) AAOS/ADA guidance — routine prophylaxis is no longer recommended for most low-risk dental procedures without specific immunocompromise or prior PJI history.

**Longitudinal surveillance:** clinical/radiographic review at 6 weeks, 1 year, then every 2–5 years thereafter (more frequent, e.g. annually, for legacy metal-on-metal bearings, known problematic implant designs under recall/registry surveillance, or symptomatic patients). New-onset pain after a prior asymptomatic interval, groin/thigh pain with weight-bearing, or a new limp should prompt earlier re-evaluation for loosening, infection, or bearing-specific failure.

**Inflammatory arthritis note:** coordinate the rehabilitation timeline with rheumatology, since disease flares can transiently limit PT participation; polyarticular involvement (contralateral hip, knees, shoulders) may slow expected milestones and requires individualized goal-setting; osteopenic bone quality reinforces adherence to any surgeon-specific loading precautions in the first 6 weeks even when WBAT is otherwise permitted.
