## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Urgent surgical drainage is indicated for flexor sheath infection (pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis) once Kanavel's cardinal signs are established — a surgical emergency requiring OR within hours, not days. Felon (subcutaneous pulp abscess), paronychia (periungual erythema/fluctuance), and "fight bite" (MCP joint laceration from a tooth, presumed joint capsule/extensor tendon violation) each have distinct drainage/exploration approaches. Early surgical consultation is both diagnostic and therapeutic; imaging should never delay debridement of a clinically clear flexor sheath infection, though radiographs are still obtained to exclude retained foreign body or osteomyelitis, and labs (CRP/WBC)/blood cultures are drawn for systemic signs.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Early cellulitis without fluctuance, without sheath distension, and without Kanavel signs does not require operative drainage — a trial of IV/oral antibiotics with close interval re-examination is appropriate, escalating to the OR promptly if signs progress or fail to improve within 24-48 hours.
- Simple, early paronychia without frank fluctuance can often be managed non-operatively (warm soaks, oral antibiotics) or with bedside eponychial elevation rather than formal OR drainage.
- A fight-bite wound that is more than a few hours old, grossly contaminated, or already showing signs of established joint sepsis is not a contraindication to surgery — if anything it raises urgency — but changes the expected extent of debridement and the threshold for staged/delayed closure or repeat washout.
- Severe systemic illness/hemodynamic instability requiring resuscitation should not delay source control of a clear surgical hand infection but may change anesthetic choice (e.g., regional/local instead of general) and staging of concurrent injuries.
- Emerging literature has explored selective non-operative management (IV antibiotics plus close monitoring) for very early/mild flexor sheath presentations without frank sheath distension, but this remains preliminary and lacks consensus guidelines — surgical drainage remains the standard of care once Kanavel signs are established, and non-operative management should not be substituted for surgery in a clinically clear case.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm clinical diagnosis (Kanavel signs for flexor sheath infection; localized fluctuance/tenderness for felon; periungual findings for paronychia; wound location and mechanism for fight bite) — imaging should not delay surgery for a clinically clear flexor sheath infection.
- Obtain radiographs to exclude a retained foreign body or osteomyelitis, and draw CRP/WBC and blood cultures for patients with systemic signs, but proceed to the OR without waiting on results if Kanavel signs are present.
- Plan empiric antibiotic coverage by mechanism before incision (see table below) so the first intraoperative dose is appropriate for the likely organism; send intraoperative cultures (aerobic, anaerobic, and — where clinically suggested by history — fungal/atypical mycobacterial) before or at the time of the first antibiotic dose when feasible.
- For fight bite specifically, plan for formal OR exploration rather than bedside management — anticipate probable joint capsule and possibly extensor tendon violation and plan the incision to allow full joint inspection and irrigation.
- Equipment: basic hand/soft tissue set, pulse lavage or large-volume syringe irrigation, a catheter (e.g., a small feeding tube or dedicated irrigation catheter) for flexor sheath catheter irrigation technique when applicable, cultures tubes/swabs.
- Anticipate the possibility of a second-look washout in 24-48 hours for advanced or necrotic flexor sheath infections and counsel the patient/family accordingly before the first operation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** regional block (axillary/Bier) or general anesthesia is typical for formal flexor sheath washout and fight-bite exploration; a digital or wrist block with local anesthetic (without epinephrine concerns in infected/inflamed tissue are generally overstated but many surgeons still avoid epinephrine in frankly infected tissue) is often adequate for isolated felon or paronychia drainage.
- **Positioning:** supine, arm extended on a hand table.
- **Tourniquet:** a tourniquet is commonly used for flexor sheath washout and fight-bite exploration to maintain a bloodless field for thorough inspection, but is released before final closure/dressing to confirm perfusion and to allow inspection for any bleeding source; tourniquet use is avoided or used cautiously in frank compartment-syndrome-risk presentations or severe systemic infection where limb perfusion assessment is critical.
- **Landmarks marked before incision:** the course of the flexor sheath (from the distal palmar crease to the DIP flexion crease along the finger), the mid-lateral line for felon incisions (dorsal to the pulp's neurovascular bundle, avoiding the volar pulp and flexion creases), and the eponychial fold for paronychia.
- **Draping:** standard sterile hand drape; prep the entire digit and hand circumferentially in case exploration needs to extend proximally into the palm or distally.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Flexor sheath (pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis) — limited-incision catheter irrigation (early/moderate cases):** make a small transverse incision at the level of the A1 pulley (distal palmar crease) and a second small incision near the DIP flexion crease over the distal sheath; open the sheath at each site, pass an irrigation catheter proximally from the distal incision (or vice versa), and irrigate copiously (typically several hundred mL of normal saline) through the sheath until effluent runs clear; leave the wounds open or loosely closed, with or without an indwelling irrigation catheter for serial postoperative irrigation depending on surgeon preference and infection severity.
2. **Flexor sheath — open exposure (advanced/necrotic cases):** for infections with frank purulence throughout the sheath, necrotic tendon, or failure of limited catheter irrigation, extend to a full Bruner or mid-lateral incision along the finger to open the sheath directly, débride purulent material and any necrotic paratenon/tendon, and irrigate thoroughly under direct vision; assess tendon viability and gliding, and leave the wound open (or loosely approximated) for planned second-look washout in 24-48 hours if concern for residual infection or tissue viability persists.
3. **Culture and antibiotics:** obtain intraoperative cultures before or with the first dose of empiric IV antibiotics (covering Staph aureus, including MRSA where prevalent, and Streptococcus for spontaneous infections; broadened per mechanism — see table), then narrow therapy once culture/sensitivity results return.
4. **Felon:** make a high-lateral longitudinal incision on the non-pinch/non-working side of the digit (typically ulnar side of the index/long/ring fingers, radial side of the small finger and thumb, to preserve pinch surface sensation), staying dorsal to the neurovascular bundle and volar to the nail; bluntly break up the pulp's fibrous septa to fully decompress all loculations of the abscess (a common technical error is draining only the compartment directly under the incision and leaving adjacent septated pockets undrained), irrigate, and leave the wound open with a wick/loose packing or a small drain for large abscesses treated through a through-and-through incision. Avoid the volar midline, avoid crossing flexion creases, and never use a full "fish-mouth" incision.
5. **Paronychia:** elevate the eponychial fold off the nail plate with a blunt instrument (eponychial marsupialization) to release the periungual abscess; if pus is present beneath the proximal nail plate, remove only the involved portion of the nail (partial nail plate removal) rather than the entire nail when possible; pack the space open briefly to allow continued drainage. Deeper or chronic paronychia (consider atypical organisms/fungal in chronic cases) may need formal nail bed exploration and, if a felon component coexists, combined drainage.
6. **"Fight bite" (MCP joint laceration from a tooth):** extend the wound as needed to formally explore the MCP joint and extensor mechanism in the OR — do not perform simple bedside closure. Inspect through a full range of MCP motion (the tooth typically penetrates the joint with the fist clenched, so the skin wound and the deep joint capsule injury are not aligned when the hand is examined in extension — check the joint capsule/extensor tendon at the position of injury, i.e., with the MCP flexed). Irrigate the joint thoroughly (pulse lavage or high-volume syringe irrigation), débride any devitalized tissue, and obtain cultures. Leave the wound open or only loosely approximated with a few widely spaced sutures or none at all — never close a fight-bite wound tightly. Start broad-spectrum antibiotics covering oral flora, including Eikenella corrodens (amoxicillin-clavulanate or equivalent).
7. **Dressing and drains:** apply a bulky, non-constrictive dressing that allows continued passive drainage; splint the hand in the intrinsic-plus/safe position for comfort and to reduce stiffness risk while still allowing wound access for dressing changes.
8. **Plan reassessment/second-look surgery** at 24-48 hours for advanced flexor sheath infections, extensive fight-bite joint contamination, or any case where tissue viability was in question at the index procedure.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- For flexor sheath infections, start with the limited-incision catheter irrigation technique when the presentation is early/moderate — it achieves adequate source control with less soft-tissue disruption and preserves the sheath's gliding surfaces better than wide open exposure.
- When opening a felon, deliberately break up all of the pulp's fibrous septa with a blunt instrument rather than relying on the incision alone to decompress the abscess — loculated pockets left undrained are a common cause of persistent felon symptoms after "adequate" incision and drainage.
- Choose the felon incision side based on which surface is functionally less critical (ulnar side of index/long/ring, radial side of thumb/small finger) to minimize postoperative pulp sensory morbidity on the working pinch surface.
- For fight bite, always examine and explore the joint with the MCP flexed (the position of injury) — a wound that looks superficial with the hand extended can directly overlie a joint capsule violation when the fist was clenched at the time of injury.
- Send cultures before the first antibiotic dose whenever feasible, but never delay source control (incision/irrigation) to wait for culture collection in a clear surgical emergency.
- Counsel patients and document clearly that fight-bite and advanced flexor sheath wounds will be left open/loosely approximated — this is a deliberate infection-control decision, not an oversight, and patients should expect delayed closure or healing by secondary intention.
- Keep a low threshold for planned second-look washout in necrotic or heavily purulent flexor sheath cases — a single aggressive debridement is not always sufficient, and re-exploration at 24-48 hours catches residual non-viable tissue early.
- Match empiric antibiotics to the mechanism of injury before the first intraoperative dose (see table) rather than defaulting to a single generic regimen for all hand infections.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Delay beyond 24-48 hours from clear Kanavel signs worsens outcomes (tendon necrosis, stiffness, amputation risk) — surgical drainage remains the standard of care once Kanavel signs are established; emerging literature (Sawyer et al. 2024; Hennessy et al. 2026) on selective non-operative management for early/mild presentations without frank sheath distension is still preliminary and lacks consensus guidelines.
- Never close a fight-bite wound primarily/tightly — presume joint violation and manage with formal exploration and delayed/loose closure.
- Never use a full fish-mouth incision for a felon; avoid the volar midline and flexion creases to prevent unstable scar and pulp necrosis.
- Match empiric coverage to mechanism: spontaneous felon/paronychia (Staph aureus incl. MRSA, Streptococcus); human/fight bite (Eikenella corrodens, Staph, Streptococcus, oral anaerobes — amoxicillin-clavulanate); animal bite (Pasteurella multocida — amoxicillin-clavulanate, cat bites especially high infection risk given puncture depth); immunocompromised/diabetic/chronic wound (polymicrobial, gram-negatives, atypical/fungal — broader empiric coverage, lower threshold for debridement); water/aquatic exposure (Aeromonas hydrophila in fresh water; Vibrio spp./Mycobacterium marinum in salt water/aquarium — M. marinum causes an indolent, culture-negative-on-routine-media infection).
- Draining only the visible fluctuant pocket of a felon while leaving adjacent septated compartments undrained leads to persistent/recurrent abscess.
- Failing to explore an MCP joint at the position of injury (flexed) in a suspected fight bite risks missing a joint capsule/extensor tendon violation that will progress to septic arthritis if untreated.
- Complications of delayed or inadequately treated hand infection include tendon necrosis/rupture, joint stiffness/contracture, osteomyelitis, digit loss/amputation, recurrent infection, and systemic sepsis in immunocompromised or delayed-presentation patients.

## Postoperative Protocol

Early motion as soon as the wound/soft tissue allows (within days once acute inflammation is controlled) to prevent tendon adhesion and joint stiffness — arguably the most important long-term determinant of outcome after flexor sheath washout. Formal hand therapy referral is standard once infection is controlled. Return to work is variable: days for desk work once wounds allow, weeks for manual labor. Full functional recovery can take months, and some stiffness/reduced motion is a realistic expectation after severe or delayed-presentation infections.
