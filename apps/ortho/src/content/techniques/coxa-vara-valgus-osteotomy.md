## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Hilgenreiner-epiphyseal angle (HEA) >60°.
- Progressive varus deformity documented on serial radiographs.
- Neck-shaft angle (NSA) <100–110° with functional abductor weakness or a Trendelenburg-type limp.
- A widening physeal defect at the inferomedial neck ("Fish sign") — a marker of an unstable, shear-loaded physis at risk of frank non-union/stress fracture if left untreated.

**Relative/observational (not yet operative)**
- Mild, non-progressive deformity (HEA <45–60°, minimal or no limp) — manage with serial radiographs at 6–12-month intervals through the period of most rapid growth, since a meaningful subset stabilizes or improves spontaneously.

**Contraindications / considerations before proceeding**
- Active local or systemic infection.
- Underlying skeletal dysplasia (e.g. cleidocranial dysplasia), fibrous dysplasia, or a syndromic bone-quality problem — correct the deformity but counsel that recurrence risk and healing behavior may differ from idiopathic developmental coxa vara; consider involving metabolic bone/genetics work-up before surgery if not already done.
- Very young age with substantial remaining growth is not an absolute contraindication but should prompt explicit counseling that a second (revision) osteotomy is plausible.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or other standard pediatric anesthetic contraindications.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP pelvis and a true lateral (or frog-leg lateral) of the hip, both hips for comparison; measure neck-shaft angle and HEA bilaterally. Repeat short-interval films (6–12 months) to document progression before committing to surgery in borderline cases.
- **Templating**: plan the wedge angle needed to bring the postoperative HEA below ~38–40° (deliberate overcorrection), and decide whether derotation will be combined with the valgus correction based on clinical rotational profile.
- **Implant selection**: a 90° fixed-angle pediatric blade plate (available in a range of blade lengths, e.g. ~30/45/60 mm) or a pediatric proximal femoral locking plate; have at least one size up and one size down on hand, since intraoperative bone quality and canal size can differ from templated estimates. Screws-only fixation should not be used — the shear forces at this level require an angle-stable construct.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy (image intensifier), oscillating saw, osteotomes, a angled chisel/blade-seating guide referenced to the femoral shaft or condylar axis, guide pins, radiolucent table.
- **Case-specific planning**: if bilateral disease (more common in syndromic coxa vara), decide whether to stage bilateral correction or perform both sides under one anesthetic depending on physiologic reserve and expected blood loss.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia; consider a regional block/catheter as an opioid-sparing adjunct in older children.
- Supine on a radiolucent table (fracture table not required), with a small bump under the ipsilateral buttock/hemipelvis to neutralize pelvic obliquity.
- Prep and drape the entire limb free, from the iliac crest to below the knee, to allow intraoperative rotation and length assessment.
- Position the C-arm to enter from the contralateral side or from the foot of the table, and obtain a baseline AP and lateral fluoroscopic image of the proximal femur before incision to confirm unobstructed views.
- Mark the greater trochanter, the femoral shaft axis, and the planned lateral incision before prepping.
- Tourniquet is generally not used at this level (too proximal for a thigh tourniquet to reliably control the surgical field); have blood loss/hemostasis strategy (bipolar, bone wax) ready instead.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Position and prep** the patient as above; obtain and save a baseline fluoroscopic image confirming the starting HEA/NSA intraoperatively.
2. **Lateral approach** — longitudinal incision centered over the proximal femur, extending from the region of the greater trochanter distally 8–10 cm depending on child size.
3. **Split the vastus lateralis** in line with its fibers (or elevate it anteriorly), and expose the subtrochanteric femur subperiosteally, staying anterior/lateral to protect the posteriorly-lying sciatic nerve throughout.
4. **Place reference pins to control rotation and correction angle** — a common technique uses one pin at the tip of the greater trochanter and a second pin distally (e.g. below the planned plate position), both referenced to the coronal/sagittal planes, so the surgeon can visually and fluoroscopically confirm the planned derotation and valgus angle before committing to the osteotomy.
5. **Prepare the blade-plate entry site** — drill/prepare the chisel entry point before seating the blade; this reduces the risk of an iatrogenic fracture or propagating crack when the chisel is driven into dense pediatric metaphyseal bone.
6. **Insert the guide pin and then the blade/chisel** along the femoral neck axis under fluoroscopic guidance, aiming toward the center of the epiphysis, at the angle dictated by the preoperative plan (e.g., for a 20° varus-to-valgus correction with a 90° system, the chisel is set at roughly 70° to the femoral shaft). Confirm position on both AP and lateral views before advancing to full depth — a malpositioned blade is difficult to correct once fully seated.
7. **Perform the subtrochanteric osteotomy** — typically a closing wedge, with the wedge angle sized to bring the corrected HEA to below ~38–40° (deliberate overcorrection) and to restore an NSA in the normal-to-mildly-valgus range; add derotation through the same osteotomy if clinically indicated.
8. **Reduce the distal shaft fragment to the blade-plate/proximal fragment**, rotating and abducting as planned, and dock the plate onto the lateral femoral shaft; provisionally hold with a bone clamp.
9. **Confirm correction on fluoroscopy** in both AP and lateral planes — re-measure HEA and NSA intraoperatively before final fixation.
10. **Complete definitive fixation** — cortical/locking screws through the plate shaft into the femur, achieving bicortical purchase where possible.
11. **Take the hip through a range of motion** to confirm stable fixation and assess for impingement or residual abductor lever-arm deficiency.
12. **Layered closure** — reapproximate vastus lateralis over the plate, fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin.
13. **Obtain postoperative fluoroscopic or radiographic images**; in a young child or where fixation stability is a concern, apply a protective spica cast in addition to internal fixation.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Deliberately overcorrect the HEA to below 38–40° at the time of surgery — some loss of correction with growth is expected, and undercorrection is the most common preventable cause of recurrence.
- Always use an angle-stable construct (blade plate or locking pediatric hip plate) rather than a simple screw-plate — the shear forces across a vertically oriented, unstable physis will gradually loosen non-locked screws.
- Use twin reference pins (trochanteric tip and a distal pin) to visually confirm the planned rotational and angular correction before cutting bone — this is far more reliable than eyeballing the correction after the osteotomy is already mobile.
- Predrill the blade/chisel entry point rather than driving it into intact cortex — this measurably reduces the risk of a propagating intraoperative fracture in dense pediatric metaphyseal bone.
- Confirm blade trajectory on both AP and lateral fluoroscopic views before full seating; a blade that looks well-centered on one view alone can still be eccentric or headed toward the physis/joint on the orthogonal view.
- Do the osteotomy as a clean, planned closing wedge rather than freehand — save the excised wedge in case it is useful as autograft for a bone-void or to buttress an unstable segment.
- In a very young child with soft bone or any concern about fixation stability, add a protective spica cast rather than relying on internal fixation alone.
- Correct any coexistent excess femoral anteversion/rotational deformity through the same osteotomy rather than as a separate procedure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Undercorrection** is a well-documented cause of recurrence requiring repeat osteotomy, especially when surgery is performed very young with substantial remaining growth — deliberately overcorrect the HEA at the time of surgery.
- **Inadequate fixation**: a simple screw construct is inadequate for the shear forces at this level — use a blade-plate or locking pediatric hip plate.
- **Blade/chisel malposition** toward the femoral neck or physis risks physeal injury or femoral head avascular necrosis — confirm trajectory on both AP and lateral views before final seating.
- **Nonunion or delayed union** at the subtrochanteric level is a recognized, if uncommon, complication; ensure a stable angle-fixed construct and adequate bony apposition at the osteotomy site.
- **Hardware prominence** requiring later removal is common given the subcutaneous location of proximal femoral plates in a thin child.
- **Neurovascular structures at risk**: the sciatic nerve lies posterior to the operative field — stay anterior/lateral during exposure; perforating branches of the profunda femoris can be encountered medially during wide subperiosteal dissection.
- **Leg-length discrepancy** from the wedge resection or from growth disturbance should be counseled preoperatively and monitored with serial exams/imaging.
- Counsel families explicitly that recurrence requiring revision osteotomy is not rare, especially when surgery is performed very young with substantial remaining growth, and that a period of continued radiographic surveillance through skeletal maturity is required regardless of initial correction quality.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: protected weight-bearing (partial, per fixation construct), active-assisted ROM, avoidance of torsional/twisting loads across the osteotomy site.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive weight-bearing to full as radiographic union is confirmed; physiotherapy for hip and thigh strengthening, gait retraining.
- **Months 3–4**: return to full unrestricted activity/sport once solid radiographic union is confirmed and strength/ROM are symmetric with the contralateral limb.
- **Long-term**: continued radiographic surveillance through skeletal maturity regardless of initial correction quality, given the meaningful minority requiring revision osteotomy for recurrence.
