## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Lichtman Stage I–IIIA Kienböck disease with persistent pain/dysfunction despite a trial of immobilization/activity modification (Stage I) — procedure selection is dictated by ulnar variance measured on a true neutral-rotation PA radiograph.
- Stage IIIB requires correction of fixed carpal collapse (scaphoid flexion, proximal capitate migration, carpal height ratio <0.54) in addition to unloading.
- Stage IV / advanced collapse with radiocarpal or midcarpal arthrosis requires salvage rather than joint-leveling/revascularization.

**Contraindications**
- **Absolute**: active or recent local infection; radiographically confirmed pancarpal arthrosis (Stage IV) treated with joint leveling alone — it will not relieve arthritic pain.
- **Relative**: radial shortening osteotomy in an ulnar-neutral or ulnar-positive patient — biomechanically ineffective and should not be performed regardless of stage; heavy smoking or peripheral vascular disease before a pedicled/free vascularized graft (impairs graft perfusion); inability to comply with a multi-month protected rehabilitation course; skeletally immature patients (open distal radius physis) where osteotomy plate placement must be adjusted or delayed; uncontrolled diabetes or connective tissue disease increasing nonunion risk for any osteotomy/fusion component.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: true neutral-rotation PA wrist/forearm radiograph (shoulder abducted 90°, elbow flexed 90°, forearm neutral) is mandatory for variance measurement — pronated or supinated views change apparent variance by several millimeters and must never be used for planning. Obtain bilateral comparison views if variance is borderline. CT with sagittal reconstructions quantifies carpal height ratio, scaphoid flexion, and lunate fragmentation/collapse. MRI (with gadolinium where available) stages marrow viability and is required for radiographically occult Stage I disease and to assess lunate perfusion before any revascularization procedure.
- **Templating**: for radial shortening, template the planned 2–4 mm resection on the true PA film, accounting for the saw blade kerf (add ~1 mm to the planned resection to compensate for bone lost to the cut) and plan plate length/screw trajectory on the volar or dorsal distal radius. For capitate shortening/STT fusion, template the amount of capitate neck resection needed to restore radiolunate/capitolunate relationships.
- **Implant/graft selection**: dedicated distal radius shortening plate/jig (H-plate or oblique step-cut system) with a parallel-cutting guide; headless compression screws or K-wires for STT/scaphocapitate fusion; if a vascularized graft is planned, decide preoperatively between a pedicled 4+5 extensor compartmental artery (ECA) graft from the dorsal distal radius versus a distal metacarpal pedicled graft, and confirm patency of the donor pedicle is not needed for the pedicled option (it stays attached) but should be documented as present.
- **Equipment**: pneumatic tourniquet, mini-fragment/small-fragment plate and screw sets, oscillating saw with irrigation, dedicated bone-shortening jig, K-wires (0.045–0.062 in), headless compression screws (STT fusion), external fixator set if temporary distraction/unloading is planned, fluoroscopy (mini C-arm), loupe magnification (2.5–4.5×) for vascular pedicle work if revascularization is chosen.
- **Case-specific planning**: decide the procedure family (radial shortening vs capitate shortening/STT fusion vs revascularization vs combined Stage IIIB reconstruction vs Stage IV salvage) before the patient is draped — this determines the incision(s) needed and cannot be reliably changed mid-case without extending exposure.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Regional anesthesia (supraclavicular or axillary brachial plexus block) with or without light sedation is typical; general anesthesia is used for longer combined procedures (e.g., vascularized graft with fusion) or patient preference. Position supine with the arm abducted 90° on a radiolucent hand table. Exsanguinate the limb and inflate a pneumatic tourniquet on the proximal arm (typically 250–280 mmHg or 100 mmHg above systolic). Prep and drape the entire forearm and hand circumferentially to allow both dorsal and volar access and free wrist positioning for fluoroscopy. Bring the mini C-arm in from the axilla/head of the table so true AP and lateral wrist and distal radius/ulna images can be obtained without breaking sterility. Mark Lister's tubercle, the radial styloid, and the subcutaneous border of the ulna before incision as landmarks for the osteotomy or fusion approach chosen.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the procedure pathway.** Re-verify ulnar variance on the true neutral-rotation view and Lichtman stage/carpal height ratio immediately before incision — these determine which of the following pathways is followed.

2. **Radial shortening osteotomy (Stage I–IIIA, ulnar-negative variance).**
   a. Make a longitudinal incision over the distal radial shaft (volar-radial, between the FCR/radial artery and brachioradialis, or a straight dorsoradial approach — either is acceptable; volar avoids the superficial radial nerve branches more reliably).
   b. Subperiosteally expose the radial shaft over a 4–6 cm segment proximal to the distal radioulnar joint, protecting the superficial radial nerve and radial artery.
   c. Apply the dedicated shortening jig/plate to the bone before cutting so the two osteotomy cuts remain perfectly parallel — this is the single most important technical step, since non-parallel cuts create a gap or wedge on compression.
   d. Perform a transverse or step-cut/oblique osteotomy, resecting a bone segment sized to shift variance from negative to neutral or 1–2 mm negative (typically 2–4 mm; add ~1 mm for saw kerf).
   e. Compress the osteotomy with the plate under direct compression (compression device or eccentric screw holes), confirm apposition and rotation under fluoroscopy in two planes, and complete screw fixation.
   f. Confirm final variance on intraoperative fluoroscopy before closure — measure it, do not assume the templated resection was achieved exactly.

3. **Capitate shortening/capitate-hamate fusion or STT fusion (Stage I–IIIA, ulnar-neutral/positive variance).**
   a. Dorsal longitudinal incision centered over the third metacarpal/capitate, splitting the extensor tendons of the third compartment or working between the second and fourth compartments.
   b. For capitate shortening: expose the capitate neck, resect a transverse wedge (typically 2–4 mm) to shorten the capitate column, and fix the capitate-hamate junction with K-wires or a headless screw to prevent settling into malalignment.
   c. For STT fusion: decorticate the scaphoid-trapezium and scaphoid-trapezoid articular surfaces, pack with local or iliac crest cancellous autograft, and fix with headless compression screws or K-wires across the fusion mass, ensuring the scaphoid is not left in excessive flexion.
   d. Confirm restoration of radiolunate alignment and lunate unloading on fluoroscopy.

4. **Revascularization procedure (any stage I–IIIA, particularly neutral variance or when addressing bone viability directly).**
   a. Harvest a pedicled vascularized bone graft based on the 4th+5th extensor compartmental artery (ECA) from the dorsal distal radius, or a pedicled graft from a dorsal metacarpal base, preserving the vascular pedicle intact throughout.
   b. Create a cortical window into the lunate (dorsally) after confirming position under fluoroscopy; curette any cystic/necrotic bone from within the lunate without violating the articular surfaces.
   c. Inset the vascularized graft into the created lunate defect, seating it securely (press-fit) so the pedicle is not kinked or under tension with wrist motion.
   d. If core decompression is used as an adjunct, drill 1–2 small channels into the lunate to stimulate revascularization and decompress intraosseous pressure before or in conjunction with graft inset.
   e. If temporary external fixation for lunate unloading/distraction is planned, apply a dynamic or static wrist-spanning external fixator, distracting modestly across the radiocarpal joint to offload the lunate during early healing; typically removed at 6–8 weeks.

5. **Stage IIIB reconstruction (fixed carpal collapse).** Combine correction of the fixed scaphoid flexion/capitate migration (STT or scaphocapitate fusion, as in step 3) with either radial shortening (if ulnar-negative, as in step 2) or a vascularized graft with structural corticocancellous support to restore lunate height — joint leveling alone is inadequate once the carpal height ratio has dropped and the scaphoid is fixed in flexion.

6. **Stage IV salvage.** Proceed to proximal row carpectomy, limited intercarpal fusion, wrist denervation, total wrist fusion/arthroplasty, or (in carefully selected patients with unreconstructable lunate collapse but no established pancarpal arthritis) pyrocarbon lunate prosthetic arthroplasty — see the SNAC/SLAC salvage ladder technique for procedural detail on PRC/4-corner fusion/total wrist fusion.

7. **Closure.** Repair periosteum/capsule where feasible, close in layers, apply a well-padded volar splint or cast incorporating the wrist in neutral, and confirm final construct alignment and hardware position on completion fluoroscopy before deflating the tourniquet and achieving hemostasis.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Apply the shortening jig/plate to the bone **before** making either osteotomy cut — this is the only reliable way to guarantee parallel cuts and avoid a wedge deformity on compression.
- Add roughly 1 mm to the templated radial shortening resection to account for saw blade kerf; under-resection is easy to correct with a repeat pass, over-resection is not.
- For capitate shortening, avoid over-resection — leaving the capitate too short can create ulnar-sided midcarpal instability; template conservatively and confirm alignment on fluoroscopy before final fixation.
- When inset-ing a vascularized graft into the lunate, orient the graft so its most robust cortical/periosteal surface faces the direction of greatest mechanical load, and confirm the pedicle has a gentle, tension-free course with the wrist taken through a full range of motion intraoperatively before closure.
- Use intraoperative fluoroscopy to directly re-measure final ulnar variance after radial shortening rather than trusting the preoperative template — small differences from planned resection are common and correctable in real time.
- For STT/scaphocapitate fusion, avoid excessive scaphoid flexion when packing the fusion site — over-flexing the scaphoid to "correct" collapse can itself create a fixed DISI-like deformity.
- If combining osteotomy with a vascularized graft (Stage IIIB), sequence the bony shortening/fusion first and inset the graft last, so the graft is not stressed by subsequent hardware manipulation.
- Keep the tourniquet time in mind when a microsurgical or pedicled vascular step is planned — release and reperfuse periodically if the combined procedure is running long, particularly for free vascularized reconstructions.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Radial shortening is ineffective if the baseline is ulnar-neutral or -positive — confirm variance on a true neutral-rotation PA view before committing to this procedure; using it in the wrong variance group will not unload the lunate.
- Lunate excision with tendon/prosthetic interposition is largely historical given poor long-term results — do not default to it; modern pyrocarbon lunate prosthetic arthroplasty is the contemporary alternative, though evidence quality remains limited and it is not first-line.
- Stage IIIB requires correcting the fixed carpal collapse itself, not just unloading the lunate — joint leveling alone (without STT/scaphocapitate fusion or structural graft support) is inadequate once carpal height ratio has dropped below 0.54.
- Non-parallel osteotomy cuts (jig applied after cutting rather than before) create a gap or wedge deformity at the osteotomy site, predisposing to delayed union/nonunion and malalignment.
- Superficial radial nerve or radial artery injury is a recognized risk with the radial shortening approach; identify and protect both before deepening the dissection.
- Vascular pedicle injury or kinking during graft inset can devitalize the graft — confirm a tension-free pedicle course through full wrist range of motion before final closure.
- Undercorrection (inadequate shortening or inadequate carpal collapse correction) leaves residual lunate overload and symptom persistence; overcorrection of radial shortening can create ulnar impaction symptoms at the DRUJ.

## Postoperative Protocol

Cast/splint immobilization 6–8 weeks (longer, up to 10–12 weeks, for limited intercarpal fusions until trabecular bridging is seen on CT); progressive AROM and strengthening from 8–12 weeks; return to heavy labor at 3–4 months. Vascularized graft procedures often follow a similar 8–12 week protected phase before loading.
