## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- **Scapula body/spine**: glenopolar angle <22°, lateral border offset >20 mm, angular deformity >45°, displaced scapular neck with concomitant clavicle fracture ("floating shoulder"), or open fracture.
- **Glenoid (intra-articular)**: articular step-off/gap >4–5 mm, >25% involvement of the anterior or >33% of the posterior glenoid with instability, or associated instability/subluxation.
- Scapulothoracic dissociation with a salvageable limb (after vascular/brachial plexus assessment) may require scapular stabilization as part of a broader reconstructive plan, though this is a distinct, more severe injury pattern than routine scapula fracture.

**Contraindications**:
- Extra-articular scapula body/spine fractures with acceptable glenopolar angle, lateral border offset, and angular deformity — these heal reliably nonoperatively given the scapula's robust muscular envelope and blood supply.
- Congruent, stable glenoid fractures with <25% articular involvement and no instability.
- Medically unstable polytrauma patient in whom scapular fixation can safely be delayed (scapula fractures tolerate delayed fixation better than most other injuries in this cluster given their excellent soft-tissue coverage and healing potential).
- Severely comminuted glenoid fossa fracture with poor bone stock in an elderly patient — consider primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty over an ORIF attempt likely to fail.
- Active infection or a compromised posterior soft-tissue envelope that cannot tolerate a Judet-type approach.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP shoulder, scapular-Y, and axillary views for initial assessment; CT with 3D reconstruction is essentially mandatory for glenoid/intra-articular fractures and for accurately measuring glenopolar angle, medialization, and angular deformity in body fractures — 3D reconstructions with the humeral head digitally subtracted are particularly useful for visualizing glenoid fossa comminution and step-off.
- **Classification and surgical plan**: apply the Ideberg classification to the glenoid pattern to decide fixation strategy (buttress/spanning plate for comminuted Ideberg VI, standard screw/plate fixation for simple rim or transverse patterns) and confirm whether the pattern is reconstructable or better suited to primary arthroplasty in an older patient.
- **Implant selection**: have small-fragment screws, a reconstruction/contourable plate system suited to the scapular spine and lateral border, and a buttress or spanning plate option available for comminuted glenoid fossa patterns; template plate contour against the 3D CT reconstruction preoperatively when possible.
- **SSSC assessment**: if an ipsilateral clavicle fracture is present ("floating shoulder"), assess combined displacement (glenopolar angle, clavicle shortening, glenohumeral alignment) as a unit rather than treating each fracture in isolation, and plan whether clavicle fixation alone will adequately stabilize the construct or whether both components need fixation.
- **Positioning aids**: confirm availability of a beanbag or lateral positioner for the lateral decubitus position typically used for posterior (Judet) approaches.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, often combined with an interscalene block for postoperative analgesia; note that a dense interscalene block can mask an evolving compartment-like posterior hematoma in the immediate postoperative period, so document a careful baseline exam.
- **Posterior (Judet/modified Judet) approach position**: lateral decubitus position with the operative side up, supported by a beanbag or lateral positioner, arm draped free on a padded arm holder to allow intraoperative manipulation and access to the posterior scapula and axillary border.
- **Anterior (deltopectoral) approach position**: modified beach-chair position as for standard proximal humerus/shoulder surgery, torso elevated, operative shoulder at the table edge for full arm excursion.
- **Table setup**: radiolucent table permitting fluoroscopic AP, scapular-Y, and axillary-equivalent views of the shoulder and scapula without repositioning.
- **Draping**: for posterior approaches, drape widely to expose the entire scapula from the spine to the inferior angle and laterally to the axillary border; for anterior approaches, drape as for a standard deltopectoral shoulder exposure.
- **Landmarks**: mark the scapular spine, inferior angle, medial and lateral borders, and acromion for posterior approaches; mark the coracoid and deltopectoral groove for anterior approaches.
- **Neuromonitoring consideration**: given the proximity of the suprascapular and axillary nerves to the posterior approach, some centers use intraoperative nerve monitoring for complex posterior glenoid neck exposures — confirm availability if planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Select the approach by fracture location.** Posterior (Judet or minimally-invasive modified Judet) for glenoid neck, scapular body, and posterior glenoid rim fractures; anterior (deltopectoral) for anterior glenoid rim fractures; consider arthroscopic-assisted fixation for anterior glenoid rim (bony Bankart-equivalent) fragments in the instability setting, offering less soft-tissue morbidity than an open approach.
2. **Posterior (Judet) exposure.** Make an incision along the scapular spine curving down along the medial border, or use the minimally-invasive modified Judet windows (a more limited incision preserving the deltoid origin and working through intervals rather than a full subperiosteal strip). Develop the interval between infraspinatus and teres minor to access the posterior glenoid neck and lateral border — this interval is the key surgical window and the location where the suprascapular nerve (medially, at the spinoglenoid notch) and axillary nerve (inferiorly, at the quadrangular space) are both at risk; stay within the interval and avoid aggressive medial or inferior retraction.
3. **Modified/minimally-invasive Judet windows.** Where the fracture pattern allows, use limited windows (e.g., a lateral window between infraspinatus and teres minor, and a medial window along the medial scapular border) rather than fully elevating the infraspinatus off the scapular body — this reduces soft-tissue morbidity and postoperative infraspinatus dysfunction while still allowing reduction and plate application along the lateral border and scapular spine.
4. **Anterior (deltopectoral) exposure.** For anterior glenoid rim fractures, use a standard deltopectoral interval, retracting the cephalic vein, and develop the subscapularis interval (split or tenotomize, per surgeon preference and fragment location) to access the anterior glenoid — protect the axillary nerve inferiorly and the musculocutaneous nerve medially within the conjoint tendon.
5. **Reduce the fracture.** For scapular body/spine fractures, use pointed reduction clamps along the lateral border and scapular spine to restore length, rotation, and the glenopolar angle; for glenoid neck/fossa fractures, use provisional K-wires and lever/reduction instruments through the exposed interval to restore articular congruity, checking reduction both visually and with intraoperative fluoroscopy.
6. **Fix the lateral border and spine (extra-articular component).** Apply a contourable reconstruction or precontoured scapula plate along the lateral border and/or scapular spine, restoring the glenopolar angle and correcting medialization — this construct often indirectly reduces and stabilizes an associated glenoid neck component.
7. **Fix the articular (glenoid) component.** For simple rim fractures, use lag screws or a small buttress plate to secure the fragment anatomically, aiming for a step-off under 1–2 mm. For comminuted glenoid fossa fractures (Ideberg VI), use a buttress or spanning plate construct across the fossa, prioritizing restoration of articular congruity as the primary technical goal even if some extra-articular comminution is bridged rather than anatomically reduced.
8. **Address a concomitant clavicle fracture (floating shoulder).** If present, fix the clavicle first (or in the same setting) to restore the superior strut — this frequently indirectly reduces and stabilizes the scapular component, and may be sufficient without formal glenoid neck fixation if displacement corrects adequately.
9. **Confirm reduction fluoroscopically.** Obtain AP, scapular-Y, and axillary-equivalent fluoroscopic views to confirm restored glenopolar angle, lateral border alignment, and articular congruity.
10. **Consider arthroplasty conversion.** In an older patient with an unreconstructable, severely comminuted glenoid fossa fracture and poor bone quality encountered intraoperatively, consider converting to (or planning a staged) primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty rather than persisting with an ORIF attempt likely to fail.
11. **Irrigate and achieve hemostasis.** This region has a robust muscular blood supply; take time for meticulous hemostasis before closure to avoid a postoperative hematoma, particularly deep to the infraspinatus.
12. **Layered closure.** Repair the infraspinatus/teres minor interval or subscapularis (per approach), close fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin; consider a drain for posterior exposures given the muscular bed's vascularity.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Favor the minimally-invasive modified Judet windows over a full subperiosteal strip whenever the fracture pattern allows — preserving the infraspinatus origin measurably reduces postoperative external rotation weakness and pain compared with a traditional full Judet exposure.
- Work strictly within the infraspinatus-teres minor interval posteriorly; both the suprascapular nerve (medially, at the spinoglenoid notch) and the axillary nerve (inferiorly, at the quadrangular space) sit just outside this window, and aggressive retraction beyond the interval is the most common mechanism of iatrogenic injury here.
- Use the lateral border and scapular spine plate construct to indirectly reduce the glenoid neck component whenever possible — restoring the extra-articular strut frequently brings the articular fragment into acceptable alignment without needing to directly instrument it.
- For floating shoulder, fix the clavicle first (or simultaneously) — in many cases this alone restores the SSSC and adequately reduces the scapular component, sparing the patient a second, more morbid posterior exposure.
- Take intraoperative fluoroscopy seriously for glenopolar angle confirmation — it is easy to achieve an aesthetically "reduced-looking" lateral border on direct visualization while still leaving the glenopolar angle undercorrected.
- For comminuted glenoid fossa fractures, prioritize articular congruity over anatomic restoration of every extra-articular comminuted fragment — a bridged, congruent joint outperforms an anatomically "complete" but incongruent reconstruction.
- Have a low threshold to convert to primary reverse total shoulder arthroplasty intraoperatively in an elderly patient with unreconstructable glenoid comminution — discovering this only after a failed, prolonged ORIF attempt is a worse outcome than planning for the possibility preoperatively.
- Place a drain for posterior (Judet-type) exposures given the muscular bed's vascularity — postoperative hematoma is a recognized nuisance complication in this approach.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The suprascapular and axillary nerves are at risk via the posterior interval; the axillary nerve, musculocutaneous nerve, and subscapularis are at risk anteriorly.
- The glenoid articular surface behaves like any other intra-articular fracture (step-off intolerant) — do not extend the same tolerance for displacement used for extra-articular scapular body/spine fractures, which heal well nonoperatively even with substantial displacement.
- When a scapular neck/glenoid fracture coexists with a clavicle fracture ("floating shoulder"), assess combined displacement rather than each fracture in isolation — true double disruption of the superior shoulder suspensory complex is unstable and usually needs fixation of at least one component (often the clavicle, which restores the strut and indirectly stabilizes the scapular component).
- Overly aggressive posterior dissection/retraction beyond the infraspinatus-teres minor interval is the principal mechanism of suprascapular or axillary nerve injury in this approach.
- Postoperative hematoma is a recognized nuisance complication of the muscular, vascular posterior exposure — meticulous hemostasis and selective drain use reduce this risk.
- Attempting anatomic ORIF of a severely comminuted glenoid fossa fracture in poor-quality bone (rather than converting to arthroplasty) risks a prolonged, technically difficult procedure with an unreliable articular result.
- Postoperative infraspinatus weakness/dysfunction is more common with a full traditional Judet exposure than with a minimally-invasive modified approach — factor this into approach selection when the fracture pattern allows either.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Sling for comfort 1–2 wk; early pendulum and passive ROM within the first week (stiffness, not nonunion, is the dominant risk for extra-articular patterns given the scapula's excellent healing potential from its muscular envelope).
- Progressive active ROM from 2–4 wk; strengthening from 6 wk.
- Intra-articular glenoid fixation follows a similar but slightly more conservative timeline respecting articular healing, with resisted loading deferred to ~8–10 wk.
