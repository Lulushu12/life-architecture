## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:** applies to all patients undergoing primary total hip arthroplasty — every THA patient requires a documented VTE risk-stratification decision and prophylaxis plan, even when the eventual choice is a lower-intensity regimen. International consensus on the specific regimen remains fragmented across CHEST, the International Consensus Meeting (ICM), NICE, and national societies, so the practical approach is individualized risk stratification rather than a single universal protocol.

**Contraindications/relative contraindications to specific regimen choices (not to prophylaxis itself — every patient gets some form of prophylaxis unless actively bleeding):**
- Active bleeding, recent major hemorrhage, or a bleeding diathesis — favors mechanical prophylaxis alone or the lowest-intensity pharmacologic option acceptable, with hematology input.
- Aspirin hypersensitivity, active peptic ulcer disease, or significant GI bleeding history — excludes aspirin as the agent; escalate to LMWH, a DOAC, or another CHEST-endorsed alternative.
- Renal impairment — dose-adjust or avoid certain DOACs/LMWH per renal clearance; fondaparinux and some DOACs carry specific renal cutoffs.
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) history — avoid unfractionated heparin and LMWH; use a DOAC or fondaparinux instead.
- Severe peripheral arterial disease or lower-extremity wounds — relative contraindication to intermittent pneumatic compression on the affected limb; use the contralateral limb and/or foot pumps, or rely more heavily on pharmacologic prophylaxis.
- Poor anticipated compliance with an oral regimen (cognitive impairment, unreliable follow-up) — favors an in-hospital injectable or a single-agent, simple-dosing regimen over multi-step sequential regimens.

## Preoperative Planning

**Risk stratification is the core planning task** and should be documented in the preoperative note, not decided reflexively on the day of surgery. Elements to gather before the day of surgery:
- Personal history of VTE (provoked vs unprovoked), known or suspected hypercoagulable state (Factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid syndrome, prior unexplained clot), active malignancy, and anticipated prolonged postoperative immobility.
- Bleeding risk factors (see Contraindications above) that would push away from higher-intensity anticoagulation.
- Current anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications and their required perioperative hold intervals (e.g., DOAC hold windows vary by renal function and agent — confirm with the prescribing timeline before surgery, not on the morning of the case).
- Renal function (creatinine clearance) to guide DOAC/LMWH dosing eligibility.
- Confirm mechanical prophylaxis equipment (intermittent pneumatic compression sleeves, correctly sized) is ordered and available for immediate postoperative application — this is easy to overlook as a "pharmacy problem" when it is actually a day-of-surgery equipment item.
- Decide and document the specific regimen, dose, and planned duration before surgery so the inpatient team and the patient's discharge prescription are aligned — ambiguity at discharge (e.g., "continue home regimen" without specifying which one) is a recognized source of prophylaxis gaps.
- For sequential regimens (e.g., a short course of a more potent agent transitioning to aspirin), pre-specify the transition day so it is not missed during a routine discharge.

**No implant/graft sizing applicable** — this is a pharmacologic/mechanical protocol layered onto the primary THA, not a device-implanting procedure in its own right.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Not applicable in the surgical-positioning sense — this is a perioperative pharmacologic and mechanical protocol rather than a positioning-dependent procedure, and it is executed around the anesthesia and positioning already described for the index THA. Two points of intersection worth planning explicitly:
- **Neuraxial anesthesia timing:** if spinal/epidural anesthesia is planned for the THA itself, any preoperative dose of a therapeutic-intensity anticoagulant (e.g., in a patient already on chronic anticoagulation for another indication) must respect standard neuraxial-anesthesia hold windows to avoid spinal/epidural hematoma — coordinate this explicitly with anesthesia before the case, since the VTE-prophylaxis plan and the anesthesia plan are not independent decisions in this population.
- **Mechanical prophylaxis application:** intermittent pneumatic compression sleeves are applied intraoperatively (once positioning allows) or immediately postoperatively in the recovery area, and continued near-universally in-hospital regardless of which pharmacologic agent is chosen.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Risk-stratify the patient** preoperatively into standard-risk versus elevated personal VTE risk (prior VTE, active malignancy, hypercoagulable state, prolonged anticipated immobility), documenting the specific factors driving the categorization.
2. **Standard-risk elective primary THA:** use aspirin 81 mg twice daily as the default per the 2022 International Consensus Meeting (ICM) on VTE — the most current unified position going into 2026 — favoring aspirin over LMWH for this population, a meaningful shift from historical LMWH-first practice. Comparative data on aspirin dosing specifically favor the 81 mg twice-daily regimen over 325 mg once daily: in direct comparison, bleeding/wound complication rates were lower with 81 mg BID (roughly 2.5% vs 7.6% with 325 mg daily in one comparative series) with VTE rates that were not worse (numerically lower in the same series), supporting low-dose twice-daily dosing as the preferred aspirin regimen rather than a higher once-daily dose.
3. **Consider a sequential regimen in selected standard-risk patients:** some contemporary pathways (modeled on trials such as EPCAT II) start with a more potent agent (e.g., a DOAC) for the first several postoperative days while VTE risk is highest and mobility is most limited, then transition to aspirin for the remainder of the prophylaxis course — this is a reasonable, evidence-informed alternative to aspirin-only dosing from day one, particularly in patients with intermediate risk factors that do not clearly meet a full "elevated-risk" threshold.
4. **Elevated personal VTE risk:** escalate to stronger anticoagulation — LMWH or a DOAC (apixaban, dabigatran, rivaroxaban) — rather than aspirin, for the full prophylaxis course.
5. **Alternative CHEST-endorsed agents** (adjusted-dose vitamin K antagonist, fondaparinux, low-dose unfractionated heparin, or mechanical compression alone) remain acceptable options depending on patient factors (renal function, HIT history, compliance) and local practice.
6. **Add mechanical prophylaxis** (intermittent pneumatic compression) regardless of pharmacologic choice, applied as soon as positioning allows intraoperatively/immediately postoperatively and continued in-hospital.
7. **Set and document duration:** continue prophylaxis for a minimum of 10–14 days per CHEST guidelines; extend toward 28–35 days in higher-risk patients per several national frameworks, including NICE VTE guidance. Confirm the discharge prescription specifies the exact agent, dose, and stop date rather than a vague "continue prophylaxis" instruction.
8. **Pair with early mobilization** (same day or POD1) as part of an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathway, which further supports VTE risk reduction alongside pharmacologic/mechanical measures.
9. **Reassess at the routine postoperative visit** (typically 2 weeks) for symptoms suggestive of VTE (calf pain/swelling, dyspnea, chest pain) and for medication adherence/tolerance before the prophylaxis course concludes.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Default to aspirin 81 mg twice daily rather than 325 mg once daily for standard-risk patients — the twice-daily low-dose regimen has a materially better bleeding/wound-complication profile with no signal of worse VTE protection in comparative series.
- Document the specific risk-stratification factors in the chart at the time the decision is made, not retrospectively — this is the detail most likely to matter if a VTE event or a bleeding complication is later reviewed.
- Build the discharge prescription and the specific stop-date into the electronic order set or discharge checklist rather than relying on a verbal handoff — ambiguous "continue home regimen" instructions are a recognized gap point at transitions of care.
- For patients already on chronic anticoagulation for an unrelated indication, plan the bridging/hold strategy in coordination with the prescribing physician (cardiology, hematology) well before the day of surgery, not as a last-minute anesthesia question.
- If using a sequential regimen (potent agent transitioning to aspirin), set a specific calendar-day trigger for the switch rather than leaving it to clinical judgment at a busy postoperative visit.
- Confirm mechanical compression sleeve sizing and function before the patient leaves the recovery area — a sleeve that is not actually cycling provides no benefit despite appearing to be "in use."
- Counsel patients explicitly that aspirin for VTE prophylaxis is being used as an anticoagulant-adjacent agent in this context, not simply as their usual over-the-counter pain reliever — this affects how seriously they take adherence and how they report new bleeding symptoms.
- When escalating to a DOAC or LMWH for elevated risk, cross-check renal function and confirm the dose is calibrated to prophylactic (not treatment) intensity unless the patient has an independent treatment-dose indication.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- International consensus remains fragmented across CHEST, ICM, NICE, and national society guidance as of 2025 — treat individualized risk stratification, not a single universal protocol, as the defensible practice standard.
- European fast-track THA/TKA protocols favor aspirin over no prophylaxis but stop short of a blanket recommendation for or against aspirin in higher-risk procedures — do not apply the standard-risk aspirin default indiscriminately to elevated-risk patients.
- Using 325 mg once-daily aspirin instead of 81 mg twice daily is associated with materially higher bleeding/wound complication rates (roughly 7.6% vs 2.5% in one comparative series) without a corresponding VTE-protection advantage — this is a common, easily avoidable dosing error.
- An injection within roughly 3 months of a planned THA is a recognized (though debated in magnitude) periprosthetic infection risk factor distinct from VTE risk, but is relevant to the same perioperative planning window and is a practical cutoff many arthroplasty surgeons use before proceeding to surgery.
- Symptomatic 90-day VTE with modern prophylaxis is <1%, and 1-year mortality after elective primary THA is <0.5% — useful context for patient counseling, but not a reason to skip formal risk stratification.
- Ambiguous discharge instructions (failing to specify agent, dose, and stop date) are a practical, underrecognized source of prophylaxis gaps or inadvertent early discontinuation — build the specifics into the discharge order set.
- Failure to coordinate neuraxial anesthesia timing with a patient's baseline (non-THA-related) anticoagulation can create a spinal/epidural hematoma risk that is entirely avoidable with preoperative planning — this is an anesthesia-coordination pitfall specific to patients who arrive already anticoagulated for another indication.
- Complications from prophylaxis itself: bleeding/wound hematoma (dose- and agent-dependent, generally lowest with low-dose aspirin), and — rarely — heparin-induced thrombocytopenia with unfractionated heparin or LMWH, which should prompt an immediate switch to a non-heparin agent (DOAC or fondaparinux) if suspected.
- Missed VTE events present atypically in the early postoperative period (pain and swelling can overlap with expected postoperative findings) — maintain a lower threshold for imaging (duplex ultrasound, CT pulmonary angiogram) in a patient with new or disproportionate calf pain, swelling, dyspnea, or chest pain during the prophylaxis window rather than attributing all such symptoms to normal postoperative recovery.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Minimum 10–14 days of chemoprophylaxis per CHEST guidelines; extend to 28–35 days in higher-risk patients per several national frameworks (including NICE VTE guidance).
- Mechanical compression (intermittent pneumatic compression) continued in-hospital regardless of pharmacologic agent.
- Early mobilization (day 0 or POD1) as a standard ERAS-pathway adjunct to pharmacologic/mechanical prophylaxis.
- No dedicated additional follow-up beyond standard THA surveillance; reassess if new-onset leg swelling, calf pain, or respiratory symptoms arise during the prophylaxis window.
