## Indications

Long cephalomedullary nailing is the standard of care for subtrochanteric femur fractures of essentially any Seinsheimer type. Non-operative management (traction) is temporizing only and not appropriate as definitive treatment. A proximal femoral locking plate with indirect reduction is the alternative when nailing is precluded — an existing ipsilateral hip implant, marked deformity, or a periprosthetic pattern.

## Positioning & setup

Lateral decubitus or supine positioning with a bump, with full-length femur imaging obtained before instrumentation to rule out a contralateral or ipsilateral incomplete atypical femur fracture (AFF) and to plan nail length/bow-matching. Full-length imaging is especially important given the biomechanically demanding, poorly vascularized nature of this cortical bone zone.

## Steps

1. **Anticipate and plan for the deforming forces** before nail passage: iliopsoas flexes/externally rotates the proximal fragment, abductors abduct it, and adductors pull the distal fragment into varus/shortening. These forces — not fixation strength — are the dominant technical challenge, and must be neutralized with the nail construct itself, not by traction alone.
2. **Achieve provisional reduction** using one or more of:
   - Blocking (Poller) screws to control reduction as the nail passes through the wide metaphyseal canal.
   - Percutaneous clamps or joysticks.
   - Cerclage cabling for long spiral/comminuted patterns — placed with a biology-preserving, minimally invasive technique to avoid devascularizing fragments.
   - Open reduction only when closed/percutaneous methods fail to restore length-alignment-rotation.
3. **Pass the long cephalomedullary nail**, confirming length/alignment/rotation restoration is anatomic or near-anatomic — varus/valgus and rotational malalignment >10–15° predispose to nonunion and abductor limp.
4. **If an AFF is suspected** (transverse pattern, lateral cortical "beak," minimal comminution, or history of bisphosphonate/denosumab use >3–5 years with prodromal thigh pain): use the same nailing principle but consider a longer nail to the level of the physeal scar, a teriparatide adjunct, and a drug-holiday discussion — AFFs carry a materially higher nonunion/delayed-union rate than typical subtrochanteric fractures.
5. **If nailing is precluded** (existing ipsilateral hip implant, marked deformity, periprosthetic pattern): use a proximal femoral locking plate with indirect reduction technique instead, accepting a load-sharing (rather than intramedullary load-sharing) construct and generally a longer weight-bearing restriction.

## Pitfalls

- Do not rely on traction alone to control the deforming muscular forces — they must be neutralized by the nail/plate construct during reduction and fixation.
- Nonunion/delayed union runs 10–20% in comminuted or malreduced cases, higher than intertrochanteric fractures owing to cortical, lower-vascularity bone and greater deforming forces.
- Implant failure (nail breakage at the screw hole) can occur if union is delayed and load cycling continues.
- In the AFF subset, contralateral fracture and delayed healing can occur independent of fixation quality — always image the contralateral femur when AFF is suspected.
- Malunion (varus, rotational malalignment) results from failure to control deforming forces during reduction, not from implant selection alone.

## Post-op protocol

- Weight-bearing status is generally more restrictive than for intertrochanteric fixation given the biomechanical demands of this zone; touch-down weight-bearing is more often used for comminuted or marginal-fixation cases, with closer radiographic follow-up (2, 6, 12 weeks) to detect early collapse or varus migration.
- Locking-plate constructs (when nailing is precluded) generally require a longer weight-bearing restriction than nailed constructs.
- Union is typically confirmed by bridging callus by 12–16 weeks; obtain a delayed-union workup (labs, CT) if no radiographic progression by 4–6 months.
- Physiotherapy progression follows the standard fragility-fracture phases: POD0–1 bed mobility/transfers; week 1–2 gait training and hip/knee AROM; week 2–6 progressive resistive exercise; week 6–12 abductor/extensor strengthening; beyond 12 weeks continued strengthening and fall-prevention.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing. High-demand recreational activity (relevant to younger subtrochanteric fracture patients) is generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.
