## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Long cephalomedullary nailing is the standard of care for subtrochanteric femur fractures of essentially any Seinsheimer type. Non-operative management (traction) is temporizing only and not appropriate as definitive treatment given the poorly vascularized, high-stress mechanical environment of this zone.

**Contraindications/relative contraindications:**

- Existing ipsilateral hip implant, marked pre-existing femoral deformity, or a periprosthetic fracture pattern that precludes safe antegrade nail passage — a proximal femoral locking plate with indirect reduction technique is the preferred alternative here.
- Active local/systemic infection — treat the septic focus before implantation, or use temporizing external fixation.
- A narrow or obliterated canal (prior diaphyseal deformity, retained hardware, or severe osteopetrotic-type bone) that makes reaming unsafe without pre-operative CT-based planning.
- Suspected pathologic fracture through tumor — biopsy and oncologic planning should precede definitive fixation unless the patient is unstable and prophylactic stabilization is emergent.
- Skeletally immature patients — different implant and entry-point strategy required, outside standard adult technique.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Full-length femur imaging** (both femurs when an atypical femur fracture, AFF, is suspected) is mandatory before instrumentation, to rule out a contralateral or ipsilateral incomplete AFF, characterize the femoral bow, and plan nail length and lateral bow-matching.
- **CT** is useful for comminuted (Seinsheimer III–IV) or combined subtrochanteric-intertrochanteric (Seinsheimer V) patterns to characterize fragment number, the posteromedial (calcar) fragment, and any coronal-plane component before committing to an indirect reduction strategy.
- **Implant selection:** a long cephalomedullary nail sized to span the entire femur to the distal metaphysis; confirm proximal diameter, lateral bow radius, and lag-screw/blade angle against the patient's anatomy, particularly in the AFF subset where a nail to the level of the physeal scar is often preferred.
- **Reduction adjuncts to have available:** blocking (Poller) screws, percutaneous reduction clamps (collinear or pointed), Schanz-pin joysticks, a femoral distractor, a ball-spiked pusher, and cerclage cable/wire instrumentation — decide which are likely needed based on the fracture pattern (spiral/comminuted patterns are the ones most likely to need cerclage).
- **Medication history:** review duration of bisphosphonate/denosumab therapy and any prodromal thigh pain; if AFF is suspected, plan concurrently for a teriparatide adjunct discussion and antiresorptive drug holiday with the patient's managing physician.
- **Blood management:** type and crossmatch given the higher expected blood loss of subtrochanteric fixation compared with intertrochanteric fixation; confirm tranexamic acid plan with anesthesia.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general or spinal anesthesia per patient factors; regional block (fascia iliaca/femoral nerve) for adjunct analgesia is standard.
- **Positioning:** lateral decubitus or supine positioning with a bump under the ipsilateral hip/buttock, chosen based on surgeon preference and body habitus — lateral decubitus can ease access to the entry point in obese patients, while supine on a fracture or radiolucent table facilitates fluoroscopic imaging and is preferred when combined manual/joystick reduction maneuvers are anticipated.
- **Traction table vs. freehand:** many surgeons favor a freehand (radiolucent table, manual traction) technique for subtrochanteric fractures rather than a traction table, because the deforming forces in this zone (proximal fragment flexed/externally rotated/abducted, distal fragment adducted/shortened) are often more effectively controlled with direct manual reduction, percutaneous clamps, and joysticks than with axial traction alone, which can distract the fracture without correcting angulation.
- **C-arm setup:** position for unobstructed AP and true lateral imaging of the entire femur from the hip to the knee, since reduction must be confirmed and nail length/distal locking performed at both ends.
- **Draping:** prep and drape from the iliac crest to below the knee to allow full-length nail passage and both proximal and distal locking.
- **Landmarks:** mark the greater trochanter tip (entry site) and, for distal locking, the anticipated screw sites based on preoperative nail-length templating.
- **Tourniquet:** not used.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Anticipate the deforming forces before instrumentation begins.** The proximal fragment is flexed and externally rotated by the iliopsoas and abducted by the intact abductors (unopposed by the fractured shaft); the distal fragment is pulled into varus and shortened by the adductors. These forces — not fixation strength — are the dominant technical challenge in this fracture and must be neutralized by the reduction maneuver and nail construct itself, not by traction alone.
2. **Make the proximal entry incision** in line with the femoral shaft axis, starting just proximal to the greater trochanteric tip, and split the abductor fibers bluntly.
3. **Obtain provisional reduction before committing to the entry point and reaming.** Use whichever combination is needed for the pattern:
   - **Blocking (Poller) screws** placed just outside the intended nail path to narrow the wide metaphyseal/proximal canal and direct the nail trajectory, preventing apex-anterior or varus/valgus drift as the nail passes through the fracture site.
   - **Percutaneous clamps or Schanz-pin joysticks** placed directly into the proximal and/or distal fragments to manually correct flexion, rotation, and varus/valgus before and during nail passage.
   - **A femoral distractor or ball-spiked pusher** for length restoration in shortened, comminuted patterns.
   - **Cerclage cabling** for long spiral or butterfly-fragment patterns (Seinsheimer II–III), placed with a biology-preserving, minimally invasive technique (limited exposure, cable passer rather than circumferential stripping) specifically to avoid devascularizing fragments — cerclage here is a reduction aid to restore length/rotation/apposition, not a load-bearing construct.
   - **Open reduction** reserved for cases where closed/percutaneous methods fail to restore length-alignment-rotation; when opened, minimize periosteal stripping and handle fragments only as needed to achieve reduction.
4. **Establish the nail entry point at the greater trochanteric tip**, confirming trajectory on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy — the same varus-avoidance principles as intertrochanteric nailing apply (a too-lateral start point drives the construct into varus as the nail seats).
5. **Ream sequentially** over a ball-tipped guidewire, re-confirming that reduction is maintained (not merely assumed) at each pass, since reaming through a comminuted or wide canal can displace a marginal reduction.
6. **Pass the long cephalomedullary nail** across the fracture, using the blocking screws/clamps/joysticks placed in step 3 to keep the trajectory centered and the reduction maintained as the nail crosses the fracture site — this is the single highest-risk moment for losing an achieved reduction.
7. **Confirm length, alignment, and rotation are anatomic or near-anatomic on both views** before proximal lag-screw and distal locking. Varus/valgus and rotational malalignment >10–15° predispose to nonunion and abductor limp and are far harder to correct after both ends are locked.
8. **Place the proximal lag screw/blade** into the femoral head using the same central-central/slightly-inferior targeting and TAD<25 mm principles used in intertrochanteric nailing.
9. **Lock distally** with a minimum of two distal screws for a long nail; favor static locking to control rotation, particularly in comminuted (Seinsheimer III–IV) or AFF patterns where interfragmentary rotational control cannot be relied upon from fracture apposition alone.
10. **If an AFF is suspected** (transverse fracture pattern, lateral cortical "beak," minimal comminution, or a history of prolonged bisphosphonate/denosumab use with prodromal thigh pain): use the same overall nailing principle but consider a longer nail extending to the level of the femoral physeal scar (near-full-length), discuss a teriparatide adjunct and antiresorptive drug holiday with the patient's medical team, and counsel the patient explicitly that healing time will likely be prolonged.
11. **Remove any percutaneous clamps/joysticks and confirm final reduction and implant position** on orthogonal fluoroscopy before closure.
12. **If nailing is precluded** (existing ipsilateral hip implant, marked deformity, periprosthetic pattern): convert to a proximal femoral locking plate placed with an indirect (biology-preserving) reduction technique, accepting a load-sharing rather than intramedullary construct and planning for a longer postoperative weight-bearing restriction.
13. **Close in layers**, repairing the split abductor fascia, with routine subcutaneous and skin closure at both the proximal and any percutaneous/distal sites.
14. **Obtain final intraoperative fluoroscopic or portable radiographic images** of the full construct (hip to knee) before the patient leaves the table.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Plan your reduction strategy before you open the entry portal — decide in advance whether Poller screws, a joystick, a clamp, or cerclage will be needed for the specific pattern, rather than reaching for adjuncts reactively after a failed nail pass.
- Use blocking (Poller) screws liberally in the wide proximal metaphyseal canal typical of this zone — they are the most reliable way to prevent the nail from drifting into apex-anterior angulation or varus as it crosses a comminuted segment.
- When cerclage cabling is used for spiral/butterfly patterns, pass the cable percutaneously with a cable passer through a limited window rather than circumferentially stripping the fragment — the goal is reduction and rotational control, not rigid fixation, and preserving periosteal blood supply materially affects union rate in this poorly vascularized bone.
- A femoral distractor spanning from a proximal to a distal pin is a highly effective, underused tool for restoring length in a shortened, comminuted subtrochanteric fracture before nail passage — it frees both of the surgeon's hands for reduction maneuvers.
- Confirm reduction is maintained after every major step (reaming, nail passage, lag-screw placement, distal locking) rather than only at the start — this fracture zone loses reduction easily during instrumentation because of the deforming muscular forces acting throughout the case.
- In AFF patients, nail to the level of the physeal scar even when a shorter nail would otherwise suffice for the fracture pattern — this protects the remaining at-risk diaphysis from a subsequent fracture at the nail tip.
- If open reduction becomes necessary, use it to achieve length/alignment/rotation rather than anatomic fragment-by-fragment reduction in comminuted patterns — restoring the mechanical axis matters more than perfect fragment apposition in this zone.
- Consider a supine freehand approach over a traction table when multiple joystick/clamp maneuvers are anticipated — traction-table positioning can make simultaneous manual fragment manipulation awkward.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not rely on traction alone to control the deforming muscular forces — they must be neutralized by the nail/plate construct during reduction and fixation, not merely resisted by axial pull.
- Nonunion/delayed union runs 10–20% in comminuted or malreduced cases, higher than intertrochanteric fractures owing to cortical, lower-vascularity bone and greater deforming forces.
- Implant failure (nail breakage at the screw hole) can occur if union is delayed and load cycling continues — this is a late-presenting complication and should prompt evaluation for nonunion rather than being treated as a primary hardware defect.
- In the AFF subset, contralateral fracture and delayed healing can occur independent of fixation quality — always image the contralateral femur when AFF is suspected, and counsel patients on this risk regardless of technique.
- Malunion (varus, rotational malalignment) results from failure to control deforming forces during reduction, not from implant selection alone — most malunions in this zone are reduction failures, not construct failures.
- Structures at risk: the profunda femoris perforating vessels with aggressive lateral dissection or cerclage cable passage (pass cables close to bone, under direct/fluoroscopic control), and the femoral neurovascular bundle medially with any open reduction maneuver on the medial cortex.
- Reduction lost during reaming or nail passage is easy to miss if fluoroscopy is not re-checked at each step — the wide metaphyseal canal in this zone allows the nail to pass through a malreduced fracture without obvious resistance.
- Excessive periosteal stripping during open reduction or aggressive cerclage placement compounds this zone's already-poor intrinsic vascularity and materially raises nonunion risk — favor indirect/percutaneous techniques wherever the pattern allows.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing status is generally more restrictive than for intertrochanteric fixation given the biomechanical demands of this zone; touch-down weight-bearing is more often used for comminuted or marginal-fixation cases, with closer radiographic follow-up (2, 6, 12 weeks) to detect early collapse or varus migration.
- Locking-plate constructs (when nailing is precluded) generally require a longer weight-bearing restriction than nailed constructs.
- Union is typically confirmed by bridging callus by 12–16 weeks; obtain a delayed-union workup (labs, CT) if no radiographic progression by 4–6 months.
- Physiotherapy progression follows the standard fragility-fracture phases: POD0–1 bed mobility/transfers; week 1–2 gait training and hip/knee AROM; week 2–6 progressive resistive exercise; week 6–12 abductor/extensor strengthening; beyond 12 weeks continued strengthening and fall-prevention.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing. High-demand recreational activity (relevant to younger subtrochanteric fracture patients) is generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.
