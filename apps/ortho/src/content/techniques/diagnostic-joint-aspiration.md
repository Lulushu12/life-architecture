## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

Diagnostic aspiration is a distinct decision point from therapeutic injection and should be considered first whenever there is diagnostic uncertainty: effusion of unclear etiology, suspected septic arthritis, suspected crystal arthropathy, or suspected prosthetic joint infection (PJI). It is also indicated purely for symptomatic decompression of a tense, painful effusion, and as a mandatory first step before any therapeutic injection into a joint with an unexplained effusion or a periprosthetic structure.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Overlying cellulitis or skin breakdown at the planned entry site — choose an alternate approach or defer if no safe window of clean skin exists (this is a relative, not absolute, contraindication when septic arthritis itself is suspected — the diagnostic need for aspiration may still outweigh the local skin risk if no alternate site exists, but document the reasoning).
- Uncorrected severe coagulopathy or bleeding diathesis for deep/vascular-adjacent joints (hip) — weigh against the diagnostic urgency; suspected septic arthritis with a coagulopathic patient still generally warrants aspiration with appropriate correction/support rather than deferral.
- Known bacteremia is not a contraindication to aspirating a suspicious joint — the opposite: a joint effusion in a bacteremic patient should be aspirated promptly to rule out hematogenous seeding.
- Inability to obtain adequate image guidance for a hip aspiration when blind technique is unreliable — defer to a setting where fluoroscopy or ultrasound is available rather than attempt a blind hip tap.
- A prosthetic joint aspiration performed while the patient is on antibiotics will have reduced culture sensitivity — not a contraindication to proceed if urgent, but a factor that should be documented and, where feasible, addressed by withholding antibiotics first (see below).

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the clinical question before the procedure: is this purely diagnostic (unexplained effusion, suspected infection, suspected crystal disease) or combined diagnostic-and-therapeutic (aspirate then inject)? This determines whether steroid/anesthetic should be available on the tray but held until fluid is sent.
- Review the patient's antibiotic history — if septic arthritis or PJI is suspected and the patient is not systemically unwell, plan to withhold antibiotics before aspiration whenever feasible (ideally ≥2 weeks off antibiotics) to maximize culture yield.
- Confirm laboratory capability and turnaround for the studies you intend to send: cell count with differential, Gram stain, aerobic/anaerobic culture (with extended culture available for suspected low-virulence organisms in a PJI workup), and polarized light microscopy for crystals — do not send a partial panel and assume you can add studies later from the same sample.
- For a suspected PJI, plan concurrent serum inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR) and, where available, alpha-defensin or leukocyte esterase testing, and know the MSIS/ICM interpretive threshold in advance (acute vs chronic presentation use different WBC/PMN% cutoffs).
- Equipment: choose the largest joint-appropriate bore needle the patient can tolerate (18–20G for knee, 20–22G for smaller joints) — undersized needles clog with fibrin/debris in purulent or hemarthrotic fluid; have a spare needle/syringe ready for exactly this reason.
- For the hip, plan image guidance (fluoroscopy with contrast arthrogram, or ultrasound) in advance — blind hip aspiration is unreliable and should not be attempted.
- Have appropriate collection tubes ready before starting (EDTA tube for cell count, plain sterile tube or blood culture bottles for microbiology, extra tube for crystal analysis) so fluid does not sit in a syringe while tubes are located.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Local anesthesia only (skin wheal with lidocaine, or ethyl chloride spray) — no sedation is typically required. Position and approach are joint-specific and largely mirror the corresponding injection technique:

- **Knee:** supine, knee extended, quadriceps relaxed; superolateral or lateral/medial midpatellar approach.
- **Hip:** supine, hip neutral or slight external rotation; always under fluoroscopic (anterior approach with contrast arthrogram) or ultrasound guidance — never landmark-only.
- **Shoulder (glenohumeral):** seated or lateral, posterior approach, ideally with ultrasound guidance particularly in a stiff or capsulitis-affected joint.
- **Ankle/elbow/wrist/small joints:** standard anatomic landmark entry points per joint, with ultrasound guidance favored whenever a tense or loculated effusion makes landmarks unreliable.

Sterile prep is more rigorous for a diagnostic aspiration than for a routine steroid injection, because a false-positive contaminated culture can trigger an unnecessary infection workup or unnecessary treatment: prep widely with chlorhexidine, allow it to dry fully, use sterile gloves, and avoid re-palpating the entry site after prep unless wearing a sterile glove. No tourniquet is used. Drape as needed to maintain a clean field.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Withhold antibiotics before aspiration whenever feasible (ideally ≥2 weeks off antibiotics) to maximize culture yield, unless the patient is systemically unwell and requires empiric treatment — in that case, aspirate first if it can be done without meaningful delay to treatment.
2. Position the patient and mark the entry site per the joint-specific landmarks above; use image guidance for the hip and for any joint where landmarks are obscured by swelling or body habitus.
3. Prep the skin widely with antiseptic, allow it to dry, and maintain a sterile field for the duration of the aspiration.
4. Anesthetize the skin and subcutaneous tract with local anesthetic, taking care not to contaminate the planned needle path with anesthetic that could dilute or alter the synovial sample if it tracks into the joint.
5. Advance the aspiration needle along the planned trajectory, using a large enough bore (18–20G knee, 20–22G smaller joints) to avoid clogging with fibrin/debris in the event of purulent or hemarthrotic fluid.
6. Aspirate fully — draw as much fluid as can be safely obtained, both for diagnostic completeness and, where relevant, for symptomatic decompression.
7. Distribute the aspirate immediately into the correct collection tubes/media for each intended study: cell count with differential, Gram stain, culture (aerobic/anaerobic, with extended culture requested for suspected low-virulence organisms in a PJI context), and polarized light microscopy for crystals.
8. Send fluid to the laboratory promptly — delayed processing degrades both crystal identification and cell count accuracy.
9. Interpret against the appropriate threshold once results return:
   - Native joint septic arthritis: classic threshold >50,000/mm³ with >90% PMNs strongly suggests septic arthritis, but a lower count (>25,000–50,000/mm³ with high clinical suspicion) should still trigger treatment — sensitivity is imperfect, especially early, with prior antibiotics, or in immunocompromised/prosthetic settings.
   - Prosthetic joint infection (MSIS/ICM criteria): acute (<90 days post-op) WBC >10,000/µL with PMN% >90%; chronic (>90 days) WBC >3,000/µL with PMN% >70–80%. Combine with serum CRP/ESR, alpha-defensin or leukocyte esterase where available, and culture.
   - Crystal arthropathy: gout shows negatively birefringent needle-shaped monosodium urate crystals; CPPD/pseudogout shows positively birefringent or non-birefringent rhomboid calcium pyrophosphate crystals. Aspiration and polarized microscopy remain the gold-standard differentiator.
10. Treat based on the full clinical picture, not a single cutoff, when counts fall in an ambiguous range — correlate with clinical presentation, prior antibiotic exposure, and immune status before ruling infection in or out.
11. If the aspiration was combined with a planned therapeutic injection, proceed to inject only once infection has been reasonably excluded or is not clinically suspected — do not inject steroid into fluid pending culture results if there is any real suspicion of infection.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Have a second syringe and needle open and ready before you start — a clogged needle mid-aspiration in a tense, purulent effusion is common, and switching quickly preserves both patient comfort and sample integrity.
- For a tense knee effusion, milking fluid toward the entry site with your non-dominant hand while aspirating materially improves yield, especially with a loculated or thick effusion.
- Distribute the aspirate into collection tubes at the bedside, not after walking to a different room — every extra minute of the sample sitting in a syringe measurably degrades crystal identification.
- For suspected septic arthritis, send blood cultures at the same visit if the patient has any systemic signs (fever, tachycardia) — bacteremia and septic arthritis frequently co-occur, and paired cultures strengthen the diagnostic picture.
- When aspirating a hip under fluoroscopy, use a small volume of contrast first to confirm intra-articular needle position before committing to a full-volume aspiration attempt — this avoids a wasted, dry pass into peri-capsular tissue.
- If the first aspiration attempt is dry despite a clinically obvious effusion, reposition slightly (redirect a few millimeters, or advance/withdraw a few millimeters) before switching sites entirely — needle tip position just outside the capsule is a more common cause of a dry tap than a truly absent effusion.
- For a suspected PJI where the patient is not acutely unwell, coordinate with the treating team to arrange the ≥2-week antibiotic-free window before aspiration whenever the clinical situation allows it — culture yield drops substantially on active antibiotics, and a false-negative culture can misdirect the entire treatment plan.
- Keep polarized microscopy readily available (or a rapid pathway to it) for any acute mono-arthritis — same-day crystal identification is often what separates an unnecessary admission/antibiotic course from a same-day gout diagnosis and treatment.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not inject a joint with untreated or unexcluded septic arthritis — aspirate and send fluid first whenever there is any diagnostic doubt (new effusion, fever, disproportionate pain, immunocompromise).
- Do not treat presumed gout/pseudogout empirically with steroid in a new acute mono-arthritis without at least attempting aspiration and crystal analysis, given overlap in presentation with septic arthritis.
- A lower synovial WBC count does not exclude infection, especially in early, partially treated (prior antibiotics), or immunocompromised/prosthetic presentations.
- Overlying joint prosthesis with unclear diagnosis of pain — aspirate to rule out PJI before any therapeutic injection into a periprosthetic soft-tissue structure.
- Undersized needles clog with fibrin/debris in purulent or hemarthrotic fluid — use the largest joint-appropriate bore the patient can tolerate.
- Blind hip aspiration is unreliable — always use image guidance for the hip.
- A dry tap does not rule out an effusion — before abandoning the attempt, reposition the needle slightly rather than immediately concluding no fluid is present; a loculated or viscous effusion can require repositioning or a larger-bore needle.
- Delayed sample processing is a silent source of false-negative crystal analysis and inaccurate cell counts — treat "send fluid promptly" as an operational requirement, not a suggestion.

## Postoperative Protocol

Not applicable in the therapeutic sense — this is a diagnostic procedure. Manage the underlying condition per the aspiration result (e.g., proceed to septic arthritis treatment pathway, crystal arthropathy management, or PJI workup) rather than following a generic post-injection protocol. If a therapeutic agent was co-administered after infection was reasonably excluded, follow the postoperative protocol for that specific agent (corticosteroid, HA, PRP, etc.).
