## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Symptomatic cavovarus foot deformity (pain, recurrent lateral ankle instability/sprains, callosities under the first and fifth metatarsal heads, or progressive deformity) that has failed bracing, orthotic accommodation, and footwear modification.
- Flexible, forefoot-driven deformity (Coleman block corrects to neutral/valgus) causing recurrent sprains or overload symptoms.
- Fixed hindfoot varus with chronic lateral instability or overload.
- Rigid or arthritic deformity with pain and functional limitation despite bracing.
- Progressive neurologic cavovarus (e.g., Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease) with functional decline, where surgical stabilization can slow progression of deformity and improve bracing tolerance even if the underlying neuropathy is not curable.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active or unaddressed progressive neurologic disease without preoperative neurology involvement and patient counseling on the likelihood of recurrence — proceeding without this discussion sets unrealistic expectations.
- Uninvestigated new or asymmetric adult-onset cavovarus — obtain MRI of the neuraxis (spinal cord/tethered cord, tumor) before committing to a "idiopathic" surgical plan.
- Significant peripheral vascular disease or uncontrolled diabetes with neuropathy (poor wound-healing capacity for multiple concurrent incisions).
- Severe, non-correctable arthritic deformity in a low-demand or medically frail patient may be better served by bracing/accommodative footwear than a major reconstruction or triple arthrodesis.
- Active infection or skin compromise over planned incisions.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Clinical assessment**: Coleman block test (block height typically 2.5–4 cm, placed under the lateral foot from heel to just proximal to the first metatarsal head, allowing the first ray to hang free medially) to determine flexibility of the hindfoot and whether the deformity is forefoot- or hindfoot-driven — this single test is the primary branch point for the entire operative plan.
- **Neurologic workup**: full neurologic exam (deep tendon reflexes, intrinsic hand muscle wasting, family history/exam of relatives), EMG/nerve conduction studies, and spine/neuraxis MRI when the diagnosis is not clearly established, the presentation is asymmetric, or there is any atypical feature — roughly 20–30% of adult cavovarus has an identifiable neurologic cause (CMT1A most common).
- **Imaging**: standing AP/lateral foot and ankle radiographs (Meary's angle, calcaneal pitch), hindfoot alignment (Saltzman/long axial) view to quantify varus, weightbearing CT where available for 3D deformity mapping in complex or revision cases; assess ankle mortise for varus tilt/early arthritis, since chronic varus overload can produce medial ankle joint degeneration.
- **Tendon/muscle mapping**: manual muscle testing of peroneus longus vs. brevis, tibialis anterior vs. posterior, and toe extensors/flexors to plan which transfers are needed; document any existing clawing of the toes and its flexibility.
- **Implant/equipment planning**: have both a peroneal tendon transfer set and osteotomy fixation (staples, screws, or plate for closing-wedge first metatarsal osteotomy; screws or plate for Dwyer/lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy) available; for triple arthrodesis, have a full fusion set (staples, screws, and/or plates) and consider structural bone graft for void filling after correction of severe deformity.
- **Case-specific planning**: sequence multiple procedures mentally before incision (typically hindfoot osteotomy first, then tendon transfers, then forefoot/toe procedures) and plan tourniquet time accordingly, as these are frequently multi-procedure cases.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic nerve block combined with general or spinal anesthesia/sedation is standard; the block provides excellent early analgesia given the multiple incisions typical of these cases.
- **Positioning**: supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to internally rotate the leg and improve access to the lateral hindfoot and foot; the leg is prepped free to allow intraoperative manipulation for Coleman-block-style dynamic assessment and tendon tensioning. Some surgeons reposition or use a lateral decubitus/bump for isolated Dwyer osteotomy exposure.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet, exsanguinated before inflation; given the length and multi-step nature of these cases, monitor tourniquet time and consider deflation/re-inflation if exceeding 90–120 minutes.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned for AP, lateral, and calcaneal axial views without repositioning the leg — needed for osteotomy wedge planning, hardware trajectory, and final alignment checks.
- **Landmarks**: mark the peroneal tendons posterior to the fibula, the sural nerve course, the planned calcaneal osteotomy incision (oblique, posterior to the peroneal tendons and sural nerve), and the first metatarsal osteotomy site before prepping.
- **Draping**: free-drape the leg circumferentially to allow full manipulation of the hindfoot and forefoot intraoperatively for repeated alignment checks after each stage of correction.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**1. Flexible, forefoot-driven deformity (Coleman block corrects)**
1. Begin with the plantar fascia release if a tight/contracted fascia is contributing to the cavus arch — release percutaneously or through a small medial incision at the calcaneal origin, taking care to preserve enough fascia to avoid iatrogenic flatfoot.
2. Perform the peroneus longus-to-brevis transfer: expose both tendons posterior to the fibula, tenodese or transfer the longus into the brevis proximal to the peroneal tubercle, removing the deforming plantarflexion force on the first ray while augmenting hindfoot eversion strength.
3. Perform a dorsiflexion (closing-wedge) osteotomy of the first metatarsal base to correct the fixed plantarflexed first ray — resect a dorsally based wedge, close it under fluoroscopic guidance, and fix with a plate or staple, confirming a level forefoot relative to the hindfoot.
4. In neurologic cases (CMT), add tibialis posterior-to-anterior transfer (or split transfer) for dorsiflexor weakness/foot drop, and correct clawing of the toes via flexor-to-extensor (Jones) transfer with interphalangeal fusion as needed — perform these in the same setting as the bony correction, not staged, since deforming forces left unaddressed will drive recurrence.
5. Reassess hindfoot alignment intraoperatively after forefoot correction — occasionally residual mild varus becomes apparent only once the plantarflexed first ray is corrected; be prepared to add a limited calcaneal procedure if needed.

**2. Fixed hindfoot varus (Coleman block does not correct)**
1. Perform all forefoot-driven steps above as indicated, then proceed to the calcaneal osteotomy.
2. Approach the calcaneus through an oblique lateral incision positioned to stay posterior to the peroneal tendons and away from the sural nerve; carry dissection directly to bone with minimal soft-tissue stripping.
3. Perform a lateralizing (for hindfoot varus without leg-length/height issues) or Dwyer closing-wedge (lateral-based wedge resection, useful when varus is combined with excessive heel height) calcaneal osteotomy under fluoroscopic guidance; shift/rotate the tuberosity fragment to correct the mechanical axis, then provisionally pin.
4. Fix the osteotomy with one or two large cannulated screws (or a plate for less stable patterns), confirming position on lateral and axial fluoroscopy before final tightening.
5. If chronic lateral ankle instability coexists (common given the varus-driven inversion sprain pattern), perform a lateral ligament reconstruction (modified Broström ± augmentation) in the same setting, after bony correction has re-established a neutral platform for the ligament repair to act on.

**3. Rigid/arthritic or severe/neglected deformity**
1. Approach via a standard lateral (Ollier or extended lateral) incision for triple arthrodesis, exposing the subtalar, talonavicular, and calcaneocuboid joints.
2. Denude all three joint surfaces of cartilage down to bleeding subchondral bone, taking care to preserve the overall foot shape/height while removing enough bone to correct the varus and cavus components.
3. Reduce the foot into a corrected, plantigrade position (neutral to slightly valgus hindfoot, corrected cavus), provisionally pin, and confirm alignment clinically (looking at the heel from behind) and fluoroscopically.
4. Fix each joint with screws (typically 2 per joint) placed in a sequence that maintains reduction (commonly talonavicular first, then subtalar, then calcaneocuboid, though sequence varies by surgeon preference); bone graft any residual voids from wedge resection.
5. Address residual forefoot deformity with a concurrent first metatarsal or midfoot osteotomy as needed to achieve a fully plantigrade foot, and perform soft-tissue balancing (tendon transfers as above) concurrently — arthrodesis alone does not correct a muscular imbalance and residual deforming force will stress the fusion or produce compensatory deformity elsewhere.
6. Close in layers with meticulous hemostasis; consider a drain given the extensive dissection.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Perform the Coleman block test yourself intraoperatively (or immediately preoperatively) if there is any diagnostic ambiguity — subtle correction can be missed by an inexperienced observer and changes the entire operative plan.
- When transferring peroneus longus to brevis, tenodese well proximal to the peroneal tubercle so the transfer does not impinge or sublux around the fibular groove postoperatively.
- Correct the deforming muscular imbalance (tendon transfer) in the same operative setting as bony correction in every neurologic cavovarus case — staging this incorrectly (bone first, tendons later) invites early recurrence before the second stage.
- In Dwyer osteotomy, resect a conservative lateral wedge first and check correction on fluoroscopy before committing to a larger resection — overcorrection to valgus is more common than under-correction and harder to salvage.
- Keep the calcaneal osteotomy incision posterior to the peroneal tendons and mark the sural nerve preoperatively; the nerve's superficial course over the lateral calcaneus makes it vulnerable to both the incision and retraction.
- When reducing a triple arthrodesis in severe cavovarus, resist the temptation to over-correct into valgus to "guarantee" clearing the varus — mild residual heel varus is easier to accommodate with a brace than an overcorrected valgus foot, which shifts load medially and predisposes to deltoid/medial ankle overload.
- Address equinus (test with Silfverskiöld) concurrently if present — an unaddressed gastrocnemius or gastroc-soleus contracture will perpetuate forefoot-driven cavus even after bony correction.
- For claw toe correction, confirm whether the deformity is flexible (flexor-to-extensor transfer suffices) or fixed (requires interphalangeal fusion) — treating a fixed deformity with soft tissue transfer alone under-corrects it.
- Counsel patients with CMT preoperatively that soft-tissue balancing durability is finite as the underlying neuropathy progresses — this sets realistic long-term expectations and supports a plan for indefinite bracing.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Recurrence of deformity if the underlying muscular imbalance or progressive neurologic disease (CMT) is not addressed alongside bony correction — the single most common reason for failure in neurologic cavovarus.
- Overcorrection to valgus is a recognized risk of Dwyer/lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy — check alignment on fluoroscopy before final fixation, not just clinically.
- Peroneal tendon injury or subluxation is a technical risk during transfer — protect the tendons throughout dissection and confirm smooth gliding in the fibular groove before closure.
- Persistent lateral instability results if ligament reconstruction is omitted when chronic instability was present preoperatively.
- Sural nerve injury/neuroma from calcaneal osteotomy incision placement or retraction.
- Undercorrection of the first ray plantarflexion (inadequate wedge resection) leaves residual forefoot-driven varus even after an otherwise well-executed hindfoot correction.
- In triple arthrodesis for severe deformity, nonunion risk is elevated relative to arthrodesis for non-deformity indications given the larger bone resection and correction forces involved — bone graft and rigid fixation are important mitigants.
- Progressive neurologic disease (CMT) can lead to recurrent deformity or new deforming forces years after an initially successful reconstruction — lifelong surveillance and bracing readiness should be part of the counseling from the outset.

## Postoperative Protocol

Splint NWB 2 weeks; cast/boot NWB to 6 weeks pending osteotomy consolidation; progressive weightbearing 6–10 weeks; formal PT for peroneal/eversion strengthening and proprioceptive/balance training (critical given the associated chronic instability); bracing for residual weakness in neurologic (CMT) cases often continues indefinitely; return to sport 4–6 months.

General return-to-work/sport principles: sedentary work resumes earliest (often 2–6 weeks post-op); standing/walking-intensive work requires solid union/wound healing plus demonstrated weightbearing tolerance; return to impact sport requires radiographic union, full painless ROM, and strength ≥85–90% of the contralateral limb on functional testing.
