## Indications

Cavovarus foot deformity — flexible, forefoot-driven, or fixed hindfoot varus, or rigid/arthritic — that has failed bracing/orthotic/footwear modification. Procedure selection is driven by the Coleman block test result and by whether the deformity is idiopathic, progressive/neurologic (e.g., Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease), or rigid/arthritic. Neurologic screening (and counseling on prognosis/durability of soft-tissue balancing) is performed before or at the time of surgical planning in any adult with new or asymmetric cavovarus.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed at the approach/room-setup level in the source material; procedure selection by Coleman block result and deformity flexibility is the operative decision point (see Steps).

## Steps

1. **Flexible, forefoot-driven (Coleman block corrects)**: peroneus longus-to-brevis transfer (removes the deforming force and augments eversion); dorsiflexion (closing-wedge) osteotomy of the first metatarsal to correct the plantarflexed ray; plantar fascia release for a tight/contracted fascia; tibialis anterior/posterior transfer for muscle imbalance in neurologic cases (e.g., CMT); claw toe correction (flexor-to-extensor transfer, interphalangeal fusion) as needed.
2. **Fixed hindfoot varus (Coleman block does not correct)**: add lateralizing or Dwyer closing-wedge calcaneal osteotomy to the forefoot procedures above; add lateral ligament reconstruction if chronic instability coexists (common given the varus-driven inversion sprain pattern).
3. **Rigid/arthritic or severe/neglected deformity**: triple arthrodesis with concurrent soft-tissue balancing, occasionally combined with a first metatarsal or midfoot osteotomy for residual forefoot deformity.

## Pitfalls

- Always correct the deforming muscular imbalance (tendon transfer) in the same setting as bony correction in neurologic cavovarus (e.g., CMT), or the deformity will recur.
- Screen and counsel for progressive underlying neurologic disease preoperatively — it affects both prognosis and the durability of soft-tissue balancing.
- Overcorrection to valgus is a recognized risk of Dwyer/lateralizing calcaneal osteotomy.
- Peroneal tendon injury/subluxation is a technical risk during transfer.
- Persistent lateral instability results if ligament reconstruction is omitted when indicated.

## Post-op protocol

Splint NWB 2 weeks; cast/boot NWB to 6 weeks pending osteotomy consolidation; progressive weightbearing 6–10 weeks; formal PT for peroneal/eversion strengthening and proprioceptive/balance training (critical given the associated chronic instability); bracing for residual weakness in neurologic (CMT) cases often continues indefinitely; return to sport 4–6 months.

General return-to-work/sport principles: sedentary work resumes earliest (often 2–6 weeks post-op); standing/walking-intensive work requires solid union/wound healing plus demonstrated weightbearing tolerance; return to impact sport requires radiographic union, full painless ROM, and strength ≥85–90% of the contralateral limb on functional testing.
