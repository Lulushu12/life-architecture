## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- The great majority of closed and Gustilo I–IIIA open tibial shaft fractures — reamed intramedullary nailing is the standard of care.
- Proximal- and distal-third fractures, where the suprapatellar semi-extended position and blocking screws allow reliable control of alignment that the infrapatellar approach struggles to achieve.
- Simple, wedge, and complex/comminuted diaphyseal patterns (AO/OTA 42-A/B/C) without significant articular extension.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Fractures with significant articular extension into the plateau or plafond — better served by a submuscular/bridge plate.
- Very distal or very proximal metaphyseal fractures where nail fixation would provide inadequate purchase.
- Active infection at the planned entry site or along the intended canal.
- Skeletally immature patients with open physes — the proximal tibial physis is at risk from a trans-physeal starting point (relative; use physeal-sparing techniques or alternative fixation in this population).
- Severe ipsilateral knee ligamentous instability or preexisting patellofemoral arthritis may make semi-extended suprapatellar positioning or portal placement less favorable (relative — consider infrapatellar technique in this scenario).
- Segmental bone loss or heavy contamination — favor a circular/hybrid external fixator with staged bone transport or Masquelet technique over primary nailing.

## Preoperative Planning

- Full-length AP and lateral radiographs of the tibia/fibula including the knee and ankle joints, with calibration markers for accurate length and diameter templating.
- CT if there is any concern for articular extension not apparent on plain films.
- Nail templating: length from the knee joint line to the plafond referenced against the contralateral limb if length is in question; diameter typically 8–11 mm reamed for adults, based on isthmus width on the AP/lateral radiographs.
- Anticipate blocking (Poller) screw positions for proximal- or distal-third fractures during preoperative planning — mark the concavity of the anticipated deformity on the templated films.
- Suprapatellar-specific instrumentation: confirm availability of the protective sleeve/cannula system that shields the patellofemoral articular cartilage during guidewire passage, reaming, and nail insertion.
- Equipment list: radiolucent table, fluoroscopy unit capable of imaging the full tibia without excessive repositioning, tibial nail system with a suprapatellar-compatible aiming jig, flexible reamers, provisional fixation instruments (Schanz pins, femoral distractor as backup for comminuted patterns).
- For open fractures: plan irrigation/debridement equipment, antibiotic bead/cement options for contaminated wounds, and — for Gustilo IIIB/IIIC — a staged plan coordinated with plastic surgery for soft-tissue coverage within roughly 5–10 days.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or spinal anesthesia is typical; a regional block (popliteal sciatic + saphenous) is useful for postoperative analgesia.
- Supine on a radiolucent table with the foot of the table free, or with a triangle/bump placed under the distal thigh to support the knee in 10–20° of flexion for the suprapatellar semi-extended position.
- A bump under the ipsilateral hip brings the limb to neutral rotation.
- Ensure the fluoroscopy unit can access the full length of the tibia — from the suprapatellar starting point to the ankle — with minimal repositioning; test this before draping.
- Tourniquet placed high on the thigh but typically left uninflated during nailing, given the need for prolonged fluoroscopic visualization and concern for reaming-related thermal effects.
- Prep and drape the entire leg circumferentially, thigh to toes, to allow full knee flexion/extension and rotational comparison against the contralateral limb (leave the contralateral leg accessible for direct comparison, or prep it free).
- Mark surface landmarks before incision: patellar borders, patellar tendon, tibial tubercle, the planned suprapatellar portal (just proximal to the superior pole of the patella, midline or slightly medial per the specific nail system), and the fracture location.
- Confirm true AP and lateral fluoroscopic views of both the knee and ankle can be obtained before final draping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Reduce the fracture provisionally by closed manipulation and longitudinal traction; confirm length and alignment on fluoroscopy.
2. Position the knee in 10–20° of flexion on the bump/triangle. Make a small incision just proximal to the superior pole of the patella, in the midline or slightly medial depending on the system.
3. Insert the protective sleeve/trocar system through the quadriceps tendon into the suprapatellar pouch, docking it against the trochlear groove to shield the patellofemoral cartilage from subsequent instrumentation.
4. **Obtain the starting point** under fluoroscopy: lateral to the tibial spine on the AP view, at the anterior articular margin (junction of the anterior tibial cortex and the articular surface) on the lateral view — confirm on both views before advancing the guide pin.
5. Advance the guide pin into the proximal fragment, confirm position on AP and lateral fluoroscopy, then pass it across the fracture site into the distal fragment, restoring the reduction as the pin crosses.
6. **Place blocking (Poller) screws** for proximal- or distal-third fractures, either before or after guide-pin passage depending on stability: for a stable but malaligned fracture, place the screw with the nail temporarily removed; for an unstable fracture, drill the screw hole with the nail in place while manually holding the correction. Site the screw on the concave side of the deformity, perpendicular to the plane of deformity, with adequate clearance from the planned nail path and the fracture site — this narrows the canal and centers the nail, preventing translation into the flared metaphyseal segment.
7. Ream sequentially in 0.5–1 mm increments to roughly 1–1.5 mm greater than the planned nail diameter, reassessing reduction and rotation between passes; stop advancing a given reamer once resistance/"chatter" increases rather than forcing through.
8. Select nail length and diameter, then insert the nail over the guidewire under fluoroscopic guidance, advancing slowly while reduction is maintained manually, with reduction clamps, or via the blocking screws.
9. Confirm the nail is seated appropriately proximally (not proud into the joint) and distally (adequate purchase, not penetrating the far cortex).
10. Perform proximal interlocking, typically 2 screws, using the jig.
11. Perform distal interlocking using either the jig or a free-hand "perfect circles" fluoroscopic technique on the lateral view, typically 2 screws in orthogonal or near-orthogonal planes.
12. Reassess length, rotation, and alignment before final tightening — clinically compare the limb's rotational profile (patella-tibial tuberosity relationship or malleolar axis) against the contralateral limb, and confirm cortical diameter matching on fluoroscopy.
13. Remove the protective sleeve and inspect the patellofemoral joint/portal for debris; irrigate the suprapatellar pouch thoroughly.
14. Close the quadriceps tendon/retinaculum, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers at the portal incision(s).
15. For open fractures being definitively nailed at the index procedure, complete formal irrigation and debridement before nail passage; for staged cases, perform initial debridement and temporary stabilization first, returning for definitive nailing per the open-fracture protocol.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Dock the protective sleeve firmly against the trochlear groove before passing any instrument through the suprapatellar pouch — free-hand instrumentation without the sleeve risks direct cartilage gouging.
- Confirm the starting point on both AP and lateral views before committing to reaming — an errant starting point is the single most correctable source of malreduction in proximal-third fractures.
- For proximal-third fractures, place at least one blocking screw (coronal and/or sagittal plane) before reaming to preempt the classic apex-anterior, valgus malreduction rather than trying to correct it afterward.
- Use manual traction/counter-traction, a femoral distractor, or provisional clamps for comminuted or segmental fractures where reduction cannot be held manually during nail passage.
- Check rotation by more than one method before final locking: compare the patella-tibial tuberosity relationship or malleolar axis to the contralateral limb clinically, and confirm cortical diameter matching on fluoroscopy — either method alone can be misleading.
- For distal-third fractures, place two or more blocking screws in orthogonal planes within the distal fragment to control the wide metaphyseal canal and prevent nail translation.
- Irrigate the suprapatellar pouch thoroughly at closure — retained reaming debris in the joint is implicated in postoperative effusion and knee pain.
- A shortened-fluted-tip drill bit for blocking screw pilot holes reduces the risk of scoring or damaging the nail if the screw trajectory is close to the planned nail path.
- Recent comparative series (2024–2025) describing an extra-articular lateral parapatellar semi-extended variant suggest it may reduce patellofemoral joint violation and postoperative knee pain relative to the standard transtendinous suprapatellar portal in some cohorts — a technical variant worth knowing about, though not yet established as a standard-of-care replacement for the conventional suprapatellar portal.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Malunion (apex-anterior/valgus deformity) is a particular risk of proximal- and distal-third shaft fractures nailed via the infrapatellar approach — a primary reason the suprapatellar semi-extended technique has gained favor.
- Anterior knee pain is a recognized morbidity of both infrapatellar and suprapatellar nailing, though generally lower with suprapatellar technique per pooled 2023–2025 meta-analytic VAS/Lysholm/Kujala data — not eliminated.
- Suprapatellar nailing carries a lower rate of acute compartment syndrome and fasciotomy compared with infrapatellar nailing across multiple 2023–2025 systematic reviews and meta-analyses, in addition to shorter operative/fluoroscopy time and at least equivalent union rates.
- Cartilage injury within the suprapatellar pouch from unprotected or malpositioned instrumentation has been documented on arthroscopic follow-up in a minority of suprapatellar cases even when a protective sleeve is used — technique and sleeve seating matter.
- An errant guide pin or drill can skive off the tibial spine into the joint if the starting point is positioned too medially or laterally — reconfirm the AP starting point before reaming, not just the lateral.
- Nonunion/delayed union is more common with comminuted (AO/OTA 42-C) shaft fractures, open fractures, unreamed nailing, and smoking — addressed with exchange reamed nailing, dynamization, plating, or bone grafting/Masquelet technique for segmental defects.
- Compartment syndrome is the must-not-miss complication most feared with tibial shaft fractures (reported incidence 2–9%) — pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, and paresthesia are the reliable early signs; do not wait for pulselessness or paralysis. Delayed fasciotomy beyond approximately 6 hours of ischemia substantially worsens the likelihood of permanent muscle/nerve damage.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** weight-bearing as tolerated is standard for most statically locked nails in simple-to-moderately comminuted patterns — a major advantage of nailing over plating/bracing; ankle and knee ROM immediately; wound checks.
- **2–6 weeks:** progressive weight-bearing to full as tolerated by pain and radiographic callus; gait training; address any knee pain from the suprapatellar/infrapatellar approach with targeted quadriceps/patellofemoral rehab.
- **6–12 weeks:** most patients achieve radiographic union in this window for simple fracture patterns (AO/OTA 42-A); complex/comminuted (42-C) patterns and open fractures take longer, often 4–6+ months.
- **Return to work:** desk work 2–4 weeks, manual labor typically 3–6 months pending union.
- **Return to sport:** running/pivoting sport generally deferred until solid radiographic union (bridging callus on 3+ cortices) and pain-free single-leg hop testing, commonly 4–6 months, longer for open/complex fractures.
