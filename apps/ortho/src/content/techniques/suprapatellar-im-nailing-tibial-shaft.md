## Indications

Reamed intramedullary nailing is the standard of care for the great majority of closed and Gustilo I–IIIA open tibial shaft fractures. Fractures with significant articular extension into the plateau or plafond, or very distal/proximal metaphyseal fractures where nail fixation would provide inadequate purchase, are better served by a submuscular/bridge plate instead.

## Positioning & setup

The suprapatellar semi-extended approach allows nailing with the knee in 10–20° of flexion, versus the forced hyperflexion required for the traditional infrapatellar approach. This is now preferred for most proximal- and mid-shaft fractures because it corrects the deforming apex-anterior/valgus tendency seen in proximal-third fractures and improves fluoroscopic visualization and starting-point accuracy. Infrapatellar nailing remains a reasonable choice for very distal or straightforward mid-shaft fractures in surgeons with greater comfort in that position, and avoids violating the suprapatellar pouch/patellofemoral joint (a theoretical concern for later patellofemoral chondral wear not yet clearly borne out in follow-up data).

## Steps

1. **Obtain an accurate starting point** — lateral to the tibial spine on the AP view, at the anterior articular margin on the lateral view — or use the semi-extended midline approach; confirm on both fluoroscopic views before reaming.
2. **Ream the canal.** Reaming (vs. unreamed) reduces nonunion risk in closed fractures (number needed to treat roughly 5–14 to prevent one nonunion, pooled trial data including SPRINT) without a clinically meaningful difference for open fractures.
3. **Use blocking (Poller) screws** for proximal- and distal-third fractures to control the reduction and prevent the nail from translating into a wide metaphyseal canal.
4. **Restore length, rotation, and alignment before final locking** — clinical rotational check against the contralateral limb, fluoroscopic cortical diameter matching.
5. **For open fractures (Gustilo I–IIIC):** perform thorough irrigation and debridement (formal debridement can reasonably occur within a broader window than the historic "6-hour rule," though earlier debridement remains preferred when feasible) and give early appropriate antibiotics (first dose within 60–75 minutes; cefazolin for all grades, add an aminoglycoside for Gustilo III, add penicillin for farmyard/fecal contamination).
6. **For severe open fractures (Gustilo IIIB/IIIC):** stage the plan — initial debridement plus temporary spanning external fixation or a provisional nail, staged soft-tissue coverage (local or free flap) within roughly 5–10 days coordinated with plastic surgery, and definitive fixation timed to coverage. Recent (2025) retrospective data suggest placing a definitive nail prior to flap coverage may not meaningfully increase complication rates compared with staged nailing after coverage — an evolving area where single-center data is outpacing consensus guidance.
7. **For segmental bone loss or heavy contamination**, favor a circular/hybrid external fixator (with subsequent bone transport or Masquelet induced-membrane technique for defect reconstruction) over primary nailing.

## Pitfalls

- Malunion (apex-anterior/valgus deformity) is a particular risk of proximal- and distal-third shaft fractures nailed via the infrapatellar approach — a primary reason the suprapatellar semi-extended technique has gained favor.
- Anterior knee pain is a recognized morbidity of both infrapatellar and suprapatellar nailing, though generally lower with suprapatellar technique per pooled 2023–2025 meta-analytic VAS/Lysholm/Kujala data — not eliminated.
- Suprapatellar nailing carries a lower rate of acute compartment syndrome and fasciotomy compared with infrapatellar nailing across multiple 2023–2025 systematic reviews and meta-analyses, in addition to shorter operative/fluoroscopy time and at least equivalent union rates.
- Nonunion/delayed union is more common with comminuted (AO/OTA 42-C) shaft fractures, open fractures, unreamed nailing, and smoking — addressed with exchange reamed nailing, dynamization, plating, or bone grafting/Masquelet technique for segmental defects.
- Compartment syndrome is the must-not-miss complication most feared with tibial shaft fractures (reported incidence 2–9%) — pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, and paresthesia are the reliable early signs; do not wait for pulselessness or paralysis. Delayed fasciotomy beyond approximately 6 hours of ischemia substantially worsens the likelihood of permanent muscle/nerve damage.

## Post-op protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** weight-bearing as tolerated is standard for most statically locked nails in simple-to-moderately comminuted patterns — a major advantage of nailing over plating/bracing; ankle and knee ROM immediately; wound checks.
- **2–6 weeks:** progressive weight-bearing to full as tolerated by pain and radiographic callus; gait training; address any knee pain from the suprapatellar/infrapatellar approach with targeted quadriceps/patellofemoral rehab.
- **6–12 weeks:** most patients achieve radiographic union in this window for simple fracture patterns (AO/OTA 42-A); complex/comminuted (42-C) patterns and open fractures take longer, often 4–6+ months.
- **Return to work:** desk work 2–4 weeks, manual labor typically 3–6 months pending union.
- **Return to sport:** running/pivoting sport generally deferred until solid radiographic union (bridging callus on 3+ cortices) and pain-free single-leg hop testing, commonly 4–6 months, longer for open/complex fractures.
