## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Displacement >2 mm on CT (the operative threshold is a CT, not plain-film, measurement) in a transitional ankle fracture (Tillaux or triplane) occurring during the roughly 12–18-month window of asymmetric distal tibial physeal closure around ages 12–14.
- Evidence of syndesmotic diastasis on imaging.
- A fully transverse triplane fracture with a large metaphyseal component, which may additionally need a metaphyseal screw or plate to control that fragment in addition to epiphyseal fixation.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Displacement ≤2 mm on CT — manage with a non-weight-bearing cast for 4–6 weeks; surgery adds risk without a clear articular-incongruity benefit at this displacement.
- Severe soft-tissue swelling/fracture blisters precluding safe percutaneous or open work — consider a brief period of splinting/elevation to allow the soft-tissue envelope to settle before proceeding, as is standard for any peri-articular lower-extremity fracture with significant swelling.
- Grossly comminuted metaphyseal or epiphyseal fragmentation beyond what percutaneous screw fixation can control — plan for a more extensile open approach and/or plate fixation from the outset.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: CT with 1–2 mm cuts is the imaging standard for surgical planning — it more accurately quantifies articular gap/step than plain film (the 2 mm operative threshold is specifically a CT measurement, not a plain-radiograph one) and defines fragment number/orientation (a triplane fracture may present as 2, 3, or 4 fragments depending on the plane of imaging and the rotational forces involved). Review the CT in all three planes before the case to build a mental 3D model of the fracture — triplane fractures are notoriously easy to under-appreciate on a single axial or coronal cut alone.
- **Implant selection**: cannulated partially-threaded epiphyseal screws (typically 3.5–4.5 mm in adolescents) sized to achieve interfragmentary compression across the epiphyseal fragment while sitting parallel to the residual open physis; have a metaphyseal screw or small plate set available as a backup for triplane fractures with a large, unstable metaphyseal fragment.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: image intensifier (biplanar capability strongly preferred given the need to confirm screw trajectory relative to both the joint surface and the physis in multiple planes), cannulated screw system with guide wires, a pointed reduction clamp or percutaneous reduction instruments (bone hook, Schanz pin as a joystick) for the epiphyseal fragment, and standard ORIF instrumentation on standby in case percutaneous reduction is inadequate.
- **Case-specific planning**: for a Tillaux fracture, plan the screw trajectory in the coronal/axial plane to run from anterolateral to posteromedial across the fragment, parallel to the joint surface and the still-open portion of the physis. For a triplane fracture, plan for at least two implants (an epiphyseal screw across the sagittal component, and a metaphyseal screw or plate across the coronal/metaphyseal component) based on the CT-defined fragment geometry.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia (with or without a popliteal/sciatic regional block for postoperative analgesia) is standard.
- **Positioning**: supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to internally rotate the leg and present the ankle in a neutral, accessible position, or with the leg free to allow intraoperative manipulation for reduction.
- **Table setup**: a radiolucent table with the foot of the bed accessible for the C-arm to obtain true AP, mortise, and lateral ankle views; a bump or leg holder to support the leg in the desired rotation without an assistant holding it for the entire case.
- **Tourniquet**: a thigh tourniquet is standard, inflated for the case.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire distal leg, ankle, and foot circumferentially to allow full visualization of anterolateral, medial, and posterior aspects of the ankle as needed, and to allow intraoperative stress/rotation testing.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the anterior tibial joint line, the anterior-inferior tibiofibular ligament region (Chaput/Tillaux fragment location), the medial malleolus, and the planned percutaneous screw start points before incision, referencing the preoperative CT fragment map.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: confirm the C-arm can obtain a true mortise view (15–20° internal rotation) in addition to standard AP and lateral views without repositioning the leg — the mortise view is essential for judging both articular reduction and physeal-parallel screw trajectory in this procedure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm fracture pattern and displacement under fluoroscopy**: obtain AP, mortise, and lateral views to correlate with the preoperative CT and confirm the direction and magnitude of displacement before making any incision.
2. **Attempt closed reduction first**: with the leg supported, apply direct manual pressure or internal rotation of the foot (for a Tillaux fragment, which is pulled by the anterior-inferior tibiofibular ligament) to reduce the epiphyseal fragment, checking reduction under fluoroscopy.
3. **Percutaneous reduction assist (if closed manipulation is inadequate)**: place a percutaneous pointed reduction clamp or a small Schanz pin/K-wire as a joystick into the displaced fragment to manipulate it directly under fluoroscopic control, or make a small (1–2 cm) mini-open incision over the fragment to allow direct palpation/visualization of the articular reduction if percutaneous maneuvers are not achieving an acceptable (≤2 mm) reduction.
4. **Confirm articular reduction**: assess the joint surface directly (through the mini-open window, if used) and/or with fluoroscopic imaging in multiple planes — do not rely on a single AP view, since articular step-off is easy to under-appreciate without a true mortise and lateral confirmation.
5. **Provisional fixation**: once reduction is confirmed, place a guide wire from an anterolateral (for a Tillaux fragment) or fragment-specific start point across the epiphysis, oriented parallel to the residual open portion of the physis — plan the trajectory using the preoperative CT to identify where the physis remains open versus closed, and deliberately avoid crossing an open portion of physis whenever the trajectory allows.
6. **Confirm guide wire position on biplanar fluoroscopy**: check AP/mortise and lateral views to confirm the wire is (a) across the fracture with good purchase in both fragments, (b) parallel to and clear of the open physis, and (c) not intra-articular.
7. **Place the epiphyseal screw**: drill over the guide wire as needed and place a partially-threaded cannulated screw, with the threads fully past the fracture line to achieve interfragmentary compression across the epiphysis; use a washer if the fragment is thin.
8. **Address the metaphyseal component (triplane fractures with a large metaphyseal fragment)**: place a second screw or a small plate across the metaphyseal fracture line, using a separate trajectory from the epiphyseal screw, to independently control that fragment — do not rely on the epiphyseal screw alone to control a large, separately-mobile metaphyseal piece.
9. **Final reduction and fixation check**: obtain AP, mortise, and lateral fluoroscopic views to confirm anatomic (≤2 mm) articular reduction is maintained after all fixation is placed, and confirm no screw crosses the open physis unnecessarily.
10. **Assess syndesmotic stability**: if there was diastasis on preoperative imaging, perform an intraoperative stress test (external rotation/Cotton test) after fixation to confirm the syndesmosis is stable; if instability persists, address with a syndesmotic screw or suture-button construct as a separate consideration.
11. **Closure**: close any mini-open incision in layers; percutaneous screw sites require only skin closure.
12. **Splinting**: apply a well-padded non-weight-bearing splint or cast before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Build a genuine 3D mental model of the fracture from the preoperative CT before scrubbing in — triplane fractures in particular are frequently more complex than they appear on a single cut, and knowing exactly where each fragment sits and where the physis remains open drives both your incision planning and screw trajectory.
- For a Tillaux fragment, gentle internal rotation of the foot is often the single most effective closed reduction maneuver, since it directly reverses the external-rotation/anterior-inferior tibiofibular ligament traction mechanism that displaced the fragment.
- Do not settle for an AP-only confirmation of reduction — get a true mortise view before committing to definitive fixation, since a fragment that looks reduced on AP can still have a persistent step-off visible only on the mortise or lateral projection.
- When there is any question about whether percutaneous reduction has achieved true articular congruity, a small (1–2 cm) mini-open window over the fragment for direct visual/tactile confirmation is a low-morbidity way to avoid fixing a subtly malreduced articular surface — do not be reluctant to open a small window rather than accept an uncertain percutaneous reduction.
- Plan your screw trajectory specifically to run parallel to, and clear of, any residual open physis — use the CT to identify exactly where the physis remains open (it closes centrally first, then medially, then anterolaterally/Chaput-Tillaux last), since this determines where a trajectory can safely avoid it.
- For triplane fractures, treat the epiphyseal and metaphyseal components as two separate fixation problems requiring two separate implants/trajectories rather than expecting a single screw to control both fragments.
- Confirm final reduction and implant position with full biplanar fluoroscopy after every screw is placed, not just at the end of the case — sequential confirmation catches a fragment that shifted during placement of a second or third implant.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Because these injuries occur near the end of growth, growth arrest is a comparatively minor concern relative to articular step-off, which is the primary driver of late post-traumatic ankle arthritis if left uncorrected — articular reduction is the priority.
- The 2 mm operative threshold is defined on CT, not plain radiographs — plain films can underestimate true displacement, so do not downgrade a borderline case to nonoperative management based on plain film alone if CT was not obtained.
- Growth arrest is uncommon given the near-complete physis at the time of injury, but follow-up to confirm maintained reduction and eventual physeal closure is still standard given the low but non-zero risk.
- Relying on a single (AP-only) fluoroscopic view to confirm articular reduction is a common technical error that can miss a persistent step-off visible only on mortise or lateral projections.
- Under-treating a triplane fracture's metaphyseal component with only an epiphyseal screw risks residual instability/malreduction of that fragment even when the articular (epiphyseal) portion looks well-reduced.
- Missed syndesmotic instability, if not specifically stress-tested intraoperatively when diastasis was present preoperatively, can lead to residual mortise widening and altered ankle joint mechanics.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Non-weight-bearing short-leg or long-leg cast for **4–6 weeks** (closer to 6 weeks for fixed/operative triplane fractures with a large metaphyseal component).
- Progressive weight-bearing in a walking boot thereafter, with physiotherapy focused on ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion ROM and gait normalization.
- Return to running typically at **8–10 weeks**, and full return to cutting/pivoting sport at **3–4 months** once radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing are achieved.
