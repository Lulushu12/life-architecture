## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Calcific tendinopathy deposits that have failed an initial NSAID/PT trial and remain symptomatic.
- Particularly Gärtner type I–II deposits (dense, well-circumscribed) that are firm enough to yield calcium on aspiration.
- Gärtner type III (fluffy/resorptive-phase) deposits can be needled if pain is severe, but they often resorb spontaneously and are technically harder to aspirate — favor watchful waiting/NSAIDs for these unless pain is refractory.
- Deposit confirmed and characterized on plain radiograph (and typically re-localized on ultrasound at the time of the procedure).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active local or systemic infection, or overlying skin breakdown at the planned needle entry site.
- Suspected septic arthritis or an inflammatory/crystal arthropathy masquerading as an acutely painful calcific deposit — rule this out before proceeding, since an acute resorptive-phase deposit can closely mimic it.
- Bleeding diathesis or therapeutic anticoagulation that cannot be safely bridged/held.
- Full-thickness rotator cuff tear at the deposit site — barbotage is not contraindicated outright but should be planned/counseled jointly with the cuff pathology, since needling through a compromised tendon carries theoretical risk of tear propagation.
- A deposit that is poorly localized or inaccessible on ultrasound (deep, obscured by bone shadow) — consider fluoroscopic or CT guidance, or defer to arthroscopic excision.
- Patient unable to tolerate an awake, local-anesthesia procedure (severe anxiety, inability to remain still) — consider light sedation or arthroscopic excision instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: plain radiograph (true AP/Grashey, axillary, outlet views) is the best initial modality — it characterizes Gärtner type (I dense/well-circumscribed, II mixed, III fluffy/resorptive) and predicts how the needling will go; obtain a same-day or recent ultrasound to confirm the deposit is still present and to plan the approach trajectory.
- **Deposit selection**: correlate radiographic type with acuity of symptoms — a "hot," acutely painful shoulder with a fluffy type III deposit is often in active spontaneous resorption and may not need intervention; a chronic dull ache with a dense type I deposit is the best technical candidate.
- **Equipment**: high-frequency linear ultrasound probe with a sterile probe cover, local anesthetic (short- and longer-acting, e.g., lidocaine and bupivacaine), an 18-gauge needle on a large syringe (16–20 mL) of sterile saline for the working/aspirating needle, a second needle if using a two-needle technique, and corticosteroid for co-instillation if desired.
- **Technique choice**: single-needle (in-plane, pump saline in and aspirate back through the same needle) versus two-needle (one needle infuses saline, a second aspirates) — single-needle is simpler and favored by many to minimize additional tendon punctures; two-needle can improve flow/lavage efficiency in a larger or more viscous deposit. Decide based on deposit size/consistency and operator preference.
- **Patient counseling**: set expectations explicitly that barbotage produces meaningful short-term relief (often notable by ~2 months) but a substantial minority require repeat intervention — this is standard preoperative counseling, not a post-procedure surprise.
- **Escalation pathway**: confirm with the patient in advance that arthroscopic excision is the next step if barbotage/ESWT fails, so expectations for a staged approach are set before the first procedure.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: performed awake under local anesthesia in an outpatient/office or procedure-suite setting — no general anesthesia or regional block required. Infiltrate skin and the subcutaneous tract down to the deposit generously; some operators also infiltrate the subacromial bursa.
- **Position**: seated or semi-reclined with the arm at the side and slightly internally/externally rotated as needed to bring the deposit into an accessible ultrasound window (internal rotation with the hand on the ipsilateral hip/back often optimizes supraspinatus deposit access, similar to positioning for a subacromial injection).
- **Setup**: sterile field prepped over the anterolateral shoulder, ultrasound machine positioned for direct operator viewing without awkward neck/arm rotation, sterile gel and probe cover.
- **No tourniquet or fluoroscopy required** for standard ultrasound-guided barbotage; fluoroscopy is reserved as a backup localization tool if the deposit is not clearly seen on ultrasound.
- **Landmarks**: mark the acromion border, coracoid, and the ultrasound-confirmed skin entry point corresponding to the deposit's most accessible in-plane trajectory before draping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm deposit morphology (Gärtner type) on plain radiograph** before proceeding — this predicts how straightforward the needling will be and helps set expectations.
2. **Position the patient and identify the deposit on ultrasound**, scanning in both long- and short-axis to confirm size, depth, and the safest in-plane needle trajectory avoiding neurovascular structures.
3. **Prep and drape the field**, infiltrate skin and the needle tract with local anesthetic under direct ultrasound visualization, and consider infiltrating the subacromial bursa as well if bursal inflammation is present.
4. **Advance the primary (working) needle under real-time ultrasound guidance**, in-plane with the transducer, directly into the calcific deposit — confirm needle-tip position within the deposit before beginning fragmentation.
5. **Fragment and lavage the deposit**: repetitively inject saline under pressure and aspirate back through the same needle (single-needle technique), or infuse through one needle while aspirating through a second needle positioned elsewhere in the deposit (two-needle technique) — continue until aspirate becomes milky/chalky and progressively clearer, or until the deposit is no longer discretely identifiable on ultrasound.
6. **Multiple passes/punctures** through different regions of the deposit are often needed for dense (type I) deposits — reposition the needle tip within the deposit rather than assuming a single puncture site will adequately fragment the entire lesion.
7. **Instill corticosteroid** into the subacromial bursa (not directly into the tendon substance) at the end of the procedure if desired, to reduce the post-procedural inflammatory flare.
8. **Confirm final deposit appearance on ultrasound**, apply a sterile dressing, and counsel the patient on expected post-procedure pain flare (common for 24–48 hours) and the likely course.
9. **Counsel on expected course explicitly at this visit**: meaningful short-term pain reduction is typical by roughly 2 months, but effect size attenuates over longer follow-up, and a substantial minority of patients require repeat intervention (further injection, repeat barbotage, or surgery) — barbotage should be framed as a treatment that may need to be repeated, not a single definitive procedure.
10. **Escalate to arthroscopic excision** if the deposit remains refractory after at least one round of barbotage/ESWT with persistent significant symptoms — intraoperative fluoroscopy or needle localization helps identify the deposit at that point, and complete excision is not always necessary or possible; combine with cuff repair if a concomitant tear is debrided down to healthy tissue.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Scan the deposit in both long- and short-axis before choosing a needle entry point — this confirms true 3D size and avoids underestimating a deposit that looks small in a single plane.
- Keep the needle strictly in-plane with the transducer throughout — losing the needle tip out of plane is the most common cause of an inaccurate or painful pass.
- For dense type I deposits, expect to reposition the needle tip multiple times within the deposit ("fanning" through it) rather than expecting complete fragmentation from a single puncture site.
- A single-needle technique minimizes additional tendon punctures and is adequate for most deposits; reserve a two-needle approach for larger or unusually viscous deposits where lavage efficiency is limiting.
- Warn the patient about a common 24–48 hour post-procedure pain flare before they leave — this is expected and does not indicate a complication, but unwarned patients often call urgently.
- Co-instilling corticosteroid into the bursa (not the tendon) at the end of the procedure reduces the inflammatory flare without exposing the tendon substance to steroid.
- If the deposit is poorly visualized on ultrasound (deep, obscured by acromion or bony shadow), do not force the procedure — reposition the arm, adjust probe angle, or convert to fluoroscopic guidance rather than needling blindly.
- Reassess symptomatically and radiographically at a defined interval (commonly 6–8 weeks) rather than leaving follow-up open-ended, so the decision to repeat barbotage, add ESWT, or proceed to arthroscopic excision is made on a schedule rather than by default.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Gärtner III deposits are technically harder to needle (soft, toothpaste-like); attempting aggressive barbotage on a deposit that is already in high-likelihood spontaneous resorption may not be worth the procedural burden — reserve intervention for severe pain in this subtype.
- Do not present barbotage as a one-time definitive treatment — network meta-analyses and dedicated efficacy studies (2023–2025) confirm effect size diminishes over longer follow-up and repeat intervention is common.
- High-energy focused ESWT and barbotage rank as the most effective nonoperative modalities in network meta-analyses (both outperforming sham and low-energy ESWT) — low-energy ESWT should not be substituted as an equivalent alternative.
- An acute, severely painful presentation with a resorptive-phase (type III) deposit can mimic septic arthritis or crystal arthropathy; rule this out before attributing the presentation to calcific tendinopathy.
- Losing the needle tip out of the ultrasound plane risks inaccurate deposit targeting and unnecessary tendon trauma from repeated blind passes — reposition the probe rather than advancing further without a clear needle-tip view.
- Post-procedure inflammatory flare (increased pain for 24–72 hours) is common and self-limited but should be anticipated and explained to the patient beforehand to avoid unnecessary urgent contact/concern.
- Incomplete fragmentation of a dense, well-organized deposit can leave a residual symptomatic remnant — counsel on the real possibility of a repeat procedure rather than an assumption of single-session cure.
- Vasovagal response during the procedure is uncommon but possible given the awake, needle-based nature of the procedure — have the patient positioned safely (reclined or seated with support) and monitor accordingly.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Sling for comfort only (days).
- Early passive/active-assisted ROM within the first 1–2 weeks — there is no tendon-to-bone repair to protect.
- Progress strengthening as tolerated by pain.
- Overall course is much faster than after rotator cuff repair, unless a concomitant cuff repair was performed at the same setting, in which case follow the cuff-repair protocol instead.
