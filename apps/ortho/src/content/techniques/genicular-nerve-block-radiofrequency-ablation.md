## Indications

Chronic knee OA pain in patients who (1) are medically unfit or unwilling for arthroplasty, (2) have failed conservative and injectable therapy, and (3) demonstrate a robust diagnostic block response. Not a substitute for arthroplasty in an eligible candidate — reasonable option for patients who are not surgical candidates or who decline/must delay arthroplasty. Poor candidates: predominantly mechanical/instability-driven pain, significant intra-articular loose body or meniscal pathology likely to need arthroscopic intervention, or pain outside the classic anteromedial/anterolateral genicular distribution.

## Positioning & setup

Fluoroscopic or US guidance is used for the diagnostic block; RFA is typically performed under fluoroscopic guidance. Target the three standard nerves at their periosteal targets at the junction of the shaft and epicondyle/condyle, visualized on AP and lateral views.

## Steps

1. **Perform a diagnostic block first** at the three standard targets — superolateral genicular nerve (SLGN), superomedial genicular nerve (SMGN), and inferomedial genicular nerve (IMGN) — using local anesthetic. Some protocols add the recurrent fibular/genicular branch or the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve for anteromedial pain coverage.
2. **Confirm an adequate diagnostic response** before proceeding to RFA: require ≥50% pain reduction (some protocols require ≥80% or a dual comparative-block paradigm with placebo/saline control to reduce false positives).
3. **Proceed to RFA at the same three anatomic targets** once the diagnostic threshold is met: conventional thermal RFA (80–90°C, 90 seconds/lesion) or cooled RFA (lower tip temperature, larger lesion volume, better nerve capture rates in device-sponsored trials).
4. **Select the candidate carefully**: best candidates are medically unfit/unwilling for arthroplasty, have failed conservative and injectable therapy, and showed a robust diagnostic block response; exclude patients with predominantly mechanical/instability-driven pain or significant intra-articular pathology likely to require arthroscopy.

## Pitfalls

- Do not proceed to RFA without a documented diagnostic block response meeting threshold (≥50%, or higher per stricter protocols) — this is required before ablation.
- Complications include transient dysesthesia/numbness (uncommon, self-limited), skin burn (rare with proper grounding), and post-procedure ecchymosis.
- Theoretical risk of accelerated Charcot-like joint changes with sensory denervation in a joint that continues to bear mechanical load — an important long-term counseling point, particularly if RFA is used as a long-term bridge rather than a bridge to arthroplasty.
- A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis (Peng et al.) supports genicular RFA for meaningful pain reduction at 6 months in appropriately selected patients, but it is not yet a CPG-tier recommendation; a large pragmatic head-to-head trial (SKOAP, Cohen et al.) comparing IA injection, genicular block, and RFA is pending maturation and should sharpen the treatment ladder once results are available.
- RFA is not a substitute for arthroplasty in a patient who remains an eligible surgical candidate.

## Post-op protocol

- Expect denervation-related numbness in the anterior knee skin distribution — this is expected, not a complication.
- Resume normal activity as tolerated within days.
- Therapeutic pain-relief window typically becomes apparent by 2–4 weeks and, in responders, lasts 6–12 months.
- No formal activity restriction beyond immediate post-procedure comfort — encourage resumption of prior activity level as pain allows, since the goal is functional pain control rather than tissue healing.
