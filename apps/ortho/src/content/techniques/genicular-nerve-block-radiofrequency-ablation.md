## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

Chronic knee OA pain in patients who (1) are medically unfit or unwilling for arthroplasty, (2) have failed conservative and injectable therapy, and (3) demonstrate a robust diagnostic block response. Not a substitute for arthroplasty in an eligible candidate — reasonable option for patients who are not surgical candidates or who decline/must delay arthroplasty.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Predominantly mechanical/instability-driven pain (ligamentous laxity, malalignment requiring correction) — RFA does not address a mechanical pain generator and outcomes are poor.
- Significant intra-articular loose body or meniscal pathology likely to require arthroscopic intervention — treat the mechanical lesion first; denervating a joint with an untreated mechanical problem risks masking a progressive issue.
- Pain outside the classic anteromedial/anterolateral genicular distribution (e.g., posterior knee pain, radicular referred pain) — poor candidate, block is unlikely to localize appropriately.
- Failure to meet the diagnostic block threshold (see below) — do not proceed to RFA on clinical impression alone.
- Active local or systemic infection, or overlying cellulitis at needle entry sites.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or supratherapeutic anticoagulation, given the multiple needle passes and proximity to the popliteal neurovascular structures with posterior/inferior targets.
- Allergy to local anesthetic class being used for the diagnostic block.
- An eligible, willing arthroplasty candidate — RFA should not be marketed as an alternative to definitive surgery in this group; reserve it for patients who are unfit, unwilling, or must delay.
- Uncontrolled expectations regarding denervation-related sensory change — patients unwilling to accept anterior knee numbness are poor candidates.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the pain distribution is consistent with anteromedial/anterolateral genicular territory on history and exam before booking a diagnostic block — this is the single biggest determinant of downstream success.
- Review imaging (recent knee radiographs, MRI if available) to exclude a correctable mechanical lesion (loose body, unstable meniscal tear, significant malalignment) that would be better addressed surgically or arthroscopically first.
- Confirm the patient has exhausted or is not a candidate for the standard treatment ladder (activity modification, PT, NSAIDs, IA corticosteroid/HA/PRP) — genicular block/RFA sits later in the ladder, not as a first-line option.
- Plan the diagnostic block protocol in advance: single block with a ≥50% relief threshold, or a stricter ≥80%/dual comparative-block (local anesthetic vs saline) paradigm to reduce false positives — decide and document which threshold you are using before the block, not retrospectively.
- Equipment: fluoroscopy unit (most practices, ~85% per survey data) or high-resolution ultrasound; RFA generator compatible with either conventional thermal probes or a cooled-RFA system if available (cooled RFA produces a larger lesion volume and has shown better nerve capture rates in device-sponsored trials); insulated RFA cannulas with active tips sized per manufacturer; nerve stimulator capability on the RFA generator for sensory/motor testing.
- Counsel the patient in advance about the two-stage nature of the procedure (diagnostic block, then a separate RFA session once the response threshold is confirmed), expected anterior knee numbness after RFA, and the realistic therapeutic window (onset over 2–4 weeks, durable benefit in responders typically 6–12 months, not permanent).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Both the diagnostic block and RFA are performed under local anesthesia with the patient positioned supine, knee extended and slightly bolstered (a small roll under the popliteal fossa can help maintain slight flexion and patient comfort without compromising fluoroscopic targeting).
- No sedation is typically required; light anxiolysis is reasonable for anxious patients but is not standard.
- Fluoroscopy: C-arm positioned for AP and lateral views of the distal femur/proximal tibia; obtain a true AP (patella centered between the femoral condyles) before marking targets, since a rotated AP view will misrepresent the epicondylar/condylar landmarks.
- Ultrasound (where used instead of or to complement fluoroscopy): linear probe, short-axis views over each target to visualize the genicular vessels running alongside the corresponding nerve — the artery is a reliable surrogate landmark since the nerves themselves are not consistently visualized on standard ultrasound.
- Surface landmarks marked before needling: the femoral shaft-epicondyle junction for the superolateral (SLGN) and superomedial (SMGN) targets, and the tibial shaft-condyle junction for the inferomedial (IMGN) target, each confirmed on both AP and lateral fluoroscopic views.
- Standard sterile prep (chlorhexidine) and a fenestrated drape over the marked entry sites; no tourniquet is used for either the block or RFA.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Perform a diagnostic block first at the three standard targets — superolateral genicular nerve (SLGN), superomedial genicular nerve (SMGN), and inferomedial genicular nerve (IMGN) — using local anesthetic. Some protocols add the recurrent fibular/genicular branch or the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve for anteromedial pain coverage.
2. Confirm needle position on both AP and lateral fluoroscopic views at each target before injecting local anesthetic; inject a small volume (typically 0.5–1 mL per site) after negative aspiration.
3. Confirm an adequate diagnostic response before proceeding to RFA: require ≥50% pain reduction (some protocols require ≥80% or a dual comparative-block paradigm with placebo/saline control to reduce false positives). Document the response with a pre- and post-block pain score and functional assessment.
4. Select the candidate carefully before scheduling RFA: best candidates are medically unfit/unwilling for arthroplasty, have failed conservative and injectable therapy, and showed a robust diagnostic block response; exclude patients with predominantly mechanical/instability-driven pain or significant intra-articular pathology likely to require arthroscopy.
5. At the RFA session, reposition and re-mark the same three targets under fluoroscopy; use a relatively perpendicular needle approach toward the periosteal surface of the femoral shaft-epicondyle or tibial shaft-condyle junction — an approach that is too parallel to the bone risks missing genicular nerve branches that run superficial to the periosteum rather than directly on it.
6. On lateral fluoroscopic view, guide needle depth so the active tip sits at roughly the medial third of the femur or tibia at each target, then confirm final position is at or just superficial (roughly 2 mm) to the periosteum, not buried in it.
7. For the inferolateral/fibular-head-adjacent target when used, confirm the cannula tip fluoroscopically at approximately 2 mm anterior to the fibular head, roughly two-thirds of the way across the tibia in that view, given its proximity to the common peroneal nerve.
8. Perform sensory stimulation (typically 50 Hz) to confirm concordant paresthesia in the expected distribution, and motor stimulation (typically 2 Hz) to confirm the absence of motor capture — a positive motor response at low current, particularly near the fibular head target, indicates the cannula is too close to the common peroneal nerve and must be repositioned before lesioning.
9. Inject a small volume of local anesthetic through the cannula immediately before lesioning to reduce procedural discomfort.
10. Lesion each target: conventional thermal RFA at 80–90°C for 90 seconds per lesion, or cooled RFA per the device's protocol (lower tip temperature, larger lesion volume) — repeat at each of the three (or more, per protocol) confirmed targets.
11. Consider a repeat pass with a slightly repositioned cannula (redirected a few millimeters) at each target if using a straight (non-dual-tined) electrode, since a single lesion may not reliably capture anatomic nerve variants that run superficial to the periosteum; some practices favor multi-tined or fan-pattern lesioning specifically to address this variability.
12. Withdraw all cannulas, confirm hemostasis at each entry site, and apply dressings.
13. Document targets lesioned, lesion parameters (temperature/duration or cooled-RFA settings), and any motor-stimulation-guided repositioning performed.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Obtain a true, non-rotated AP fluoroscopic view before marking any target — a rotated view shifts the apparent epicondylar/condylar landmarks enough to meaningfully change lesion placement.
- Favor a relatively perpendicular needle trajectory to the periosteal surface rather than a shallow, parallel approach — genicular nerve branches frequently "float" superficial to the periosteum, and a parallel approach is more likely to miss them even with an anatomically correct entry point.
- Always perform motor stimulation before lesioning near the fibular-head-adjacent (inferolateral) target — this is the single most important safety check in the entire procedure given the proximity of the common peroneal nerve.
- Consider that newer anatomic mapping suggests a slightly more distal and posterior placement for the superomedial and superolateral targets may capture a broader set of nerve branches than the classic periosteal landmarks alone — worth incorporating into your target selection as evidence continues to mature.
- Use the diagnostic block not just as a gatekeeping step but as a trial run for your fluoroscopic landmarks — if a target was hard to confirm on the block day, expect the same difficulty at RFA and plan extra time.
- When using a straight (non-dual-tined) RFA electrode, consider a second lesion at each target with the cannula redirected a few millimeters, rather than relying on a single lesion per target, to better capture anatomic variability in nerve position.
- Counsel patients before the RFA session (not just before the diagnostic block) that expected anterior knee numbness is a sign of technical success, not a complication — this materially reduces anxious postoperative calls.
- Avoid full circumferential denervation of the knee — target the standard anatomic set rather than attempting to ablate every possible branch, both to preserve some protective sensation and because of the theoretical Charcot-joint risk discussed below.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not proceed to RFA without a documented diagnostic block response meeting threshold (≥50%, or higher per stricter protocols) — this is required before ablation.
- Complications include transient dysesthesia/numbness (uncommon, self-limited), skin burn (rare with proper grounding), and post-procedure ecchymosis.
- Theoretical risk of accelerated Charcot-like joint changes with sensory denervation in a joint that continues to bear mechanical load — an important long-term counseling point, particularly if RFA is used as a long-term bridge rather than a bridge to arthroplasty.
- A 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis (Peng et al.) supports genicular RFA for meaningful pain reduction at 6 months in appropriately selected patients, but it is not yet a CPG-tier recommendation; a large pragmatic head-to-head trial (SKOAP, Cohen et al.) comparing IA injection, genicular block, and RFA is pending maturation and should sharpen the treatment ladder once results are available.
- RFA is not a substitute for arthroplasty in a patient who remains an eligible surgical candidate.
- Classic three-nerve periosteal targets can miss anatomic variants of the genicular nerves that run superficial to the periosteum rather than directly on it — a purely parallel needle approach compounds this risk; use a relatively perpendicular approach and consider the broader/more posterior target placement supported by newer anatomic mapping.
- Motor capture on stimulation near the inferolateral/fibular-head target signals proximity to the common peroneal nerve — reposition before lesioning rather than proceeding, to avoid a symptomatic peroneal nerve injury.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Expect denervation-related numbness in the anterior knee skin distribution — this is expected, not a complication.
- Resume normal activity as tolerated within days.
- Therapeutic pain-relief window typically becomes apparent by 2–4 weeks and, in responders, lasts 6–12 months.
- No formal activity restriction beyond immediate post-procedure comfort — encourage resumption of prior activity level as pain allows, since the goal is functional pain control rather than tissue healing.
