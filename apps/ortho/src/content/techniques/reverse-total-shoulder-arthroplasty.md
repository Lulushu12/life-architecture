## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.**
- Cuff-tear arthropathy (Hamada ≥3, or symptomatic Hamada 1–2 with pseudoparalysis).
- Massive irreparable cuff tears with pseudoparalysis but no arthritis in older/lower-demand patients.
- Proximal humerus fracture in the elderly.
- Revision of failed hemiarthroplasty/aTSA.
- Glenohumeral OA with severe glenoid deformity (Walch B3/C) or subscapularis insufficiency.
- Favored generally at age ≥65–70 or lower functional demand even with an intact cuff, given rTSA's more reliable pain relief and lower glenoid-component revision rates relative to aTSA in this setting.

**Contraindications.**
- Absolute: active infection, deltoid paralysis or irreparable deltoid deficiency (the deltoid is the sole remaining elevator — the construct fails without it), and insufficient glenoid bone stock to achieve baseplate fixation even with augmentation/grafting.
- Relative: young, high-demand patients where implant longevity and constraint-related complications are a greater long-term concern (favor aTSA if the cuff is intact and version is correctable); axillary nerve palsy (deltoid is intact but its innervation is not, functionally similar to deltoid deficiency); severe glenoid bone loss requiring extensive grafting in a medically frail patient who may not tolerate a longer/more complex procedure; and patients unwilling to accept lifelong activity/lifting modifications and the implant-constraint-related dislocation risk profile.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/templating**: true AP/Grashey, axillary lateral, and scapular Y radiographs; preoperative CT with 3D glenoid vault modeling is essential, particularly for B2/B3/C glenoids, Favard E2–E4 erosion patterns, or revision cases, to plan baseplate central-peg/screw trajectory, tilt, and augmentation strategy.
- **Erosion-pattern-specific planning**: use the Favard classification to plan baseplate tilt and augmentation — superior erosion (E2/E3) drives inferior tilt and/or a superior wedge augment or bone graft with a longer central peg; combined posterior-superior deficiency (E4) needs a combined augmentation strategy addressing both planes.
- **Implant selection**: decide on baseplate augmentation strategy (eccentric reaming vs. metal augment vs. BIO-RSA bone graft) and glenosphere lateralization/size, plus humeral component design (inlay vs. onlay) based on version/inclination correction needs, notching-avoidance strategy, and bone quality.
- **Cuff and deltoid assessment**: MRI to grade fatty infiltration of infraspinatus/teres minor (Goutallier/Fuchs) when preoperative external rotation is compromised — a positive external rotation lag sign predicts poor postoperative external rotation regardless of implant choice, and may prompt planning for a concomitant tendon transfer.
- **Instrumentation/equipment**: glenoid baseplate system with matched augment inventory (metal wedge/step augments in more than one size/angle) or graft-preparation instruments for BIO-RSA, trial glenospheres of varying lateralization/size, humeral trial system, and patient-specific instrumentation/navigation if used for high-deformity cases.
- **Retained hardware and revision planning**: review prior implants/hardware on CT for removal planning in revision cases; have extraction and bone-graft/augment contingencies available.
- **Medical optimization**: as for aTSA — diabetes control, smoking cessation, ≥3-month washout from the most recent intra-articular corticosteroid injection.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia combined with an interscalene regional block (single-shot or catheter) for intraoperative and early postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: modified beach-chair position (typically 30–45°) in a dedicated shoulder positioner allowing full arm mobility, extension off the table edge, and lateral positioning of the shoulder at the table edge for exposure; controlled hypotensive anesthesia to aid visualization.
- **Table setup**: secured headrest clear of the field; padded arm positioner or assistant-controlled arm holder for intraoperative rotation/extension during humeral preparation and trial reduction.
- **Draping**: wide circumferential prep/drape of the entire arm and shoulder girdle, exposing the clavicle, acromion, and scapular spine, to allow free intraoperative manipulation and assessment of impingement-free arc through abduction/adduction and rotation.
- **Landmarks**: mark the clavicle, acromion (anterolateral corner and posterolateral corner/scapular spine), coracoid, and deltopectoral interval; standard incision follows the deltopectoral groove from the coracoid tip toward the deltoid insertion (a more superior/anterior "reverse-specific" variant is used by some surgeons for improved glenoid access).
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy available for revision cases or when intraoperative confirmation of baseplate central-peg/screw position is desired; have the full range of glenosphere/augment trials and, if used, patient-specific guides or navigation set up and registered before glenoid exposure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and deltopectoral exposure**: as for aTSA — deltopectoral interval, protect the cephalic vein, identify the conjoint tendon and clavipectoral fascia, and protect the musculocutaneous and axillary nerves throughout.
2. **Subscapularis management**: tenotomize, peel, or perform an LTO as needed for exposure; in cuff-tear arthropathy the subscapularis may already be attenuated or absent — assess and plan repair (if feasible) or accept that it will not be repaired, since rTSA does not depend on subscapularis function for elevation.
3. **Humeral head resection**: resect the humeral head at the anatomic neck with reduced retroversion and often a more varus cut than aTSA, per the specific implant system's targets, to optimize the humeral component's contribution to stability and impingement-free ROM.
4. **Circumferential glenoid exposure**: place anterior, posterior, and inferior retractors; perform a thorough inferior capsular release, taking down roughly 1 cm of the inferior capsule and a portion of the triceps origin off the inferior glenoid neck — this is essential to allow low baseplate positioning and adduction clearance for the humeral component, both of which reduce scapular notching risk.
5. **Glenoid preparation**: release the labrum circumferentially, identify and mark the true center point of the glenoid (not the eroded/worn center, especially in Favard E2–E4 patterns), then place the guide pin and ream the central cannulated channel per the implant system.
6. **Select and execute the version-correction/augmentation strategy**:
   - **Eccentric reaming** — limited correction capacity, sacrifices bone, and risks exposing weak subchondral/cancellous bone and jeopardizing baseplate fixation if >15° correction is attempted.
   - **Metal-augmented baseplates** (posterior or superior wedge/step augments) — technically reproducible, avoid graft-related complications, provide immediate fixation strength, but add cost and require inventory.
   - **BIO-RSA** (bony increased-offset RSA) — autograft (typically the resected humeral head) or allograft fixed beneath the baseplate corrects version/inclination while simultaneously lateralizing the joint line, reducing scapular notching and improving stability/ROM.
   - Whichever strategy is chosen, achieve central compression-screw fixation and reliable press-fit primary stability at implantation.
7. **Baseplate positioning**: position the baseplate with slight inferior tilt and inferior overhang of the glenosphere — dialing in roughly 2–3 mm of glenosphere overhang below the inferior glenoid rim is a reproducible way to create an impingement-free arc and reduce inferior scapular notching (impingement of the humeral polyethylene against the inferior scapular neck); avoid a superiorly-placed baseplate, which is specifically associated with higher notching rates.
8. **Peripheral screw fixation**: place peripheral locking/compression screws per the implant system's targets (typically superior, inferior, and one or more angled screws into the scapular pillar/coracoid base) to supplement the central peg/screw and neutralize shear.
9. **Glenosphere selection and trial**: choose glenosphere size and lateralization based on preoperative plan and intraoperative soft-tissue tension; lateralization (glenoid-side via BIO-RSA/lateralized baseplate, or humeral-side via an onlay component) reduces scapular notching, restores an external-rotation lever arm when teres minor is intact, and reduces acromial stress-fracture risk relative to purely medialized (classic Grammont) designs, at the cost of increased shear/torque across the baseplate-bone interface.
10. **Humeral canal preparation and trial reduction**: broach/ream the humeral canal, place trial humeral component and glenosphere, and perform a trial reduction to assess soft-tissue tensioning, stability, and impingement-free ROM through full adduction/abduction and internal/external rotation.
11. **Balance soft-tissue tensioning**: undertensioning raises instability risk; overtensioning raises acromial/scapular-spine stress-fracture risk — use intraoperative arm-length/offset assessment and a stability exam (resistance to inferior/anterior/posterior translation) rather than tension alone to judge the balance.
12. **Final implantation**: cement or press-fit the definitive humeral component per system/bone quality, then impact the final glenosphere and humeral liner/insert.
13. **Subscapularis repair**: repair when feasible to reduce anterior instability risk, though its effect on functional outcome remains debated; if the tendon is too attenuated or absent, proceed without forcing a repair under excessive tension.
14. **Closure**: layered closure of the deltopectoral interval, subcutaneous tissue, and skin; drain per surgeon preference.
15. **Final check**: reconfirm the reduction is stable throughout the trialed arc and document the impingement-free ROM achieved to guide the postoperative therapy prescription.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Perform a generous, deliberate inferior capsular release (including a portion of the triceps origin off the inferior glenoid neck) before baseplate placement — this single step does more to enable a low, notch-avoiding baseplate position than any implant-side adjustment.
- Mark the true anatomic center of the glenoid before reaming, not the visually eroded center, especially with superior (Favard E2/E3) or combined (E4) erosion patterns, or the central peg trajectory will inherit the deformity.
- Dial in roughly 2–3 mm of inferior glenosphere overhang past the inferior glenoid rim as a reproducible, reportedly effective way to reduce scapular notching; avoid superior baseplate positioning, which is specifically associated with more notching.
- Favor lateralization (glenoid-side BIO-RSA/lateralized baseplate or humeral-side onlay) when preserving external rotation and reducing notching/acromial stress are priorities, understanding the tradeoff of increased shear across the baseplate-bone interface.
- Use patient-specific instrumentation, navigation, or a sterile 3D-printed glenoid model in high-deformity or revision cases — guided techniques improve alignment accuracy relative to preoperative plans and have been associated with fewer short-term complications.
- Assess external rotation lag sign preoperatively; if teres minor is deficient, consider a concomitant latissimus dorsi or lower trapezius transfer rather than expecting the implant alone to restore active external rotation.
- Judge soft-tissue tensioning functionally (stability on exam, deltoid resting tension, arm-length change) rather than by a fixed numeric target — both under- and over-tensioning carry distinct, serious failure modes.
- In revision settings or with severe bone loss, have both metal-augment and bone-graft (BIO-RSA) contingencies available intraoperatively, since the degree of deficiency found may differ from the preoperative plan.
- Protect the axillary nerve specifically during inferior capsular release and retractor placement — this is the highest-risk step in the case for nerve traction injury.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Comparative data show baseplate failure rates trending lower with metal augments than bone graft (reported ≈0% vs. ≈7% in some series), with marginally better early pain/ASES/UCLA scores — but BIO-RSA restores host bone stock, which is valuable in younger patients or those who may need future revision, and avoids augmented-implant cost. Graft healing/resorption remains its chief specific risk, particularly with peripheral or thin grafts.
- Instability/dislocation occurs in 2–8% of cases, higher in revision settings and with subscapularis deficiency.
- Scapular notching is seen in a substantial minority of medialized-design cases; clinical significance is debated and notching does not always correlate with worse clinical outcomes, though higher-grade notching does associate with eventual glenoid component loosening. Higher baseplate position (measured from the baseplate's inferior aspect to the inferior glenoid rim) is specifically associated with more notching.
- Acromial and scapular spine stress fractures occur in 2–5%, related to deltoid overtensioning and osteoporotic bone, with a significant negative impact on outcome when they occur.
- Baseplate loosening is more common with bone-graft augmentation than metal augmentation, and with severe pre-op bone loss.
- Neurologic injury (axillary nerve traction, brachial plexopathy from arm lengthening) can occur; intraoperative nerve monitoring is used selectively. The inferior capsular release/retraction step and aggressive arm lengthening at trial reduction are the highest-risk moments.
- For B2/B3 glenoids with an intact cuff, comparable ROM/ASES/Constant/VAS outcomes versus aTSA mean the choice should be driven by bone stock, version-correctability, subscapularis quality, and age — not expected functional superiority of either implant.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: sling immobilization full-time except for hygiene and elbow/wrist/hand exercises; passive/active-assisted ROM only, with less restricted external rotation limits than aTSA given the absence of a subscapularis-protective repair in many cases. No active internal/external rotation, no lifting.
- **Weeks 2–6**: continued passive/active-assisted ROM progression; begin gentle active ROM below shoulder height; sling is often weaned faster than aTSA, around 2–4 weeks, given different loading demands.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling; progress to full active ROM; begin light resistance/strengthening (isometrics progressing to isotonic).
- **Months 3–6**: progressive deltoid/periscapular-focused strengthening; functional and sport-specific training as appropriate.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work 2–4 weeks; light-manual work 6–8 weeks; heavy manual/overhead labor 4–6 months, with permanent lifting modification often advised, particularly after rTSA.
- **Return to sport**: low-demand recreational activity typically 4–6 months; contact/overhead athletic activity is individualized and often discouraged long-term given implant constraints.
