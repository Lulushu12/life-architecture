## Indications

Cuff-tear arthropathy (Hamada ≥3, or symptomatic Hamada 1–2 with pseudoparalysis); massive irreparable cuff tears with pseudoparalysis but no arthritis in older/lower-demand patients; proximal humerus fracture in the elderly; revision of failed hemiarthroplasty/aTSA; and glenohumeral OA with severe glenoid deformity (Walch B3/C) or subscapularis insufficiency. Also favored generally at age ≥65–70 or lower functional demand, even with an intact cuff, given rTSA's more reliable pain relief and lower glenoid-component revision rates relative to aTSA in this setting.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed in the source beyond the general shoulder arthroplasty exposure principles it shares with aTSA (deltopectoral approach). Preoperative CT with 3D glenoid vault modeling is essential, particularly for B2/B3/C glenoids or revision cases.

## Steps

1. **Position the baseplate with slight inferior tilt and inferior overhang of the glenosphere** to reduce inferior scapular notching (impingement of the humeral polyethylene against the inferior scapular neck) — tilt/positioning strategy should also account for Favard-type glenoid erosion pattern (superior erosion drives inferior tilt/inferior augment; combined posterior-superior deficiency needs combined augmentation).
2. **Select a glenoid version-correction strategy**:
   - Eccentric reaming — limited correction capacity, sacrifices bone, risks exposing weak subchondral/cancellous bone and jeopardizing baseplate fixation if >15° correction is attempted.
   - Metal-augmented baseplates (posterior or superior wedge/step augments) — technically reproducible, avoid graft-related complications, provide immediate fixation strength, but add cost and require inventory.
   - BIO-RSA (bony increased-offset RSA) — autograft (typically the resected humeral head) or allograft fixed beneath the baseplate corrects version/inclination while simultaneously lateralizing the joint line, reducing scapular notching and improving stability/ROM.
   - Regardless of augment type, achieve central compression-screw fixation and reliable press-fit primary stability at implantation.
3. **Choose glenosphere lateralization and humeral inlay/onlay design** — both affect notching rate and external rotation; lateralization (at the glenoid via BIO-RSA/lateralized baseplate, or at the humerus via onlay component) reduces scapular notching, restores an external-rotation lever arm when teres minor is intact, and reduces acromial stress-fracture risk relative to purely medialized (classic Grammont) designs, at the cost of increased shear/torque across the baseplate-bone interface.
4. **Balance soft-tissue tensioning** — undertensioning raises instability risk; overtensioning raises acromial/scapular-spine stress-fracture risk.
5. **Repair the subscapularis when feasible** to reduce anterior instability risk, though its effect on functional outcome remains debated.

## Pitfalls

- Comparative data show baseplate failure rates trending lower with metal augments than bone graft (reported ≈0% vs. ≈7% in some series), with marginally better early pain/ASES/UCLA scores — but BIO-RSA restores host bone stock, which is valuable in younger patients or those who may need future revision, and avoids augmented-implant cost. Graft healing/resorption remains its chief specific risk, particularly with peripheral or thin grafts.
- Instability/dislocation occurs in 2–8% of cases, higher in revision settings and with subscapularis deficiency.
- Scapular notching is seen in a substantial minority of medialized-design cases; clinical significance is debated and notching does not always correlate with worse clinical outcomes, though higher-grade notching does associate with eventual glenoid component loosening.
- Acromial and scapular spine stress fractures occur in 2–5%, related to deltoid overtensioning and osteoporotic bone, with a significant negative impact on outcome when they occur.
- Baseplate loosening is more common with bone-graft augmentation than metal augmentation, and with severe pre-op bone loss.
- Neurologic injury (axillary nerve traction, brachial plexopathy from arm lengthening) can occur; intraoperative nerve monitoring is used selectively.
- For B2/B3 glenoids with an intact cuff, comparable ROM/ASES/Constant/VAS outcomes versus aTSA mean the choice should be driven by bone stock, version-correctability, subscapularis quality, and age — not expected functional superiority of either implant.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: sling immobilization full-time except for hygiene and elbow/wrist/hand exercises; passive/active-assisted ROM only, less restricted external rotation limits than aTSA given the absence of a subscapularis-protective repair in many cases. No active internal/external rotation, no lifting.
- **Weeks 2–6**: continued passive/active-assisted ROM progression; begin gentle active ROM below shoulder height; sling is often weaned faster than aTSA, around 2–4 weeks, given different loading demands.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling; progress to full active ROM; begin light resistance/strengthening (isometrics progressing to isotonic).
- **Months 3–6**: progressive deltoid/periscapular-focused strengthening; functional and sport-specific training as appropriate.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work 2–4 weeks; light-manual work 6–8 weeks; heavy manual/overhead labor 4–6 months, with permanent lifting modification often advised, particularly after rTSA.
- **Return to sport**: low-demand recreational activity typically 4–6 months; contact/overhead athletic activity is individualized and often discouraged long-term given implant constraints.
