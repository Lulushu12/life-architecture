## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Surgery is indicated for Eaton-Littler stage II-IV thumb CMC arthritis refractory to a splint/injection trial. Stage IV (pantrapezial, with STT joint involvement) favors trapeziectomy-based procedures over isolated arthrodesis or STT-sparing implants. CMC arthrodesis is reserved for younger, high-demand manual laborers with Eaton II-III disease and no STT involvement.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Eaton stage I disease without significant subluxation should be managed non-operatively (splint, NSAIDs, injection); volar ligament reconstruction alone is reserved for the rare young, high-demand patient with symptomatic instability but preserved cartilage, not for established arthritis.
- CMC arthrodesis is relatively contraindicated in patients who need thumb CMC motion for their occupation/activities (e.g., certain manual trades, musicians) and in any patient with STT joint involvement, since fusing the CMC joint concentrates load on an already-arthritic STT joint.
- Implant (pyrocarbon/metal-on-polyethylene) arthroplasty is relatively contraindicated in high-demand patients and where bone stock for implant fixation is compromised, given higher revision rates compared with trapeziectomy-based procedures; it has fallen out of favor in most centers for this reason.
- Active infection, inadequate skin/soft-tissue envelope, or uncontrolled inflammatory arthritis with active synovitis should be optimized before elective reconstruction.
- Significant thumb MCP hyperextension instability (>30-40°) should be identified preoperatively, since it may require concomitant MCP stabilization (volar plate advancement, sesamoidesis, or fusion in severe cases) at the time of CMC reconstruction to avoid a persistently unstable thumb ray postoperatively.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm Eaton-Littler stage with a true AP (Robert view, hyperpronated) for early joint space narrowing/osteophytes, a lateral view, and stress (Eaton) views for subluxation; assess specifically for STT joint involvement, which changes the procedure menu away from isolated arthrodesis or STT-adjacent implant options.
- Examine thumb MCP joint stability in extension; a hyperextension deformity >30-40° should be planned for concomitant management (this is easy to miss if the CMC exam dominates attention) since untreated MCP hyperextension undermines the result of an otherwise well-executed CMC reconstruction.
- Choose the reconstructive technique (simple trapeziectomy alone, trapeziectomy + LRTI, trapeziectomy + suture-suspensionplasty, CMC arthrodesis, or — rarely now — implant arthroplasty) based on patient age/activity demand, STT involvement, surgeon experience, and the current evidence that simple trapeziectomy, LRTI, and suspensionplasty achieve comparable mid-term outcomes; discuss this explicitly with the patient as part of shared decision-making rather than defaulting to a single "best" technique.
- If LRTI is planned, confirm an intact donor tendon (FCR or a strip of APL, per surgeon preference) on exam; if suture-suspensionplasty is planned, confirm the specific implant system (suture button/internal brace construct) availability and size options.
- If arthrodesis is planned, template the fusion position (typically ~30-40° palmar abduction, ~10-15° radial abduction, and slight pronation to allow functional pinch/grasp) and select fixation (plate, screws, or combined K-wire/tension-band constructs) with attention to bone stock.
- Equipment: standard hand set, small oscillating saw or osteotome for trapezial excision, suture anchors or drill for tendon/suspension fixation if LRTI or suspensionplasty planned, mini fragment plate/screw or headless screw set if arthrodesis planned, fluoroscopy available intraoperatively for arthrodesis position/fixation confirmation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** WALANT (lidocaine + epinephrine, no tourniquet) is used by many surgeons for trapeziectomy-based procedures, allowing intraoperative active thumb motion/pinch testing; regional (axillary/Bier) block or general anesthesia with tourniquet remains common, particularly for arthrodesis or when a tendon harvest adds operative time.
- **Positioning:** supine, arm extended on a hand table, thumb ray accessible with the forearm typically in neutral-to-slight pronation.
- **Tourniquet:** standard arm tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination if not using WALANT.
- **Fluoroscopy:** a mini C-arm is set up and draped in for all arthrodesis cases to confirm position and fixation, and is useful (though not mandatory) for confirming adequate trapezial excision and suspensionplasty/implant tunnel placement in soft-tissue reconstructions.
- **Landmarks marked before incision:** the dorsoradial or volar (Wagner) approach to the CMC joint is marked based on surgeon preference; the course of the radial sensory nerve branches and the radial artery (which crosses the field near the base of the thumb metacarpal/trapezium) are identified and marked as at-risk structures before incising.
- **Draping:** standard sterile hand drape exposing the thumb ray and, if LRTI is planned, the volar forearm proximally for FCR harvest exposure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach.** Make a dorsoradial (Wagner-type, between the first dorsal compartment and the radial artery) or a volar approach centered over the CMC joint; carefully identify and retract branches of the superficial radial sensory nerve, which cross this field superficially and are the most commonly injured sensory structure in this procedure.
2. **Identify and protect the radial artery**, which crosses the field at the base of the thumb metacarpal/trapezium (particularly relevant in the anatomic snuffbox region and during volar approaches) — encircle or clearly visualize and retract it before proceeding with joint capsulotomy.
3. **Open the CMC joint capsule** longitudinally, preserving a capsular flap where possible for later repair/reinforcement, and expose the trapezium circumferentially.
4. **Excise the trapezium (trapeziectomy).** Using an osteotome, small saw, or rongeur, remove the trapezium piecemeal or as a single block, staying subperiosteal/extracapsular to protect the FCR tendon (which runs in a groove on the volar aspect of the trapezium and is at risk during excision) and the radial artery. Confirm complete excision by direct visualization and palpation of the scaphoid distal pole and the base of the first metacarpal, and address any remaining STT arthritic changes as indicated by preoperative imaging.
5. **Assess the resulting space** and decide on (or confirm the planned) suspension/interposition technique based on intraoperative findings — for example, poor capsular quality may favor a more robust suspension construct.
6. **For LRTI:** harvest half (or all) of the FCR tendon (or a strip of APL, per surgeon preference) leaving the distal insertion intact, pass it through a drill hole in the base of the first metacarpal, weave it through/around the remaining capsule and the abductor pollicis longus insertion to suspend the metacarpal base and prevent proximal migration, then use any residual tendon to fashion an interposition "anchovy" packed into the trapeziectomy space; tension the construct with the thumb held in appropriate palmar/radial abduction before securing final sutures.
7. **For suture-suspensionplasty:** after trapeziectomy, drill a bone tunnel (or tunnels) through the base of the first metacarpal and the base of the second metacarpal (or through the FCR/APL tendon complex, per specific technique), pass a strong non-absorbable suture or suture-tape construct (e.g., a suture-button/"mini TightRope"-type device) between the two, and tension it with the thumb metacarpal held reduced (restoring trapezial height, avoiding both over-shortening and residual subsidence) before securing the button/knot; some techniques additionally weave the suture through a strip of FCR or the joint capsule to reinforce the construct and pack any remaining space with a rolled capsular or tendon-remnant "anchovy."
8. **For CMC arthrodesis (Eaton II-III, no STT involvement):** prepare the CMC joint surfaces by removing remaining cartilage down to bleeding subchondral bone, position the thumb metacarpal in the templated fusion position (~30-40° palmar abduction, ~10-15° radial abduction, slight pronation), and achieve rigid fixation with a mini fragment plate and screws, crossed headless screws, or a combined K-wire/tension-band construct; confirm position and compression fluoroscopically before final tightening.
9. **For implant arthroplasty** (selected cases only): prepare the metacarpal and trapezial/STT bed per the specific implant system, size and place trial and then final components, and confirm stable, well-tensioned range of motion without impingement or instability before final fixation — used selectively given higher revision rates relative to trapeziectomy-based options.
10. **Test and tension.** For all soft-tissue suspension techniques, hold the thumb metacarpal reduced (restoring near-normal trapezial space height, avoiding excessive shortening that predisposes to metacarpal-scaphoid impingement) while tensioning and securing the construct; under WALANT, actively test thumb opposition, pinch, and CMC stability before closure.
11. **Close in layers**, repairing the capsule over the reconstruction where tissue allows, then subcutaneous and skin closure; apply a well-padded thumb spica splint before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify the radial artery and superficial radial sensory nerve branches early in the approach, before deepening dissection toward the joint capsule — both are frequently at risk in this exposure and are easier to protect once clearly visualized than to search for after bleeding or retraction obscures the field.
- Excise the trapezium in a subperiosteal, extracapsular plane to protect the FCR tendon groove on its volar surface — inadvertent FCR injury during trapeziectomy is an avoidable technical error.
- Confirm complete trapezial excision by directly visualizing/palpating the scaphoid distal pole and first metacarpal base — an incompletely excised trapezial remnant is a recognized cause of persistent pain and can mimic a failed reconstruction.
- When tensioning any suspension construct (LRTI or suture-suspensionplasty), avoid over-shortening the trapeziectomy space — restoring near-anatomic height reduces the risk of scapho-metacarpal impingement, a source of late pain even after an otherwise successful reconstruction.
- Given that current evidence (including a 2025 network meta-analysis of 21 RCTs) shows no clinically important difference in mid-term outcomes among simple trapeziectomy, LRTI, and suspensionplasty, choose the technique you can execute most reliably and efficiently rather than assuming a more complex reconstruction is inherently superior — operative time and donor-site morbidity are real costs without a proven durability benefit.
- For arthrodesis, template and confirm the fusion position under fluoroscopy before final fixation — a fused thumb in too little palmar abduction functionally cripples opposition, while excessive abduction impairs adjacent-finger pinch.
- Under WALANT, use active intraoperative pinch and opposition testing to confirm construct stability and adequate thumb positioning before closing — this is a meaningful advantage over assessing tension purely on a passively anesthetized thumb.
- Preserve and later repair a capsular flap where tissue quality allows; even when not the primary stabilizer, capsular reinforcement over the reconstruction adds a layer of soft-tissue support and helps contain the interposition/suspension construct.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Multiple RCTs, including long-term follow-up data, show LRTI does not reliably outperform simple trapeziectomy or trapeziectomy with simple hematoma-distraction arthroplasty on validated outcome scores, while adding donor-site morbidity and operative time — do not assume LRTI is automatically superior to simple trapeziectomy.
- 2022-2024 RCTs (Morais et al. 2022; Graesser et al. 2024) show suspensionplasty achieves comparable pain relief, grip/pinch strength, and patient-reported outcomes to LRTI at 1-year follow-up with generally faster early recovery.
- A 2025 network meta-analysis (Challoumas et al.) of 21 RCTs found no clinically important differences among simple trapeziectomy, LRTI, and suspensionplasty in mid-term outcomes — technique choice should weigh surgeon experience, operative time, and recovery profile over an assumed superiority of any single reconstruction.
- CMC arthrodesis nonunion rates are historically 10-25% depending on technique/fixation — counsel patients accordingly.
- Implant arthroplasty carries higher revision rates compared with trapeziectomy-based procedures, which is why it has fallen out of favor in most centers.
- Other recognized complications: persistent pain/incomplete relief, loss of pinch/grip strength (variable by technique), scapho-metacarpal impingement/subsidence of the trapeziectomy space over time, sensory branch (radial sensory/palmar cutaneous median) neuroma, hardware-related symptoms with arthrodesis fixation, and implant loosening/dislocation with prosthetic arthroplasty.
- Incomplete trapezial excision leaves a bony remnant that can cause persistent impingement pain and is easily mistaken for a "failed" soft-tissue reconstruction rather than an incomplete bony resection.
- Overlooking preoperative thumb MCP hyperextension instability and failing to address it concomitantly can leave the thumb ray functionally unstable despite a technically sound CMC reconstruction.
- Injury to the FCR tendon (volar trapezial groove) or the radial artery during trapeziectomy are avoidable technical complications tied directly to dissection plane discipline.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Trapeziectomy ± suspensionplasty/LRTI: thumb spica splint/cast immobilization 3-4 weeks (shorter for suture-suspensionplasty in many protocols given internal stabilization, longer for tendon-graft-based LRTI), then transition to a removable splint with progressive AROM 4-6 weeks.
- Strengthening begins at 6-8 weeks; light activity resumes around 6 weeks; full unrestricted grip/heavy lifting at 3 months.
- CMC arthrodesis: immobilization 6-8 weeks until radiographic union, then progressive mobilization of adjacent joints (IP, wrist) that were spared, with heavy loading deferred to approximately 3 months.
