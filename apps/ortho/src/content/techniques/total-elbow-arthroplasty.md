## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.**
- Advanced elbow osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, or post-traumatic arthritis in lower-demand, typically older (>65–70) patients.
- Comminuted distal humerus fractures in the elderly, not amenable to stable internal fixation.
- Young patients with systemic inflammatory arthritis and low functional demand who accept the lifelong lifting restrictions.

**Contraindications.**
- Absolute: active local or systemic infection, and an absent or irreparably compromised triceps/extensor mechanism (in a linked design this can sometimes still be tolerated functionally, but risk-benefit shifts heavily against surgery).
- Relative: age under roughly 60–65 with high functional demand — TEA carries substantially higher long-term loosening/revision rates in this group from activity-related mechanical stress on the linkage and bone-implant interface, and is generally reserved for low-demand elderly patients or select young patients with systemic inflammatory arthritis who accept the restrictions; heavy laborers/manual workers unwilling to accept a lifelong lifting restriction (<2.3–4.5 kg / 5–10 lb); active local skin compromise or poor soft-tissue envelope (the elbow's thin soft-tissue coverage already elevates infection risk relative to lower-extremity arthroplasty); and inadequate bone stock for stem fixation without adjunctive grafting/revision-length stems.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/templating**: AP/lateral (± oblique) elbow radiographs for baseline assessment of bone stock, deformity, and implant sizing; CT for complex bone loss, prior trauma/hardware, or revision planning.
- **Implant selection**: choose constraint type based on collateral ligament integrity and bone stock — linked/semiconstrained designs (e.g., Coonrad-Morrey) for most degenerative and inflammatory indications (forgiving of collateral ligament deficiency, offers superior stability with acceptable constraint-related loosening rates); unlinked designs when collateral ligaments are intact and native kinematics/bone preservation are prioritized, accepting a higher instability risk.
- **Instrumentation/equipment**: humeral and ulnar broaching/reaming system matched to the chosen implant, trial components spanning a range of sizes, cement and cementing instrumentation (most designs are cemented), and revision-length stem/allograft contingency for unexpected bone loss.
- **Prior hardware**: review for retained plates/screws from prior fracture fixation that will need removal, and plan the exposure and hardware-extraction sequence accordingly.
- **Nerve assessment**: document baseline ulnar nerve function preoperatively — the ulnar nerve is mobilized in nearly every approach and postoperative neuropathy needs a documented baseline for comparison.
- **Medical optimization**: infection risk is inherently higher at the elbow than lower-extremity arthroplasty given the thin soft-tissue envelope, so skin condition, diabetes control, and smoking cessation deserve particular attention; obtain rheumatology clearance/disease-activity optimization in inflammatory arthritis patients on immunosuppression, coordinating a perioperative hold per standard protocols.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia often combined with a regional block (supraclavicular or infraclavicular brachial plexus block) for intraoperative and postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: lateral decubitus or supine with the arm across the chest/over a bolster, depending on surgeon preference and approach; a well-padded bump or arm-positioning device is used to hold the elbow in flexion, with a sterile tourniquet applied high on the arm.
- **Table setup**: an arm board or arm-positioning tower to stabilize the extremity in flexion throughout the exposure and component implantation; tourniquet inflated after exsanguination, with the pressure and duration documented (release before final closure for hemostasis, or per surgeon protocol).
- **Draping**: circumferential prep and drape of the entire upper extremity from above the tourniquet to the hand, allowing free intraoperative flexion/extension for exposure and trialing.
- **Landmarks**: mark the olecranon tip, medial and lateral epicondyles, and the course of the ulnar nerve (palpable in the cubital tunnel posterior to the medial epicondyle) before incision; the standard posterior midline skin incision is made slightly lateral to the tip of the olecranon to keep the scar off the subcutaneous olecranon border, then curved to avoid crossing directly over the medial epicondyle.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy available for stem/cement positioning confirmation in revision or complex cases; have hardware-removal instrumentation on the field if prior fixation is present.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision**: posterior midline incision curving slightly around (not directly over) the olecranon tip and medial epicondyle, roughly 12–15 cm in length centered over the joint.
2. **Ulnar nerve identification and mobilization**: identify the ulnar nerve proximally in the intermuscular septum, trace it through the cubital tunnel, and mobilize it distally to (and past) its first motor branch into the flexor carpi ulnaris; isolate it with a vessel loop and protect it throughout the remainder of the case — most surgeons transpose it anteriorly (subcutaneous or submuscular) at the end of the procedure. Do not hang instruments or retractors from the vessel loop, which risks a traction neurapraxia.
3. **Extensor mechanism approach**: elevate full-thickness medial and lateral flaps off the triceps and forearm fascia; then take down the triceps insertion using the surgeon's preferred technique:
   - **Triceps-reflecting (Bryan-Morrey)**: elevate the triceps, along with the periosteum and the anconeus in continuity, off the olecranon from medial to lateral, leaving the extensor mechanism attached laterally and preserving a repairable sleeve.
   - **Triceps-splitting**: split the triceps tendon and muscle in line with its fibers, retracting medially and laterally, preserving a central attachment.
   - **Triceps-sparing (Alonso-Llames/extracapsular)**: develop medial and lateral windows around an intact triceps insertion without detaching it, reducing extensor-mechanism healing risk at the cost of more limited exposure — used selectively, more often in fracture cases than in stiff/deformed arthritic elbows.
4. **Collateral ligament and capsular release**: release the medial and lateral collateral ligaments from the humerus (necessary for dislocation/subluxation of the joint and full exposure in linked designs, since the implant itself provides constraint) and excise the diseased synovium/capsule; in unlinked designs the collateral ligaments must instead be preserved and protected.
5. **Joint subluxation/dislocation**: with the extensor mechanism reflected and collateral ligaments released, subluxate or dislocate the ulnohumeral joint to expose the distal humerus and proximal ulna articular surfaces.
6. **Humeral preparation**: resect/prepare the distal humerus per the implant system, remove the olecranon fossa (often fenestrated or removed entirely to accept the linked component's hinge mechanism), and broach/ream the humeral canal to the templated stem size, respecting the humerus's natural anterior bow.
7. **Ulnar preparation**: resect the tip of the olecranon and/or coronoid as needed per the implant system, then broach/ream the ulnar canal to the templated stem size, taking care not to perforate the thin ulnar cortex, particularly in osteoporotic or previously-fractured bone.
8. **Trial reduction**: place trial humeral and ulnar components, link them (for linked designs), and assess flexion-extension arc, varus/valgus stability, and clearance of the olecranon/coronoid from their respective fossae without impingement.
9. **Cementing and final implantation**: cement the humeral and ulnar components (most contemporary designs are cemented) with attention to cement pressurization and complete canal filling while protecting the ulnar nerve and surrounding soft tissue from thermal injury; link the components per the implant's locking mechanism.
10. **Extensor mechanism repair**: repair the triceps to the olecranon/ulna using a secure transosseous or suture-anchor technique matched to the takedown method (Bryan-Morrey reflection, splitting, or sparing), testing repair integrity through a gentle passive flexion-extension arc before closure.
11. **Ulnar nerve transposition**: complete the anterior transposition (subcutaneous or submuscular per surgeon preference) if planned, ensuring the nerve has a tension-free, kink-free course and is not compressed by the transposed flap or fascial sling.
12. **Closure**: release the tourniquet and achieve hemostasis before final closure; close fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers; a drain is commonly used given the elbow's limited soft-tissue reserve for hematoma absorption.
13. **Immobilization**: apply a well-padded posterior splint or extension-block splint with the elbow in near-full extension (or per surgeon protocol) to protect the triceps repair and control postoperative swelling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Mobilize the ulnar nerve past its first motor branch into flexor carpi ulnaris, not just through the cubital tunnel, to allow a truly tension-free anterior transposition and avoid a kink at the fascial edge.
- Never suspend or hang retractors, sponges, or instruments from the ulnar nerve vessel loop — this is a well-recognized, entirely avoidable cause of postoperative ulnar neurapraxia.
- Match the extensor-mechanism takedown technique to the case: the Bryan-Morrey triceps-reflecting approach gives the most reliable exposure for stiff or deformed arthritic elbows, while triceps-sparing approaches are best reserved for cases (often fracture) where the extensor mechanism is not the limiting factor for exposure.
- Remove or fenestrate the olecranon fossa generously in linked designs — inadequate bony clearance here is a common cause of mechanical impingement/restricted flexion at trial reduction.
- Respect the humerus's natural anterior bow when broaching; forcing a straight broach can perforate the anterior cortex, particularly in shorter or more bowed humeri.
- Protect the thin ulnar cortex during ulnar canal preparation — perforation here is more common than on the humeral side and can compromise stem fixation.
- Test the triceps repair with a gentle passive flexion arc intraoperatively before closing, so any early gapping or insecure fixation can be reinforced before the wound is closed.
- Counsel patients on the lifelong lifting restriction before surgery, not just postoperatively — the restriction is a direct consequence of the linked implant's polyethylene bushing wear tolerance, not a temporary rehabilitation-phase limitation, and framing this preoperatively avoids postoperative dissatisfaction.
- In patients with prior hardware, plan the extraction sequence before beginning canal preparation — hardware removal can meaningfully change the exposure and bone-stock picture from what CT alone suggested.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Aseptic loosening is the dominant long-term failure mode, accelerated by activity/loading — the central reason for lifting restrictions and the relative contraindication in young/high-demand patients.
- Infection risk (≈3–5%) is higher than lower-extremity arthroplasty, given the thin soft-tissue envelope around the elbow.
- Triceps insufficiency/rupture is a recognized complication of extensor-mechanism takedown and repair, particularly with triceps-splitting/reflecting approaches — protect the repair per the postoperative restrictions below.
- Ulnar neuropathy can result from nerve mobilization, retraction, thermal injury during cementing, or postoperative transposition-site compression; document a preoperative baseline for comparison.
- Periprosthetic fracture can occur intraoperatively (canal perforation during broaching, particularly on the thin ulnar cortex) or postoperatively (fall onto a constrained, less-compliant construct).
- Bushing/polyethylene wear requiring revision is the expected long-term consequence of activity/loading beyond the recommended lifting restriction in linked designs.
- In patients under roughly 60–65, the loosening/revision-rate penalty from mechanical stress on the linkage is substantial enough to favor interposition arthroplasty instead when the patient will not accept lifelong restrictions.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Splint/sling immobilization 1–2 weeks (extension-block or posterior splint per surgeon preference).
- Gentle active-assisted ROM from 2 weeks; avoid resisted extension against the triceps repair for 6–8 weeks if a triceps-splitting/reflecting approach was used.
- Lifelong lifting-restriction counseling begins at the first postoperative visit — a definitive restriction, not merely a rehab-phase limit.
- Return to light ADLs 6–8 weeks; permanent avoidance of repetitive lifting >2.3–4.5 kg.
- Target functional outcome: flexion contracture <30°, functional arc 30–130°.
