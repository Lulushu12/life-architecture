## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:** Chronic PJI with an identified organism preoperatively, an adequate soft-tissue envelope for primary closure, absence of gross purulence or extensive bone loss requiring staged reconstruction, and a host fit for a single larger procedure. Historically reserved for a narrow European (originally Endo-Klinik) cohort; indications have broadened in recent registries and RCT data.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Unknown or culture-negative organism preoperatively — one-stage depends on being able to select definitive systemic and local antibiotic therapy against a known pathogen; proceeding "blind" removes the main safety margin of the strategy.
- Inadequate soft-tissue envelope for a tension-free primary closure, active sinus tract with extensive undermining, or need for flap coverage — plan a staged approach instead so soft-tissue reconstruction is not rushed.
- Gross intraoperative purulence or extensive bone loss anticipated to require structural allograft/augments — favors a staged approach where reconstruction can be planned with known bone stock.
- Multidrug-resistant or otherwise difficult-to-treat organisms (fungi, resistant Gram-negatives) unless the team has confirmed the ability to achieve adequate local and systemic coverage against that specific organism.
- Host unable to tolerate a single longer anesthetic and larger blood loss (severe cardiopulmonary disease, marked frailty) — a two-stage approach spreads physiologic burden across two shorter procedures.
- Lack of team/institutional infrastructure for immediate intraoperative antibiogram-guided cementing and a genuine "clean phase" transition (separate instrument sets, regowning, redraping) — this is as much a determinant of candidacy as the clinical picture.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Organism identification is the central preoperative task.** Confirm at least one, ideally two concordant, preoperative aspirations/cultures with a clear antibiogram before committing to one-stage; if the organism is unclear, default to two-stage rather than proceeding on a presumptive diagnosis.
- **Imaging/templating:** Full revision templating as for any major exchange — radiographs (and CT where bone loss is a concern) to assess implant fixation, bone stock, osteolysis, and anticipated extraction difficulty; template both femoral/acetabular (hip) or femoral/tibial (knee) sizing for the reimplantation components across a range in case intraoperative bone loss changes the plan.
- **Implant selection:** Have a full range of revision-capable implants available (stems of varying length/offset, augments, cones/sleeves as needed) since bone loss is only fully assessed after explantation — the reimplantation plan must be able to flex based on what is found.
- **Two entirely separate sterile setups must be organized in advance:** one for explantation/debridement and one for reimplantation. Both trays may be opened before the patient arrives to save time, but the reimplantation tray must remain covered with a sterile drape and untouched until the clean phase begins.
- **Cement and local antibiotic plan:** Decide in advance (with pharmacy/ID input) which antibiotics will be hand-mixed into the definitive cement based on the known organism's sensitivity, and confirm heat-stable agents are used (vancomycin, aminoglycosides) since the cement polymerization exotherm will inactivate heat-labile antibiotics.
- **Staffing and logistics:** Confirm operating room turnover capacity for a genuine mid-case "clean" transition — a second scrub team or at minimum a full regowning/reglvoing and redraping protocol, restocked prep solution, and a plan for how long the wound will be temporarily closed/dressed between phases.
- **Consent and counseling:** Discuss with the patient that this is treated internally as two sequential operations under one anesthetic, with a realistic discussion of increased operative time/blood loss compared with either DAIR or a single stage of a two-stage protocol.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** General anesthesia is generally preferred over neuraxial alone given the anticipated length of the combined procedure; a neuraxial technique may still be used as an adjunct for postoperative analgesia. Anticipate a longer case than a standard revision and plan blood product availability accordingly.
- **Positioning:** Standard revision arthroplasty positioning for the joint involved (lateral or supine with positioner for the hip; supine with leg holder/tourniquet mount for the knee) — position to allow full access for both the explantation exposure and any extended exposure (extended trochanteric osteotomy, quadriceps snip/turndown) that bone loss or scarring may require.
- **Draping:** Prep and drape wide enough to accommodate an extensile approach if needed. Because the case is treated as two sequential operations, plan the draping so a full re-prep and re-drape with fresh drapes is genuinely feasible at the clean-phase transition, not merely a change of gloves over the same drapes.
- **Tourniquet:** Use per surgeon preference for the knee exposure, released prior to the clean-phase lavage/closure check and for the reimplantation phase to allow assessment of tissue perfusion.
- **Landmarks:** Mark the prior incision and standard bony landmarks before the first prep; the same landmarks are used to re-orient after redraping for the reimplantation phase.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Phase 1 — explantation and debridement**

1. Incise through the prior scar; excise any sinus tract in continuity with the deep dissection.
2. Expose the joint and obtain deep tissue cultures from multiple sites before any lavage, even though the organism is presumptively known — confirm intraoperative concordance with preoperative cultures and detect any additional organism.
3. Complete removal of all components and cement. This is frequently the most time-consuming portion of the case — allow for extended exposure techniques (extended trochanteric osteotomy for a well-fixed femoral stem, tibial tubercle osteotomy or extensile quadriceps exposure for a stiff/scarred knee) rather than forcing extraction through an inadequate window.
4. Perform extensive debridement of infected and necrotic tissue and bone, including complete synovectomy and excision of any devitalized bone at the cement-bone or component-bone interface.
5. Perform thorough pulsatile lavage of the wound.
6. Assess remaining bone stock and confirm the reimplantation plan (implant sizes, augments, need for bone graft) is still valid — adjust if bone loss is more extensive than anticipated.
7. Achieve provisional hemostasis and temporarily close or loosely approximate the wound with a sterile dressing.

**Clean-phase transition**

8. Treat this as the end of one operation and the start of a second: the explantation instruments and drapes are entirely removed from the field.
9. The entire surgical team regowns and regloves; the operating room undergoes a fresh clean-down.
10. The patient is re-prepped with fresh antiseptic solution and re-draped with new sterile drapes.
11. A new, previously unopened/covered instrument and implant set — the reimplantation tray — is opened only now.
12. The wound is reopened, any temporary sutures/staples discarded, and a further complete lavage of the entire surgical field is performed before any reimplantation instrument touches the wound.

**Phase 2 — reimplantation**

13. Hand-mix antibiotics into the definitive cement (or select the appropriate antibiotic-loaded implant coating/local adjunct) guided by the known organism's sensitivity — use heat-stable agents only, since the cement exotherm inactivates heat-labile antibiotics.
14. Reimplant the definitive components, respecting the same fixation principles as any complex revision (cementless press-fit with adjunctive screws/augments for major bone loss, or antibiotic-loaded cement fixation where cementing is planned).
15. Consider adjunctive local antibiotic delivery — antibiotic-loaded calcium sulfate/hydroxyapatite beads or intrawound vancomycin powder — to raise local concentrations above the biofilm minimum biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC), recognizing that recent high-quality RCT data have found no significant difference in infection/wound-complication outcomes for some of these specific adjuncts, so they should be viewed as reasonable-but-unproven rather than mandated.
16. Check final stability and range of motion through a full functional arc.
17. Close in layers with a tension-free closure; consider plastic surgery input intraoperatively if soft tissue is marginal despite preoperative screening.
18. Start (or continue) empiric broad-spectrum systemic antibiotics, narrowing to targeted therapy once final cultures/sensitivities return; add rifampin combination therapy (never as monotherapy) for confirmed rifampin-susceptible staphylococcal biofilm once the wound has stabilized/is closed.
19. Plan for a minimum 12-week total systemic antibiotic duration.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Open both instrument trays' outer wrap before the patient is brought to the room to save turnover time, but keep the reimplantation tray's sterile inner wrap closed and covered until the clean phase genuinely begins — this preserves the infection-control logic of the "two operations" concept while still saving real time.
- Do not force a stem or cup out through an inadequate exposure to save time — commit early to an extended trochanteric osteotomy (hip) or extensile quadriceps exposure (knee) if extraction is proving difficult; a controlled planned osteotomy heals predictably, an uncontrolled fracture during forced extraction does not.
- Take confirmatory deep cultures at explantation even when the organism is "known" from preoperative work-up — polymicrobial or unexpected additional organisms are found often enough to change postoperative antibiotic selection.
- Hand-mix cement antibiotics fresh at the time of reimplantation rather than relying on premixed low-dose antibiotic cement — the point of one-stage is organism-specific, high-dose local therapy, and generic premixed cement typically under-doses for this purpose.
- Reassess bone stock honestly after debridement before committing to the reimplantation plan — one-stage should not be forced through if debridement reveals bone loss that would be better served by a staged reconstruction; be willing to convert intraoperatively to a spacer if the wound is genuinely unsalvageable in a single stage.
- Keep the clean-phase transition genuinely disciplined — a full regowning, redraping, and fresh lavage, not just a glove change over the same drapes — since the infection-control benefit of the two-phase concept depends on actually executing it as two separate procedures.
- Have vancomycin- and aminoglycoside-sensitivity confirmed before the case so the definitive cement mix does not have to be guessed intraoperatively; a common working combination is on the order of 2 g vancomycin and comparable aminoglycoside dosing per 40 g cement pack, adjusted to the specific organism and cement volume needed.
- Build in a low threshold to abandon the one-stage plan intraoperatively (convert to spacer/staged) if debridement reveals findings inconsistent with the preoperative plan — this is a legitimate intraoperative pivot, not a failure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Success depends heavily on preoperative organism identification and adequate soft tissue — two-stage remains the default for unknown-organism, poor-soft-tissue, or highly resistant-organism chronic PJI.
- Requires immediate intraoperative antibiogram-guided cementing capability and surgical team experience/infrastructure to execute safely — this determines whether a team should attempt one-stage rather than the clinical picture alone.
- Forcing implant extraction through an inadequate exposure risks uncontrolled periprosthetic fracture — plan extended exposures proactively rather than reactively.
- A rushed or incomplete "clean phase" (skipping full regowning/redraping, inadequate repeat lavage) undermines the entire rationale of the strategy and risks reinoculating the wound with the same organism being treated.
- Using heat-labile antibiotics in hand-mixed cement is ineffective — the cement polymerization exotherm denatures many agents; confirm the chosen agents are heat-stable (vancomycin, aminoglycosides) before mixing.
- The historical dogma that two-stage is the unconditional "gold standard" is being revised: a 2024 pooled meta-analysis (40 studies, ~8,700 patients) found no significant difference in reinfection or reoperation rates between one- and two-stage approaches overall, and a 2024 multicenter RCT (AAHKS-presented) reported 2-year success of 98% for one-stage vs 91% for two-stage — including patients with resistant organisms and draining wounds — though this remains a single trial requiring confirmation.
- The deciding factor for one- vs two-stage is patient/organism selection (known organism, adequate soft tissue, team infrastructure), not a fixed hierarchy of stages.
- Longer combined operative time and greater blood loss than either DAIR or a single stage of a two-stage protocol — plan blood product availability and counsel the patient accordingly.
- Postoperative wound complications remain the main driver of failure when soft tissue was borderline preoperatively — do not skip plastic surgery consultation preoperatively if there is any doubt about closure.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not detail a distinct rehabilitation pathway for one-stage exchange — its rehabilitation section is explicitly organized around DAIR versus two-stage pathways. The antibiotic strategy follows the same systemic framework as the rest of the treatment algorithm: minimum 12-week total systemic antibiotic course (the ICM 2025-endorsed floor), with indefinite suppressive therapy considered in McPherson host C, difficult-to-treat organisms, or incomplete source control. Serial CRP/ESR monitoring and wound surveillance at follow-up apply as for any PJI treatment pathway, with a low threshold to re-image or re-aspirate if pain, swelling, drainage, or a marker plateau/rebound occurs. In practice, early rehabilitation generally follows a standard post-revision-arthroplasty protocol (protected weight-bearing guided by fixation method and any structural bone-grafting/augmentation used), progressing to full weight-bearing and strengthening as wound healing and antibiotic response allow.
