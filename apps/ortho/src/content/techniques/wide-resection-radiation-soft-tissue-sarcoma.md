## Indications

- Wide resection with a negative margin is the cornerstone of treatment for soft tissue sarcoma (STS).
- (Neo)adjuvant radiotherapy is added for large (>5 cm), deep, high-grade tumors to reduce local recurrence.
- Neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy is considered for high-risk histology/grade extremity STS (informed by trials such as ISG-STS 1001).
- Low-grade or small superficial STS is generally managed with surgery alone, without radiation or chemotherapy.
- Any soft tissue mass >5 cm, or any deep mass regardless of size, or any growing/painful mass, should be worked up with MRI and referred to a treating center before excision — this referral threshold applies before the resection decision is made.

## Positioning & setup

MRI (with contrast) of the entire compartment for local staging — defining soft tissue extension, neurovascular relationship, and skip lesions — is essential before any biopsy or surgical planning. Biopsy (core needle preferred over incisional for most lesions) should be performed at, or in direct coordination with, the treating multidisciplinary sarcoma center, along a tract oriented longitudinally that the same team will later excise en bloc with the tumor during definitive resection.

## Steps

1. Stage locally with MRI of the entire compartment and stage systemically with CT chest (lung is the dominant site of hematogenous spread in most sarcomas).
2. Grade the tumor (differentiation, mitotic count, necrosis — FNCLCC system, most widely used); grade and size/depth drive prognosis more than histologic subtype in most extremity STS.
3. Biopsy at or in coordination with the treating center, with the tract planned as part of the eventual resection incision, avoiding crossing anatomic compartments or neurovascular structures.
4. For high-risk histology/grade extremity STS, consider neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy.
5. Decide preoperative versus postoperative radiation for large (>5 cm), deep, high-grade tumors: the classic randomized comparison (O'Sullivan et al.) found equivalent local control and survival between the two sequences, but a higher acute wound-complication rate with preoperative RT (~35% vs ~17%) and more late fibrosis/joint stiffness/lymphedema with postoperative RT — make this a case-by-case choice based on anticipated wound-healing risk versus late functional morbidity.
6. Perform wide resection with a negative margin (through normal tissue, with a cuff of uninvolved tissue circumferentially), incorporating the biopsy tract en bloc with the specimen.
7. For low-grade or small superficial STS, manage with surgery alone once the diagnosis is confident, without adding radiation or chemotherapy.
8. For lesions with MRI features atypical for a simple lipoma (thick/nodular septations >2 mm, non-fatty enhancing components, size >10 cm), obtain core needle biopsy and refer to a sarcoma center rather than proceeding directly to local excision.

## Pitfalls

- An "excisional biopsy" of a suspected liposarcoma or other STS performed outside a treating center risks the same referral-rule consequences as bone tumor biopsy — contaminated planes and the need for a larger re-resection.
- A poorly placed biopsy tract (wrong tract, contamination of an adjacent compartment, hematoma spread outside the planned resection field, transverse rather than longitudinal extremity incision) can convert a limb-salvage candidate into an amputation and independently worsen survival.
- Preoperative radiation carries a substantially higher acute wound-complication rate (~35% vs ~17%) than postoperative radiation; postoperative radiation carries more late fibrosis, joint stiffness, and lymphedema — neither sequence is uniformly superior, and the choice should be individualized.
- Regional lymphadenopathy is rare in sarcoma except in epithelioid sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma — a regional node check is specifically worthwhile for these histologies but should not be over-interpreted as a marker of spread in typical STS.
- Deep-to-fascia masses carry a higher liposarcoma/STS pretest probability than superficial masses of the same size — do not reassure based on size alone when a mass is deep to fascia.

## Post-op protocol

The monograph does not specify a phased weight-bearing/ROM protocol specific to STS resection; rehabilitation is dictated by the extent of resection and any reconstruction performed rather than the diagnosis itself, following the general oncologic reconstruction framework (protection → early mobilization → progressive loading → strengthening/return to function).

Post-treatment surveillance for high-grade STS: physical exam ± local MRI every 3–4 months years 1–3, then every 6 months years 4–5; CT chest every 3–4 months years 1–3, spacing out thereafter; total surveillance duration 5–10 years.
