## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Wide resection with a negative margin is the cornerstone of treatment for soft tissue sarcoma (STS).
- (Neo)adjuvant radiotherapy is added for large (>5 cm), deep, high-grade tumors to reduce local recurrence.
- Neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy is considered for high-risk histology/grade extremity STS (informed by trials such as ISG-STS 1001).
- Low-grade or small superficial STS is generally managed with surgery alone, without radiation or chemotherapy.
- Any soft tissue mass >5 cm, or any deep mass regardless of size, or any growing/painful mass, should be worked up with MRI and referred to a treating center before excision — this referral threshold applies before the resection decision is made.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Unresectable disease at presentation (extensive multicompartmental involvement, encasement of major neurovascular structures without a reconstructive option) — treat with systemic therapy/radiation with resection reconsidered if downstaging is achieved, or refer for specialized vascular/plastic co-management.
- Distant metastatic disease at presentation does not automatically preclude resection of the primary, but changes the goal of surgery (local control/palliation) and requires multidisciplinary discussion before committing to an aggressive resection.
- A prior "excisional biopsy" or unplanned marginal excision performed outside a treating center is not a contraindication to definitive treatment, but changes the plan: it typically mandates a wider secondary re-resection of the entire prior surgical bed (and often radiation) because the true margins and any residual disease are unknown.
- Medical unfitness for the planned magnitude of resection/reconstruction, or for perioperative radiation given expected wound-healing demands, should prompt multidisciplinary discussion of sequencing or alternative approaches.

## Preoperative Planning

- MRI (with contrast) of the entire compartment for local staging — defining soft tissue extension, neurovascular relationship, and skip lesions — is essential before any biopsy or surgical planning.
- Stage systemically with CT chest (lung is the dominant site of hematogenous spread in most sarcomas).
- Biopsy (core needle preferred over incisional for most lesions) should be performed at, or in direct coordination with, the treating multidisciplinary sarcoma center, along a tract oriented longitudinally that the same team will later excise en bloc with the tumor during definitive resection.
- Grade the tumor (differentiation, mitotic count, necrosis — FNCLCC system, most widely used) from the biopsy specimen; grade and size/depth drive prognosis more than histologic subtype in most extremity STS, and grade determines whether radiation/chemotherapy are added.
- Decide preoperative versus postoperative radiation sequencing before surgery, in a joint discussion with radiation oncology: the classic randomized comparison (O'Sullivan et al.) found equivalent local control and survival between the two sequences, but a higher acute wound-complication rate with preoperative RT (~35% vs ~17%) and more late fibrosis/joint stiffness/lymphedema with postoperative RT — this is a case-by-case choice based on anticipated wound-healing risk versus late functional morbidity, and should be settled ahead of the operative date since it changes the plan (RT is delivered before or after surgery, not decided intraoperatively).
- For high-risk histology/grade extremity STS, coordinate timing of neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy with medical oncology, with surgery scheduled after adequate count recovery.
- Plan the resection specimen boundaries and any expected reconstruction (local flap, free flap, skin graft) with plastic surgery involvement when a large defect or a compromised soft-tissue envelope (from preoperative radiation) is anticipated.
- Template imaging against named neurovascular and musculotendinous structures the resection will need to preserve or sacrifice, and consent the patient for the range of possible outcomes (nerve sacrifice, need for flap coverage, low but real amputation risk if margins prove inadequate).
- Order intraoperative frozen section availability if margin status at a critical structure (major nerve/vessel) may need real-time confirmation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia, often combined with a regional block for postoperative analgesia depending on the limb and planned dissection extent.
- Positioning is dictated by the tumor's anatomic location and the planned approach — commonly supine, lateral, or prone depending on compartment (posterior thigh/popliteal fossa lesions, for example, are approached prone or in a modified lateral position).
- Use a radiolucent table if intraoperative imaging may be needed, and position to allow extensile access along the full length of the involved compartment.
- Prep and drape the entire limb/compartment circumferentially, incorporating the biopsy tract and scar into the prepped field so the tract can be excised en bloc, oriented longitudinally with the limb axis.
- Mark surface landmarks (palpable mass margins if present, neurovascular bundle course, biopsy scar, planned incision) before draping, referencing the preoperative MRI for deep extent that is not palpable.
- If preoperative radiation was given, inspect and account for the irradiated skin/soft-tissue field in the incision and closure plan — irradiated tissue heals poorly and closure/coverage decisions should anticipate this.
- Have plastic surgery and their equipment (flap sets) available in the room or on standby if reconstruction is anticipated.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Stage locally with MRI of the entire compartment and stage systemically with CT chest (lung is the dominant site of hematogenous spread in most sarcomas).
2. Grade the tumor (differentiation, mitotic count, necrosis — FNCLCC system, most widely used); grade and size/depth drive prognosis more than histologic subtype in most extremity STS.
3. Biopsy at or in coordination with the treating center, with the tract planned as part of the eventual resection incision, avoiding crossing anatomic compartments or neurovascular structures.
4. For high-risk histology/grade extremity STS, complete neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy as planned before proceeding to surgery.
5. Complete the chosen radiation sequence — preoperative radiation is delivered and surgery scheduled after an appropriate interval (typically several weeks) to allow acute radiation effects to settle before incision, or postoperative radiation is deferred until after resection and adequate wound healing.
6. Incise, incorporating the biopsy tract en bloc with the specimen, and develop the approach in the plane(s) that will achieve a wide margin without entering the tumor's pseudocapsule/reactive zone.
7. Identify the major neurovascular structures of the compartment in normal tissue proximal and distal to the tumor first, then trace them past the mass, preserving them when not directly involved and planning formal resection/reconstruction when they are.
8. Develop the dissection circumferentially around the tumor, maintaining a cuff of grossly normal tissue on all sides (through normal tissue, with a cuff of uninvolved tissue circumferentially) — a wide margin, not a marginal shell-out along the pseudocapsule.
9. Ligate and divide feeding vessels and any muscle origins/insertions that must be sacrificed to achieve the margin, working systematically around the specimen rather than leaving one difficult margin (typically the deepest, against bone or major vessel) for last under time pressure.
10. Remove the specimen en bloc with the biopsy tract, orient it for the pathologist (ink or suture-mark key margins, particularly the closest anatomic margin such as the neurovascular bundle or periosteum), and send for permanent margin assessment; use frozen section intraoperatively if a specific margin's adequacy will change the immediate operative plan (e.g., whether to sacrifice a nerve).
11. Achieve hemostasis in the resection bed, which can be extensive given the size of many STS specimens; place drain(s) as needed.
12. Reconstruct the defect as planned — primary closure, local muscle/fasciocutaneous flap, free flap, or skin graft — coordinating with plastic surgery for anything beyond straightforward primary closure, and especially in a previously or soon-to-be irradiated field.
13. For low-grade or small superficial STS, manage with surgery alone once the diagnosis is confident, without adding radiation or chemotherapy.
14. For lesions with MRI features atypical for a simple lipoma (thick/nodular septations >2 mm, non-fatty enhancing components, size >10 cm), obtain core needle biopsy and refer to a sarcoma center rather than proceeding directly to local excision.
15. If postoperative radiation was the chosen sequence, coordinate its initiation with radiation oncology once the wound has healed sufficiently to tolerate it.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify the compartment's major neurovascular bundle in clean, uninvolved tissue away from the tumor first, then trace it toward and past the mass — this is safer and faster than searching for it directly adjacent to distorted, tumor-involved anatomy.
- Work circumferentially and systematically around the specimen rather than leaving the technically hardest margin (often the deep margin against bone, joint, or a major vessel) until last under time pressure — plan the operative sequence so the difficult margin is addressed with full attention and, if needed, frozen section support.
- Orient the specimen and mark the closest anatomic margins for the pathologist at the time of removal — a positive margin discovered days later on permanent section is far more actionable if the surgeon can say exactly which anatomic surface it corresponds to.
- When preoperative radiation has been given, expect tissue planes to be more fibrotic and less distinct; dissect more deliberately and anticipate slower, more meticulous hemostasis.
- Involve plastic surgery early (before the operative date, not intraoperatively) whenever the defect, location, or radiation field suggests flap coverage may be needed — this avoids an intraoperative scramble and allows proper flap planning/consent.
- Keep the incision longitudinal along the limb axis and incorporate the biopsy tract precisely — a transverse or off-axis incision, or leaving the biopsy tract unexcised, both compromise the oncologic margin regardless of how clean the deep dissection is.
- For retroperitoneal or deep truncal sarcomas, plan the approach and any needed adjacent organ resection (en bloc) with the relevant surgical subspecialty preoperatively — these resections are rarely a single-surgeon operation.
- Send frozen section on any margin whose status will change the immediate plan (e.g., whether to sacrifice a nerve or extend to a more radical margin) — deciding this preoperatively as part of the consent conversation avoids an intraoperative ethical/consent gap.
- Document estimated blood loss and transfusion needs proactively for large compartmental resections; these cases can be substantially more hemorrhagic than their skin incision suggests.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- An "excisional biopsy" of a suspected liposarcoma or other STS performed outside a treating center risks the same referral-rule consequences as bone tumor biopsy — contaminated planes and the need for a larger re-resection.
- A poorly placed biopsy tract (wrong tract, contamination of an adjacent compartment, hematoma spread outside the planned resection field, transverse rather than longitudinal extremity incision) can convert a limb-salvage candidate into an amputation and independently worsen survival.
- Preoperative radiation carries a substantially higher acute wound-complication rate (~35% vs ~17%) than postoperative radiation; postoperative radiation carries more late fibrosis, joint stiffness, and lymphedema — neither sequence is uniformly superior, and the choice should be individualized.
- Regional lymphadenopathy is rare in sarcoma except in epithelioid sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma — a regional node check is specifically worthwhile for these histologies but should not be over-interpreted as a marker of spread in typical STS.
- Deep-to-fascia masses carry a higher liposarcoma/STS pretest probability than superficial masses of the same size — do not reassure based on size alone when a mass is deep to fascia.
- Leaving a difficult deep margin (against bone, joint capsule, or a major vessel) under-dissected due to time pressure or fear of injury is a recognized, avoidable cause of a positive margin — plan explicitly for this margin rather than treating it as the last, rushed step.
- Inadequate hemostasis in a large resection bed can produce a postoperative hematoma that both compromises wound healing (particularly in an irradiated or soon-to-be-irradiated field) and complicates subsequent radiation planning — achieve and confirm hemostasis before closure.
- Wound-healing complications are meaningfully more common after preoperative radiation and should be anticipated in postoperative counseling and follow-up scheduling, not treated as unexpected findings.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not specify a phased weight-bearing/ROM protocol specific to STS resection; rehabilitation is dictated by the extent of resection and any reconstruction performed rather than the diagnosis itself, following the general oncologic reconstruction framework (protection → early mobilization → progressive loading → strengthening/return to function).

Post-treatment surveillance for high-grade STS: physical exam ± local MRI every 3–4 months years 1–3, then every 6 months years 4–5; CT chest every 3–4 months years 1–3, spacing out thereafter; total surveillance duration 5–10 years.
