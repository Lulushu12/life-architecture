## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- 1–2 level radiculopathy in younger patients with preserved disc height, minimal facet arthropathy, no significant kyphosis, and no instability.
- Motion preservation is the point of the procedure — it is designed to reduce (not eliminate) adjacent segment degeneration risk relative to fusion.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Significant facet arthropathy (facet-mediated pain will not be addressed by an implant that preserves facet loading, and degenerate facets are a major heterotopic-ossification and subsidence risk).
- Kyphotic segmental alignment — the implant needs a reasonably lordotic or neutral segment to load correctly.
- Ossification or bridging osteophyte across the disc space, or pre-existing ankylosis.
- Prior fusion at the level (adjacent to a fused level is fine; at the level itself is not).
- Osteoporosis — inadequate endplate bone quality risks subsidence.
- Active infection, local or systemic.
- Myelopathy or predominant central/OPLL pathology — CDA is a radiculopathy/soft-disc procedure, not a decompressive strategy for cord compression from a rigid mass.

## Preoperative Planning

- MRI to confirm disc height preservation, absence of significant facet degeneration, and pathology confined to 1–2 levels; CT to assess facet joint arthropathy morphology and rule out bridging osteophyte or endplate sclerosis that would compromise implant seating.
- Flexion-extension radiographs to confirm segmental mobility (a level that is already auto-fused or severely stiff is a poor arthroplasty candidate) and to rule out instability.
- **Implant selection and sizing**: choose device footprint (AP depth and width) to maximize endplate coverage without overhang, and select the core height/design that restores, but does not overdistract, native disc height — oversized implants increase facet loading and subsidence/expulsion risk, undersized implants risk migration.
- Templating on preoperative imaging for the anticipated implant size range and midline landmarks; have the full range of trial sizers and at least one size up/down available intraoperatively since final sizing is often only accurate once endplates are prepared.
- Confirm segmental alignment goal — the implant should sit centered at the natural center of rotation of the segment, which is a planning consideration distinct from simple disc-height restoration.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General endotracheal anesthesia; standard anterior cervical positioning as for ACDF — supine, shoulders taped caudally for fluoroscopic access to lower levels, slight extension on a shoulder roll, head neutral on a horseshoe/gel doughnut.
- Because CDA is reserved for radiculopathy rather than high-grade stenosis/myelopathy, the extension precautions used for severe stenosis are less critical here, but avoid unnecessary hyperextension regardless.
- Biplanar (AP and lateral) fluoroscopy access is essential throughout the case, more so than in ACDF, since accurate midline determination and implant centering under both views drives the result — position the C-arm and confirm clean AP and lateral shots before draping.
- Mark true midline preoperatively using bony landmarks (spinous process alignment on AP fluoroscopy) since rotational malalignment of the implant is a major technical pitfall.
- Prep and drape as for ACDF, chin to sternal notch.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm patient selection intraoperatively** one final time: 1–2 level radiculopathy, preserved disc height, minimal facet arthropathy, neutral/lordotic alignment, no instability, no prior fusion at the level.
2. **Standard anterior exposure** as for ACDF — develop the plane medial to the carotid sheath, lateral to the trachea/esophagus, subperiosteal elevation of longus colli bilaterally, retractor placement under (not on) longus colli.
3. **Establish true midline** under AP fluoroscopy before any bony work — use the spinous process silhouette or a marker pin, and mark it on the vertebral bodies above and below; all subsequent steps depend on this reference.
4. **Discectomy**, technique aimed at preserving the endplates and segmental motion rather than achieving fusion — remove disc material completely including posterior annulus/PLL as needed for decompression, but avoid burring the endplates themselves beyond removing cartilage.
5. **Bilateral uncovertebral decompression** as needed for foraminal stenosis, taking care not to over-resect the uncinate processes, which can destabilize the segment or compromise implant seating laterally.
6. **Endplate preparation.** Remove cartilaginous endplate to bleeding bone while preserving the cortical endplate contour and the natural sagittal profile — do not flatten a normally concave/convex endplate pair, since many implant designs rely on matching this native geometry for load transfer. This is the single most important technical safeguard against subsidence.
7. **Distraction and trial.** Apply gentle, symmetric distraction (avoid over-distraction, which both masks true disc height and risks segmental kyphosis on release) and place trial implants sequentially — go with the largest footprint that seats flush without overhang, and the height that restores native disc height without over-tensioning the facets.
8. **Confirm centering under fluoroscopy.** With the trial in place, confirm on AP view that the implant is centered on the marked midline (not rotated) and on lateral view that it is positioned at the posterior third of the vertebral body (near the segment's natural center of rotation), not too far anterior.
9. **Insert the definitive implant** under fluoroscopic guidance, seating it fully against the posterior vertebral body margin (residual anterior gap is far more forgiving than a posteriorly under-seated implant, which risks canal encroachment and inadequate decompression).
10. **Final fluoroscopic and, where available, 3D/O-arm confirmation** of AP and lateral position before releasing distraction and closing.
11. **Release distraction gradually**, observe the segment move through a range of motion if visually assessable, and inspect for any facet impingement or implant malposition.
12. **Hemostasis and closure** as for ACDF; drain is used less routinely than in multilevel ACDF/corpectomy given the more limited exposure, but is reasonable per surgeon judgment.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Verify midline with an AP fluoroscopic shot using bony landmarks (not just soft tissue symmetry) before starting the discectomy — rotational or off-center implant placement is one of the most common preventable technical errors and predicts later facet overload and pain.
- Prioritize endplate preservation over aggressive decortication — unlike ACDF, you are not trying to create a bleeding fusion bed; over-aggressive endplate violation directly predisposes to subsidence in arthroplasty.
- Complete uncovertebral joint resection is sometimes described as improving decompression and implant seating in higher-grade spondylosis, but resect conservatively and symmetrically — asymmetric uncovertebral resection can tilt implant seating and bias loading toward one facet.
- Trial multiple implant sizes rather than committing to the pre-templated size; true intraoperative fit after endplate preparation often differs from preoperative imaging estimates, and the widest and deepest implant that seats flush without overhang typically performs best.
- Position the implant posteriorly enough to sit near the segment's natural center of rotation (roughly aligned with the posterior third of the vertebral body on lateral fluoroscopy) rather than centered in the disc space — anterior malposition is a recognized cause of suboptimal motion preservation and facet overload.
- Avoid over-distraction during trialing; it both overestimates the needed implant height and risks the segment settling into kyphosis once distraction is released and the (now oversized) implant loads the facets.
- Confirm final implant position with orthogonal fluoroscopy (and 3D imaging if available) before leaving the room — malposition found postoperatively is a revision, not a bedside fix.
- Counsel patients that early, active range of motion postoperatively is part of the mechanism of the procedure, not just a comfort measure — prolonged immobilization is associated with a higher rate of heterotopic ossification.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Heterotopic ossification (HO)**: reported in a meaningful minority of levels at long-term follow-up, rates varying by implant design and follow-up duration; can progressively restrict segmental motion and, if severe, functionally converts the arthroplasty into an ankylosed segment. Higher-grade HO is more common with longer follow-up. Minimizing bone debris left in the disc space/paravertebral soft tissue at closure and thorough irrigation are reasonable technical measures, though evidence for their effect on HO rates is not definitive.
- **Implant subsidence/migration**: uncommon but a recognized reason for revision; prevented primarily through meticulous endplate preparation and correct implant sizing/positioning. Subsidence and expulsion are both consequences of poor technique — implant either too thin/undersized (migration) or too thick/oversized (facet overload, subsidence, kyphosis).
- **Facet overload/axial pain**: results from over-distraction, oversized implant height, or anterior/rotational malposition — a mechanical consequence of technical error rather than a fixed complication rate, which is why meticulous sizing and positioning matter more here than in fusion.
- **Adjacent segment disease**: occurs at a lower rate than after ACDF per pooled long-term comparative data, but is not eliminated — motion preservation reduces, rather than removes, adjacent segment risk.
- **Poor candidate selection is the dominant driver of complications**: patients with advanced spondylosis, osteophytic disease, severe facet arthropathy, osteoporosis, sagittal deformity, or preoperative instability are poor candidates and more prone to subsidence, expulsion, and postoperative kyphosis — selection discipline matters more here than in most cervical procedures.
- **Wrong-level or malrotated implant**: preventable with disciplined midline marking and orthogonal fluoroscopic confirmation before final seating.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Faster early mobilization than fusion — there is no fusion to protect. Soft collar is optional/brief (days); early ROM is encouraged, since preserving segmental motion is part of the point of the procedure.
- Avoid prolonged immobilization, which could promote heterotopic ossification.
- Return to sedentary work often within 1–2 weeks; manual labor 4–8 weeks, guided by symptom resolution rather than a fusion-healing timeline.
- PT focus: early active ROM, cervical/scapular strengthening.
- **10-year comparative data** (2023–2025 meta-analyses of pooled RCT/cohort data, plus independent prospective RCT and single-center ≥10-year cohort data): CDA shows statistically superior overall clinical success, lower reoperation/revision rates, and fewer adverse events than ACDF at 10 years; NDI and VAS differences favor CDA but do not consistently reach minimal clinically important difference thresholds — the durability/reoperation advantage is more clinically meaningful than the pain-score advantage.
- Return to contact/collision sport is individualized — motion preservation does not remove risk to adjacent unprotected levels.
