## Indications

Displaced acetabular fracture with dome/weight-bearing surface involvement (roof arc <45°), articular step-off >2 mm, marginal impaction, or an incongruent/unstable hip after reduction; also associated patterns (both-column, T-shaped, transverse+posterior wall) in patients with reasonable bone stock and functional demand.

## Positioning & setup

Approach is selected by Judet-Letournel fracture pattern. Kocher-Langenbeck for predominantly posterior patterns places the sciatic nerve at risk — position with the hip flexed and knee extended, and avoid excessive retraction on the short external rotators throughout the exposure. Ilioinguinal or anterior intrapelvic (Stoppa) approaches for anterior/both-column patterns place the corona mortis (an aberrant obturator-to-external iliac vascular anastomosis, present in 20–30% of patients, crossing the superior pubic ramus roughly 5–9 cm from the symphysis) at risk during dissection. Combined or extensile (extended iliofemoral) approaches are reserved for complex, delayed (>3 weeks), or both-column patterns not amenable to a single-window exposure.

## Steps

1. Select approach per Judet-Letournel pattern: Kocher-Langenbeck for posterior-predominant patterns (posterior wall, posterior column, transverse+posterior wall); ilioinguinal or anterior intrapelvic Stoppa for anterior-predominant patterns (anterior column/wall, most both-column); combined or extensile for complex, delayed, or both-column patterns not amenable to a single window.
2. Favor anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) over ilioinguinal for anterior column/both-column patterns — it gives direct quadrilateral surface visualization and carries lower risk of femoral nerve/vessel injury.
3. Achieve indirect reduction using a ball-spike pusher and Schanz pins.
4. Identify and elevate marginal impaction, bone-grafting the defect before proceeding to column fixation.
5. Fix with interfragmentary lag screws plus neutralization/buttress plating along the columns; contour pelvic reconstruction plates to the pelvic brim and posterior column.
6. Buttress the quadrilateral surface (spring plate or dedicated quadrilateral plate) in both-column fractures and medial wall comminution to prevent central protrusio.
7. Confirm reduction with intraoperative fluoroscopy (obturator oblique, iliac oblique, and Judet views) before final fixation.
8. Target anatomic reduction (≤1 mm residual displacement) — this is the single strongest predictor of long-term joint survival (Matta criteria).
9. In high-risk patients (extensile/Kocher-Langenbeck approach, extensive soft tissue stripping, male sex, head injury), consider heterotopic ossification prophylaxis with indomethacin or single-fraction radiotherapy.

## Pitfalls

- Missed marginal impaction is a common technical error — it must be elevated and bone-grafted before column fixation, not fixed in situ.
- Incomplete reduction of the posterior wall leaves residual hip instability.
- Iatrogenic sciatic nerve injury during Kocher-Langenbeck exposure — protect throughout with hip flexed/knee extended positioning and avoid excessive retraction; the peroneal division is more vulnerable than the tibial division.
- Heterotopic ossification occurs in up to 20–30% of Kocher-Langenbeck and extended iliofemoral approaches without prophylaxis.
- Watch for the corona mortis during Stoppa/ilioinguinal dissection of the superior pubic ramus — it is a recognized, if rare, source of life-threatening hemorrhage.
- Anatomic reduction quality (not age) is the dominant driver of long-term outcome: 7-year joint survival after ORIF is reported around 80% with anatomic reduction versus ~65–70% with imperfect reduction — this should guide the reduction effort more than any age-based cutoff.

## Post-op protocol

- Weeks 0–6: touch-down weight-bearing (10–15 kg / foot-flat) on the operated limb; early passive/active-assisted hip ROM (avoid resisted abduction if abductors were taken down); CPM not routinely required. Hip precautions if posterior wall was repaired (avoid combined flexion-adduction-internal rotation, analogous to posterior THA precautions, for 6 weeks) or if an anterior approach was used (avoid extension-external rotation).
- Weeks 6–12: progress to partial then full weight-bearing guided by radiographic union and surgeon assessment; closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, pool therapy; continue HO prophylaxis monitoring if an extensile approach was used.
- Months 3–6: full weight-bearing, progressive resistance training, return to low-impact recreational activity; assess for early post-traumatic osteoarthritis signs.
- Return to sport: non-contact low-impact activity (cycling, swimming) at 4–6 months; pivoting/contact sport at 9–12 months, contingent on radiographic union, full painless ROM, and functional strength testing — many surgeons counsel against return to high-impact pivoting sport indefinitely given articular cartilage injury burden.
