## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications for ORIF:**
- Displaced acetabular fracture with dome/weight-bearing surface involvement (roof arc <45° on any of the three roof-arc measurements).
- Articular step-off or gap >2 mm on CT.
- Marginal impaction of the articular surface.
- Incongruent or unstable hip after closed reduction of a fracture-dislocation.
- Associated (complex) patterns — both-column, T-shaped, transverse + posterior wall — in patients with reasonable bone stock and functional demand, regardless of age if the fracture is reconstructible.
- Irreducible fracture-dislocation, or a dislocation that re-displaces after closed reduction (loose osteochondral fragment, incarcerated labrum, or posterior wall fragment blocking congruent reduction).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Nondisplaced or minimally displaced fractures with roof arcs >45° in all planes, a congruent and stable hip on exam or dynamic fluoroscopic stress testing under anesthesia — treat with protected weight-bearing instead.
- Low-demand elderly patients with a "roof-intact" both-column pattern that maintains secondary congruence — often better served by nonoperative treatment or acute total hip arthroplasty (THA) with limited fixation rather than anatomic ORIF (see the fix-and-replace technique guide).
- Severe dome comminution/impaction that cannot be reconstructed to a congruent articular surface — favor acute THA with column fixation instead of committing to an ORIF that will not achieve anatomic reduction.
- Active local or systemic infection, or skin/soft-tissue compromise over the planned approach (degloving, Morel-Lavallée lesion) — stage soft tissue management first or select an alternate window.
- Medical instability precluding a long anesthetic — definitive ORIF should wait until damage-control resuscitation is complete (physiology normalized: lactate clearing, base deficit corrected, not coagulopathic), typically 2–10 days post-injury; operating too early in an unresuscitated patient increases blood loss and complication risk.
- Delayed presentation beyond 3 weeks with early callus and fracture-line obliteration substantially changes the operative plan — often requires an extensile approach and should be anticipated and consented for separately, not treated as a routine single-window case.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP pelvis, obturator oblique and iliac oblique (Judet) views, and fine-cut CT with 3D reconstruction are mandatory before approach selection — do not plan from plain films alone. Measure roof arcs (medial, anterior, posterior) on the AP and both obliques. Review axial CT specifically for marginal impaction (a sclerotic, depressed fragment of subchondral bone, often at the posterior wall) and intra-articular loose fragments, since these change the plan (elevation and bone grafting) regardless of approach.
- **Classify the fracture** by Judet-Letournel pattern — this single step drives approach selection (see table below) and should be documented explicitly in the operative plan, not inferred intraoperatively.
- **Templating**: trace the fracture pattern onto the 3D reconstruction or use surgical planning software to anticipate plate contours (pelvic reconstruction plates conform poorly to the true pelvic brim curvature out of the box and nearly always need intraoperative bending). Plan screw trajectories relative to the joint and the sciatic notch/greater sciatic notch neurovascular bundle in advance.
- **Implant/equipment list**: pelvic reconstruction plate set (3.5 mm, multiple lengths, pre-curved options if available), lag screws (3.5 mm and 6.5–4.5 mm cancellous), a dedicated quadrilateral surface buttress plate or infra-pectineal spring plate for both-column/medial wall comminution, a large pelvic reduction frame (Farabeuf and Weber clamps, ball-spike pushers, Schanz pins with a T-handle or universal chuck for joystick reduction), a femoral distractor for chronic/delayed cases, and a radiolucent table compatible with intraoperative fluoroscopy in multiple planes.
- **Anticipate blood loss** — acetabular ORIF, particularly extensile approaches, can involve substantial blood loss; cross-match and have cell salvage available.
- **Heterotopic ossification (HO) prophylaxis planning**: identify high-risk patients (planned extensile/Kocher-Langenbeck approach with extensive muscle stripping, male sex, associated head injury) preoperatively so indomethacin or single-fraction radiotherapy can be arranged perioperatively rather than decided on the fly.
- **Multidisciplinary input**: involve urology preoperatively if there is a concurrent bladder/urethral injury, and general/vascular surgery if a laparotomy has been or may be performed through the same abdominal domain (relevant to Stoppa/ilioinguinal timing and incision planning).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia with a secured airway is standard given prone or lateral positioning is frequently required and case length can run several hours; a working arterial line and large-bore IV access are routine given anticipated blood loss. Consider a cell-saver setup for extensile or combined approaches.
- **Positioning by approach**:
  - **Kocher-Langenbeck**: lateral decubitus or prone position on a radiolucent table. Position with the hip flexed and knee extended to relax the sciatic nerve (this reduces tension on the nerve compared with an extended-hip position) — some surgeons instead maintain 90° of knee flexion with the hip extended intraoperatively during retraction to the same end; the key point is to avoid combined hip extension with knee extension, which maximizes sciatic tension. Free-drape the leg to allow intraoperative manipulation for reduction and to assess hip stability/ROM.
  - **Ilioinguinal / anterior intrapelvic (Stoppa)**: supine on a radiolucent table, often with a bump under the ipsilateral buttock; the abdomen and thigh are prepped into the field to allow a femoral joystick (Schanz pin) if needed for column reduction. A Foley catheter is placed to decompress the bladder before the medial (Stoppa) dissection.
  - **Combined/extensile (extended iliofemoral)**: lateral decubitus, full circumferential prep from costal margin to knee, both anterior and posterior windows accessible without repositioning.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm draped and positioned to obtain AP pelvis, obturator oblique, and iliac oblique (Judet) views intraoperatively without breaking sterility — confirm this is achievable before draping, since the position needed for the KL approach differs from that for Stoppa.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: greater trochanter, posterior superior iliac spine and iliac crest (Kocher-Langenbeck); ASIS, pubic tubercle, and the linea semilunaris (ilioinguinal/Stoppa); iliac crest circumferentially for extensile exposures.
- **Prophylaxis**: preoperative antibiotics within 60 minutes of incision; sequential compression devices given the high VTE risk of pelvic/acetabular trauma surgery.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm approach selection** against the Judet-Letournel pattern: Kocher-Langenbeck for posterior-predominant patterns (posterior wall, posterior column, transverse + posterior wall); ilioinguinal or anterior intrapelvic Stoppa for anterior-predominant patterns (anterior column/wall, most both-column); combined or extensile approaches for complex, delayed (>3 weeks), or both-column patterns not amenable to a single window. Favor the modified Stoppa over ilioinguinal for anterior column/both-column patterns — it gives direct visualization of the quadrilateral surface and carries lower risk of femoral nerve/vessel injury than the classic three-window ilioinguinal exposure.

2. **Kocher-Langenbeck exposure** (if selected): incise centered over the greater trochanter, curving posteriorly toward the PSIS. Keep the horizontal (distal) limb of the incision not too far posterior — this avoids inadvertent injury to the sciatic nerve when the fascia is incised. Split the gluteus maximus in line with its fibers, then identify and tag the short external rotators (piriformis, obturator internus/gemelli, quadratus femoris) 1–1.5 cm off their trochanteric insertion, leaving a cuff for later repair — do not release the quadratus femoris insertion, which carries the terminal retinacular branches supplying the femoral head. Use the tagged rotators as a protective sling over the sciatic nerve as you retract them medially.

3. **Protect the sciatic nerve** throughout the KL exposure: keep the hip flexed/knee extended (or maintain the knee-flexed, hip-neutral position per surgeon preference) to minimize nerve tension, pad the retractor blade against the nerve with a moist sponge rather than placing it directly on the nerve, and use the lesser sciatic notch — not the greater sciatic notch — as the safe zone for placing a retractor around the ischium when needed. Never apply substantial retractor pressure directly on the nerve; palpate/visualize it intermittently through the case rather than assuming a single early check is sufficient.

4. **Extend exposure as needed**: for additional cranial/anterior visualization (e.g., an associated posterior column component extending toward the sciatic notch, or a T-type fracture), consider a trochanteric flip (digastric) osteotomy — predrill the trochanter for later screw reattachment before performing the osteotomy, and take the osteotomy in continuity with the vastus lateralis and gluteus medius sleeve to preserve the abductor and external rotator attachments.

5. **Achieve indirect reduction**: use a ball-spike pusher against the ilium or ischium for translational control, and place Schanz pins into the ischial tuberosity or posterior column as joysticks for rotational control. A large pointed (Weber) reduction clamp across the fracture, or a specialized acetabular reduction clamp engaging the posterior column and the intact ilium, provides compression once provisional alignment is obtained.

6. **Identify and manage marginal impaction before column fixation**: if present, create a small cortical window or use the fracture plane itself to access the impacted subchondral fragment, elevate it back to the articular surface using a curved osteotome or bone tamp under direct/fluoroscopic control, and pack the resulting metaphyseal defect with autograft (local bone from the ilium/greater trochanter is convenient) or allograft before proceeding to definitive column fixation. Elevating impaction after column fixation is far more difficult and is a common source of residual incongruity — sequence this step correctly.

7. **Provisional fixation**: once the articular reduction is judged anatomic by direct visualization/palpation of the articular surface (where visible) and fluoroscopy, place provisional K-wires or small fragment screws across the major fracture lines to hold reduction while definitive plates are contoured.

8. **Definitive fixation — posterior patterns**: place an interfragmentary lag screw across the posterior wall or posterior column fracture where geometry allows, then apply a neutralization/buttress pelvic reconstruction plate spanning from the ilium across the posterior column to the ischial tuberosity, contoured to sit flush against the posterior column without impinging the sciatic notch. For a posterior wall fragment, a spring/hook plate at the inferior and superior margins of the fragment prevents late displacement, particularly when the fragment is comminuted or small.

9. **Definitive fixation — anterior patterns (Stoppa/ilioinguinal)**: expose the quadrilateral surface and pelvic brim, protecting the obturator neurovascular bundle and external iliac vessels throughout (see Pitfalls). Reduce the anterior column with a combination of a ball-spike pusher on the iliac wing, a Farabeuf clamp across the iliac crest and pubis, and/or a femoral (Schanz pin) joystick. Fix with a pelvic brim plate contoured along the pectineal line, supplemented by a quadrilateral surface buttress (a dedicated infra-pectineal spring plate or a contoured reconstruction plate placed just above the quadrilateral surface) in both-column fractures or medial wall comminution to prevent central (medial) protrusio of the femoral head.

10. **Combined/both-column patterns**: fix the posterior column first if using a combined approach (restores a stable platform), then reduce and fix the anterior column against it; alternatively, in an extensile exposure, work sequentially column-by-column starting with the least comminuted, best-reducible column to re-establish an anatomic reference for the rest.

11. **Confirm reduction with fluoroscopy** before final tightening of all screws: obtain AP pelvis, obturator oblique, and iliac oblique (Judet) views intraoperatively to check the roof, both columns, and the joint space for step-off or gap. Direct visualization of the articular surface through the fracture line or via a limited capsulotomy, where the approach allows, remains the gold standard where feasible.

12. **Target anatomic reduction** — ≤1 mm residual displacement (Matta criteria) is the single strongest predictor of long-term joint survival; 1–3 mm is considered an acceptable/imperfect reduction, and >3 mm a poor reduction with substantially higher post-traumatic arthritis (PTOA) risk. Do not close on a reduction you are not confident meets this standard — revisit the reduction rather than accept it.

13. **Closure**: repair the short external rotators back to their trochanteric footprint (or reduce and fix the trochanteric osteotomy with screws if performed) for the KL approach; repair the rectus sheath/linea alba meticulously for the Stoppa/ilioinguinal approach to avoid later hernia. Place a deep drain in the KL approach given the dead space created by rotator elevation; consider drains for extensile exposures given the larger dissection.

14. **Administer HO prophylaxis** intraoperatively/perioperatively in high-risk patients (extensile or KL approach with extensive stripping, male sex, head injury) — indomethacin for 6 weeks or a single fraction of radiotherapy (typically given within 24–72 hours postoperatively) per institutional protocol.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Sequence marginal impaction elevation and bone grafting **before** column fixation — once the columns are plated, the impacted fragment becomes far harder to access and elevate accurately.
- Use the piriformis and obturator internus tendon sleeve as a living retractor/sciatic-nerve cushion during the KL approach rather than relying on a metal retractor blade alone.
- When placing a retractor around the ischium in the KL approach, use the lesser sciatic notch as the safe zone — it keeps the retractor tip away from the sciatic nerve, which courses across the greater sciatic notch.
- Predrill the greater trochanter for screw fixation before performing a trochanteric flip osteotomy — it is far easier to align drill holes before the fragment is mobile.
- In the modified Stoppa exposure, identify and ligate the corona mortis early and deliberately, before subperiosteal dissection along the pelvic brim proceeds — do not wait for it to bleed. Vascular clips or suture ligation both work; have both available.
- Favor a malleable/spatula retractor placed medial to the obturator neurovascular bundle in the Stoppa approach rather than retracting the bundle itself — this reduces traction neurapraxia of the obturator nerve, a common and usually self-limited but avoidable postoperative finding.
- Use a femoral distractor spanning the iliac wing to the proximal femur in chronic (delayed presentation) cases — it restores working length across a fracture that has begun to callus and makes reduction dramatically easier than manual traction alone.
- When contouring a pelvic reconstruction plate to the pelvic brim or posterior column, slightly under-contour rather than over-contour the plate at the outset — it is easier to add a final bend against bone than to correct an over-bent plate that is levering the fracture apart.
- Keep a low threshold to obtain an intraoperative CT or repeat fluoroscopic series in obese patients or when standard oblique views are degraded by overlying bowel gas/soft tissue — do not accept a reduction check you cannot actually interpret.
- In combined/extensile cases, fix the more anatomically reliable column first to re-establish a stable reference before tackling the more comminuted side.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Missed marginal impaction** is a common technical error — it must be elevated and bone-grafted before column fixation, not fixed in situ or addressed after the columns are already plated.
- **Incomplete reduction of the posterior wall** leaves residual hip instability and predisposes to early failure and PTOA.
- **Iatrogenic sciatic nerve injury** during Kocher-Langenbeck exposure remains the most feared complication of the posterior approach — protect throughout with hip-flexed/knee-extended (or equivalent tension-reducing) positioning, avoid excessive or sustained retractor pressure, and use the lesser sciatic notch as the safe retractor zone; the peroneal division is more vulnerable than the tibial division and injury classically presents as foot drop.
- **Gluteal neurovascular bundle injury** at the supra-acetabular/greater sciatic notch region — dissect carefully in this zone and avoid aggressive dissection directly against the acetabular rim, which also risks devascularizing rim fragments.
- **Heterotopic ossification** occurs in up to 20–30% of Kocher-Langenbeck and extended iliofemoral approaches without prophylaxis; risk is driven by the extent of muscle stripping, male sex, and associated head injury — plan prophylaxis (indomethacin or single-fraction radiotherapy) in these patients rather than reacting to it postoperatively.
- **Corona mortis hemorrhage** during Stoppa/ilioinguinal dissection of the superior pubic ramus — this vascular anastomosis between the obturator and external iliac/inferior epigastric systems is present in roughly 20–30% (some anatomic series report even higher rates of some communicating vessel) of patients and crosses the superior pubic ramus roughly 5–9 cm from the symphysis; it is a recognized, if rare, source of life-threatening hemorrhage and should be actively sought and ligated before it is torn, not discovered by bleeding.
- **Obturator nerve/vessel traction injury** during Stoppa exposure of the quadrilateral surface — protect with a malleable retractor placed medial to the bundle rather than retracting the bundle directly; these structures are in direct contact with the quadrilateral surface and are the structures most at risk during that portion of the dissection.
- **Femoral nerve or vessel injury** is a recognized risk of the classic three-window ilioinguinal approach (lateral/middle window dissection) — this risk is part of why the modified Stoppa approach has supplanted ilioinguinal in many centers for anterior column/both-column patterns.
- **Central protrusio / recurrent medial displacement** after both-column or medial wall comminution fixation without adequate quadrilateral surface buttressing — always add a spring plate or dedicated quadrilateral buttress plate in this fracture pattern.
- **Anatomic reduction quality (not age) is the dominant driver of long-term outcome**: 7-year joint survival after ORIF is reported around 80% with anatomic (≤1 mm) reduction versus roughly 65–70% with imperfect reduction — this should guide the reduction effort more than any age-based cutoff, and is the single most important postoperative predictor to communicate to the patient.
- **Infection** — deep surgical site infection rates run higher with extensile approaches given the larger dissection and longer operative time; meticulous hemostasis, drain use, and antibiotic timing mitigate but do not eliminate this risk.
- **Postoperative avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral head** is associated with fracture-dislocation, delayed reduction (>6–12 hours from injury to hip reduction), and posterior wall injury — this argues for urgent closed reduction of any dislocated hip before definitive ORIF, independent of when ORIF itself is scheduled.
- **DVT/PE** — pelvic and acetabular trauma surgery carries among the highest VTE risk of any orthopaedic procedure; maintain mechanical and pharmacologic prophylaxis per institutional protocol, extended in immobilized patients.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: touch-down weight-bearing (10–15 kg / foot-flat) on the operated limb; early passive/active-assisted hip ROM (avoid resisted abduction if abductors were taken down); CPM not routinely required. Hip precautions if the posterior wall was repaired (avoid combined flexion-adduction-internal rotation, analogous to posterior THA precautions, for 6 weeks) or if an anterior approach was used (avoid extension-external rotation).
- **Weeks 6–12**: progress to partial then full weight-bearing guided by radiographic union and surgeon assessment; closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, pool therapy; continue HO prophylaxis monitoring if an extensile approach was used.
- **Months 3–6**: full weight-bearing, progressive resistance training, return to low-impact recreational activity; assess for early post-traumatic osteoarthritis signs.
- **Return to sport**: non-contact low-impact activity (cycling, swimming) at 4–6 months; pivoting/contact sport at 9–12 months, contingent on radiographic union, full painless ROM, and functional strength testing — many surgeons counsel against return to high-impact pivoting sport indefinitely given articular cartilage injury burden.
