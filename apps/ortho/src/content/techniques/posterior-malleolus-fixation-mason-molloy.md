## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Posterior malleolus fragment involving ≥25% of the articular surface on lateral radiograph.
- CT-defined criteria per Mason & Molloy: incisural involvement, articular step-off >2 mm, comminution, or syndesmotic instability persisting after fibula/medial malleolus fixation. CT morphology now drives the decision more than a single fragment-size cutoff, since posterior malleolus fractures are increasingly understood as syndesmosis-ligament-avulsion equivalents rather than purely a size problem.
- Any fragment whose direct reduction is judged necessary to restore syndesmotic stability, even below the 25% threshold, when the fragment carries a substantial PITFL insertion.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Small, extra-incisural (Type 1) fragments not carrying meaningful PITFL insertion or articular load — treat by stabilizing the syndesmosis directly rather than chasing the fragment itself.
- Severely compromised posterior soft-tissue envelope (fracture blisters, degloving) over the planned posterolateral/posteromedial incision — delay until resolved, or accept indirect reduction/percutaneous fixation if delay is not possible.
- Medically unstable or non-ambulatory patients where the added surgical time/positioning change for a posterior approach is not justified relative to overall treatment goals.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: CT (thin-cut, with sagittal and axial reconstructions through the incisura and posterior malleolus) is essentially mandatory whenever a posterior malleolus fragment is visible on the lateral ankle radiograph — plain films alone systematically understate fragment size and morphology. Classify the fragment using the Mason & Molloy system (Types 1, 2A, 2B, 3) to select the approach and fixation strategy before the day of surgery.
- **Implant selection**: buttress/antiglide small-fragment plates (one-third tubular or precontoured posterior/posterolateral plates) and 2.7/3.5 mm lag screws for Type 2A/2B fragments; a buttress plate suited to a large "die-punch" fragment for Type 3. Have a second small plate or fragment-specific screws available for Type 2B's additional intermediate/comminuted fragment.
- **Equipment**: mini pointed reduction clamps, a dental pick or small elevator for disimpacting a die-punch fragment, and image intensifier positioned for lateral and axial views of the posterior tibial plafond without repositioning.
- **Case-specific planning**: decide the patient position (prone vs lateral vs supine with leg roll) based on which combination of approaches will be needed — posterolateral alone can often be done supine with the leg internally rotated and a bump, but a posteromedial or combined approach, or a Type 2B pattern needing both posterolateral and posteromedial windows, is more reliably done prone. Plan concurrently for fibula and/or medial malleolus fixation in the same setting, and sequence the case (posterior malleolus is often addressed either first, to serve as a stable buttress for reducing the fibula against, or immediately after fibular length/rotation is restored — surgeon preference and fragment size both matter here).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal anesthesia with a popliteal sciatic ± saphenous block.
- **Position**: prone position gives the most direct and extensile access to both posterolateral and posteromedial structures and is preferred for Type 2B and Type 3 fragments or when combined posterior exposure is anticipated; a lateral decubitus or supine position with a bump/internal rotation is adequate for an isolated posterolateral (Type 2A) approach when the fibula is being addressed through the same general field. If prone, pad all pressure points (chest rolls, face, iliac crests, knees) carefully and secure the airway appropriately given the position change from standard supine ankle surgery.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet, inflated for the posterior exposure and reduction.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm positioned to obtain true lateral and axial views of the posterior tibial plafond in the chosen position — confirm this is achievable before prepping and draping, since prone positioning changes the C-arm approach angle compared to standard supine ankle fluoroscopy.
- **Draping**: prep circumferentially from above the tourniquet to the toes to allow access to both posterolateral and posteromedial windows if needed, and to permit concurrent anterior/lateral incisions for fibula and medial malleolus fixation.
- **Landmarks**: mark the interval between the peroneal tendons and flexor hallucis longus (posterolateral approach), the course of the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle and flexor digitorum longus/tibialis posterior tendons (posteromedial approach), and the Achilles tendon border, before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Classify the fragment on CT** using the Mason & Molloy system before committing to an approach — this decision should be made preoperatively, not improvised intraoperatively.
2. **Position the patient** according to the planned approach(es) as above.
3. **Type 1** (small extra-incisural posterolateral fragment): use the standard lateral/anterolateral approach for the fibula; do not attempt direct fixation of the malleolus fragment itself — instead stabilize the syndesmosis directly with a screw or suture-button after fibular fixation.
4. **Type 2A** (posterolateral fragment extending into the incisura, two-part): approach posterolaterally through the interval between the peroneal tendons (retracted anteriorly) and the flexor hallucis longus (retracted medially, protecting it as it lies just anterior to the neurovascular bundle). Expose the fracture, clear hematoma, and reduce the fragment anatomically under direct vision, using the intact anterior tibial cortex/incisura as a reduction reference. Provisionally pin, then fix with a posterolateral buttress/antiglide plate placed just distal to the fracture line to resist proximal migration, supplemented by an interfragmentary lag screw where the fragment size allows.
5. **Type 2B** (2A plus a comminuted/intermediate fragment): after reducing and provisionally fixing the main posterolateral fragment as in step 4, address the additional intermediate fragment through the same posterolateral window if accessible, or add an accessory posteromedial exposure (protecting the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle and flexor tendons) if the fragment is not reachable posterolaterally. This often requires a second, smaller plate or fragment-specific screws placed independently of the main buttress plate.
6. **Type 3** (posteromedial extension, often a large "die-punch" fragment): approach posteromedially, working in the interval medial to the flexor digitorum longus and posterior to the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle (identify and protect the bundle before deep dissection). Disimpact the depressed die-punch fragment with a dental pick or small elevator, restoring articular congruity, then apply a direct buttress plate along the posteromedial tibia to resist redisplacement. This is frequently combined with medial malleolus fixation through the same or an adjacent incision.
7. **Confirm articular reduction** fluoroscopically (lateral and axial views) and, where feasible, by direct visualization of the articular surface through the fracture gap before final plate/screw placement.
8. **Sequence with fibula and medial malleolus fixation**: many surgeons reduce and provisionally fix the posterior malleolus before final fibular fixation, since a well-reduced posterior fragment can serve as a stable buttress that facilitates anatomic fibular reduction against the restored incisura; others fix the fibula first and use it as the reference for posterior malleolus reduction. Either sequence is acceptable — the key technical requirement is that both are anatomically reduced and cross-checked against each other before final tightening of hardware.
9. **Reassess syndesmosis stability after posterior malleolus fixation, not before** — perform the Cotton and external rotation stress tests only once the posterior fragment (which carries the PITFL insertion) is fixed, since direct buttress fixation frequently restores syndesmotic stability without needing an additional syndesmotic implant.
10. **If the syndesmosis remains unstable** despite anatomic posterior malleolus, fibula, and medial malleolus fixation, proceed to syndesmotic screw or suture-button stabilization as described in the ankle fracture ORIF technique.
11. **Final fluoroscopic check**: lateral and axial views confirming articular congruity of the posterior malleolus, appropriate plate/screw position without joint penetration, and overall mortise reduction.
12. **Release the tourniquet, confirm hemostasis, and close in layers**, protecting the neurovascular structures identified during exposure; reposition the patient (if prone) for any remaining anterior/lateral work, or complete closure and apply a well-padded splint.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Decide the patient position (prone vs supine/lateral) based on the *worst-case* fragment complexity anticipated from CT, not the simplest scenario — repositioning mid-case is far more disruptive than starting in the position that covers a possible Type 2B or 3 pattern.
- Use the intact anterior tibial cortex at the incisura as your reduction landmark for Type 2A/2B fragments — it is the most reliable fixed reference point when the posterior fragment itself is displaced and rotated.
- Place the posterolateral buttress plate distal enough to the fracture line to genuinely resist the proximal migration force the fragment experiences under axial load — a plate positioned too proximal functions more as a neutralization device than a true buttress.
- For a Type 3 die-punch fragment, disimpact before you reduce — attempting to lever a depressed, impacted fragment back into position without first freeing it from the surrounding bone risks further comminution.
- Fix the posterior malleolus before final syndesmotic stress testing in every case — this single sequencing decision frequently eliminates the need for a separate syndesmotic screw or suture-button, sparing the patient an implant.
- When a Type 2B fragment is not reachable through the posterolateral window, do not force it — add the accessory posteromedial exposure rather than accepting an incomplete reduction of the intermediate fragment.
- Cross-check the posterior malleolus and fibular reductions against each other fluoroscopically before finalizing either construct — an anatomically reduced posterior malleolus makes fibular malreduction easier to detect, and vice versa.
- Keep the flexor hallucis longus and posterior tibial neurovascular bundle under direct vision (not just retracted blindly) throughout the posterolateral and posteromedial dissections respectively — both approaches place these structures close to the working field.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- A 2023 interobserver reliability study (20 raters, 38 CT scans) found meaningful interobserver variability in this system, particularly in distinguishing Type 2A from 2B — do not rely on the classification in isolation for surgical planning; correlate with the CT images directly.
- Treating the decision purely as a fragment-size cutoff understates risk — the current framework treats an adequately sized fragment as a syndesmotic ligament avulsion (it carries the PITFL insertion), not just an articular fracture.
- Assessing syndesmosis stability *before* posterior malleolus fixation risks unnecessary syndesmotic implants, since restoring the posterior malleolus often restores stability on its own.
- Injury to the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle or flexor hallucis longus/flexor digitorum longus tendons is a recognized risk of the posterolateral and posteromedial approaches respectively — meticulous identification and protection during dissection is essential, not optional.
- Malpositioning the buttress plate too proximal on a Type 2A/2B fragment converts it functionally into a neutralization plate rather than a true buttress, risking loss of reduction under axial load.
- Failing to plan patient positioning for the worst-case fragment pattern (based on preoperative CT) can force an intraoperative repositioning that adds time and risk — commit to the position that covers the anticipated complexity before the patient is prepped.
- Comminuted die-punch fragments (Type 3) that are levered into place without first being disimpacted risk further fragmentation, compounding an already technically demanding reduction.

## Postoperative Protocol

Follows the same phased protocol as ankle fracture ORIF overall: splint/boot with strict elevation and NWB/toe-touch for the first 2 weeks, transition to protected weight-bearing from 2 weeks post-ORIF per contemporary evidence (WAX trial, 2024 AAOS guideline) if fixation is stable and anatomically reduced, progressive weight-bearing to full WB by 6–12 weeks, and return to impact activity by 12 weeks once radiographic union, full painless ROM, and ≥90% strength symmetry are achieved. Return to sedentary work 2–6 weeks, manual labor 3–4 months.
