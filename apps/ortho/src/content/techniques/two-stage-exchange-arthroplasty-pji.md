## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:** Chronic PJI without a reliably identified organism preoperatively, extensive bone/soft-tissue loss requiring interval reconstruction planning, difficult-to-treat or multidrug-resistant organisms, a sinus tract requiring interval soft-tissue management, or host/local factors favoring a staged, lower-single-procedure-burden approach.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Host unable to tolerate two separate major anesthetics/procedures within a reasonable interval — extreme frailty or a very limited life expectancy may favor suppression, resection arthroplasty, or arthrodesis over a staged eradication attempt.
- Bone and soft-tissue loss so extensive that no realistic reconstruction is achievable at Stage 2 regardless of infection clearance — discuss salvage options (resection arthroplasty, arthrodesis, amputation) with the patient before committing to a spacer.
- Patient unwilling or unable to comply with a prolonged interval of protected weight-bearing, antibiotic therapy, and repeat surgery — staged protocols depend heavily on patient adherence across months.
- Known severe hypersensitivity/allergy to all heat-stable antibiotic classes suitable for spacer loading — an alternative local-delivery strategy or a one-stage approach with careful systemic-only coverage may need to be considered.
- McPherson host C with local grade 3 and an already-failed prior eradication attempt is a relative contraindication to a further aggressive staged attempt — reframe the goal with the patient as functional/quality-of-life rather than eradication in this group.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** Radiographs and, where bone loss is a concern, CT to assess implant fixation, osteolysis, cortical bone stock, and periprosthetic fracture risk — this drives both the spacer construct choice (static vs articulating, need for intramedullary stemming) and the Stage 2 reconstruction plan (need for allograft, augments, cones/sleeves, revision stem length).
- **Spacer construct planning:** Decide in advance whether an articulating or static spacer is planned based on bone stock, soft-tissue/extensor mechanism integrity, and collateral ligament competence (knee) or abductor/capsular integrity (hip); have both a mold system (commercial articulating spacer mold, or a handmade silicone mold from trial components) and a plan for intramedullary stem/rod reinforcement of a static spacer if extensive canal bone loss is present.
- **Antibiotic and cement planning:** Plan spacer antibiotic composition in advance — a heat-stable agent (vancomycin) combined with a Gram-negative-active aminoglycoside (tobramycin or gentamicin) is standard; a widely used working dose is on the order of 2 g vancomycin and roughly 2.4 g tobramycin (or equivalent gentamicin dosing) per 40 g cement pack, tailored upward or downward once culture results return and adjusted for renal function and total cement volume needed. Confirm pharmacy can supply powdered (not liquid) antibiotic formulations for hand-mixing, since liquid formulations compromise cement mechanical properties.
- **Implant/instrumentation for Stage 2:** Template the reimplantation as a full revision case — range of stem lengths/offsets, augments, cones/sleeves, and allograft availability — since bone stock is only fully known after Stage 1 debridement and may change further over the interval (mechanical spacer subsidence, interval fracture).
- **Multidisciplinary planning:** Involve infectious disease early for both the empiric and interval antibiotic plan and the criteria that will be used to clear the patient for reimplantation (marker trend, symptom resolution, optional repeat aspiration).
- **Interval logistics:** Set explicit expectations with the patient and referring providers about the total interval length (commonly 6–12 weeks total, varying by center and organism), the antibiotic-free washout window before reassessment, and the criteria for proceeding to Stage 2.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** General or neuraxial anesthesia per standard revision arthroplasty practice, chosen independently for each stage — the two stages do not need to use the same technique.
- **Positioning — Stage 1 and Stage 2 use the same setup as any revision arthroplasty for the joint:** lateral or supine with a positioner for the hip (matching the prior approach where feasible), supine with a leg holder or tourniquet mount for the knee.
- **Draping:** Wide sterile prep in both stages to allow for extensile exposure — Stage 1 debridement in particular may need to extend further than the index incision to reach an abscess or sinus tract, and Stage 2 reimplantation may require an extensile approach if scarring from the interval has stiffened the soft-tissue envelope.
- **Tourniquet:** Use per surgeon preference for knee exposure in either stage; consider avoiding or releasing before closure in Stage 1 given the compromised soft-tissue bed of an actively infected joint.
- **Fluoroscopy:** Not routinely required for spacer placement, but useful intraoperatively to confirm intramedullary rod/stem position in a reinforced static spacer, and preoperatively before Stage 2 to reassess spacer position, subsidence, or interval fracture.
- **Landmarks:** Mark the prior incision and standard bony landmarks before each stage; re-mark at Stage 2 since interval swelling or spacer bulk can distort surface anatomy.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Stage 1 — debridement and spacer placement**

1. Incise through the prior scar; excise any sinus tract in continuity with the deep dissection.
2. Expose the joint and obtain multiple deep tissue cultures from distinct sites before any lavage or antibiotic exposure, using a separate instrument per site.
3. Perform radical debridement: complete synovectomy, excision of all infected/necrotic soft tissue, and removal of all components and cement, including cement restrictor/plug material in the canal.
4. Assess remaining bone stock, extensor mechanism (knee) or abductor/capsular integrity (hip), and collateral ligament competence (knee) — this determines whether an articulating or static spacer construct is feasible.
5. Perform thorough pulsatile lavage of the entire wound.
6. **Fabricate and place the antibiotic-loaded cement spacer.** Articulating is preferred where bone stock, ligamentous, and soft-tissue integrity allow, since it preserves soft-tissue length and joint motion, eases the Stage 2 exposure, and improves interim function; a static spacer (block or intramedullary rod/nail-reinforced) is used where ligamentous instability, extensor mechanism disruption, or extensive bone loss make an articulating construct unsafe. For the knee, an articulating spacer can be fabricated intraoperatively using trial components as a mold (commercial mold systems or a handmade silicone impression mold) to create femoral and tibial cement components that approximate normal joint geometry; for the hip, an individually hand-formed or mold-cast femoral spacer component (with or without a smooth metal/cement head articulating against native or spacer acetabular cement) is used.
7. **For a static spacer with significant canal bone loss, reinforce with an intramedullary rod** (e.g., a cement-coated Steinmann pin, flexible nail, or purpose-made antibiotic cement rod construct) embedded in the cement column to reduce the risk of spacer fracture and to maintain limb length/alignment during the interval.
8. Compound spacer antibiotics by combining a heat-stable agent (vancomycin) with a Gram-negative-active agent (an aminoglycoside such as tobramycin or gentamicin); mix by hand into the cement powder before adding monomer, using powdered (not liquid) antibiotic formulations to avoid compromising cement mechanical strength. Tailor the mix upward once culture results become available if the initial empiric combination does not cover the identified organism.
9. Assess spacer stability, limb length, and soft-tissue tension before closure; correct gross malalignment or leg-length discrepancy at this stage where possible, since it is difficult to fully correct at Stage 2 through a scarred interval.
10. Close in layers with a tension-free closure.
11. Start empiric broad-spectrum systemic antibiotics after cultures are secured, narrowing once sensitivities return.

**Interval**

12. Continue systemic antibiotic therapy, typically for around 6 weeks (IV or a highly bioavailable oral regimen), followed by a variable antibiotic-free "washout" window before reimplantation (commonly 2–4 weeks) to allow inflammatory markers to reflect the true infection status rather than being suppressed by ongoing treatment; total interval is commonly quoted as 6–12 weeks but varies by center, organism, and clinical response.
13. Reassess serum inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR trend) toward the end of the washout window; repeat joint aspiration if the clinical or serologic picture is equivocal.
14. Monitor for interval spacer complications at each follow-up — spacer fracture, dislocation, progressive bone loss, or leg-length change — and be prepared to intervene (spacer exchange) if a mechanical problem develops before reimplantation is otherwise indicated.

**Stage 2 — reimplantation**

15. Re-expose the joint, typically through the same incision; anticipate a stiffer, more scarred soft-tissue envelope than at a routine revision and be prepared for an extensile exposure if needed.
16. Remove the spacer and any residual cement completely.
17. **Take deep cultures again at reimplantation regardless of preoperative work-up** — this is mandatory even when the interval course was uneventful and markers have normalized, since occult persistent infection can be culture-silent until direct sampling at surgery.
18. Perform a further debridement/lavage of the reimplantation bed before proceeding, treating any unexpected purulence or gross infection as a reason to place a fresh spacer and repeat the interval rather than proceeding to definitive reimplantation.
19. Reconstruct bone deficiency as needed (structural or morselized allograft, metal augments, cones/sleeves, longer-stem revision components) per the templated plan, adjusted for what is actually found.
20. Reimplant the definitive components, respecting standard revision fixation principles for the bone stock present.
21. Check final stability and range of motion through a full functional arc before closure.
22. Close in layers with a tension-free closure.
23. Narrow systemic antibiotics to targeted therapy per culture results; add rifampin combination therapy (never as monotherapy) for confirmed rifampin-susceptible staphylococcal biofilm once the wound has stabilized/is closed.
24. Plan for a minimum 12-week total systemic antibiotic duration from reimplantation.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use trial components (or a purpose-made mold system) as the mold for an articulating knee spacer rather than free-hand shaping cement in situ — it produces a more anatomic articulating surface, reduces the risk of gross malalignment, and makes the Stage 2 exposure noticeably easier.
- Correct gross limb-length or alignment problems at Stage 1, not Stage 2 — the scarred interval soft-tissue envelope makes late correction much harder than getting the spacer geometry right the first time.
- Reinforce a static spacer with an intramedullary rod/pin whenever there is significant canal bone loss — an unreinforced cement-only static spacer is a well-recognized fracture risk during the interval, particularly under any weight-bearing.
- Recent series report acceptable outcomes allowing unrestricted or near-unrestricted weight-bearing during the interval with a well-fabricated cement-on-cement articulating spacer construct in the knee — confirm construct stability intraoperatively before committing to a more liberal postoperative weight-bearing prescription, and individualize based on bone stock and spacer fixation.
- Use powdered, not liquid, antibiotic formulations when hand-mixing spacer cement — liquid formulations and excessive antibiotic loading both measurably weaken cement mechanical strength, which matters more for a load-bearing static spacer than for a low-dose surface coating.
- Do not treat the reimplantation cultures as a formality — take them with the same rigor as Stage 1 cultures, since a culture-silent low-grade persistent infection is a recognized cause of "successful" two-stage failures.
- If reimplantation findings (gross purulence, unexpected frozen-section positivity) suggest persistent infection, place a fresh spacer and repeat the interval rather than proceeding to definitive components — resist the pressure (from the patient or the calendar) to "just finish the case."
- Plan the Stage 2 exposure assuming it will be stiffer and more scarred than a routine revision — have extensile exposure options (quadriceps snip/turndown, tibial tubercle osteotomy for the knee; extended trochanteric osteotomy for the hip) available rather than forcing a tight exposure.
- Screw augmentation of an acetabular cement spacer can improve interval stability in hip cases with significant acetabular bone loss where a purely cemented spacer would be at risk of migration.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Spacer-related complications during the interval: spacer fracture or dislocation, femoral/tibial bone loss around a static spacer, leg-length discrepancy, stiffness (especially with static spacers or prolonged intervals), and interval periprosthetic fracture.
- Reported two-stage infection-free survival is roughly 85–90% at mid-term; one-stage exchange in well-selected chronic PJI cohorts now approaches similar figures (roughly 85–98% in recent RCT/registry data) — selection criteria matter more than "stage number."
- Two-stage remains the default for unknown-organism, poor-soft-tissue, or highly resistant-organism chronic PJI.
- Even successful two-stage exchange (particularly of the knee) generally yields lower functional outcome scores than primary arthroplasty or aseptic revision — set this as a realistic expectation preoperatively.
- Reinfection risk is highest with McPherson host C, S. aureus/resistant organisms, inadequate debridement, and premature or incomplete antibiotic courses.
- An unreinforced static spacer under load is a recognized fracture risk — reinforce with an intramedullary rod/pin construct when canal bone loss is significant.
- Skipping or under-sampling Stage 2 cultures on the assumption that a normalized CRP/ESR "proves" clearance risks missing a culture-silent persistent infection — always resample.
- Excessive antibiotic loading or use of liquid antibiotic formulations in hand-mixed spacer cement can compromise cement mechanical strength, raising fracture risk in load-bearing constructs.
- Prolonged static spacer intervals are associated with greater stiffness at Stage 2 than articulating constructs — factor this into both the exposure plan and the postoperative ROM expectations.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Stage 1 to reimplantation interval (weeks 0–6 to 12):** Protected weight-bearing per spacer construct stability — articulating spacers generally allow partial weight-bearing with an assistive device (some well-fabricated cement-on-cement articulating knee constructs may tolerate more liberal weight-bearing per surgeon assessment of construct stability); static spacers or significant bone loss may mandate touch-down/non-weight-bearing, surgeon-specific based on spacer fixation and bone stock. Gentle ROM work with an articulating spacer to preserve soft-tissue excursion and ease reimplantation exposure; static spacers are managed more conservatively with immobilization/bracing.

**Post-reimplantation (Stage 2), weeks 0–2:** Standard early post-arthroplasty protocol — weight-bearing as tolerated (hip/knee primary-style protocol) unless bone-grafting, allograft, or extensile exposure dictates protected loading — with cryotherapy, edema control, and active-assisted ROM.

**Weeks 2–6:** Progressive closed-chain strengthening, gait training, and continued ROM progression, watching closely for recurrent drainage, fever, or rising inflammatory markers as red flags for reinfection.

**Weeks 6–12:** Advance to a full strengthening program, proprioception/balance work, and functional task-specific training (stairs, uneven terrain).

**Months 3–6:** Continue strengthening; formal outcome/functional scoring (e.g., HOOS/KOOS, Oxford scores) at follow-up to track trajectory against expected post-revision benchmarks (typically lower peak function than primary arthroplasty).

**Return to work:** Sedentary 6 weeks; moderate physical work 3 months; heavy manual labor individualized (often 4–6 months) and contingent on confirmed infection clearance.

**Return to sport/high-demand recreational activity:** Low-impact activity from roughly 3–4 months if progressing well; higher-impact/higher-demand activity deferred to 6–12 months, with explicit counseling that revision-for-infection constructs generally carry lower functional ceilings and stricter activity modification than primary arthroplasty, given elevated re-revision risk with high-demand loading.

**Monitoring:** Serial CRP/ESR at defined intervals as a treatment-response surrogate (not a stand-alone "cure" criterion), wound surveillance at every visit, and a low threshold to re-image or re-aspirate if pain, swelling, drainage, or marker plateau/rebound occurs.
