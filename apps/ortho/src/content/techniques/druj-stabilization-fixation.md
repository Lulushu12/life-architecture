## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: acute reducible DRUJ instability persisting after anatomic radius/ulnar styloid fixation; irreducible DRUJ dislocation (typically ECU tendon or extensor tendon/periosteal entrapment); basilar ulnar styloid fracture with confirmed DRUJ instability after radius fixation; Galeazzi-equivalent injury patterns (DRF with gross DRUJ disruption) — low threshold to pin. Instability may be occult until the radius is anatomically reduced and fixed — always reassess DRUJ stability intraoperatively after radius fixation, not just preoperatively, since restoring radial length/rotation can either unmask or resolve apparent instability.

**Contraindications**:
- Isolated ulnar styloid tip fracture with a clinically stable DRUJ — fixation confers no proven functional benefit and adds hardware-related risk for no gain.
- DRUJ instability that reduces and remains stable through a functional rotational arc on stress testing — manage with position-specific immobilization rather than transfixion.
- Active joint infection or compromised soft-tissue envelope over the planned pin/plate site — address the soft-tissue problem first.
- Severe sigmoid notch comminution precluding a congruent reduction — may need arthroplasty-oriented salvage planning rather than acute fixation; involve a hand/wrist subspecialist.
- Chronic/neglected DRUJ instability presenting weeks to months out — acute transfixion techniques are not appropriate; this moves into the ligament reconstruction/salvage pathway (outside acute technique scope).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: dedicated PA/lateral wrist and forearm films to characterize the ulnar styloid fracture level (tip vs base/basilar — through or proximal to the fovea, since this distinction predicts instability risk more than fragment size alone); CT is useful for occult sigmoid notch fractures or when planning fragment-specific fixation of a comminuted styloid base. Assess sigmoid notch morphology (Tolat classification — flat/ski-slope types have less intrinsic bony stability and a lower threshold for treating soft-tissue instability).
- **Implant/equipment selection**: 0.062" or 0.045" smooth K-wires (avoid threaded wires, which risk fracturing on removal or eroding the joint) for DRUJ transfixion; tension-band wire/suture, a small headless compression screw (typically 2.0–3.0 mm), or a mini hook plate for basilar ulnar styloid fixation — decide preoperatively based on fragment size (screw needs adequate bone stock; small/comminuted fragments favor tension-band technique).
- **Equipment list**: mini fluoroscopy unit (essential — DRUJ stress testing and wire trajectory are fluoroscopy-dependent), wire driver, wire cutter/bender, small fragment set if screw fixation planned, sterile ruler/caliper for confirming wire depth.
- **Case-specific planning**: this procedure is almost always performed as an intraoperative extension of DRF/ulnar styloid fixation rather than a standalone booking — plan the stress-test step into the sequence of every distal radius fixation case, and have DRUJ pinning equipment open/available on the back table for any AO/OTA 23-B/C or Fernandez IV/V pattern.
- **Anticipate the irreducible dislocation**: if pre-operative imaging or exam suggests a widened or incongruent DRUJ that does not reduce with gentle closed manipulation, plan for open exploration (dorsal or volar approach depending on direction of dislocation) rather than assuming closed pinning will suffice.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or regional (supraclavicular/axillary block) anesthesia, consistent with whatever anesthesia was used for the associated distal radius fixation, since this step is almost always performed in continuity with that procedure.
- **Position**: supine, arm on a radiolucent hand table, shoulder abducted ~90°.
- **Tourniquet**: pneumatic upper-arm tourniquet, inflated per case (typically only needed if open exploration is required; percutaneous transfixion of an already-fixed radius can often be done tourniquet-down if hemostasis is adequate).
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: C-arm positioned for true PA and lateral wrist/forearm views, and critically, the ability to obtain dynamic pronation/supination views with the elbow flexed 90° — plan the C-arm and table orientation so the forearm can be rotated through its full arc without repositioning the image intensifier.
- **Landmarks**: mark Lister's tubercle, the ulnar styloid, and the dorsal DRUJ interval (just distal to the ulnar head) before pin insertion; palpate the ECU subsheath to avoid transfixing through it.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Complete anatomic fixation of the distal radius fracture (and ulnar styloid, if separately fixed) before assessing the DRUJ — do not assess DRUJ stability before the radius is anatomically restored, since malreduction of the radius can produce a false picture of instability.
2. Perform the fluoroscopic DRUJ stress test: with the elbow at 90° and forearm in neutral rotation, an assistant applies a dorsal/volar translation force to the ulna relative to the radius; repeat with the forearm stressed in pronation and in supination. Compare translation and the "clunk" endpoint to the contralateral, uninjured side (image the other wrist pre- or intra-operatively for comparison if instability is borderline).
3. Document the arc of forearm rotation within which the joint remains reduced and stable — this becomes the immobilization position if a nonoperative or pin-only strategy is chosen.
4. **If stable through a functional arc**: no transfixion needed; proceed to immobilize in the stable rotational position (commonly neutral-to-supination) postoperatively.
5. **If acutely unstable but reducible**: hold the DRUJ manually reduced in the stable rotational position identified above, then pass 1–2 smooth K-wires from the ulna into the radius (or radius into ulna, per surgeon preference), oriented roughly parallel and proximal to the DRUJ articulation itself — do not cross the sigmoid notch or joint surface. Confirm wire position and DRUJ congruity on PA and lateral fluoroscopy before bending and cutting the wires outside the skin.
6. **If irreducible**: convert to open exploration. Approach depends on the direction of dislocation — a dorsal approach for a dorsally dislocated ulnar head (most common; usually blocked by ECU tendon subluxation dorsal to the ulnar head or interposed extensor retinaculum/periosteum) or a volar approach for volar dislocation. Identify and reduce the entrapped structure (most often the ECU tendon or extensor tendons/periosteum), confirm congruent reduction under direct vision and fluoroscopy, then proceed to transfixion as in step 5 and repair the capsule/TFCC if disrupted.
7. For a basilar ulnar styloid fracture with confirmed instability, fix the fragment: place a tension-band construct (two K-wires plus an 18-gauge wire loop) or a small headless compression screw directed to restore the foveal ligament insertion, or a mini hook plate for a comminuted/small fragment not amenable to screw purchase. Confirm restoration of DRUJ stability on repeat stress testing after fixation — styloid fixation alone may or may not resolve the instability, and this must be re-checked, not assumed.
8. Recheck DRUJ stability once more after all fixation (radius, styloid, ± transfixion wires) is complete, through the full pronation-supination arc, before leaving the OR.
9. Bend and cut transfixion wires outside the skin, cap the ends, and dress; document the final stable rotational arc in the operative note for the immobilization prescription.
10. Immobilize the forearm in a long-arm cast or Muenster-type orthosis in the documented stable rotational position (commonly neutral-to-supination for volar-sided injury) for 4–6 weeks.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always image the contralateral, uninjured wrist for comparison when DRUJ laxity is borderline — physiologic ligamentous laxity varies enough between patients that a side-to-side comparison prevents both over- and under-diagnosis of instability.
- Perform the stress test with the forearm actively rotated through pronation and supination under live fluoroscopy rather than static single-position views — instability is often position-dependent and can be missed on a single static image.
- When placing transfixion wires, aim them slightly proximal to the DRUJ articular margin and confirm on both PA and lateral views that the wires have not entered the sigmoid notch or radiocarpal joint — an intra-articular wire is a common technical error that accelerates cartilage wear.
- Use smooth (not threaded) wires for DRUJ transfixion — threaded wires are harder to remove in clinic and more likely to fracture at the bone-wire interface with forearm micromotion.
- Cut and bend wires so the tips sit just proud of the skin for easy clinic removal, but not so proud that they catch on clothing/dressings.
- When exploring an irreducible dislocation, look first for the ECU tendon dorsal to the ulnar head (the classic culprit in dorsal dislocation) before assuming a bony block — releasing the entrapped tendon is often all that is needed to achieve reduction.
- Restore the foveal ligament insertion, not just bony apposition, when fixing a basilar styloid fragment — a fragment that is anatomically reduced but does not restore foveal tension will not resolve DRUJ instability.
- Objective, reproducible DRUJ instability grading remains an unmet need in the literature despite routine use of ballottement/fovea sign/press testing — document your specific findings (translation quality, presence/absence of a clunk, comparison to the contralateral side) in the note rather than a bare "stable/unstable" conclusion, both for clinical clarity and because grading tools are still evolving.
- Re-test DRUJ stability after every meaningful change to the construct (after radius fixation, after styloid fixation, after transfixion) — stability is not a single checkpoint but a cumulative one.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- DRF-associated ulnar styloid tip nonunion is generally benign (no functional penalty per pooled meta-analytic data) when the DRUJ is clinically stable — do not over-treat.
- Isolated/basilar ulnar styloid nonunions are the exception: 2024 data show reduced motion, lower functional scores, more pain, and a higher rate of positive ballottement testing in this group — a distinction that should be drawn explicitly from the generally benign DRF-associated tip nonunion.
- K-wires are typically removed at 4–6 weeks once soft-tissue healing (TFCC/capsule) is presumed adequate; premature removal or premature rotational loading risks recurrent instability, while overly delayed removal increases pin-tract irritation/infection risk without added benefit.
- Objective, reproducible DRUJ instability grading remains an unmet need despite routine use of ballottement/fovea sign/press testing — document findings carefully rather than relying on a single binary "stable/unstable" call.
- Missing an irreducible dislocation and repeatedly attempting closed reduction delays definitive treatment — irreducibility itself, not a fixed number of failed attempts, is the trigger to convert to open exploration.
- Intra-articular wire placement (through the sigmoid notch or radiocarpal joint) is a recognized technical error that accelerates cartilage wear and can itself cause pain/stiffness — always confirm wire position on orthogonal fluoroscopic views.
- Pin-site infection with transfixion wires is generally superficial and managed with local care ± oral antibiotics; a deep/septic DRUJ is rare but requires urgent washout.
- Unrecognized or under-treated DRUJ instability progresses to chronic instability, painful clicking, weakness in grip and forearm rotation, and accelerated DRUJ arthrosis — this is the principal argument for a low threshold to reassess and treat intraoperatively rather than deferring to "watch and see" in a borderline case.
- Loss of forearm rotation postoperatively most often stems from DRUJ incongruity/malunion, heterotopic ossification of the interosseous membrane after high-energy injury, or under-recognized TFCC/DRUJ pathology — a pronosupination deficit should always trigger a specific search for DRUJ-level pathology rather than being attributed to generic "stiffness."
- Neglected/missed DRUJ instability presenting late (weeks to months) narrows treatment options to soft-tissue reconstruction or, in low-demand/degenerative presentations, salvage procedures (Sauvé-Kapandji, Darrach, or DRUJ arthroplasty) — reinforcing why intraoperative and early follow-up DRUJ assessment matters.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: immobilized in the stable rotational position; digit AROM.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: forearm rotation blocked until wire removal (~4–6 weeks); focus therapy on digit/elbow/shoulder motion.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: progressive rotational ROM after wire removal.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: full strengthening once union/repair maturity confirmed.
- **Imaging**: post-op, then at wire removal (~4–6 wk), then at return-to-rotation milestones.
