## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Progressive infantile (<4y), juvenile (4–10y), or adolescent (>10y) Blount disease (tibia vara) that has failed, or is not a candidate for, brace treatment.
- Technique is selected by Langenskiöld stage, age, and growth remaining:
  - **Guided growth (lateral proximal tibial ± proximal fibular hemiepiphysiodesis):** younger children with adequate growth remaining and mild-moderate deformity — classically <4–5 years, Langenskiöld I–III, but juvenile/adolescent patients with substantial growth remaining are increasingly accepted candidates.
  - **Proximal tibial valgus osteotomy:** more severe deformity, older children, or guided-growth failure/relapse.
  - **Elevation of the depressed medial plateau:** added for Langenskiöld IV–V.
  - **Physeal bar resection:** a focal Stage VI bar with substantial growth remaining.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Active infection or skin compromise over the planned incision.
- Guided growth specifically is a poor choice when: less than ~1 year of growth remains (insufficient time to correct before physiologic closure), the family/caregiver cannot reliably attend follow-up every 3–4 months (unmonitored overcorrection or undercorrection is the main failure mode), or the deformity is already severe/multiplanar (large metaphyseal-epiphyseal step, significant procurvatum, or marked internal tibial torsion) — these usually need osteotomy for a single-stage correction.
- Morbid obesity does not contraindicate surgery but should be discussed explicitly: it is a driver of recurrence and implant stress, and weight management counseling should be part of the treatment plan.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or medical instability precluding anesthesia.
- A Langenskiöld VI bar occupying a large proportion of the physis with minimal growth remaining is better served by combined osteotomy/epiphysiodesis (with or without contralateral epiphysiodesis) than by bar resection alone.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** standing AP long-leg (hip-to-ankle) radiograph is mandatory for both diagnosis and templating — measure the mechanical axis, the tibiofemoral angle, and the Levine-Drennan metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle (MDA; >11° in a child <2–3y is suspicious for pathologic rather than physiologic bowing). Stage by Langenskiöld on the AP tibia. Obtain a lateral view to assess procurvatum/recurvatum, which guided growth alone will not correct. Consider a rotational profile exam and, in complex/revision cases, a long-leg CT scanogram or EOS biplanar imaging for precise deformity analysis (level, magnitude, and plane of the CORA — center of rotation of angulation).
- **Growth remaining:** estimate remaining proximal tibial physeal growth (~6 mm/yr at the proximal tibia) using bone age or the Paley/Green-Anderson data if timing a hemiepiphysiodesis or planning epiphysiodesis of the contralateral limb.
- **Implant selection:** for guided growth, a two-hole tension-band plate ("8-plate") with two cannulated screws sized to the child's bone (typically 4.5 mm cannulated screws in a 6–8-year-old, smaller in an infantile patient); have both a standard and a "mini" plate size available. Confirm screw length templating off the AP and lateral radiographs — screws that are too long risk contralateral cortex/soft-tissue impingement, too short risk pull-out.
- **Osteotomy planning:** decide acute versus gradual correction based on deformity magnitude — as a working rule, corrections beyond roughly 20° or in an older/heavier child are safer performed gradually with an external fixator to avoid acute neurovascular injury and compartment syndrome. Preoperative templating should define the osteotomy level (typically proximal tibial metaphysis just distal to the tibial tuberosity), the wedge angle/degree of correction (target 3–7° of valgus overcorrection beyond neutral mechanical axis, since these children tend to recur into varus), and fixation method (staples, plate, external fixator).
- **Equipment/instrumentation list:** fluoroscopy (C-arm), pediatric 8-plate/tension-band system with cannulated screwdriver, oscillating saw and osteotomes, K-wires, a calibrated wedge-resection guide if performing a closing-wedge osteotomy, tourniquet, and (if planned) external fixator system (Ilizarov ring or monolateral/hexapod frame) with the appropriate wire/half-pin set.
- **Consent discussion:** explicitly discuss recurrence risk (higher with higher Langenskiöld stage at presentation), the possibility of needing a second procedure (implant removal, revision osteotomy, or bar resection), and peroneal nerve palsy/compartment syndrome risk with acute correction of larger deformities.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia, typically with an endotracheal tube or LMA depending on institutional protocol; a regional block (adductor canal or femoral/sciatic) or local infiltration is a useful adjunct for postoperative analgesia, particularly for osteotomy cases.
- **Positioning:** supine on a radiolucent table, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip if needed to neutralize rotation and keep the patella facing directly anterior — rotational malalignment is easy to introduce if the limb is allowed to externally rotate during exposure.
- **Tourniquet:** a well-padded thigh tourniquet is standard for osteotomy cases; guided growth alone can often be done without a tourniquet given its minimal-exposure nature, though many surgeons still apply one for a clean field.
- **Fluoroscopy:** C-arm brought in from the contralateral side (or foot of bed) positioned to obtain true AP and lateral images of the proximal tibia/knee without repositioning the limb; confirm fluoroscopic images are square (no rotation) before making correction/fixation decisions — a rotated fluoroscopic image is a common source of malreduction.
- **Draping:** prep and drape the entire limb free from mid-thigh to the toes so the whole mechanical axis and rotational alignment can be assessed intraoperatively; a sterile tourniquet is preferred if a very proximal exposure is anticipated.
- **Landmarks:** mark the joint line, tibial tuberosity, fibular head, and the planned incision (lateral parapatellar for the plate/screws in guided growth; anterolateral proximal tibial incision, and a separate posterolateral/distal fibular incision if a fibular osteotomy is planned) before draping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### A. Guided growth (lateral proximal tibial ± proximal fibular hemiepiphysiodesis)

1. Confirm the correct side and re-review the standing long-leg film and MDA in the room ("time-out").
2. Under fluoroscopy, identify the lateral proximal tibial physis on a true AP view.
3. Make a small (2–3 cm) longitudinal incision centered over the lateral physis, just anterior to the fibular head, splitting the iliotibial band/fascia longitudinally.
4. Place the plate extraperiosteally, straddling the physis with one hole proximal (epiphyseal) and one distal (metaphyseal), confirmed centered on the physis in both AP and lateral fluoroscopic views.
5. Insert the epiphyseal screw first, angled slightly to stay extraphyseal and parallel to the joint line; then insert the metaphyseal screw, again confirming both screws and the plate are extraperiosteal and the screws diverge slightly from parallel (this reduces the tether stiffness minimally but is not mandatory — many surgeons use fully parallel screws).
6. If proximal fibular growth is contributing to persistent lateral tethering or if fibular overgrowth is a concern, a proximal fibular hemiepiphysiodesis (screw or staple) is added — take care to identify and protect the common peroneal nerve, which wraps around the fibular neck immediately deep to this exposure.
7. Confirm screw position and plate seating on final fluoroscopy; close in layers.
8. Repeat contralaterally if bilateral disease.

### B. Proximal tibial valgus osteotomy (acute)

1. Make an anterolateral incision centered over the proximal tibial metaphysis, roughly 1–1.5 cm distal to the joint line and just distal to the tibial tuberosity.
2. Expose the anterolateral tibial metaphysis subperiosteally; protect the patellar tendon insertion medially and the anterior compartment musculature laterally.
3. If a concomitant fibular osteotomy is needed to allow correction, perform it through a **separate posterolateral or distal fibular incision** (not through the same anterior window as the tibial osteotomy) — a **distal-third fibular osteotomy** is preferred over a proximal one specifically to keep dissection away from the common peroneal nerve, unless a proximal tibiofibular synostosis mandates a more proximal cut.
4. Under fluoroscopic guidance, mark the osteotomy level (typically just distal to the tibial tuberosity, above the deltoid ligament's tibial insertion is not relevant here — stay well distal to the physis) and perform the osteotomy transversely or as a planned closing/opening wedge, using multiple drill holes followed by osteotome completion, or an oscillating saw with copious irrigation to avoid thermal injury.
5. For a lateral closing-wedge correction: resect a laterally based wedge sized to the preoperative template, hinge on the medial cortex, and close the osteotomy to achieve 3–7° of valgus overcorrection past neutral mechanical axis.
6. For an opening-wedge (medial) correction: create the osteotomy, open it medially to the templated angle, and fill the defect with structural allograft or the resected fibular/tibial wedge if available; this avoids shortening but risks slower union and requires stable fixation to hold the gap open.
7. Correct any residual internal tibial torsion and procurvatum concurrently by rotating and angulating the distal fragment as needed before final fixation — Blount deformity is frequently multiplanar and correcting the frontal plane alone leaves a residual torsional deformity.
8. Provisionally pin the osteotomy, confirm mechanical axis and rotation on fluoroscopy (compare thigh-foot angle and patellar orientation to the contralateral limb), then place definitive fixation — step staples, a periarticular locking plate, or crossed screws depending on age/bone quality.
9. Release the tourniquet before closure and inspect for bleeding; consider prophylactic anterior compartment fasciotomy or a low threshold for postoperative compartment monitoring in large acute corrections (see Pitfalls).
10. Close in layers; a bulky dressing/splint or long-leg cast is applied.

### C. Gradual correction with external fixator (larger deformities)

1. Perform the tibial (and, if needed, distal fibular) osteotomy through a limited approach as above, but leave it as a low-energy corticotomy-style cut rather than resecting a wedge.
2. Apply the external fixator (Ilizarov ring or monolateral/hexapod frame) with reference wires/pins placed parallel to the joint line proximally and to the ankle mortise distally, confirmed on fluoroscopy before completing the osteotomy.
3. Complete the osteotomy percutaneously, preserving periosteal and soft-tissue attachments as much as possible to promote bone healing during distraction.
4. Begin distraction after a latency period (typically 5–7 days), at a rate of roughly 0.75–1 mm/day in divided increments, with serial radiographs to monitor regenerate bone formation and correction rate.
5. Correct gradually to the templated mechanical axis and rotation; a hexapod (computer-assisted) frame allows multiplanar correction (varus/valgus, procurvatum/recurvatum, rotation, and any translation) to be programmed and adjusted based on interval films.
6. Once the target correction and slight overcorrection are achieved, hold the frame in neutral fixation mode until adequate regenerate consolidation (typically assessed by cortical bridging on at least 3 of 4 cortices) before frame removal.

### D. Elevation of the depressed medial tibial plateau (Langenskiöld IV–V)

1. Performed in conjunction with the valgus osteotomy above.
2. Expose the medial proximal tibial metaphysis through a separate medial incision (or extend the anterolateral exposure medially with care), staying extraperiosteal to protect the medial physis where possible.
3. Under fluoroscopy, create a metaphyseal osteotomy beneath the depressed medial epiphyseal fragment, leaving the fragment attached to its overlying articular cartilage.
4. Elevate the depressed fragment to restore the medial joint line, and fill the resulting metaphyseal defect with autograft (iliac crest) or structural allograft to support the elevated segment.
5. Fix the elevated fragment with smooth K-wires or small screws placed to avoid crossing the physis; combine with the concurrent valgus osteotomy fixation.

### E. Physeal bar resection (focal Stage VI bar)

1. Localize the bar precisely with preoperative CT and/or MRI, correlating with intraoperative fluoroscopy.
2. Approach through a metaphyseal window (often a trapdoor of bone) directly over the bar, taking a route that avoids the neurovascular structures and articular surface.
3. Resect the bony bridge completely using curettes and a high-speed burr under fluoroscopic and, where available, direct/loupe magnification, confirming complete resection by visualizing normal-appearing physeal cartilage circumferentially at the resection margins.
4. Interpose fat, bone wax, cranioplast (PMMA), or a silicone spacer into the defect to prevent bar reformation.
5. Consider concurrent contralateral epiphysiodesis (if a length discrepancy is anticipated) or guided growth of the remaining physis if the bar is extensive and residual growth is asymmetric.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always obtain a true AP fluoroscopic image (patella pointing straight up, no rotation) before finalizing 8-plate screw position — an oblique image makes the plate look centered on the physis when it is actually angled, risking asymmetric growth arrest.
- When placing the guided-growth plate, aim for the screws to diverge slightly rather than run perfectly parallel; this is a minor technical nuance some surgeons favor to reduce the risk of the plate acting as a rigid strut, though clinical evidence for its necessity is limited — prioritize correct physeal straddling over screw divergence.
- Template the wedge angle for an acute osteotomy generously enough to achieve 3–7° of valgus overcorrection, not just neutral — Blount patients recur into varus more readily than typical genu varum, so undercorrection is a common and avoidable cause of relapse.
- Correct rotation and sagittal-plane deformity (procurvatum) at the same sitting as the coronal correction whenever performing an osteotomy — treating only the varus and leaving residual internal tibial torsion is a frequent source of persistent gait abnormality and patient/family dissatisfaction even after a "successful" coronal correction.
- Keep the fibular osteotomy (when needed) in the distal third and through a separate incision from the tibial osteotomy — combining both cuts anterior to the coronal plane through one incision measurably increases peroneal nerve injury risk.
- For gradual correction with a hexapod frame, slightly overcorrect the mechanical axis (a few degrees of valgus) at the end of distraction, anticipating some settling/relapse during consolidation and remodeling.
- In the very young child (infantile Blount, Langenskiöld I–II), a KAFO brace trial and close radiographic follow-up may still convert a would-be surgical case into a non-operative one — confirm the brace trial has genuinely failed (progression of MDA or Langenskiöld stage despite compliance) before proceeding to guided growth.
- Use iliac crest autograft liberally when elevating a depressed medial plateau or filling an opening-wedge osteotomy defect in younger children — it incorporates reliably and avoids the cost/availability issues of structural allograft.
- Book guided-growth follow-up visits at fixed 3–4 month intervals from the day of surgery and communicate this explicitly to the family at consent — unmonitored guided growth is the most common preventable cause of both undercorrection (missed removal window) and overcorrection (implant left in too long).

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Peroneal nerve palsy and compartment syndrome:** the dominant risks with rapid/large acute corrections — favor gradual (external fixator) correction for larger deformities. Keep the fibular osteotomy distal-third and in a separate incision from the tibial osteotomy; combined proximal cuts through one anterior incision carry the highest reported nerve injury rates. Have a low threshold for prophylactic fasciotomy or overnight compartment monitoring (serial neurovascular checks, pain out of proportion, pain with passive toe stretch) after a large acute correction.
- **Recurrence:** can follow undercorrection or premature removal of the guided-growth implant — do not remove the plate at the first sign of correction; confirm adequate (ideally mild over-) correction on standing films before removal, and counsel families that some rebound toward varus is expected after implant removal.
- **Physeal arrest/bar formation:** can occur after an osteotomy performed too close to the growth plate, or from thermal injury during saw use without irrigation — keep the osteotomy well distal to the physis and irrigate continuously.
- **Higher Langenskiöld stage at presentation** correlates with higher recurrence risk after correction and a greater likelihood that guided growth alone will fail, favoring primary osteotomy in these cases — set expectations accordingly at consent.
- **Hardware-related complications:** screw breakage, plate/screw prominence causing irritation, or pin-tract infection with external fixation — monitor at each follow-up visit.
- **Non-union or delayed union**, particularly with opening-wedge osteotomy without adequate graft, or in obese adolescents where compressive forces across the osteotomy are high.
- **Under-recognized sagittal/rotational deformity:** correcting only the coronal-plane varus while leaving procurvatum or internal tibial torsion uncorrected is a technical error that leaves the patient with residual deformity despite a technically adequate valgus correction.
- **Vascular injury:** the popliteal trifurcation and its branches are at risk with posterior extension of dissection or overly aggressive osteotome use near the posterior cortex — stay subperiosteal and use image guidance for osteotomy depth.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Guided growth:** weight-bearing as tolerated, no formal rehab beyond routine activity; radiographic follow-up every 3–4 months to monitor correction rate (typically 0.5–1°/month) and plan implant removal.
- **Osteotomy:** protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks (longer with external fixator until adequate callus), progressive weight-bearing and knee/ankle ROM physiotherapy through weeks 6–12, full activity typically by 3–4 months.
