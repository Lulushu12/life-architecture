## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Sanders II/III displaced intra-articular fractures where the patient and surgeon elect operative treatment after shared decision-making (see below) — ORIF via extensile lateral or sinus tarsi approach.
- Sanders IV comminuted fractures — primary subtalar arthrodesis is strongly favored given the unreconstructable posterior facet.
- Selected Sanders III fractures in older or lower-demand patients — primary fusion is a reasonable alternative to ORIF to avoid a later secondary fusion.
- Significant heel widening, varus/valgus hindfoot malalignment, or lateral wall blowout causing (or threatening) peroneal tendon impingement, even in borderline displacement.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Sanders I (nondisplaced) fractures — always non-operative regardless of fragment count.
- Uncontrolled soft-tissue swelling/fracture blisters without resolution of the "wrinkle sign" — proceeding through compromised skin risks wound necrosis with the extensile lateral approach in particular.
- Active infection, severe peripheral vascular disease, uncontrolled diabetes, or heavy smoking — each independently raises wound complication risk; consider non-operative treatment or a minimally invasive (sinus tarsi/percutaneous) approach instead of extensile lateral exposure.
- Poor surgical candidacy or patient preference to avoid surgical risk — per UK HeFT long-term data, non-operative treatment yields equivalent 60-month functional scores to ORIF with fewer secondary procedures, so this is a legitimate shared decision, not merely a fallback.
- Compartment syndrome of the foot present at evaluation requires fasciotomy first; definitive ORIF is staged after the soft-tissue envelope recovers.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: lateral, axial (Harris), and Broden radiographic views; CT (coronal, sagittal, axial cuts through the widest part of the posterior facet) is the gold standard for Sanders classification and defines the fracture personality (primary and secondary fracture lines, degree of comminution, lateral wall blowout) that dictates approach and fixation strategy.
- **Timing**: plan surgery once soft-tissue swelling permits — classically the positive "wrinkle sign" (skin creases reappear with passive dorsiflexion/eversion), typically 10–14 days after injury — unless using the sinus tarsi approach, which tolerates earlier surgery (often within the first week) because it avoids the large extensile flap.
- **Implant selection**: precontoured lateral calcaneal locking plate (low-profile) for the extensile lateral approach; cannulated or headless compression screws ± a small buttress plate for the sinus tarsi approach. Structural or cancellous bone graft/substitute is occasionally needed for large subchondral voids after posterior facet elevation, though most contemporary constructs rely on the plate/screws for support rather than routine grafting.
- **Equipment**: mini and large pointed reduction clamps, a Freer/Schanz pin or K-wire "joystick" placed in the tuberosity for indirect reduction of height/varus, a laminar spreader for the sinus tarsi, and a dedicated calcaneal (Essex-Lopresti-style) reduction instrument set if available.
- **Case-specific planning**: for bilateral calcaneus fractures or a fall-from-height mechanism, screen the thoracolumbar spine and contralateral heel before finalizing the surgical plan (~10% co-incidence of spine fracture). Discuss primary subtalar fusion with the patient preoperatively for any Sanders IV pattern so consent covers both ORIF and fusion contingencies.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, often combined with a popliteal sciatic block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: lateral decubitus with the injured side up and a bump/bean bag for the extensile lateral approach (allows gravity-assisted retraction of the flap and direct posterior-to-anterior visualization); supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for the sinus tarsi approach, which is more forgiving of positioning.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet, inflated for the duration of the exposure and reduction; deflate before final closure to confirm hemostasis and flap perfusion, particularly for the extensile lateral approach where flap vascularity is the limiting factor for wound healing.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm positioned for lateral, axial (Harris heel), and Broden views without repositioning the leg; a mini C-arm is often sufficient and easier to maneuver for the sinus tarsi approach.
- **Draping**: prep the entire limb circumferentially from above the tourniquet to the toes so the foot can be manipulated for reduction (traction, varus/valgus correction) and axial views obtained intraoperatively.
- **Landmarks**: for the extensile lateral approach, mark the classic "L"- or hockey-stick-shaped incision — vertical limb midway between the posterior border of the fibula and the Achilles tendon, curving anteriorly at the junction of the plantar and lateral skin (the "no-man's-land" junction) toward the base of the 5th metatarsal, staying just proximal/plantar to the peroneal tendons. For the sinus tarsi approach, mark a shorter oblique incision from the tip of the fibula toward the base of the 4th metatarsal, centered over the sinus tarsi.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Position, prep, and apply the tourniquet** as above.
2. **Choose the approach based on fracture pattern and soft-tissue status.**
   - *Extensile lateral approach*: gives full visualization of the posterior facet and lateral wall for open reduction and plate fixation — historically the standard for Sanders II/III/IV but with a meaningful wound complication rate.
   - *Sinus tarsi (minimally invasive) approach*: gives direct visualization of the posterior facet through a smaller incision with percutaneous screw ± limited plate fixation, with lower wound complication rates and comparable reduction quality in experienced hands for II/III patterns — now preferred at many high-volume centers.
3. **Extensile lateral approach — incision and flap**: incise directly to bone at the corner of the "L" to raise a true full-thickness flap in one layer (periosteum, subcutaneous tissue, and skin together) — do not develop a subcutaneous plane, which devascularizes the flap. Identify and protect the sural nerve and peroneal tendons within the flap; some surgeons tag the sural nerve with a vessel loop proximally where it is more reliably found superficial to the peroneal tendon sheath.
4. **Elevate the flap subperiosteally off the lateral wall** and retract it anteriorly-superiorly using 0.062" K-wires bent into a retractor and driven into the talus, cuboid, and/or fibula ("no-touch" technique) rather than handheld retractors, minimizing direct pressure/trauma on the flap edges.
5. **Remove the lateral wall fragment** (kept as a free fragment or hinged) to expose the depressed posterior facet and the primary/secondary fracture lines.
6. **Reduce the posterior facet first**: identify the constant (sustentacular) fragment, which stays reduced to the talus via strong interosseous ligaments, and use it as the fixed reference. Elevate the depressed posterior facet fragment(s) back to the sustentacular fragment under direct vision, restoring articular congruity; provisionally pin with K-wires.
7. **Correct tuberosity height, length, varus, and width**: place a large Schanz pin or pin-and-clamp into the tuberosity fragment as a joystick; apply longitudinal traction and a valgus-directed force to disimpact the tuberosity, restore heel height, and correct varus malalignment. Confirm restoration of Böhler's angle (target 20–40°) and Gissane's angle (target 120–145°) fluoroscopically.
8. **Reduce and provisionally fix the lateral wall fragment** back over the reconstructed posterior facet, restoring heel width.
9. **Apply the precontoured lateral locking plate**, placing screws into the sustentacular fragment (subtalar joint support), the anterior process, and the tuberosity to buttress the reconstruction; avoid screw penetration into the subtalar or calcaneocuboid joints — check with intraoperative fluoroscopy (axial and lateral) and direct visualization.
10. **Address the lateral wall exostosis/peroneal impingement** at index surgery if the blown-out lateral wall would otherwise abut the peroneal tendons — trim prominent bone before closure.
11. **Sinus tarsi approach alternative**: after the shorter oblique incision, use a laminar spreader in the sinus tarsi to distract and directly visualize the posterior facet; reduce the facet under direct vision and fluoroscopic guidance, provisionally pin, then place percutaneous screws from the tuberosity into the sustentaculum/anterior process, supplemented by a small lateral plate if additional buttress support is needed.
12. **For Sanders IV, or Sanders III in an older/lower-demand patient**, consider converting to (or primarily performing) subtalar arthrodesis: prepare the posterior facet cartilage surfaces, restore height/alignment as above, and compress the joint with two or three large cannulated screws from the tuberosity into the talar body/neck — recent series report union rates around 94% with good functional outcomes and avoidance of a staged secondary fusion.
13. **Final fluoroscopic check**: confirm Böhler's angle, Gissane's angle, heel width/height, screw length (no intra-articular or subtalar penetration), and plate position on lateral, axial, and Broden views.
14. **Release the tourniquet before closure** and confirm flap perfusion; close the extensile lateral approach in layered fashion (deep fascia/periosteum as one layer, subcutaneous, then a modified Allgöwer-Donati or horizontal mattress skin closure to minimize tension on the corner of the flap) over a drain. Apply a bulky Jones dressing/well-padded splint.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Raise the extensile lateral flap as a single full-thickness layer straight down to bone — a thin, purely subcutaneous flap is the single most common technical driver of wound-edge necrosis with this approach.
- Use bent K-wires driven into adjacent bone (talus, cuboid, fibula) as retractors ("no-touch" technique) rather than handheld or self-retaining retractors that place continuous pressure on the flap edges.
- Identify the sural nerve early, proximally, where it runs superficial to the peroneal sheath — a tonsil clamp used to gently spread the subcutaneous tissue helps locate and tag it before deeper dissection risks injury; no surgical approach to the calcaneus is a truly "safe zone" for the nerve, so deliberate identification beats relying on anatomic landmarks alone.
- Use the sustentacular fragment as your fixed reduction reference — it reliably stays attached to the talus via the strong interosseous talocalcaneal ligaments even in severely comminuted fractures, making it the most reproducible starting point for reduction.
- A large Schanz pin in the tuberosity is far more effective than manual manipulation for disimpacting and de-rotating a varus-collapsed tuberosity — apply firm longitudinal traction before attempting valgus correction, since an impacted tuberosity will not correct rotationally until length is restored first.
- Trim the lateral wall exostosis and address peroneal impingement at the index procedure — deferring this to a second surgery is avoidable in the vast majority of cases.
- For the sinus tarsi approach, a formal laminar spreader (not just a small periosteal elevator) placed directly in the sinus tarsi dramatically improves visualization of the posterior facet without requiring the extensile flap.
- Confirm screw length and trajectory with both axial (Harris) and Broden views intraoperatively — the lateral view alone will not reliably show intra-articular screw penetration into the posterior facet.
- Consider a two-team or staged approach (spanning external fixation followed by definitive ORIF once swelling resolves) in high-energy, severely swollen, or polytrauma presentations rather than forcing an early extensile exposure through compromised soft tissue.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Wound complication rates are highest with the extensile lateral approach (historically cited up to ~25% in some series, lower in contemporary practice with careful soft-tissue technique/no-touch flap handling); the sinus tarsi approach substantially reduces this risk.
- UK HeFT long-term (60-month) data show no significant difference in Kerr-Atkins functional score between ORIF and non-operative treatment for Sanders II/III (79.2 vs 76.4), but significantly more additional surgical procedures in the ORIF group (10 vs 4 patients, p=0.043) — use this to counsel patients that surgery does not guarantee superior long-term function and carries its own reoperation burden.
- ORIF still edges out primary arthrodesis on pooled functional scores for Sanders II/III specifically — reserve primary fusion for Sanders IV or select older/lower-demand Sanders III patients.
- Compartment syndrome of the foot occurs in up to 10% of displaced intra-articular calcaneus fractures — assess and measure compartment pressures with clinical suspicion; missed compartment syndrome produces claw-toe deformity disproportionate to the index fracture.
- Screen the contralateral calcaneus and thoracolumbar spine for co-incident fracture (~10%) in any fall-from-height mechanism, especially with bilateral calcaneus fractures.
- Proceeding to the extensile lateral approach before the wrinkle sign is positive is a well-recognized, avoidable cause of major flap necrosis — when in doubt, wait, or switch to the sinus tarsi approach.
- Subfibular/peroneal tendon impingement from unaddressed lateral wall blowout causes chronic lateral pain and tendinopathy — address at index surgery rather than leaving for a secondary procedure.
- Screw penetration into the subtalar or calcaneocuboid joint is a common, avoidable technical error — always confirm with axial and Broden fluoroscopic views, not lateral alone.
- Undercorrection of varus tuberosity malalignment or persistent heel widening leads to lateral wall impingement and abnormal gait loading — use a tuberosity joystick and confirm correction fluoroscopically before final fixation.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks**: elevation, NWB, early subtalar/ankle active ROM exercises started as soon as pain allows (even before weight-bearing) — this is one of the most important modifiable elements of hindfoot rehab.
- **2–8 weeks**: continued NWB with ROM progression; wound surveillance, especially with the extensile lateral approach.
- **8–12 weeks**: progressive weight-bearing introduced once radiographic consolidation is present (~10–12 weeks typical); sinus tarsi approach patients may progress slightly earlier than extensile-lateral patients.
- **3–6 months**: full weight-bearing, strengthening, gait normalization; heavy manual labor/return to sport often delayed 6–12 months. A meaningful minority (10–15%+) require secondary procedures (hardware removal, subtalar fusion for post-traumatic arthritis) in the first 1–2 years, higher after ORIF than after non-op per HeFT long-term data.
