## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- The most demanding distal femur fracture patterns: very short/comminuted distal segments (33-C2/C3), periprosthetic distal femur fractures with poor bone stock, and fractures at high risk of varus collapse or nonunion with a single-implant strategy.
- Periarticular nonunion salvage, where a prior nail-only or plate-only construct has failed to achieve union and additional rigidity is needed without sacrificing the residual bone stock further.
- Medially comminuted or medially deficient patterns where a lateral plate alone is at recognized risk of varus collapse, and central intramedullary support is desired in addition to fixed-angle lateral fixation.
- Osteoporotic distal femur fractures where neither implant alone is expected to provide durable fixed-angle stability through the healing period.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Simple extra-articular or simple intra-articular fractures with good bone stock and an adequate distal segment — a single implant (nail or plate alone) achieves comparable stability with less soft-tissue dissection and shorter operative time; reserve the combined construct for genuinely demanding patterns rather than applying it routinely.
- Active infection at either the intended nail entry portal or the lateral plating field.
- Situations where the added operative time and blood loss of a combined construct are not tolerable given the patient's physiologic status (e.g., an unstable polytrauma patient) — damage-control external fixation is the appropriate interim step instead.
- Very limited bone stock such that neither a nail nor a plate can achieve adequate purchase — distal femoral replacement may be the more appropriate salvage strategy in a frail, elderly, unreconstructable pattern.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP/lateral femur plus dedicated knee imaging and CT with coronal/sagittal reconstruction — essential for characterizing both the articular pattern and the extent/location of metaphyseal (particularly medial) comminution that justifies the combined-construct decision in the first place.
- **Implant selection**: plan both implants together rather than sequentially — select a retrograde nail (standard length or a shorter "mini" nail depending on the system and the extent of proximal shaft involvement) sized for the canal, and a lateral distal femoral locking plate long enough to span the comminuted zone and integrate proximally with the nail's working length. Confirm the two implant systems are compatible in terms of screw trajectories (avoid planned nail interlocking screws and plate screws colliding within the same bone corridor) — review the specific systems' compatibility notes or prior described techniques, since implant conflict is a real and system-specific planning issue.
- **Equipment**: full retrograde nailing set (see the dedicated retrograde nailing technique) plus a full lateral locked plating/MIPO set (see the dedicated LISS/locked plating technique), reduction clamps and a femoral distractor, headless compression screws for any coronal (Hoffa) fragment, and fluoroscopy capable of imaging the entire construct.
- **Case-specific planning**: for periprosthetic fractures, confirm compatibility of the retrograde nail's proximal extent and the existing prosthesis (the nail must not impinge on a retained, well-fixed femoral component); for periarticular nonunion salvage, review prior implant position/removal needs before planning trajectories for the new combined construct.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia standard, given the longer combined operative time; regional block adjuncts for postoperative analgesia as in other distal femur procedures.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table (fracture table traction is not required and can complicate simultaneous nail and plate work); a bolster/triangle under the distal thigh to hold the knee in flexion facilitates both the retrograde nail entry portal exposure and lateral plate passage.
- **Draping**: wide prep and drape from the hip (or at minimum well proximal to the anticipated proximal extent of the plate) to the foot, since the knee must be accessible for the nail's entry portal while the lateral thigh must be accessible for submuscular plate passage — this is a larger surgical field than either procedure performed in isolation.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: confirm the C-arm can image the knee (for nail entry and distal interlocking) and the full length of the planned plate (proximal shaft) without needing to reposition the patient, only the C-arm.
- **Landmarks**: mark the standard retrograde nail entry incision (medial parapatellar or trans-tendon, centered over Blumensaat's line/PCL origin) as well as the distal and proximal lateral incisions for the submuscular plate before prepping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Establish the retrograde nail entry portal** just anterior to the PCL origin, in line with Blumensaat's line on the lateral fluoroscopic view, following the standard retrograde nailing entry technique — under fluoroscopic guidance, center the entry guidewire in the AP view in the middle of the intercondylar notch and in line with the medullary canal axis.
2. **Ream and pass the retrograde nail** across the fracture into the femoral canal, achieving central axial and rotational control first — the nail is placed before the plate in the standard sequence, since it establishes the core alignment that the plate will then reinforce.
3. **Achieve and confirm reduction** of the articular block (with lag screws if a coronal/Hoffa fragment or simple articular split is present) and of the overall metaphyseal alignment before locking the nail distally.
4. **Lock the nail distally** with multiple interlocking screws in a divergent/multiplanar configuration for rotational and coronal control of the distal fragment.
5. **Create the lateral plating exposure/incisions** (distal, over the lateral condyle, and proximal, for submuscular plate insertion), planning screw trajectories to avoid the already-placed nail and its interlocking screws.
6. **Pass the lateral locking plate submuscularly**, selecting a longer plate than might be used in an isolated plating case — a longer plate allows the proximal integration of the two constructs together, spanning well past the nail's distal interlocking screws to distribute load across a longer working length.
7. **Confirm plate position** against the lateral condyle and check alignment relative to the joint line on fluoroscopy, exactly as in isolated locked plating — the presence of the nail does not exempt this step.
8. **Place distal plate screws first**, choosing trajectories and lengths that avoid the nail and its distal interlocking screws (this generally requires slightly different screw angles/lengths than a nail-free plating case, planned preoperatively from the implant compatibility review).
9. **Reassess and fine-tune alignment** (coronal, sagittal, rotational) after distal plate screw placement — this is the last opportunity to correct any residual malalignment before proximal fixation locks the entire construct.
10. **Place proximal plate screws**, again selecting positions that avoid the nail shaft and the nail's proximal interlocking screw (if present) within the overlap zone.
11. **Final fluoroscopic assessment** of the complete combined construct — articular reduction, nail position and interlocking screws, plate position and screws, and overall limb alignment/length/rotation compared to the contralateral limb.
12. **Close all incisions in layers**, achieving hemostasis at each of the (typically three to four) incision sites used across the combined procedure.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Place the nail first, then the plate — this sequence, described in the nail-plate combination literature, establishes the central axial/rotational reduction first and allows the plate to be applied around an already-corrected alignment rather than fighting for reduction with two implants simultaneously.
- Use a longer plate than you would choose for isolated lateral locked plating of a similar fracture — the combined construct's biomechanical advantage depends partly on the plate spanning well past the nail's distal interlocks to create a long, gradually-loaded working length rather than a short, stress-concentrated overlap zone.
- Plan screw trajectories for both implants together, preoperatively, rather than improvising intraoperatively — implant collision (a plate screw striking the nail, or vice versa) is a real, avoidable problem that preoperative planning largely prevents.
- Place distal screws (both nail interlocks and plate distal screws) before final proximal fixation of either implant, checking alignment at each stage — this staged approach allows correction of residual malalignment before the construct becomes fully rigid.
- For periprosthetic cases, confirm on preoperative imaging that the nail's planned proximal extent will not abut or impinge on the retained femoral component; adjust nail length/entry accordingly.
- When treating a periarticular nonunion with a combined construct, remove prior hardware methodically and assess the local bone stock directly before finalizing implant choice — nonunion bone is frequently more sclerotic/atrophic than preoperative imaging suggests.
- Reserve the combined construct for the patterns that truly need it (severe comminution, medial deficiency, short distal segment, nonunion salvage) — for simple patterns, a single well-placed implant achieves comparable outcomes with less dissection and operative time.
- Consider tranexamic acid and meticulous staged hemostasis given the longer combined operative time and larger dissection footprint relative to either procedure alone.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Increased soft-tissue dissection/OR time compared with a single-implant strategy is the main technical trade-off.
- The evidence base (multiple 2024-2025 comparative series) is still mostly retrospective, though it increasingly supports nail-plate as at least equivalent, and often preferred, for AO/OTA 33-C fractures with severe comminution or medial bone loss.
- Comparative studies show mixed results versus dual (medial + lateral) plating: some series favor nail-plate for lower operative time/blood loss, while others show dual plating providing similarly robust mechanical control — treatment choice meaningfully affects knee alignment, clinical outcomes, and quality of life, so individualize based on local expertise and fracture-specific factors.
- Implant collision between the nail (or its interlocking screws) and the plate's screws is a specific, avoidable technical pitfall of the combined construct not present in either single-implant technique — prevent with careful preoperative trajectory planning and intraoperative fluoroscopic confirmation before final screw seating.
- Extending the nail-first, plate-second sequence to a plate-first approach without deliberate reason can make subsequent nail passage and distal interlocking more difficult once the plate and its screws are already in place — most described techniques favor nail placement first for this reason.
- Underestimating the added exposure needed for two implants can lead to inadequate visualization of the medial column or coronal fragments — plan incisions generously enough to confirm reduction quality at each stage rather than relying solely on fluoroscopy.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Nail-plate combined constructs, by virtue of added stability, increasingly allow earlier progressive weight bearing than single-implant plating in several 2024-2025 series.
- Otherwise follows the general distal femur fracture protocol: early protected knee ROM (0-90°) to prevent arthrofibrosis, progressive flexion toward 90-110° by 2-6 weeks and ≥110-120° by 6-12 weeks, progression to full weight bearing as radiographic healing allows, and return to high-demand/pivoting sport generally deferred 6-9 months pending union, painless full ROM, and quadriceps/hamstring strength ≥85-90% of the contralateral limb.
- Given the added construct rigidity, some series report faster progression to weight bearing and low rates of metalwork failure and nonunion compared with single-construct cohorts — confirm progression against serial radiographic healing rather than a fixed calendar timeline alone, particularly in the most comminuted or previously failed (nonunion salvage) cases.
