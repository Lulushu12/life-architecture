## Indications

Applies to any cemented femoral (or other medullary) component insertion, with particular importance in hip hemiarthroplasty for fracture, long-stem/revision femoral components, and patients with pathologic or heavily sclerotic/osteoporotic bone — all situations where bone cement implantation syndrome (BCIS) risk is elevated. Goal: prevent the pulmonary microembolization and hemodynamic collapse that follows forced intravasation of fat, marrow, air, and cement monomer during canal pressurization.

## Positioning & setup

No technique-specific positioning beyond standard positioning for the procedure being performed (e.g. hemiarthroplasty, THA). Setup requires explicit pre-cementation communication with anesthesia so hemodynamic monitoring is heightened and vasopressors are drawn up and ready before the cementing sequence begins.

## Steps

1. **Communicate explicitly with anesthesia immediately before cementation** so hemodynamic monitoring is heightened and vasopressors (phenylephrine or ephedrine) are drawn up and ready.
2. **Perform femoral canal lavage** (pulsatile lavage) before cement introduction to clear fat/marrow debris and reduce intramedullary pressure at the time of pressurization.
3. **Use a vent hole or retrograde ("distal-to-proximal") cement filling technique** to allow egress of marrow contents and air ahead of the cement column, avoiding a closed-system pressure spike.
4. **Avoid excessive/forceful pressurization**, particularly in osteoporotic canals where compliance is high and pressure transmission to the venous system is more efficient.
5. **In the very highest-risk patients** (severe cardiopulmonary disease, ASA IV, frail hip-fracture patients where a hemodynamic hit could be catastrophic), consider an uncemented stem as an alternative — individualized rather than routine, since registry data generally favor cemented fixation for hip fracture hemiarthroplasty on aggregate revision/mortality outcomes.
6. **If Grade 2–3 BCIS occurs** (per Donaldson grading): stop the cementing/impaction sequence if not yet complete, give 100% oxygen, IV fluid bolus, vasopressor for hypotension, and escalate to full ACLS if cardiovascular collapse develops.

## Pitfalls

- Risk is substantially higher in hip hemiarthroplasty for fracture than elective THA (osteoporotic, unprepared canal, frailer/higher-ASA cohort) — anticipatory readiness should scale accordingly rather than being uniform across all cemented cases.
- Long-stem or revision femoral components displace more medullary content at pressurization and carry correspondingly higher risk; canal lavage and vent-hole/retrograde filling technique are especially important in these cases.
- Do not continue seating the prosthesis through an evolving Grade 2 event — stop the sequence, do not "push through" hemodynamic compromise once it has started.
- The decision to convert to an uncemented stem in high-risk patients is a genuine trade-off (accepting higher periprosthetic fracture risk to eliminate BCIS risk), not a universally superior choice — national joint registry data favor cemented fixation on aggregate revision/mortality outcomes for hip fracture hemiarthroplasty.

## Post-op protocol

No distinct postoperative pathway specific to cementation technique; patients follow the same phased rehabilitation pathway as the underlying procedure (see the general perioperative rehabilitation protocol), with any intraoperative BCIS event documented and factored into postoperative monitoring intensity (e.g. extended cardiopulmonary observation after a Grade 2–3 event).
