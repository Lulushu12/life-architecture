## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Cemented femoral (or other medullary) component insertion for hip hemiarthroplasty, total hip arthroplasty, and cemented stem revision.
- Particularly relevant — and particularly high-stakes — in hip hemiarthroplasty for fracture, long-stem/revision femoral components, and patients with pathologic, heavily sclerotic, or osteoporotic bone, all of which raise bone cement implantation syndrome (BCIS) risk.
- Goal of the technique bundle below: prevent the pulmonary microembolization and hemodynamic collapse that follows forced intravasation of fat, marrow, air, and cement monomer during canal pressurization and component impaction.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications to cementing (favor uncemented fixation instead):**
- Severe cardiopulmonary disease, pulmonary hypertension, or ASA IV status where a hemodynamic hit from BCIS could be catastrophic — individualized decision, not an absolute rule.
- Active local or systemic infection precluding any implant insertion (applies equally to cemented and uncemented options).
- Canal geometry unsuitable for adequate cement mantle (e.g., very narrow/sclerotic canal where reaming for an uncemented press-fit stem may actually be preferable) — a template-driven, not intraoperative-improvised, decision.
- History of prior severe BCIS event on the contralateral side is not an absolute contraindication but should prompt explicit anesthesia discussion and consideration of an uncemented alternative.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Templating:** confirm femoral canal diameter and morphology on preoperative AP pelvis/hip and cross-table lateral radiographs; a Dorr classification assessment (A/B/C) helps anticipate cement mantle behavior — Dorr C (stovepipe, thin cortices, osteoporotic) canals are simultaneously the ones most prone to cement pressurization complications and the ones where cementing is often still preferred for immediate mechanical stability in fracture patients.
- **Implant/cement selection:** confirm stem size range against templated canal fill; select cement viscosity (low-viscosity cement reduces intramedullary pressure during pressurization and is generally preferred for femoral cementing) and confirm vacuum-mixing system availability — vacuum mixing meaningfully reduces cement porosity and has been associated with lower BCIS severity in geriatric hemiarthroplasty cohorts.
- **Equipment checklist:** pulsatile lavage system, appropriately sized distal cement restrictor/plug, cement gun with long cannula for retrograde delivery, cement pressurizer matched to the stem system, distal suction catheter (if using the vent-and-suction technique), and a stem trial set.
- **Anesthesia planning:** flag the case explicitly to anesthesia at the preoperative huddle — not just immediately before cementing — so arterial line placement (for high-risk patients), vasopressor infusions, and invasive monitoring can be set up proactively rather than reactively.
- **Risk stratification:** identify hemiarthroplasty-for-fracture patients, long-stem/revision cases, and patients with pathologic or heavily sclerotic bone as the highest-risk subgroup preoperatively so the whole team enters the case with heightened vigilance rather than being surprised at the moment of pressurization.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia type:** spinal/regional anesthesia is often preferred where suitable (reduces overall physiologic stress and may ease recognition of an awake patient's early symptoms of BCIS such as dyspnea or altered mentation), but general anesthesia is used routinely and is not itself a BCIS mitigation strategy — the mitigation is procedural, described below.
- **Monitoring:** standard ASA monitors at minimum; strongly consider an arterial line in ASA III–IV patients, long-stem/revision cases, or anyone with known cardiopulmonary compromise — continuous beat-to-beat blood pressure is far more useful than intermittent NIBP for catching the abrupt drop that characterizes BCIS.
- **Positioning:** standard positioning for the procedure being performed (lateral or supine for hip hemiarthroplasty/THA per surgeon approach preference) — cementation technique itself is not positioning-dependent, but ensure the leg can be adducted/externally rotated as needed for canal access without kinking lines or losing monitor contact.
- **Draping and field:** standard sterile drape for the approach; keep a clear line of communication (verbal, not just visual) with anesthesia across the drape — the anesthesia team cannot see the surgical field and depends entirely on the surgeon's verbal cues to time heightened vigilance to the actual moments of canal pressurization and stem impaction.
- **Landmarks:** no cementation-specific skin marking; standard approach-specific landmarks apply (greater trochanter, anterior superior iliac spine, etc., per chosen surgical approach).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Brief anesthesia explicitly, by name, immediately before starting canal preparation** — state that cementation is imminent, confirm vasopressors (phenylephrine or ephedrine) are drawn up, and confirm heightened hemodynamic monitoring is active. Do not assume the earlier preoperative flag substitutes for this real-time verbal cue.
2. **Broach or ream the femoral canal** to the templated size, removing loose cancellous debris as you go.
3. **Perform pulsatile lavage of the canal** before cement introduction — this is the single most effective mechanical step for reducing embolic load, clearing fat and marrow debris and lowering intramedullary pressure at the moment of pressurization. Follow with canal drying (epinephrine-soaked sponge packing is a traditional adjunct to reduce cancellous bleeding and improve cement interdigitation).
4. **Seat a distal cement restrictor/plug**, typically 1.5–2cm distal to the intended stem tip, to create a closed compartment for pressurization while still allowing a vent path for gas/marrow egress depending on restrictor type (vented vs. non-vented restrictors are both in use; know which your system uses).
5. **Mix cement under vacuum** if available — vacuum mixing lowers cement porosity, improves the cement-bone interface, and has been specifically associated with reduced BCIS severity in geriatric hemiarthroplasty series.
6. **Deliver cement in retrograde (distal-to-proximal) fashion using a cement gun with a long cannula**, withdrawing the cannula as the canal fills from the bottom up. Retrograde gun delivery gives more even pressure distribution than manual finger-packing and reduces the volume of trapped debris and air — do not finger-pack a deep canal as a substitute for the gun technique.
7. **If using a distal suction catheter (vent-and-suction technique):** place the catheter through the restrictor before cementing, deliver cement retrograde over it, and withdraw the catheter progressively as cement occludes it — this actively evacuates marrow/fat/air ahead of the cement column rather than relying on passive venting alone, and is a particularly valuable adjunct in your highest-risk cases.
8. **Pressurize the cement** using the appropriate pressurizer for the stem system, applying firm but controlled pressure for approximately 30–60 seconds to drive interdigitation into cancellous bone. **Avoid excessive or forceful pressurization**, particularly in osteoporotic (Dorr C) canals, where compliance is high and pressure is transmitted to the venous system more efficiently — this is the step where an over-aggressive surgeon most directly manufactures a BCIS event.
9. **Insert the stem slowly and deliberately, not in a rapid single thrust.** Rapid high-force impaction displaces a bolus of medullary content into the venous system essentially instantaneously; a slower, controlled insertion allows some egress along the stem-cement interface as it advances and gives anesthesia a more gradual hemodynamic signal to respond to rather than a step-change.
10. **Hold the stem in position while cement cures**, maintaining version/alignment; avoid repositioning maneuvers once cement has begun to set, since disrupting a partially cured mantle both weakens fixation and can provoke a second embolic load.
11. **Reduce the joint and complete trialing/final component assembly.** BCIS events can also occur at reduction (a second, smaller embolic load) and — rarely — at tourniquet deflation if one was used; keep vigilance through these steps, not just through pressurization.
12. **If Grade 2–3 BCIS develops at any point in the sequence** (per Donaldson grading — see Pitfalls table below): stop the cementing/impaction sequence immediately if it is not yet complete, deliver 100% oxygen, give an IV fluid bolus, administer vasopressor (phenylephrine or ephedrine) for hypotension, and escalate to full ACLS if cardiovascular collapse develops. Completing the stem insertion "because you're almost done" is the wrong instinct once a Grade 2 event is underway.
13. **In the very highest-risk patients** (severe cardiopulmonary disease, ASA IV, frail hip-fracture patients where a hemodynamic hit could be catastrophic), the alternative is to abandon cementing altogether in favor of an uncemented stem — this decision is best made preoperatively from imaging/risk stratification rather than improvised mid-case, though it remains available as a fallback if the canal proves unexpectedly favorable for press-fit fixation intraoperatively.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use **low-viscosity cement** for femoral applications — it lowers peak intramedullary pressure during pressurization compared with higher-viscosity formulations, directly reducing embolic load.
- **Vacuum-mix** whenever the system is available; beyond the mechanical cement-quality benefit, vacuum mixing has a specific evidence base for reducing BCIS severity in geriatric cemented hemiarthroplasty.
- Keep the **cement gun cannula long enough to reach the bottom of the prepared canal** and withdraw it steadily as cement fills from distal to proximal — a cannula that is too short or withdrawn too quickly recreates the finger-packing problem the gun is meant to avoid.
- If using a **vented cement restrictor with a distal suction catheter**, withdraw the catheter progressively rather than all at once as cement occludes it — this maximizes the window of active evacuation.
- **Time stem insertion to the cement's working phase**, not too early (cement too liquid, poor interdigitation, more embolization of unset material) or too late (cement too viscous, forces excessive pressure to seat the stem) — know your cement system's working-time window.
- **Verbally re-brief anesthesia at each embolization-risk moment** — canal preparation/lavage, cement pressurization, stem insertion, joint reduction, and tourniquet release if used — rather than a single briefing at the start; each is a discrete opportunity for a bolus event.
- In hemiarthroplasty for fracture, consider whether an **arterial line is warranted before draping** rather than placed reactively after a hypotensive event — these patients are frailer and less able to compensate.
- **Epinephrine-soaked sponge packing** of the canal before cementing reduces cancellous bleeding, giving a drier interface for cement interdigitation and a cleaner view of any ongoing bleeding that might otherwise be mistaken for the wrong problem intraoperatively.
- Consider **staged limb exsanguination/lavage volume awareness** in bilateral or complex revision cases where cumulative embolic load across two cementing events compounds risk.

## Pitfalls & Complications

**Donaldson grading of BCIS:**

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| **1** | Moderate hypoxia (SpO₂ <94%) or hypotension (drop >20% of baseline systolic) |
| **2** | Severe hypoxia (SpO₂ <88%) or hypotension (drop >40%), or unexpected loss of consciousness |
| **3** | Cardiovascular collapse requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation |

- Risk is substantially higher in **hip hemiarthroplasty for fracture** than elective THA (osteoporotic, unprepared canal, frailer/higher-ASA cohort) — anticipatory readiness should scale accordingly rather than being applied uniformly across all cemented cases.
- **Long-stem or revision femoral components** displace more medullary content at pressurization and carry correspondingly higher risk; canal lavage and vent-hole/retrograde filling technique are especially important in these cases.
- Do **not continue seating the prosthesis through an evolving Grade 2 event** — stop the sequence, do not "push through" hemodynamic compromise once it has started; completing the insertion does not make the embolic load go away and delays treatment.
- Drilling a formal cortical **vent hole** (as distinct from a vented restrictor) relieves intramedullary pressure and reduces air-embolus risk, but a vent hole larger than a critical diameter itself raises femoral fracture risk — size and position it deliberately rather than as an afterthought, and this is a genuine trade-off, not a free intervention.
- The decision to **convert to an uncemented stem** in high-risk patients is a real trade-off (accepting higher periprosthetic fracture risk to eliminate BCIS risk), not a universally superior choice — national joint registry data favor cemented fixation on aggregate revision/mortality outcomes for hip fracture hemiarthroplasty, so this substitution should be individualized rather than routine.
- **Structures/systems at risk beyond the cardiopulmonary axis:** watch for a secondary coagulopathic picture in severe Grade 2–3 events (fat embolism-associated consumptive changes are described, though uncommon) — a Grade 3 event that does not respond promptly to standard ACLS measures warrants consideration of this differential.
- **Postoperative recognition:** a milder embolic load that did not produce dramatic intraoperative hypotension can still manifest postoperatively as unexplained hypoxia, tachycardia, or altered mentation in recovery — maintain a lower threshold for pulse oximetry/ABG evaluation in patients who had a difficult cementing sequence even if they "recovered" intraoperatively.

## Postoperative Protocol

No distinct postoperative pathway specific to cementation technique; patients follow the same phased rehabilitation pathway as the underlying procedure (see the general perioperative rehabilitation protocol), with any intraoperative BCIS event documented and factored into postoperative monitoring intensity — for example, extended cardiopulmonary observation, continued pulse oximetry, and a lower threshold for ABG/chest imaging after a Grade 2–3 event, even once the patient appears hemodynamically stable in the immediate postoperative period.
