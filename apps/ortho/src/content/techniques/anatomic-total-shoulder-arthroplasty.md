## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.**
- Glenohumeral osteoarthritis with an intact, functioning rotator cuff (subscapularis repairable and functional) and correctable glenoid version/bone stock — Walch A1/A2, B1, and selected B2/B3/C with augmentation.
- Historically the "gold standard" for cuff-intact OA; still favored over rTSA in younger, higher-demand patients with correctable version (<15°, Walch A/B1) and an intact subscapularis, given more native biomechanics, lower dislocation risk, and easier future revision.
- Post-traumatic or secondary OA (e.g., Samilson-Prieto post-instability arthritis) with adequate bone stock and cuff integrity.

**Contraindications.**
- Absolute: active or unresolved infection, an irreparable or non-functioning subscapularis, a deficient/non-functioning rotator cuff (favors rTSA instead), and an unstable/insufficient deltoid.
- Relative: severe uncorrectable glenoid retroversion or bone loss (Walch B3/C beyond augmentation capability), age ≥65–70 with lower functional demand (rTSA more reliably durable in this group), prior instability surgery with retained hardware causing chondral damage without being addressed first, active smoking/uncontrolled diabetes (wound healing and infection risk), and neuromuscular conditions affecting shoulder girdle control (e.g., cuff tear arthropathy misdiagnosed as simple OA, or axillary neuropathy).
- A functionally deficient subscapularis discovered intraoperatively should prompt conversion to rTSA rather than proceeding with aTSA.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/templating**: true AP/Grashey, axillary lateral (mandatory for posterior subluxation/version assessment), and scapular Y radiographs as first-line. CT with 3D reconstruction and glenoid vault modeling (planning software such as Blueprint, ExactechGPS, VIP, or TrueSense) is essential for every candidate, particularly B2/B3/C glenoids, to measure version/inclination and plan the correction strategy and screw/peg trajectory.
- **Cuff assessment**: MRI when subscapularis integrity or cuff status is clinically uncertain, since a torn/non-functional subscapularis is a contraindication to aTSA.
- **Implant/graft selection**: decide preoperatively (with intraoperative flexibility) among standard glenoid component, posterior-augmented (step-cut or wedge) component, or structural bone graft, based on Walch type and the degree of correctable retroversion. Template humeral stem size/version and head size to restore native anatomy, not to overstuff the joint.
- **Instrumentation/equipment**: standard shoulder arthroplasty tray, glenoid reaming system (including eccentric/augmented reamers matched to the chosen implant system), trial components, patient-specific instrumentation or navigation if available/preferred for high-retroversion cases, and a sterile 3D-printed glenoid model in complex revision or severe deformity cases if the practice supports it.
- **Retained hardware**: review CT for prior instability-surgery hardware (anchors, screws) that may need removal and can independently explain chondral damage.
- **Medical optimization**: address diabetes (target HbA1c <7–8%), smoking cessation counseling, and a ≥3-month washout from the most recent intra-articular corticosteroid injection before elective surgery to reduce periprosthetic infection risk.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: combined general anesthesia with an interscalene regional block (single-shot or catheter) is standard for postoperative analgesia and intraoperative hypotensive-anesthesia benefit; discuss block timing with anesthesia if an intraoperative neuromonitoring or immediate postoperative neuro exam is planned.
- **Position**: modified beach-chair position, typically 30–45° of trunk elevation, using a dedicated shoulder positioner that allows the head to be secured, the operative shoulder to be brought to the lateral edge of the table, and the arm to be freely mobile through a full range of motion (including extension off the table edge for humeral exposure).
- **Table setup**: secure the head in a headrest with the face clear of the surgical field; a padded arm-positioning device or an assistant-held arm holder to control rotation/abduction during exposure and broaching; controlled hypotensive anesthesia (mean arterial pressure typically 60–65 mmHg) to improve visualization.
- **Draping**: wide sterile prep and drape exposing the clavicle, acromion, and the entire arm circumferentially to allow intraoperative manipulation through full ROM; an impervious stockinette/extremity drape secured well proximal to the field.
- **Landmarks**: mark the clavicle, coracoid process, acromion (including the anterolateral corner), and the deltopectoral interval before incision; the standard skin incision runs from just lateral to the coracoid toward the deltoid insertion, following the deltopectoral groove.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy is not routinely required for primary aTSA but should be available in revision or fracture-sequela cases; have C-arm access planned if there is any suspicion of retained hardware needing intraoperative localization.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and deltopectoral interval**: make a deltopectoral incision from the coracoid tip toward the deltoid insertion; identify and preserve the cephalic vein, retracting it either medially with the pectoralis major or laterally with the deltoid.
2. **Deep exposure**: develop the interval between deltoid and pectoralis major; identify the conjoint tendon and clavipectoral fascia, and incise the fascia lateral to the conjoint tendon while protecting the musculocutaneous nerve (variable entry point into coracobrachialis, sometimes close to the coracoid) and axillary nerve (palpate along the undersurface of the subscapularis/deltoid at the inferior border of the field throughout the case).
3. **Subscapularis management** — choose one based on preoperative plan and intraoperative tissue quality:
   - **Peel**: subperiosteal elevation of subscapularis off the lesser tuberosity, repaired end-to-end or transosseously; avoids bone-healing dependency but relies entirely on soft-tissue repair integrity.
   - **Tenotomy**: tag and divide the tendon 1–1.5 cm medial to its insertion, leaving a cuff for repair; simplest and most reproducible, similarly dependent on soft-tissue healing.
   - **Lesser tuberosity osteotomy (LTO)**: osteotomize a bone fragment with the subscapularis insertion attached, allowing bone-to-bone healing and radiographic confirmation of union, but carries a nonunion/fragmentation risk, especially in osteoporotic bone.
   - Tag the released subscapularis/biceps for later identification and protect the anterior humeral circumflex vessels at the inferior border of subscapularis ("3 o'clock" vessels) — ligate or cauterize deliberately rather than avulsing them.
4. **Anterior capsulectomy and humeral head exposure**: release the anteroinferior capsule off the humeral neck, then externally rotate and extend the arm to deliver the humeral head into the wound; release any remaining inferior capsular adhesions carefully, staying on bone to protect the axillary nerve.
5. **Humeral head resection**: use anatomic landmarks (the native head, the bicipital groove) or a resection guide to reproduce native height and version (typically 20–30° retroversion referenced off the forearm/transepicondylar axis); resect conservatively — restoring native anatomy rather than overstuffing, since overstuffing limits ROM and accelerates glenoid wear.
6. **Glenoid exposure**: place retractors (anterior, posterior, and inferior) to circumferentially expose the glenoid; complete a full circumferential capsulolabral release, taking care with the posterior and inferior retractors to avoid axillary nerve traction injury and posterior circumflex vessel injury.
7. **Glenoid assessment and version-correction strategy** — select based on Walch type and preoperative CT planning:
   - **Eccentric/selective reaming** for correctable retroversion (<15°) with adequate remaining bone — ream sparingly and centrally/posteriorly as needed to true up the surface without exposing weak subchondral or cancellous bone.
   - **Posterior-augmented glenoid components** (step-cut or wedge) for B2/B3 patterns, avoiding excessive reaming and preserving subchondral bone stock.
   - **Structural bone grafting** for severe deficiency (used less commonly in aTSA than the equivalent BIO-RSA technique in reverse arthroplasty).
   - Correct no more than necessary: avoid posterior reaming that thins the vault and risks perforation or component malposition, and avoid overcorrecting into anteversion, which increases anterior instability risk.
8. **Glenoid component preparation and trial**: drill/ream the central peg or keel and any peripheral fixation holes per the chosen implant system; trial the component to confirm coverage, seating, and version before final cementing.
9. **Glenoid fixation**: cement (or, for select cementless designs, press-fit) the definitive glenoid component, achieving reliable seating and, where applicable, central compression-screw fixation for primary stability; remove all extruded cement and confirm no peripheral lift-off.
10. **Humeral canal preparation and stem trial**: broach/ream the humeral canal to the templated size, respecting native version; place trial stem and head, then perform a trial reduction to assess ROM, stability (anterior/posterior drawer, "50% translation" rule), soft-tissue tension, and impingement-free arc of motion.
11. **Final humeral implantation**: cement or press-fit the definitive humeral stem (per implant design and bone quality) and impact the final humeral head onto the trunnion; avoid oversizing the head, which both limits motion and accelerates glenoid wear.
12. **Subscapularis repair**: repair meticulously using the technique matched to the takedown method — transosseous or suture-anchor repair for peel/tenotomy, or heavy non-absorbable suture/screw/tension-band fixation for LTO with confirmation of anatomic reduction; this repair is the single strongest predictor of aTSA outcome and instability risk.
13. **Closure**: reapproximate the deltopectoral interval, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers; consider a drain per surgeon preference, particularly if hemostasis is imperfect.
14. **Final check**: confirm passive ROM (elevation, external rotation) is adequate and stable through the trial arc, and document intraoperative ROM/stability findings to guide the rehabilitation prescription.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Reference humeral head resection off multiple landmarks (native articular margin, bicipital groove at ~30° posterior to the resection plane) rather than any single one, since osteophytes can distort the anatomic neck.
- Use a trial reduction deliberately: assess the "50% posterior translation" stability rule and a full passive ROM arc before committing to final component impaction — this is the last easy opportunity to adjust head size/version.
- When reaming a B2/B3 glenoid, ream the high (anterior) side down to the low (posterior) side rather than reaming the posterior deficiency further, to avoid excessive removal of vault bone.
- Keep retractors on bone and avoid aggressive circumferential capsular release beyond what is needed for exposure — most axillary nerve injuries in aTSA are traction-related from prolonged retractor pressure rather than direct laceration.
- Protect the "3 o'clock" anterior humeral circumflex vessels at the inferior subscapularis border with deliberate cautery/ligation rather than blunt avulsion, which causes troublesome bleeding.
- If choosing LTO, fashion a fragment thick enough (generally 5 mm) to allow secure fixation without fragmenting, and use a repeatable fixation construct (heavy suture plus screw, or transosseous sutures) — confirm union on follow-up radiographs before releasing subscapularis-protective restrictions.
- Do not chase a perfect version correction in older or lower-demand B2/B3 patients when augmentation or a switch to rTSA achieves a more durable, lower-risk result — the goal is a stable, well-fixed construct, not a textbook version number.
- Irrigate thoroughly and consider a dilute betadine or antibiotic lavage prior to closure, given the recognized *Cutibacterium acnes* infection risk in male shoulder arthroplasty patients.
- Document and photograph the intraoperative subscapularis and cuff findings — if the subscapularis is unexpectedly deficient/non-repairable, be prepared to convert to rTSA rather than force an aTSA repair that will fail.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Glenoid component loosening/failure is the leading cause of revision, higher in B2/B3 glenoids with inadequate version correction at index surgery.
- Subscapularis failure (rupture/attenuation causing anterosuperior instability) is reported in 4–10% of cases — the single most important technical failure mode to avoid through meticulous repair.
- Instability is most commonly anterior, related to subscapularis failure or an oversized/overstuffed humeral head.
- Deep infection (~1%) is more common with *Cutibacterium acnes* in male patients.
- Rotator cuff tear can progress over time in shoulders with pre-existing degeneration.
- Periprosthetic fracture is a recognized complication, particularly during forceful humeral canal broaching in osteoporotic bone.
- Axillary nerve traction injury and posterior circumflex vessel injury are risks of aggressive circumferential capsular release/retraction — palpate the nerve and retract on bone.
- LTO nonunion is a specific risk of that subscapularis-management choice, particularly in osteoporotic bone or with an inadequately sized fragment.
- For B2/B3 glenoids, neither aTSA-with-augmentation nor rTSA has clearly superior functional outcomes — frame counseling around bone-stock preservation, revision burden, and durability rather than promising superiority of either implant.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: sling immobilization full-time except for hygiene and elbow/wrist/hand exercises; passive/active-assisted ROM only (pendulums, supine passive forward elevation to tolerance, passive external rotation to a surgeon-specified limit — typically 0–30° if subscapularis was repaired/peeled). No active internal/external rotation, no lifting.
- **Weeks 2–6**: continued passive/active-assisted ROM progression; begin gentle active ROM below shoulder height; sling weaning as tolerated.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling; progress to full active ROM; begin light resistance/strengthening (isometrics progressing to isotonic); subscapularis-protective restrictions typically lifted around 6–8 weeks once healing is presumed (correlate with LTO union on radiographs if osteotomy was used, generally united by 6–8 weeks).
- **Months 3–6**: progressive rotator cuff-focused strengthening; functional and sport-specific training as appropriate.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work 2–4 weeks; light-manual work 6–8 weeks; heavy manual/overhead labor 4–6 months.
- **Return to sport**: low-demand recreational activity (golf, swimming) typically 4–6 months.
