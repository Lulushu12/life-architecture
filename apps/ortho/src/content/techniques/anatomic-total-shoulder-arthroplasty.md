## Indications

Glenohumeral osteoarthritis with an intact, functioning rotator cuff (subscapularis repairable and functional) and correctable glenoid version/bone stock (Walch A1/A2, B1, and selected B2/B3/C with augmentation). Historically the "gold standard" for cuff-intact OA. Favors younger, higher-demand patients with correctable version (<15°, Walch A/B1) and an intact subscapularis — offering more native biomechanics, lower dislocation risk, and easier future revision than rTSA. Reverse shoulder arthroplasty is favored instead at age ≥65–70, with B3/C glenoids, subscapularis insufficiency, or cuff-tear arthropathy.

## Positioning & setup

Deltopectoral approach. The monograph does not specify additional positioning or room-setup detail beyond the approach itself; CT with 3D reconstruction and glenoid vault modeling (e.g., Blueprint, ExactechGPS, VIP, TrueSense) is essential preoperatively for version/inclination measurement and implant sizing, particularly for B2/B3 glenoids.

## Steps

1. **Deltopectoral approach**, then manage subscapularis by peel, tenotomy, or lesser tuberosity osteotomy (LTO) — LTO permits radiographic union assessment but carries nonunion risk; peel/tenotomy avoids bone-healing dependency but relies entirely on soft-tissue repair integrity.
2. **Select a version-correction strategy based on Walch type**:
   - Eccentric/selective reaming for correctable retroversion (<15°) with adequate remaining bone.
   - Posterior-augmented glenoid components (step-cut or wedge) for B2/B3, avoiding excessive reaming and preserving subchondral bone.
   - Structural bone grafting for severe deficiency (used less commonly in aTSA than the equivalent BIO-RSA technique in rTSA).
3. **Correct no more than necessary** — avoid posterior reaming that thins the vault and risks perforation or component malposition; overcorrection into anteversion increases anterior instability risk.
4. **Resect the humeral head restoring native height/version** — overstuffing limits ROM and accelerates glenoid wear.
5. **Achieve central compression-screw fixation and reliable press-fit primary glenoid stability** at implantation regardless of augment type.
6. **Repair the subscapularis meticulously** — this is the single strongest predictor of aTSA outcome and instability risk.

## Pitfalls

- Glenoid component loosening/failure is the leading cause of revision, higher in B2/B3 glenoids with inadequate version correction at index surgery.
- Subscapularis failure (rupture/attenuation causing anterosuperior instability) is reported in 4–10% of cases.
- Instability is most commonly anterior, related to subscapularis failure or an oversized/overstuffed humeral head.
- Deep infection (~1%) is more common with *Cutibacterium acnes* in male patients.
- Rotator cuff tear can progress over time in shoulders with pre-existing degeneration.
- Periprosthetic fracture is a recognized complication.
- For B2/B3 glenoids, neither aTSA-with-augmentation nor rTSA has clearly superior functional outcomes — frame counseling around bone-stock preservation, revision burden, and durability rather than promising superiority of either implant.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: sling immobilization full-time except for hygiene and elbow/wrist/hand exercises; passive/active-assisted ROM only (pendulums, supine passive forward elevation to tolerance, passive external rotation to a surgeon-specified limit — typically 0–30° if subscapularis was repaired/peeled). No active internal/external rotation, no lifting.
- **Weeks 2–6**: continued passive/active-assisted ROM progression; begin gentle active ROM below shoulder height; sling weaning as tolerated.
- **Weeks 6–12**: discontinue sling; progress to full active ROM; begin light resistance/strengthening (isometrics progressing to isotonic); subscapularis-protective restrictions typically lifted around 6–8 weeks once healing is presumed (correlate with LTO union on radiographs if osteotomy was used, generally united by 6–8 weeks).
- **Months 3–6**: progressive rotator cuff-focused strengthening; functional and sport-specific training as appropriate.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work 2–4 weeks; light-manual work 6–8 weeks; heavy manual/overhead labor 4–6 months.
- **Return to sport**: low-demand recreational activity (golf, swimming) typically 4–6 months.
