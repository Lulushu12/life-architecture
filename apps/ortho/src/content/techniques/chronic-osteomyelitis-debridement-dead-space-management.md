## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Chronic osteomyelitis staged by the Cierny-Mader classification, anatomic Type III (localized, well-marginated sequestrum, segment remains mechanically stable after debridement) or Type IV (diffuse/circumferential involvement, bone unstable before or after debridement) requiring surgical source control.
- A confirmed sequestrum, sinus tract, or radiographic/MRI evidence of necrotic bone with surrounding involucrum — these findings define chronicity and are not expected to resolve with antibiotics alone.
- Failure of a prior course of suppressive or curative-intent antibiotic therapy without surgical debridement.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- A **C-host** (treatment morbidity would exceed that of the disease itself — severe medical comorbidity, very limited functional reserve, or anatomy where cure is biologically unrealistic) is a relative contraindication to an aggressive curative-intent operation; suppressive antibiotics or amputation may be the more appropriate strategy, and this should be discussed explicitly with the patient rather than defaulted into a limb-salvage pathway.
- Uncontrolled active systemic sepsis should be stabilized (fluid resuscitation, source-control-adjacent antibiotics) before an elective-timed staged reconstruction, though urgent debridement for sepsis source control is not itself contraindicated — the distinction is between emergent source control and elective staged reconstruction.
- Unoptimized modifiable host factors (markedly uncontrolled diabetes, active smoking, severe malnutrition) are not absolute contraindications but should be addressed pre-operatively wherever the clinical timeline allows, since B-host status is often at least partially reversible and materially affects cure probability.
- Inadequate soft-tissue coverage planning is a contraindication to proceeding with bony debridement alone — do not debride bone without a concrete plan (local flap, free flap, or delayed coverage strategy) for the resulting soft-tissue defect.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI is the primary modality for defining marrow edema, sequestrum extent, abscess collections, and soft-tissue involvement (interpret cautiously around existing metalwork, which causes artifact); CT better delineates the sequestrum itself, cortical destruction, and the bone-implant interface when metalwork is present, and is useful for 3D surgical planning of the resection margin. Plain radiographs remain useful for baseline deformity/alignment assessment.
- **Culture planning**: whenever possible, hold antibiotics for a defined pre-operative window (coordinate with infectious disease) so that intra-operative deep cultures are not suppressed by recently started empiric therapy, unless the patient is systemically septic and cannot safely wait.
- **Defect sizing**: estimate the anticipated bony defect after debridement from imaging — this determines whether dead-space management will require a local antibiotic carrier alone (small-to-moderate defect) versus staged bone transport, an induced-membrane (Masquelet) technique, or vascularized bone transfer (large segmental defect). Plan this before the debridement, since the debridement margin should be dictated by the disease, not artificially limited by an unplanned reconstruction strategy.
- **Implant/carrier selection**: decide in advance between non-absorbable PMMA beads/spacer (requires a planned second procedure for removal, allows very high local antibiotic loading, and can be fashioned as a rigid spacer for the Masquelet technique) and absorbable calcium sulfate (± hydroxyapatite composite, avoids a second surgery, resorbs and allows bone-void filling) — base the choice on dead-space geometry, whether staged reconstruction is planned anyway (favoring PMMA, since removal coincides with the next stage), and local antibiotic heat-stability requirements (not all antibiotics tolerate PMMA polymerization exotherm; vancomycin and aminoglycosides are heat-stable, many others are not).
- **Antibiotic loading**: confirm with pharmacy/microbiology which antibiotic(s) will be loaded into the carrier based on known or most-likely organism sensitivities, and confirm heat-stability if PMMA is chosen.
- **Fixation/stabilization equipment for Type IV disease**: have external fixation (circular/Ilizarov-type frame if staged bone transport across a segmental defect is anticipated, or a simpler uniplanar/multiplanar frame if only temporary stabilization is needed), Masquelet-technique instrumentation (cement spacer molds, later bone graft harvest set), or vascularized bone graft planning (microsurgical set, recipient vessel mapping via CT angiogram if a free fibula or iliac crest transfer is being considered) available and, where relevant, a plastic/microsurgical colleague scheduled concurrently.
- **Soft-tissue coverage plan**: involve plastic surgery pre-operatively for any anticipated free or complex local flap coverage — plan bony reconstruction and soft-tissue coverage as a single integrated operative plan, not sequential afterthoughts.
- **Host optimization**: check and document HbA1c (target <8% where achievable without delaying urgent surgery), nutritional markers (albumin/prealbumin), and smoking status; arrange smoking cessation counseling and nutritional supplementation pre-operatively when the clinical timeline permits.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is typical for most chronic osteomyelitis debridements given case length and the frequent need for tourniquet/positioning changes; regional/neuraxial technique may supplement for post-operative analgesia in lower-limb cases per anesthesiology input.
- **Position**: dictated by anatomic site — supine for most femoral/tibial anterior and medial approaches, lateral decubitus for posterior/lateral approaches, prone for posterior tibial or calcaneal (Gaenslen split-heel) access. Confirm positioning allows both adequate exposure of the debridement site and simultaneous access for any flap harvest (e.g., ipsilateral gastrocnemius or soleus flap donor site, or a separate limb for free fibula harvest) without repositioning mid-case.
- **Table setup**: radiolucent table for cases anticipating intra-operative fluoroscopy (implant removal, alignment assessment, frame application); a bump or bolster to elevate the operative limb for a circumferential prep.
- **Tourniquet**: a pneumatic tourniquet is useful for the initial exposure and bony debridement of an extremity lesion to improve visualization of margins, but should be **released before assessing bleeding at the debridement margin** — the "paprika sign" (punctate cortical/cancellous bleeding confirming viable bone) cannot be judged under tourniquet ischemia, so plan a tourniquet-down interval specifically for margin assessment.
- **Fluoroscopy**: position the C-arm for the anatomic region before draping and confirm an unobstructed arc for both the debridement site and, if applicable, any planned external fixator pin/wire placement.
- **Draping**: wide circumferential or near-circumferential prep of the entire limb segment (proximal to the joint above and distal to the joint below the disease when feasible) to allow extensile exposure if the debridement margin proves more extensive than anticipated on imaging, and to accommodate flap harvest incisions in the same field.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the known sinus tract(s), prior surgical incisions (to be excised en bloc if involved by chronic infection), and the planned bony resection margins based on pre-operative imaging before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the clinical stage** (anatomic type + host class, e.g., "Type IIIBs") to finalize the operative strategy before incision — this composite should appear in the operative note.
2. **Optimize modifiable host factors** pre-operatively where feasible (glycemic control, smoking cessation, nutritional optimization) — document this was addressed, since B-host status is often at least partially reversible.
3. **Excise the sinus tract and any chronically scarred/unhealthy skin** en bloc with the underlying approach whenever a draining sinus is present — do not leave chronically diseased skin in situ expecting it to heal once the bone is treated.
4. **Expose the bone** through the planned approach, extending proximally and distally beyond the radiographically apparent disease to allow assessment of a clear margin.
5. **Remove all hardware** present at the infected site unless a specific implant-retention strategy has been separately justified (this is a chronic osteomyelitis scenario, distinct from early FRI/DAIR indications) — retained hardware in established chronic osteomyelitis perpetuates biofilm and undermines eradication.
6. **Perform radical debridement to punctate bleeding ("paprika sign") bone** — release the tourniquet if one was used and confirm diffuse punctate bleeding across the entire cut surface before considering the margin adequate; this is the single most important determinant of cure, and inadequate debridement is the leading cause of recurrence.
7. **Excise all sequestrum, granulation tissue, and the fibrous/purulent lining of any abscess cavity** — send representative samples for histopathology as well as microbiology (histopathology helps distinguish active infection from sterile inflammatory change and can reveal an unsuspected diagnosis, e.g., malignancy, in an atypical presentation).
8. **Obtain ≥3–5 deep tissue cultures** with separate, dedicated instruments for each site before any local antibiotic is applied to the wound (local antibiotic carriers placed before cultures are taken will suppress subsequent culture yield).
9. **Assess mechanical stability of the remaining segment.** For a Type III lesion, if the segment remains mechanically stable after debridement, proceed directly to dead-space management and closure/coverage planning. For a Type IV lesion (bone unstable before or rendered unstable by debridement), proceed to concurrent stabilization:
   - **External fixation**: apply a uniplanar/multiplanar frame for straightforward stabilization, or a circular (Ilizarov-type) frame if staged bone transport across a segmental defect is planned from the outset.
   - **Induced-membrane (Masquelet) technique, stage 1**: after debridement, insert a PMMA cement spacer shaped to fill the entire bony defect and stabilize with a plate, nail, or external fixator; the induced pseudo-synovial membrane that forms around the cement over the following 6–8 weeks will later be preserved and used as a biologic chamber for bone grafting at stage 2.
   - **Vascularized bone transfer** (free fibula or iliac crest): plan microsurgical anastomosis to a mapped recipient vessel; typically reserved for large segmental defects, often performed as part of a staged sequence after infection is controlled rather than at the index debridement.
   - **Acute shortening with planned re-lengthening**: an option for shorter segmental defects, accepting temporary limb-length discrepancy in exchange for earlier bone-to-bone contact and healing, followed by staged lengthening (distraction osteogenesis) once infection is controlled.
10. **Manage dead space.** Fill the debrided cavity with a local antibiotic carrier — non-absorbable PMMA beads/spacer or absorbable calcium sulfate (± hydroxyapatite composite) — a well-vascularized muscle flap, or both. Match the carrier choice to the dead-space geometry: PMMA beads on a wire (for later sequential removal) suit an irregular cavity; a molded PMMA spacer suits a segmental Masquelet defect; calcium sulfate pellets are convenient for filling smaller, more contained voids without a planned second-look procedure.
11. **Plan and, where staged with plastics, perform soft-tissue coverage** concurrently — a local rotational muscle flap (e.g., gastrocnemius or soleus for proximal/mid tibial defects) or free flap for larger or more distal defects. Coverage should not be an afterthought once the bony work is complete; it is typically planned as part of the same operative sequence, sometimes literally the same anesthetic.
12. **Close in layers over closed suction drainage** where appropriate, or leave the wound open with negative-pressure wound therapy if further debridement/re-look is anticipated (common when margin adequacy is uncertain at the index procedure).
13. **Start or continue pathogen-directed systemic antibiotics** once adequate deep-tissue sampling is complete — do not delay the debridement itself to await culture results, but sequence the antibiotic start after sampling.
14. **Plan the re-look/second stage** explicitly before leaving the OR: a repeat debridement at 48–72h if margin adequacy was uncertain, or the definitive bone-grafting second stage of a Masquelet reconstruction at 6–8 weeks.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Release the tourniquet before final margin assessment — the "paprika sign" is a bleeding phenomenon and cannot be reliably judged under ischemia; many inadequate debridements are the result of assessing margins tourniquet-up.
- Take deep cultures **before** placing any local antibiotic carrier or irrigating with antiseptic solution — sequence matters, and getting this wrong is a common and avoidable cause of false-negative cultures.
- When uncertain whether a margin is adequate, err toward a wider resection and a planned staged reconstruction rather than a marginal resection performed to preserve bone stock — recurrence from inadequate debridement is far costlier than a slightly larger defect that is reconstructable.
- For a Masquelet spacer, mold the PMMA to match the exact contour of the resected segment, including slight distal/proximal overlap into the medullary canal at each end — this both maximizes the induced-membrane surface area for stage 2 and prevents soft-tissue collapse into the defect.
- Antibiotic-loaded PMMA beads on a wire loop are easy to remove at a planned second-look and allow reliable, direct visual confirmation of local antibiotic elution/bead surface condition — consider this over a solid spacer when a second-look debridement is already planned regardless of carrier type.
- When using calcium sulfate, warn the patient and the ward team that a serous wound discharge in the first 1–3 weeks is an expected finding as the carrier resorbs — this is frequently mistaken for a surgical site infection by staff unfamiliar with the material, leading to unnecessary reoperation or antibiotic escalation if not anticipated.
- Involve plastic surgery early enough that flap harvest can be planned into the same draping/positioning setup as the debridement — repositioning mid-case for coverage that could have been planned upfront adds avoidable operative time and infection risk.
- Photograph the debridement bed before dead-space management is placed — this creates a useful record for the second-look team and for planning the extent of any subsequent debridement.
- In circular-frame staged bone transport, plan the corticotomy site and transport vector before the index debridement wherever feasible, since the frame is often applied at the same sitting as debridement.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Inadequate debridement is the leading cause of recurrence — do not compromise on margins to preserve bone stock.
- Evidence that calcium sulfate carriers are superior to PMMA on hard infection-eradication endpoints remains limited to comparative cohort data rather than definitive RCTs — treat the choice as pragmatic, not evidence-mandated.
- Recurrence after "definitive" treatment is reported in roughly one-fifth to one-third of cases even with modern staged protocols, materially higher in B/C hosts and with inadequate initial debridement.
- Amputation may ultimately be required for uncontrolled Type IV disease in a severely compromised host — discuss it as a legitimate, sometimes superior, option early rather than only after repeated failed limb-salvage attempts.
- Retained hardware left in situ "to maintain stability" at the index chronic-osteomyelitis debridement is a common source of persistent biofilm and treatment failure — remove hardware unless a specific, justified retention strategy is documented.
- Placing a local antibiotic carrier before deep cultures are obtained sterilizes the culture and undermines subsequent pathogen-directed therapy — sequence sampling before carrier placement every time.
- Underestimating the defect size pre-operatively leads to an unplanned reconstruction decision made under time pressure in the OR — size the anticipated defect on imaging before the case, and have a Type IV strategy ready even for a lesion that looks like it might be Type III.
- Failure to plan soft-tissue coverage concurrently with bony debridement risks a chronically exposed bone/hardware surface that reinfects before definitive coverage is arranged.
- PMMA polymerization exotherm can denature heat-labile antibiotics (e.g., some beta-lactams) — confirm with pharmacy which agents are heat-stable (vancomycin, aminoglycosides typically are) before loading beads.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Weeks 0–2:** Protected/non-weight-bearing per fixation stability and soft-tissue status; wound and flap surveillance; early passive/active-assisted ROM of adjacent joints unless a re-look procedure is imminent.

**Weeks 2–6:** Progressive weight-bearing as fixation and infection control allow; continue systemic antibiotics per culture-directed duration (guided by clinical/biochemical response rather than a fixed number of days); physiotherapy to restore joint ROM and prevent contracture.

**Beyond 6 weeks:** Strengthening progresses once bony union/soft-tissue healing is secure. Return to work is individualized to job physical demand and wound/bone status, often 3–6 months for physically demanding roles after major reconstruction. Return to sport only after radiographic union and infection-marker normalization sustained off antibiotics.
