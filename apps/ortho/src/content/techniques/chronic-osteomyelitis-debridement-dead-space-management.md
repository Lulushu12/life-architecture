## Indications

Chronic osteomyelitis staged by the Cierny-Mader classification. Type III (localized, well-marginated sequestrum, segment remains mechanically stable after debridement) and Type IV (diffuse/circumferential involvement, bone unstable before or after debridement) lesions requiring surgical source control. Modifiable host factors (Cierny-Mader B-host) should be optimized where possible; a C-host (treatment morbidity exceeding disease morbidity) may be better served by suppressive antibiotics or amputation than by an attempted cure.

## Positioning & setup

Determined by the anatomic site of infection. Soft-tissue coverage (local or free flap) must be planned concurrently with bony reconstruction from the outset, not addressed as an afterthought once debridement is complete.

## Steps

1. Confirm the clinical stage (anatomic type + host class) to select the overall strategy.
2. Optimize modifiable host factors pre-operatively where feasible — glycemic control (HbA1c target <8%), smoking cessation, nutritional optimization (trending albumin/prealbumin) — since B-host status is often at least partially reversible.
3. Perform radical debridement to punctate ("paprika sign") bleeding bone — this is the single most important determinant of cure.
4. For Type III lesions, debridement can be carried out without destabilizing the segment.
5. For Type IV lesions, concurrent skeletal stabilization/reconstruction is required: external fixation (circular frame for staged bone transport across segmental defects), the induced-membrane (Masquelet) two-stage technique, vascularized fibular or iliac crest transfer for large defects, or acute shortening with subsequent lengthening.
6. Manage dead space with a local antibiotic carrier — non-absorbable PMMA beads/spacer (requires a second procedure for removal) or absorbable calcium sulfate (± hydroxyapatite composite, avoids a second surgery and allows bone-void filling) — a muscle flap, or both; selection is often pragmatic based on dead-space geometry, need for staged reconstruction, and cost.
7. Send deep cultures and start pathogen-directed systemic antibiotics only after adequate sampling; individualize duration to clinical/biochemical response and surgical source control rather than a fixed universal number, with early oral step-down once the patient is stabilized and the organism/susceptibility is known.

## Pitfalls

- Inadequate debridement is the leading cause of recurrence — do not compromise on margins to preserve bone stock.
- Evidence that calcium sulfate carriers are superior to PMMA on hard infection-eradication endpoints remains limited to comparative cohort data rather than definitive RCTs — treat the choice as pragmatic, not evidence-mandated.
- Recurrence after "definitive" treatment is reported in roughly one-fifth to one-third of cases even with modern staged protocols, materially higher in B/C hosts and with inadequate initial debridement.
- Amputation may ultimately be required for uncontrolled Type IV disease in a severely compromised host — discuss it as a legitimate, sometimes superior, option early rather than only after repeated failed limb-salvage attempts.

## Post-op protocol

**Weeks 0–2:** Protected/non-weight-bearing per fixation stability and soft-tissue status; wound and flap surveillance; early passive/active-assisted ROM of adjacent joints unless a re-look procedure is imminent.

**Weeks 2–6:** Progressive weight-bearing as fixation and infection control allow; continue systemic antibiotics per culture-directed duration (guided by clinical/biochemical response rather than a fixed number of days); physiotherapy to restore joint ROM and prevent contracture.

**Beyond 6 weeks:** Strengthening progresses once bony union/soft-tissue healing is secure. Return to work is individualized to job physical demand and wound/bone status, often 3–6 months for physically demanding roles after major reconstruction. Return to sport only after radiographic union and infection-marker normalization sustained off antibiotics.
