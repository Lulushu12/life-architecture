## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Confirmed HAGL lesion — avulsion of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (anterior band) from its humeral, rather than glenoid, insertion.
- Glenoid-side pathology may coexist ("floating IGHL" — avulsion at both the glenoid and humeral ends of the ligament), and this should be assessed and addressed concurrently if present.
- HAGL occurs in an estimated 4–9% of instability cases, more common after low-energy/subluxation mechanisms in older or hyperlaxity-adjacent patients, and is easily missed because the glenoid-side labrum can appear normal on both exam and MRI — a normal labrum in a patient with a convincing instability history should raise, not lower, suspicion.
- A trial of physical therapy is rarely successful once a HAGL is diagnosed; surgical repair is indicated once the lesion is confirmed rather than delaying for a rehab trial.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Chronic, retracted, or scarred-down HAGL where the ligament tissue quality is too poor for anchor fixation — may require augmentation (capsular reconstruction/graft) rather than primary repair; assess tissue quality intraoperatively before committing to a simple repair.
- Multidirectional/global capsular laxity as the dominant pathology (rather than a discrete HAGL) — these patients are better served by an inferior capsular shift/plication procedure addressing the whole capsule rather than isolated humeral-sided repair.
- Active infection or compromised anterior soft-tissue envelope (rare).
- Posterior HAGL (PHAGL) or combined anterior-posterior HAGL lesions require modification of the standard anterior approach/repair strategy and should be planned for specifically once suspected on imaging.

## Preoperative Planning

- **MR arthrogram** is the key study: look for the **J-sign** or a drooping/absent inferior glenohumeral ligament on axial images, and an irregular, redundant, or discontinuous capsular contour at the humeral neck rather than the normal smooth capsular insertion.
- Correlate imaging with exam: instability with a relatively unremarkable glenoid-side exam/imaging picture should specifically prompt a dedicated look for HAGL, since the diagnosis is frequently made or confirmed only at arthroscopy.
- Determine anterior vs posterior (or combined) HAGL location on imaging, since this dictates surgical approach and repair strategy.
- **Implant planning**: suture anchors (typically 2–4, 3.0–4.5 mm biocomposite or all-suture) sized for humeral metaphyseal/metadiaphyseal bone at the anatomic humeral neck footprint.
- Decide preoperatively between an **open** approach (deltopectoral with subscapularis split or peel — still the more common approach given exposure demands) and an **arthroscopic** approach (increasingly described by experienced arthroscopists, often via an accessory anterior/axillary or posterior portal for direct access to the axillary pouch); base the choice on surgeon experience with arthroscopic HAGL repair and lesion characteristics (isolated vs floating, anterior vs posterior).
- Consent should cover the possibility of concurrent glenoid-side (labral) repair if a floating IGHL is confirmed intraoperatively.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically with an interscalene block for postoperative analgesia; discuss block timing with anesthesia if an early postoperative axillary nerve check is planned given the nerve's proximity to the surgical field.
- **Positioning**: **beach chair** is standard for the open deltopectoral approach (allows arm positioning through a full range of rotation/abduction to aid exposure); lateral decubitus with traction may be used if an arthroscopic-first approach is planned.
- Position the arm free-draped so it can be moved through abduction/external rotation (to tension the coracoacromial/deltoid interval during exposure) and adduction/internal rotation (to help expose the inferior subscapularis and axillary pouch) during the case.
- Pad all bony prominences; standard shoulder prep and drape widely enough to allow conversion between arthroscopic and open technique if the plan changes.
- Mark the deltopectoral interval, coracoid, and axillary fold before incision if an open approach is planned.
- Fluoroscopy is not routinely required.
- Perform an exam under anesthesia; a relatively benign glenoid-side laxity picture with a positive apprehension/inferior sulcus supports the working diagnosis and should keep HAGL on the differential going into arthroscopy.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy first, regardless of planned definitive approach.** Establish standard posterior and anterior portals; perform a circumferential survey. Confirm or refute glenoid-side labral pathology, and specifically inspect the **axillary pouch** — abduct and externally rotate the arm and look for a redundant, patulous, or frankly avulsed capsule off the humeral neck, or a J-shaped deformity of the IGHL as it falls away from its normal humeral attachment.
2. **Confirm the diagnosis directly** rather than relying solely on preoperative imaging — the axillary pouch inspection is definitive.
3. **Decide on approach** based on confirmed lesion location/extent and surgeon experience: proceed open (most cases) or continue arthroscopically (selected cases in experienced hands).
4. **Open approach — exposure.** Deltopectoral incision from the coracoid toward the axillary fold. Develop the deltopectoral interval, retract the cephalic vein medially (with the deltoid) or laterally per preference, and expose the conjoint tendon and subscapularis.
5. **Open approach — subscapularis management.** Use a subscapularis split (preferred for isolated HAGL, preserving the tendon's insertion) rather than a full tenotomy/peel when adequate exposure can be achieved: create an L-shaped split in the lower/inferior portion of the subscapularis, beginning the vertical limb roughly 1.5 cm medial to the lesser tuberosity insertion and extending distally to just proximal to the anterior circumflex humeral vessels, then extend medially 1.5–2 cm to create the horizontal limb of the L.
6. **Protect the axillary nerve.** Before making the horizontal limb of the split, palpate with a finger along the inferior border of the subscapularis to confirm the axillary nerve lies medial and inferior to the field; external rotation of the arm helps move the nerve further away. Maintain awareness of the nerve throughout inferior dissection — this is the single most important safety step of the entire exposure.
7. **Expose the HAGL lesion.** Bluntly spread the inferior subscapularis fibers proximal to the circumflex vessels (Cobb elevator or Metzenbaum scissors) and retract the inferior corner of the subscapularis superiorly to expose the avulsed IGHL and the humeral neck footprint.
8. **Prepare the humeral footprint.** Lightly decorticate the anatomic humeral neck attachment site of the IGHL to a bleeding bony bed.
9. **Place anchors and repair.** Place suture anchors along the anatomic footprint and pass sutures through the avulsed ligament/capsular tissue, then tie sequentially to reattach the IGHL to its native humeral insertion, restoring normal capsular tension without overtightening (test passive external rotation and abduction after tying).
10. **Address glenoid-side pathology if present** (floating IGHL) — repair the glenoid-side labral component either through the same open exposure (less common) or arthroscopically in the same setting.
11. **Arthroscopic approach (selected cases).** If pursued, access the axillary pouch through a low anterior or accessory posterior/posteroinferior portal, decorticate the humeral footprint with a shaver/burr, and place anchors percutaneously or through a cannula directed at the humeral neck, taking particular care with instrument trajectory given the proximity of the axillary nerve inferiorly.
12. **Closure.** Repair the subscapularis split anatomically (side-to-side sutures) if used, close the deltopectoral interval in layers, and close skin. Place the arm in a sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Actively look for HAGL at every instability arthroscopy where the glenoid-side labrum looks disproportionately normal for the reported instability history — this is the classic scenario in which the diagnosis is otherwise missed.
- Favor a subscapularis split over a full tenotomy/peel for isolated HAGL — it preserves the tendon insertion, simplifies closure, and is adequate exposure for most isolated lesions.
- Always palpate for the axillary nerve before creating the horizontal limb of the subscapularis split, and use arm external rotation to help move the nerve out of the field before deepening dissection inferiorly.
- Move the arm through abduction/external rotation and adduction/internal rotation intraoperatively to alternately open the superior working window and the inferior axillary pouch — static retraction alone often under-exposes the lesion.
- Decorticate the humeral footprint before anchor placement — soft-tissue-to-cortical-bone healing is less reliable than soft-tissue-to-cancellous/bleeding-bone healing.
- Test passive ROM (external rotation, abduction) after tying the repair, before closing the subscapularis split, to confirm the ligament has been restored to appropriate tension rather than overtightened.
- If a floating IGHL is found, plan to address both ends in the same anesthetic setting where feasible — leaving either the glenoid- or humeral-side avulsion unrepaired predicts persistent instability.
- Keep an arthroscopic HAGL repair setup available as a fallback plan documentation point even when planning open surgery, and vice versa — intraoperative findings (lesion location, tissue quality) sometimes change the optimal approach.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- HAGL is the classic "missed lesion" in instability surgery — a normal-appearing labrum on arthroscopy should not end the search for a structural cause; check the axillary pouch.
- A trial of physical therapy is rarely successful once a HAGL is diagnosed; do not delay surgical repair on the assumption that rehab alone will resolve it.
- Axillary nerve proximity is a specific risk with the open approach, particularly during the horizontal limb of the subscapularis split and during exposure/retraction of the inferior capsule — palpate and protect it explicitly before deepening dissection.
- Overtightening the humeral-sided repair can restrict external rotation; test ROM intraoperatively before final closure.
- Failure to recognize a floating IGHL (missing the glenoid-side component) leaves the joint incompletely stabilized despite a technically sound humeral repair.
- Attempting primary repair on chronic, poor-quality ligament tissue without recognizing the need for augmentation risks early failure — assess tissue quality directly and consider augmentation/reconstruction if primary repair is not durable.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not detail a dedicated rehabilitation protocol for HAGL repair specifically. Reported recurrent instability after HAGL repair is low provided the isolated lesion is correctly identified and repaired, with low reoperation rates. General instability return-to-sport criteria apply: full painless ROM within 5–10° of the contralateral side, ≥90% strength symmetry, negative apprehension testing, and successful functional progression before return to sport. Given the subscapularis split/repair component of the open approach, a subscapularis-protective early phase (limiting early resisted internal rotation and forced external rotation stretching), analogous to that used after Latarjet, is a reasonable extrapolation until repair maturity is established.
