## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Displaced navicular body fractures (>1–2 mm articular step-off or any translation) — recent series report much higher fracture-healing/lower refracture rates with operative treatment (~98% success, ~1% refracture) versus non-operative management of the same displaced injuries (~78% success, ~24% refracture).
- Cuboid ("nutcracker") fractures with lateral column shortening, since uncorrected shortening drives forefoot abduction and midfoot collapse.
- Isolated navicular or cuboid stress fractures that fail an adequate trial of conservative management (typically 6–8 weeks of NWB immobilization without healing), particularly in athletes wanting a faster, more reliable return to sport.
- Any navicular/cuboid fracture associated with a Lisfranc or calcaneocuboid injury requiring concurrent midfoot stabilization.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Nondisplaced avulsion, tuberosity, and most nondisplaced stress fractures without sclerosis or nonunion signs — treat with NWB cast immobilization rather than surgery.
- Active soft-tissue infection or compromised dorsal/medial midfoot soft-tissue envelope — delay until resolved.
- Established asymptomatic nonunion in a low-demand patient — not every radiographic nonunion requires surgery if the patient is functional and pain-free.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease compromising the already-tenuous navicular blood supply — weigh surgical risk carefully and consider prolonged non-operative management or vascular optimization first.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: standard weight-bearing foot series to assess displacement and arch height; CT (thin-cut, with sagittal/coronal reconstructions) defines body fracture pattern, comminution, and articular involvement for surgical planning. MRI or bone scan is reserved for suspected stress fracture with normal or equivocal radiographs — classic in track/jumping athletes with vague dorsal midfoot pain and focal "N spot" tenderness over the navicular.
- **Classification/pattern assessment**: for the navicular, characterize the fracture as a cortical avulsion, tuberosity fracture, or body fracture (further typed by the direction of the primary fracture line — sagittal, coronal, or comminuted); for the cuboid, assess the degree of comminution and lateral column shortening on CT, since this determines whether structural grafting will be needed.
- **Implant selection**: small-fragment (2.4–3.5 mm) screws ± a mini-fragment plate for navicular body fixation; consider a small buttress plate for comminuted/impacted fragments. Have structural allograft or autograft (tricortical iliac crest or femoral head allograft) available for comminuted navicular fractures with bone loss, and for cuboid fractures requiring lateral column length restoration.
- **Equipment**: mini pointed reduction clamps, a small distractor or external fixator frame (spanning talus-to-metatarsal or calcaneus-to-metatarsal) useful for restoring length before fixing a comminuted cuboid, and a headlamp for the deep, narrow midfoot exposure.
- **Case-specific planning**: because navicular and cuboid fractures frequently co-occur with Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid injuries, plan to image and manually stress the entire midfoot intraoperatively regardless of the primary diagnosis, and have Lisfranc fixation implants available on standby.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal anesthesia with a popliteal sciatic ± saphenous block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent table with a small bump under the ipsilateral hip for a dorsomedial approach to the navicular, or neutral positioning for a dorsolateral approach to the cuboid; a bump under the heel improves fluoroscopic access.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet inflated for the duration of the case to maintain a clear field in this compact anatomic region.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm positioned for AP, oblique, and lateral views of the midfoot; confirm the ability to obtain a true lateral to judge arch height (calcaneal-to-5th-metatarsal height) both before and after fixation.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire foot free to allow manual stress testing of adjacent joints (Lisfranc, calcaneocuboid, talonavicular) intraoperatively.
- **Landmarks**: mark the tibialis anterior and tibialis posterior tendon courses (dorsomedial approach to the navicular passes between them or just medial to tibialis anterior), the course of the superficial peroneal nerve branches over the dorsolateral foot, and the peroneal tendons along the lateral cuboid border before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Position, prep, and inflate the tourniquet** as above.
2. **For displaced navicular body fractures**, approach via a dorsomedial incision (between or just medial to the tibialis anterior tendon) for medial and central fragments, or a dorsolateral incision (protecting superficial peroneal nerve branches) for lateral fragments — some comminuted patterns require both incisions.
3. **Clear hematoma and débride the fracture site**, identifying the primary fracture plane(s) and any central comminution/impaction.
4. **Reduce the navicular body**, restoring the medial column length and the talonavicular and naviculocuneiform joint relationships — use a small distractor or manual traction through the midfoot if the fragment is impacted/shortened, and pointed reduction clamps to compress the major fragments once length is restored.
5. **Bone-graft impacted/comminuted defects**: after elevating a depressed central fragment, pack the resulting void with structural or cancellous graft to support the articular surface and prevent secondary collapse.
6. **Fix with screws ± a plate**: place interfragmentary screws across the major fracture planes, and add a small dorsal or medial plate for comminuted patterns or where screw purchase alone is insufficient to resist collapse — the goal is a construct rigid enough to allow early protected motion while healing occurs across the hypovascular central-third watershed zone.
7. **For cuboid ("nutcracker") fractures with lateral column shortening**, expose via a dorsolateral incision, protecting the peroneal tendons along the plantar-lateral border. Use a small distractor (calcaneus to 4th/5th metatarsal, or a lamina spreader within the fracture site) to restore lateral column length, reducing the comminuted fragments around the restored length.
8. **Bone-graft the resulting metaphyseal defect** (structural graft is often required, since simple compression of a comminuted cuboid without grafting reproduces the shortening) and fix with a small plate spanning the fracture, contoured to the lateral column.
9. **Assess and treat associated injuries**: manually stress and fluoroscopically image the Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid joints before closing, since cuboid and navicular fractures are associated with Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid injuries frequently enough that the entire midfoot must be evaluated intraoperatively regardless of the preoperative imaging impression.
10. **For isolated stress fractures failing conservative management**, use a percutaneous or mini-open approach with cannulated screws directed along the fracture line (commonly dorsal-to-plantar or medial-to-lateral for navicular stress fractures, following the classic sagittal fracture orientation), avoiding the need for wide exposure or grafting in most cases.
11. **Confirm fluoroscopically**: restored arch height (calcaneal-to-5th-metatarsal height on lateral view), medial and lateral column length, and no screw penetration into adjacent joints.
12. **Release the tourniquet, achieve hemostasis, and close in layers**, protecting the tendons and nerves identified during exposure; apply a well-padded splint.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a small distractor or manual longitudinal traction to restore length *before* attempting to reduce a comminuted or impacted navicular/cuboid fracture — compressing comminuted fragments without first restoring length recreates the shortening/collapse you are trying to fix.
- Do not skip structural grafting for a comminuted, impacted cuboid or navicular defect — screws and plates alone will not reliably hold length against physiologic load if there is a residual metaphyseal void.
- Identify and protect the tibialis anterior and posterior tendons early in the dorsomedial navicular approach — working between or just medial to tibialis anterior avoids injury while giving direct access to the body.
- For isolated navicular stress fractures, orient screws along the classic sagittal fracture plane (dorsal-to-plantar) rather than assuming a coronal trajectory — this follows the fracture's natural orientation and improves compression.
- Manually stress the Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid joints intraoperatively in every navicular/cuboid case, not just when preoperative imaging raises suspicion — these injuries are commonly missed on static imaging alone.
- In athletes with vague dorsal midfoot pain and normal initial radiographs, have a low threshold for CT or MRI before committing to a "soft tissue strain" diagnosis — delayed navicular stress fracture diagnosis is a well-known driver of nonunion.
- Favor NWB cast immobilization over a removable boot for navicular fractures specifically, given the notoriously slow and unreliable union across the hypovascular central-third watershed zone — patient compliance with a removable boot is a real risk factor for nonunion in this fracture.
- When both medial and lateral navicular fragments require visualization, do not hesitate to use combined dorsomedial and dorsolateral incisions rather than forcing exposure through a single window and risking incomplete reduction.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The navicular's central-third watershed zone is relatively hypovascular, predisposing to stress fracture and nonunion — navicular fractures are notoriously slow/prone to nonunion and generally err toward NWB cast immobilization over a removable boot.
- Subtle navicular and cuboid fractures are frequently missed on initial radiographs in athletes with vague dorsal midfoot pain (the "N spot" tenderness) — maintain a low threshold for CT or MRI with persistent dorsal midfoot pain and a normal radiograph to avoid delayed diagnosis and nonunion.
- Uncorrected cuboid lateral column shortening leads to forefoot abduction and midfoot collapse — restore length with structural grafting rather than accepting shortening or simply compressing comminuted fragments.
- Nonunion is a recognized risk specifically in comminuted navicular fractures given the central watershed hypovascularity; if it develops, revision with structural grafting and rigid fixation is generally required rather than continued observation.
- Injury to the tibialis anterior/posterior tendons (dorsomedial approach) or superficial peroneal nerve branches/peroneal tendons (dorsolateral/cuboid approach) is avoidable with careful identification before deep dissection.
- Failing to stress-test the Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid joints intraoperatively risks leaving an associated injury untreated, a common source of persistent postoperative pain that is otherwise attributed to the index fracture.

## Postoperative Protocol

NWB cast 6–8 weeks for displaced/operatively fixed fractures (navicular in particular, given watershed vascularity and historically slow/unreliable union), then progressive weight-bearing in a boot over 2–4 weeks, with full activity by 4–6 months. Stress fractures treated operatively may return to sport somewhat faster (10–12 weeks) than those treated with prolonged non-operative immobilization. Sedentary work 4–8 weeks, manual labor 3–5 months.
