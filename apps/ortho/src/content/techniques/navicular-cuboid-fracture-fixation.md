## Indications

Displaced navicular body fractures are treated operatively — recent series report much higher fracture-healing/lower refracture rates with operative treatment of displaced fractures (~98% success, ~1% refracture) versus non-operative management of the same displaced injuries (~78% success, ~24% refracture); displacement is the key trigger for surgery. Cuboid ("nutcracker") fractures with lateral column shortening also warrant fixation/grafting, since shortening leads to forefoot abduction and midfoot collapse. Isolated navicular/cuboid stress fractures failing conservative management are treated with screw fixation, particularly in athletes for faster/more reliable return to sport.

## Positioning & setup

Minimal specific setup beyond standard foot positioning; CT defines body fractures/comminution for surgical planning, and the entire midfoot should be imaged and stressed intraoperatively given frequent association with Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid injuries.

## Steps

1. **For displaced navicular body fractures**, approach via dorsomedial/dorsolateral incision and fix with screws ± plating to restore the medial column.
2. **For comminuted/impacted navicular fractures**, add structural bone graft to restore length.
3. **For cuboid ("nutcracker") fractures with lateral column shortening**, perform ORIF/structural bone grafting to restore lateral column length.
4. **Assess and treat associated injuries** — cuboid and navicular fractures are associated with Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid injuries frequently enough that the entire midfoot must be imaged and stressed intraoperatively.
5. **For isolated stress fractures failing conservative management**, use percutaneous or open screw fixation, particularly in athletes, for faster and more reliable return to sport.

## Pitfalls

- The navicular's central-third watershed zone is relatively hypovascular, predisposing to stress fracture and nonunion — navicular fractures are notoriously slow/prone to nonunion and generally err toward NWB cast immobilization over a removable boot.
- Subtle navicular and cuboid fractures are frequently missed on initial radiographs in athletes with vague dorsal midfoot pain (the "N spot" tenderness) — maintain a low threshold for CT or MRI with persistent dorsal midfoot pain and a normal radiograph to avoid delayed diagnosis and nonunion.
- Uncorrected cuboid lateral column shortening leads to forefoot abduction and midfoot collapse — restore length with structural grafting rather than accepting shortening.
- Nonunion is a recognized risk specific to comminuted navicular fractures given the central watershed hypovascularity.

## Post-op protocol

NWB cast 6–8 weeks for displaced/operatively fixed fractures (navicular in particular, given watershed vascularity and historically slow/unreliable union), then progressive weight-bearing in a boot over 2–4 weeks, with full activity by 4–6 months. Stress fractures treated operatively may return to sport somewhat faster (10–12 weeks) than those treated with prolonged non-operative immobilization. Sedentary work 4–8 weeks, manual labor 3–5 months.
