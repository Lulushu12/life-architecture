## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- High-grade trochlear dysplasia (Dejour B–D, particularly C/D) with a supratrochlear spur or a convex/absent trochlear groove, in a patient with recurrent patellar instability where MPFL reconstruction alone carries persistently elevated redislocation risk.
- Revision instability surgery where an index MPFL-R (± TTO) failed and high-grade dysplasia was the unaddressed driver.
- Adolescents approaching skeletal maturity with high-grade dysplasia and recurrent instability — per ESSKA consensus, trochleoplasty can be performed safely close to skeletal maturity rather than deferred indefinitely.
- Painful patellar maltracking/instability from a dysplastic trochlea even without a formal dislocation event, when conservative management and lesser procedures have failed to control symptoms.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Significant open growth remaining — trochleoplasty is a bony procedure on the distal femoral epiphysis/physeal region and is generally deferred until the patient is close to skeletal maturity; in the truly immature patient, favor soft-tissue and guided-growth strategies first.
- Low-grade dysplasia (Dejour A) — MPFL-R alone is typically sufficient, and adding trochleoplasty exposes the patient to unnecessary chondral and stiffness risk for a shallow-but-symmetric trochlea.
- Advanced patellofemoral arthritis — deepening a groove with degenerated cartilage does not reliably improve outcomes and may accelerate symptomatic breakdown; consider arthroplasty-directed management instead in this population.
- Surgeon/center inexperience — this is a technically demanding procedure with a real chondral-injury learning curve; it is best performed, or at minimum proctored, in a high-volume center.
- Active infection or compromised soft-tissue envelope over the planned approach.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: axial CT or MRI to formally grade dysplasia (Dejour A–D) using the crossing sign, supratrochlear spur height, and double-contour sign; true lateral radiograph for the crossing sign and for Caton-Deschamps index (uncorrected concurrent patella alta undermines the benefit of trochleoplasty — plan concomitant distalizing TTO if alta is present); MRI to assess cartilage thickness/quality across the trochlea and patella, since chondral status affects both surgical feasibility and prognosis.
- **Menu planning**: formally document the full risk-factor profile (Dejour grade, Caton-Deschamps index, TT-TG, rotational profile) before the day of surgery to decide which procedures will be combined — trochleoplasty is rarely performed in isolation; plan explicitly whether MPFL-R, TTO, or derotational osteotomy will be added in the same setting.
- **Technique selection**: deepening trochleoplasty (Bereiter "thin-flap" or Dejour/Lyon "thick-shell" technique) is the most widely used approach; understand the difference before choosing — the Bereiter technique elevates a thin (2–3 mm), elastic, moldable osteochondral flap, while the Lyon/thick-shell technique elevates a thicker (~5 mm) shell. The thinner Bereiter-style flap is generally easier to remodel into a new groove contour but places a premium on careful, uniform elevation to avoid fracture or perforation.
- **Equipment**: fine osteotomes of graduated width, a high-speed burr for subchondral bone removal and groove shaping, suture anchors or heavy sutures for flap fixation, fluoroscopy for confirming trochlear geometry/patellar tracking, and standard retractors for a generous anterior knee exposure.
- **Consent and expectation-setting**: counsel explicitly on the arthrofibrosis rate (a meaningfully reported complication, especially in pediatric series, reported up to roughly 13%) and the technical demand of the procedure, and on the likelihood of a combined procedure (MPFL-R ± TTO) rather than trochleoplasty alone.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia, typically combined with regional/peripheral nerve block (adductor canal or femoral) for postoperative analgesia given the extensive dissection and expected postoperative pain.
- Supine on a standard radiolucent table with a well-padded thigh tourniquet, leg prepped and draped free from mid-thigh to foot to allow full ROM assessment and any combined procedure (MPFL-R harvest, TTO) in the same setting.
- A leg holder or bump permitting knee flexion through a full arc for intraoperative tracking assessment.
- Fluoroscopy C-arm positioned and tested for both AP and lateral distal femur views before draping, since geometry and (if combined) MPFL femoral tunnel position both require intraoperative confirmation.
- EUA of the knee before prep — confirm the instability pattern, apprehension, and glide, and correlate with preoperative imaging grade.
- Mark surface landmarks: patellar borders, tibial tubercle, and the planned anterior midline or medial parapatellar incision, sized generously enough for wide exposure of the trochlear groove — this is not a minimally invasive approach.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make an anterior midline or medial parapatellar incision and perform a lateral or medial parapatellar arthrotomy sufficient to fully expose and evert/subluxate the patella, giving direct wide access to the entire trochlear surface.
2. Inspect the trochlear cartilage and confirm the dysplasia pattern (supratrochlear spur, flat/convex groove, facet asymmetry) matches the preoperative imaging grade; assess cartilage quality across the trochlea and patella.
3. Outline the planned osteochondral flap with a fine osteotomy, staying just deep to the subchondral bone plate, encompassing the trochlear groove and extending onto the medial and lateral facets as needed to allow the flap to be remolded into a new, more physiologic contour.
4. Elevate the osteochondral flap using graduated osteotomes, working systematically to free it from the underlying cancellous bone while preserving cartilage–subchondral bone continuity — for a Bereiter-style thin-flap technique, aim for a thin (2–3 mm), elastic flap that will be flexible enough to conform to a newly shaped groove; for a thick-shell technique, a thicker (~5 mm) shell is elevated and requires less remolding but a more extensive underlying resection.
5. Deepen and reshape the trochlear groove by removing cancellous/subchondral bone beneath the elevated flap with a burr or straight osteotome, working from proximal-lateral toward distal, until a more anatomic groove contour (deepened centrally, with an appropriate lateral facet slope) is achieved — remove enough bone to create a groove of normal depth without overresecting, since overzealous deepening risks flap fracture or thinning the flap to the point of chondral necrosis risk.
6. Trial-fit the flap into the newly shaped bed repeatedly during reshaping — the flap should mold passively into the new contour without being forced; continue adjusting the underlying bone bed rather than forcing flap conformity.
7. Once the bed is shaped, press the remolded flap down into the new groove and secure it with sutures passed through peripheral drill holes, suture anchors, or a combination, ensuring stable, gap-free contact between the flap and the underlying bed across its full extent.
8. Reduce the patella and take the knee through a full ROM arc, directly observing patellar tracking within the newly contoured groove — confirm centralized tracking without residual lateral subluxation and without new mechanical catching from the reshaped groove.
9. Reassess patellar stability after the bony correction: if residual laxity remains on manual glide/apprehension testing, proceed to MPFL reconstruction in the same setting (graft harvest, femoral and patellar tunnel preparation, and tensioning as described in the MPFL-R technique) — tension the MPFL graft only after the trochleoplasty geometry is finalized.
10. If concurrent patella alta or elevated TT-TG was identified preoperatively, complete the planned tibial tubercle osteotomy (distalization and/or medialization) before final MPFL graft tensioning, following the same fixation and buttress principles as an isolated TTO.
11. Close the arthrotomy in layers, repair the retinaculum, and close the subcutaneous tissue and skin.
12. Apply a hinged knee brace and place the patient on a protected early-motion protocol before leaving the OR — early, controlled motion is prioritized given the meaningful arthrofibrosis risk associated with this procedure.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Take a generous arthrotomy — this is a wide-exposure open bony procedure, and inadequate visualization of the full trochlear surface is a common contributor to an imprecise groove contour.
- Elevate the osteochondral flap systematically and symmetrically rather than levering aggressively from one corner — uneven elevation force is a common cause of flap fracture or an inadvertent full-thickness perforation.
- Favor a thinner, more elastic flap (Bereiter-style) when the goal is precise remolding to a markedly abnormal native contour — a thinner flap conforms more readily to the newly shaped bed with less risk of gapping at the flap edges.
- Trial-fit the flap repeatedly during bony reshaping rather than committing to a single resection and hoping the flap conforms — iterative fitting is the single best defense against both under-correction (residual shallow groove) and over-resection (thinned, fracture-prone flap).
- Shape the new groove to blend smoothly into the native proximal and distal trochlear cartilage rather than creating an abrupt transition — an abrupt step is a source of mechanical catching and focal chondral overload postoperatively.
- Secure the flap with broad, evenly distributed fixation (sutures/anchors around the full perimeter) rather than a few central points — uneven fixation risks a flap edge lifting under early motion, undermining the correction.
- Always reassess patellar tracking dynamically (full ROM, direct visualization) before deciding whether MPFL-R is still needed — trochleoplasty alone sometimes restores adequate stability, and adding an unnecessary MPFL-R increases overall procedure risk without added benefit.
- Address patella alta concurrently when present — a beautifully executed trochleoplasty does not compensate for an unaddressed high-riding patella that still engages the groove late in flexion.
- Prioritize early, closely supervised motion in the postoperative plan given the documented arthrofibrosis risk of this specific procedure — this is a case where slightly more aggressive early ROM management (within the limits of bony/flap healing) is often justified compared with a routine soft-tissue stabilization.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Technically demanding, with risk of **chondral injury** during flap elevation and groove deepening — overzealous deepening or uneven flap elevation risks chondral necrosis or fracture of the osteochondral flap; work systematically and trial-fit repeatedly rather than resecting in one pass.
- **Arthrofibrosis** is a meaningfully reported complication, particularly in pediatric series (~13%) — mitigate with a structured, closely supervised early motion protocol.
- **Patellofemoral overcorrection/stiffness** is a recognized risk from over-deepening the groove or over-constraining tracking when combined with MPFL-R.
- **Avascular change to the trochlear fragment** is a rare complication, related to compromise of the flap's blood supply during elevation.
- Underappreciating concurrent **patella alta** — failing to address height alongside dysplasia can leave residual instability even after a technically sound trochleoplasty.
- Best performed in high-volume centers given the technical demand and complication profile; consider referral or co-management for surgeons without regular experience in the technique.
- An abrupt or asymmetric groove contour from imprecise reshaping can produce new mechanical symptoms (catching, focal overload) distinct from the original instability complaint — this is avoidable with careful, iterative bed shaping.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Rehabilitation follows the same combined patellar-instability protocol used after MPFL-R with a bony procedure: brace-protected early ROM, protected weight-bearing pending osseous healing, progressive strengthening, and criteria-based return to sport at 6–9 months.
- Pediatric outcomes data show low recurrent instability (~1%) but a notable arthrofibrosis rate (~13%), reinforcing the value of a structured, closely supervised motion protocol in younger patients.
