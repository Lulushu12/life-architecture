## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Patellar tendinopathy ("jumper's knee"), Blazina stages I–III — progressive tendon loading is the central, first-line conservative treatment across all three stages, with intensity and adjuncts titrated to severity.
- In-season athletes needing same-session analgesia to continue training/competition (isometric phase).
- Post-surgical debridement/tendon scraping cases, as the rehabilitation backbone once initial wound healing allows loading.
- Recurrent or refractory tendinopathy after a prior inadequate loading trial — a properly dosed, supervised re-trial is indicated before considering surgery, since many "failed" programs were under-dosed or non-adherent rather than truly recalcitrant.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- **Blazina stage IV (complete tendon rupture)** — this stage exits the tendinopathy loading pathway entirely and requires surgical repair; do not attempt loading a ruptured tendon.
- Acute, high-grade partial tear on imaging with a positive extensor lag — evaluate for surgical indication before committing to a loading program.
- Uncontrolled pain that prevents any tolerable loading (rare) — start with a lower-intensity isometric-only phase or address pain with adjuncts before progressing.
- Suspicion of an alternative diagnosis mimicking tendinopathy (fat pad impingement, plica syndrome, bipartite patella, referred hip pathology) — confirm the diagnosis before committing the patient to a 12-week program targeted at the wrong structure.
- Poor expected adherence to a supervised, multi-month program — set expectations explicitly, since under-dosed loading is a common cause of apparent "failure."

## Preoperative Planning

*(Program planning and baseline assessment before starting structured loading.)*

- **Confirm the diagnosis and stage**: focal tenderness at the proximal patellar pole with the knee extended and quadriceps relaxed, pain reproduced on single-leg decline squat, and correlate with Blazina stage (pain only after activity = I; pain during and after activity but performance preserved = II; pain limiting performance = III).
- **Baseline outcome measure**: obtain a baseline **VISA-P score** (Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment–Patella) to track progress objectively across the program rather than relying on pain report alone.
- **Imaging**: ultrasound (hypoechoic thickening, neovascularity on Doppler) is a useful confirmatory and monitoring tool; MRI reserved for atypical presentations or when a partial tear needs to be excluded before committing to an aggressive loading program.
- **Kinetic chain assessment**: screen ankle dorsiflexion range, quadriceps and hip strength, and landing/jump mechanics — these contributors are addressed in parallel with tendon-specific loading, not after it.
- **Load history**: quantify recent training volume (jump counts, session frequency, surface, footwear) to identify the load-management error driving the pathology and to set a realistic reduced-but-not-zero training baseline during the program.
- **Equipment needed**: 25° decline board for eccentric/decline squat work and for the decline single-leg squat test; leg press, squat rack, or hack squat machine for heavy slow resistance (HSR) training; means of load progression tracking (training log, load monitoring app, or simple written log).
- **Set the timeline expectation up front**: a minimum of 12 weeks of structured, supervised loading is required before the program can be considered a genuine failed conservative trial — communicate this at the outset so early discomfort or slow progress does not trigger premature abandonment.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

*(Setup and patient positioning for each loading modality — no anesthesia is required for this non-operative technique.)*

- **Isometric holds (Spanish squat)**: patient stands with a strap or band anchored behind the knees at approximately mid-thigh height, looping forward around a fixed post; leans back against the strap with knees flexed to roughly 60–90°, keeping the shin vertical to bias load onto the quadriceps/patellar tendon rather than the hip extensors; hold for the prescribed duration with even weight-bearing on both legs, or single-leg for higher-intensity loading in advanced stages.
- **Isometric holds (leg extension)**: seated in a leg extension machine, knee positioned at roughly 60° flexion (adjustable to the angle that best reproduces controlled loading without provoking sharp pain), sustained isometric contraction against a fixed pad.
- **Eccentric decline squat**: patient stands on a 25° decline board, single-leg, and performs a slow squat emphasizing the eccentric (lowering) phase while allowing the contralateral leg to assist only on the concentric (rising) phase in the classic protocol — this asymmetric loading is deliberate and central to the eccentric method.
- **Heavy slow resistance (HSR) training**: performed on a leg press, back squat, or hack squat machine with the patient positioned per standard machine setup; emphasize a controlled, slow tempo (roughly 3 seconds concentric, 3 seconds eccentric) rather than fast/ballistic repetitions.
- **Supervision cadence**: initial sessions should be directly supervised (physical therapist or trained clinician) to confirm correct positioning, tempo, and load selection before transitioning to a largely home/gym-based program with periodic check-ins.
- **Warm-up**: a brief general warm-up (stationary bike or light movement) before loading sessions is reasonable, though the loading program itself does not require a specialized warm-up beyond starting at a tolerable working load.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Weeks 0–2 (isometric phase)**: begin isometric loading — e.g., 5 × 45 seconds at approximately 70% maximal voluntary contraction (MVC), performed once or twice daily — used acutely for same-session analgesia and to allow continued training/competition during in-season management. The analgesic effect is well established, though the magnitude of its superiority over isotonic loading for pain relief has been questioned by newer trials; treat it as a bridge and adjunct, not the definitive long-term stimulus.
2. In parallel from week 0, reduce but do not fully eliminate aggravating load if tolerable — complete rest is not required and may delay the transition to progressive loading; address any identified training-load error (jump volume, surface, footwear) at this stage.
3. **Weeks 2–12 (progressive isotonic phase)**: transition to one of two well-supported progressive loading strategies, chosen based on patient preference, tolerance, and available equipment:
   - **Heavy slow resistance (HSR) training** — leg press, squat, or hack squat; 3–4 sets, 6–15 rep-max load, tempo approximately 3 seconds concentric/3 seconds eccentric, 3×/week with progressive load increases session to session, continued for the full 12 weeks. HSR shows comparable-or-superior VISA-P outcomes to eccentric decline squat protocols with better patient adherence and tolerance; 2024 mixed-treatment meta-analyses rank HSR and moderate slow resistance training favorably for VISA-P improvement relative to eccentric-only protocols.
   - **Eccentric decline squat program** (Alfredson-type/Silbernagel protocol) — 25° decline board, 3×15 reps, twice daily, continued for 12 weeks. An effective, well-validated alternative, particularly where HSR equipment is unavailable; expect discomfort during early sessions (acceptable pain, typically ≤5/10, that settles).
4. Progress load weekly within the chosen modality based on tolerance — increase resistance (HSR) or add single-leg/loaded variations (decline squat) once the current load is well tolerated (pain ≤3–5/10 during loading, settling within 24 hours).
5. Throughout weeks 2–12, continue addressing kinetic chain contributors identified in planning — ankle dorsiflexion mobility work, hip and quadriceps strengthening outside the tendon-specific protocol, and landing mechanics retraining.
6. **Months 3–6 (energy storage/plyometric phase)**: once the patient tolerates heavy loading pain-free, introduce jump and landing mechanics retraining, followed by a graded plyometric progression (double-leg to single-leg, low to high amplitude), while gradually restoring full training volume.
7. Reassess VISA-P score at intervals (e.g., every 4–6 weeks) to objectively track progress and to decide whether to advance phases, extend the current phase, or add adjuncts.
8. If pain or VISA-P plateau despite adherent loading through 12 weeks, consider adjuncts (extracorporeal shockwave therapy, PRP as an investigational option) alongside continued loading rather than as a replacement for it — do not abandon the loading program while adding adjuncts.
9. Continue the program until VISA-P has plateaued or normalized and the patient tolerates full training load and sport-specific plyometric demand without symptom recurrence; this typically requires 3–6 months, occasionally longer for elite jumping-sport athletes.
10. Only after a minimum of 6 months of a comprehensive, adherent loading program has failed should surgical debridement/tendon scraping be considered.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Front-load patient education on the 12-week minimum timeline — the single most common reason a loading program is prematurely labeled a "failure" is stopping at 3–4 weeks before the stimulus has had time to work.
- Use the decline single-leg squat as both a diagnostic test and a simple in-session monitoring tool — a consistent, reproducible pain response on this test tracks well with clinical progress.
- Teach the "acceptable pain" rule explicitly and concretely: pain up to about 3–5/10 during loading that settles within 24 hours is acceptable and expected, not a sign to stop; pain that escalates session-to-session or fails to settle by the next day signals the load needs to be reduced.
- Use isometric loading strategically around competition/training sessions during in-season management — it is most valuable as a same-day analgesic bridge, not as a substitute for the progressive isotonic phase that drives the actual tendon adaptation.
- Bias HSR toward a leg press or squat variant that allows precise, incremental load progression (small plate increments) — coarse load jumps make it hard to stay within the target 6–15RM band as strength improves week to week.
- Keep the contralateral leg assisting only on the concentric phase of the decline squat if using the classic eccentric protocol — allowing bilateral concentric assistance defeats the purpose of isolating eccentric load on the symptomatic side.
- Track objective load (weight × reps × sets) alongside VISA-P, not pain scores alone — pain fluctuates session to session for reasons unrelated to tendon healing (sleep, prior day's activity), while progressive load tolerance is a more reliable marker of true tissue adaptation.
- Address ankle dorsiflexion restriction early — limited dorsiflexion increases compensatory knee flexion and patellar tendon load during landing, and is a modifiable contributor that is easy to miss if attention stays fixed on the tendon itself.
- Reassess and adjust jump/training volume caps throughout the program rather than only at the start — athletes often quietly escalate volume as pain improves, which can outpace the tendon's adaptation and stall progress.
- When transitioning to the plyometric phase, progress amplitude and complexity gradually (double-leg landings before single-leg, low box heights before high) — this phase is the most common point of symptom flare if progressed too quickly.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Corticosteroid injection** into or around the patellar tendon is **not recommended** — associated with increased risk of subsequent tendon rupture.
- **PRP** has mixed-quality evidence — some trials show VISA-P improvement over placebo/eccentric exercise alone at 6–12 months, but preparations and outcomes are heterogeneous; considered adjunctive/investigational for refractory Blazina II–III rather than first-line. Several 2023–2024 network meta-analyses continue to rank exercise-based interventions above injectable therapies.
- **Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT)** is a reasonable adjunct for refractory tendinopathy (stage II–III) after a failed initial loading program, with moderate evidence for pain reduction and low morbidity — not a substitute for loading.
- **Dry needling/percutaneous tenotomy** has modest evidence and is typically combined with structured loading rather than used standalone.
- A minimum of **12 weeks** of a structured, supervised loading program is required before considering it a "failed" conservative trial — many athletes need 3–6 months for full return to high-level jumping sport; stopping early is the most common reason for perceived treatment failure.
- Under-dosed or non-adherent loading (too infrequent, too light, or abandoned at the first flare) is a common, correctable cause of apparent program failure — before escalating to adjuncts or surgery, confirm the program was actually delivered at the prescribed dose.
- Unaddressed loading errors can allow progression from tendinopathy to partial or complete rupture (Blazina IV) — persistent, worsening pain despite adherent loading, or any new extensor lag, warrants re-imaging rather than simply continuing the same program.
- Progressing the plyometric/energy-storage phase too quickly, before heavy loading is pain-free, is a frequent cause of late-stage symptom flare and program setback.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2 (isometric phase)**: isometric holds for analgesia (5×45 sec, ~70% MVC), activity modification (reduce but don't fully cease loading if tolerable), address training load.
- **Weeks 2–12 (isotonic/HSR or eccentric phase)**: progressive HSR (leg press/squat, 3–4 sets 6–15RM, 3×/week) or eccentric decline squat program (3×15, 2×/day) — expect gradual VISA-P improvement over the full 12 weeks; acceptable pain during loading (≤3–5/10 that settles within 24h) is not a reason to stop.
- **Months 3–6 (energy storage/plyometric phase)**: introduce jump/landing mechanics retraining, plyometric progression once pain-free with heavy loading, gradual return to full training volume.
- **Return to sport**: criteria-based — VISA-P score improvement plateaued or near-normal, pain ≤2–3/10 with full training load, symmetric single-leg hop/landing mechanics; may take 3–6 months for Blazina II–III, occasionally longer for elite jumping-sport athletes.
