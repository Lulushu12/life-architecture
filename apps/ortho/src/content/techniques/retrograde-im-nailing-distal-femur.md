## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Extra-articular (AO/OTA 33-A) and simple articular (33-C1, occasionally C2) distal femur fractures with adequate distal bone stock to accept multiple interlocking screws and enough intact metaphysis for the nail to gain fixed-angle-like stability through its screws.
- Particularly favorable for comminuted/osteoporotic diaphyseal-metaphyseal junction fractures, ipsilateral shaft extension, floating knee (as the femoral half of a nail-nail construct), and bilateral injuries needing rapid sequential fixation.
- Ipsilateral femoral neck fracture requiring supine positioning without traction, morbid obesity, pregnancy, or the need for rapid bilateral simultaneous fixation — situations where retrograde entry offers practical positioning advantages over antegrade nailing.
- Periprosthetic distal femur fractures with a retained, well-fixed femoral component and adequate distal bone stock proximal to the component (Su Type I pattern).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Fracture located within approximately 5 cm of the lesser trochanter — insufficient working length remains for adequate proximal fixation via a retrograde approach.
- Less than 45° of achievable knee flexion, which makes safe access to the optimal entry point difficult or impossible.
- Prior or active knee joint infection — the entry portal violates the joint, risking seeding infection into the femoral canal.
- Significant soft-tissue injury or open wounds directly over the anterior knee at the planned entry site.
- Patella baja, which can obstruct safe access to the intercondylar entry point.
- Very short or severely comminuted distal segment unable to accept adequate interlocking screw purchase — consider locked plating or a combined nail-plate construct instead.
- Complex, comminuted intra-articular (33-C2/C3) fractures requiring extensive open articular reconstruction before any bridging fixation — a locked plate (with direct articular exposure) is often better suited to allow simultaneous articular fixation under direct vision.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: dedicated knee AP/lateral/oblique radiographs and CT with coronal/sagittal reconstruction to confirm the articular pattern is simple enough for retrograde nailing (or amenable to percutaneous/limited-open lag screw fixation before nail passage) and to rule out an occult coronal (Hoffa) fragment that would change the fixation strategy.
- **Templating**: confirm adequate distal bone stock and metaphyseal length to accept the nail's distal interlocking screws in a divergent/multiplanar configuration; template nail length and diameter against the canal, noting that the optimal coronal-plane entry point is medialized relative to the intercondylar notch midpoint (approximately 4 mm medial) to align with the femoral anatomic axis rather than the geometric notch center.
- **Implant selection**: choose a nail system offering multi-planar or spiral-blade distal interlocking options where possible, since two or more distal screws in a divergent configuration meaningfully improve rotational and coronal control over a single-plane interlocking pattern, particularly in comminuted or osteoporotic metaphyseal bone.
- **Equipment**: standard reaming set, a retrograde-specific targeting jig for distal (near-entry) and proximal interlocking, headless compression screws or standard lag screws on standby for any simple articular component requiring fixation before nail passage, and fluoroscopy positioned to capture the knee, femoral canal, and hip in sequence.
- **Case-specific planning**: for floating knee patterns, plan the overall fixation sequence with the tibial fixation team/self in mind — an antegrade femoral nail plus tibial nail ("nail-nail") is the most biomechanically favorable combination when feasible, but retrograde femoral nailing is a reasonable and practical alternative that allows a single positioning/incision strategy shared with the tibial nail; decide and communicate this plan before the case starts. For bilateral or multi-limb trauma requiring rapid sequential fixation, retrograde nailing's supine, non-traction-table positioning is a specific practical advantage worth factoring into OR sequencing.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia standard; regional block adjuncts for postoperative analgesia as in other femoral procedures.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent flat table (no fracture table/traction needed) with the knee flexed over a padded triangle or bolster to approximately 60-90° to expose the intercondylar entry point and relax the extensor mechanism over the entry portal; this positioning is a genuine practical advantage in patients where fracture-table traction is contraindicated or impractical (ipsilateral femoral neck fracture, floating knee, morbid obesity, pregnancy, bilateral injuries).
- **Draping**: wide prep and drape from mid-thigh to well below the knee, keeping the limb free to move between the flexed entry-portal position and an extended position for reduction/length/rotation assessment.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm to obtain a true lateral of the knee (essential for identifying Blumensaat's line) and a true AP centered on the intercondylar notch before draping; confirm the C-arm can subsequently image the full femoral shaft and, if needed, the hip for length/rotation comparison.
- **Landmarks**: mark the inferior pole of the patella, the patellar tendon borders (for a trans-tendon or medial/lateral parapatellar portal, per surgeon preference and implant system), and the anticipated proximal interlocking incision sites before prepping.
- **Tourniquet**: use is surgeon-dependent; many avoid a tourniquet for the reaming/nailing portion to reduce thigh compartment pressure concerns in a comminuted fracture, using it only briefly if at all for the initial arthrotomy/entry exposure.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm eligibility**: reconfirm the fracture pattern has adequate distal bone stock and sufficient intact metaphysis for the nail's screws to achieve fixed-angle-like stability, and that knee flexion to at least 45-90° is achievable without excessive fracture-site motion or pain.
2. **Position the knee in flexion** over a bolster/triangle, flex to the degree needed for entry portal access while keeping the fracture reasonably reduced.
3. **Establish the entry portal** through a small medial or lateral parapatellar arthrotomy (or a trans-patellar-tendon splitting approach, per surgeon/implant preference), placing the entry guidewire just anterior to the PCL origin, aligned with Blumensaat's line on the lateral fluoroscopic view. On the AP view, center the entry point in the intercondylar notch, keeping in mind that the optimal coronal entry point for accurate axial alignment is slightly medialized (roughly 4 mm) relative to the notch's geometric midpoint, aligning instead with the anatomic axis of the canal.
4. **Confirm entry trajectory on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy** before opening the canal with the awl/reamer — an off-axis entry is a leading cause of iatrogenic anterior cruciate ligament, posterior cruciate ligament, or articular cartilage injury, as well as subsequent malalignment.
5. **Reduce the fracture** using a bone hook and gentle manual manipulation through the minimal-incision approach — reduction using a percutaneous bone hook with minimal additional incision is generally advised over an open reduction of the metaphyseal comminution, to preserve fracture biology.
6. **Ream sequentially** across the fracture, watching fluoroscopically to confirm central tracking through both the distal metaphysis and proximally toward the shaft.
7. **Pass the guidewire proximally**, advancing it up to the region of the greater trochanter, where it should achieve good purchase to prevent slipping out during subsequent reamer exchanges — advancement into the proximal main fragment is typically easier with retrograde nailing than the equivalent proximal targeting step in antegrade nailing, and is performed under image intensifier guidance throughout.
8. **Pass the nail** to the appropriate depth, seating the proximal end appropriately below the articular entry point per the implant system's design so as not to leave prominent hardware within the joint.
9. **Lock distally first**, placing a minimum of two (ideally more, in a divergent/multiplanar configuration using modern multi-planar distal interlocking nails) screws through the near-entry targeting jig for rotational and coronal control of the distal fragment.
10. **Reduce and confirm length, rotation, and alignment** before proximal locking — check rotation against the contralateral limb (cortical diameter comparison or "perfect circle" technique) and confirm no coronal or sagittal malalignment of the distal fragment, since the deforming forces at the distal femur (particularly gastrocnemius-driven apex-posterior angulation) can subtly displace the fragment even after nail passage.
11. **Lock proximally**, typically free-hand under fluoroscopic guidance using the "perfect circle" technique for the proximal interlocking holes.
12. **For floating knee patterns**, proceed with tibial nailing under the same anesthetic once the femoral component is secured, sharing positioning where practical.
13. **Final fluoroscopic confirmation** of nail position, interlocking screws, articular congruity (if a simple articular component was addressed), and overall length/alignment/rotation.
14. **Close the arthrotomy/entry portal and interlocking incisions in layers**, confirming no residual intra-articular hardware prominence with a final range-of-motion check.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Medialize the coronal-plane entry point slightly (roughly 4 mm medial to the geometric center of the intercondylar notch) to align with the femoral anatomic axis — entry precisely at the notch midpoint can direct the nail slightly lateral to the true canal axis and predispose to a subtle valgus or malalignment tendency.
- Use a bone hook for reduction through the minimal-incision entry approach rather than converting to a formal open reduction of the metaphyseal fragment — this preserves the biologic advantage of the closed/minimally invasive technique.
- Advance the guidewire proximally to the greater trochanter region before reaming to ensure good purchase and prevent the wire slipping out during reamer exchanges — this is a more forgiving step in retrograde than antegrade nailing and worth using to full advantage.
- Lock distally before proximally, and use this sequence deliberately to fine-tune distal fragment reduction (rotation, coronal, sagittal alignment) before the construct is rigidly fixed to the shaft.
- Use a divergent/multiplanar distal interlocking configuration whenever the implant system offers it — this measurably improves rotational and coronal-plane control in comminuted or osteoporotic distal bone compared with a single-plane pattern.
- Flex the knee sufficiently (generally ≥45°, more if achievable) before attempting entry — inadequate flexion is a recognized relative contraindication and, even when the case proceeds, inadequate flexion during the entry step itself increases the risk of an off-axis or articular-cartilage-damaging portal.
- In floating knee cases, decide the femoral fixation strategy (antegrade nail-nail vs retrograde) at the start of the case based on positioning logistics with the tibial nail, not as an afterthought — retrograde nailing's shared supine positioning with tibial nailing is a specific practical advantage worth planning for deliberately.
- Screen for occult multi-ligament knee injury once the fracture is stabilized enough to permit safe stress testing — this is easy to defer and forget after a technically demanding nailing case but changes both vascular surveillance intensity and rehabilitation planning.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Malrotation and inadequate distal purchase in very short distal segments are the main technical risks.
- Joint violation makes this approach unsuitable in active septic arthritis and requires reasonably preserved articular anatomy for safe passage.
- Careless insertion and manipulation at the entry portal risks injury to the popliteal neurovascular structures posteriorly and to the anterior/posterior cruciate ligaments and articular cartilage — minimize soft-tissue trauma and use a protected, image-confirmed trajectory rather than forceful freehand instrumentation.
- Nonunion may be lower with nailing than plating in comparative series (17/173 nail vs 29/172 plate in one pooled analysis), but the evidence base is not yet high-quality RCT data — individualize rather than treat as definitively superior.
- Functional outcomes after floating knee fixation are driven more by the articular (knee/ankle) injury components than by the diaphyseal fixation choice, so any intra-articular extension must be identified and anatomically reduced regardless of nail vs plate choice.
- Relative contraindications are easy to overlook under time pressure — a fracture within 5 cm of the lesser trochanter, inadequate achievable knee flexion, prior knee infection, significant peri-knee soft-tissue injury, or patella baja should each prompt reconsideration of the approach before the entry portal is committed.
- An off-axis entry point (too lateral, or insufficiently medialized) is a preventable cause of coronal malalignment — confirm the entry trajectory on both fluoroscopic views before reaming begins, not after.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0-2 weeks**: knee ROM started early (0-90°) when fixation stability allows, to prevent arthrofibrosis. Many stable retrograde-nail constructs allow weight bearing as tolerated (WBAT).
- **2-6 weeks**: progressive knee flexion toward 90-110°, quadriceps activation (isometric progressing to active), patellar mobilization.
- **6-12 weeks**: progress weight bearing to full as callus/healing progresses on serial radiographs (often earlier than comminuted plated patterns); ROM progression toward ≥110-120° flexion; begin closed-chain strengthening.
- **3-6 months**: full weight bearing, strengthening progression, gait normalization. Union in distal femur fractures is generally slower and less certain than shaft fractures (union rates cluster ~85-95% in comparative series).
- **Return to work**: sedentary 6-10 weeks; manual labor 4-6+ months, contingent on union and functional knee ROM/strength.
- **Return to sport**: high-demand/pivoting sport generally deferred 6-9 months, contingent on radiographic union, painless full ROM, and quadriceps/hamstring strength ≥85-90% of the contralateral limb.
