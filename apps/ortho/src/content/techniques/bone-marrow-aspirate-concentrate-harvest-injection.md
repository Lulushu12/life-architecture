## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Knee OA (moderate), particularly as an adjunct or alternative to PRP in patients who have failed PRP, or combined with microfracture/cartilage procedures.
- Focal chondral defects as an adjunct to microfracture ("one-step" cartilage repair).
- Early-stage osteonecrosis of the femoral head — core decompression + BMAC augmentation has reasonable supportive evidence for precollapse Ficat-Arlet stage I–II disease.
- Nonunion augmentation via percutaneous BMAC injection at the fracture site (historically the Connolly technique, revisited with modern concentration technology).
- Reserve for failed-PRP or specific cartilage/AVN indications rather than routine first-line OA orthobiologic, given added cost and harvest morbidity versus PRP.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Active local infection at the harvest site (PSIS/iliac crest) or at the target joint/site — treat or exclude first.
- Uncontrolled coagulopathy or supratherapeutic anticoagulation — the iliac crest is highly vascular cancellous bone and harvest carries a real (if low) bleeding risk; hold anticoagulation per usual perioperative protocol before harvest.
- Ficat-Arlet stage III–IV (post-collapse) osteonecrosis — core decompression + BMAC is a precollapse strategy; do not offer it as a substitute for arthroplasty once collapse or joint incongruity has occurred.
- Active malignancy, especially hematologic — theoretical concern regarding stimulation of a marrow-based process; discuss with the patient's oncology team if active or recent.
- Pregnancy — fluoroscopic guidance (if used) and unproven fetal safety data argue against elective use.
- Inability to tolerate prone or lateral positioning for the harvest (severe cardiopulmonary disease, recent abdominal surgery) — plan an alternative harvest site (anterior iliac crest supine) or defer.
- Unrealistic patient expectations — BMAC is not a disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy outside its established niche; do not proceed until expectations are calibrated.
- Unclear reimbursement/cost pathway — not an absolute contraindication, but confirm the patient understands the added cost before committing to harvest, since most systems remain largely out-of-pocket.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** plain radiographs of the target joint at minimum; MRI to size a focal chondral defect or stage osteonecrosis (Ficat-Arlet); CT of the nonunion site when planning percutaneous fracture-site augmentation to confirm gap size and hardware position.
- **Harvest site selection:** posterior superior iliac spine (PSIS) is preferred when positioning allows — larger accessible cancellous volume and avoids the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve territory that lies near the anterior iliac crest (AIC). AIC (supine) is the fallback when prone/lateral positioning is not feasible. Proximal tibia is a lower-yield alternative used occasionally when iliac access is difficult (e.g., combined ipsilateral knee procedure, positioning already supine).
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** multi-hole or side-port aspiration trocar (single-entry-point systems designed for repositioning without withdrawal are preferred over standard Jamshidi needles for yield); ACD-A anticoagulant pre-loaded into 10 mL collection syringes; point-of-care centrifuge/concentration kit compatible with the chosen aspiration system; local anesthetic and standard sterile prep tray for the harvest site; delivery needle/cannula appropriate to the target (IA injection needle, arthroscopic cannula for microfracture augmentation, decompression cannula, or percutaneous fracture-site needle).
- **Volume planning:** plan total aspirate volume against the intended use — roughly 60 mL divided into 2 mL draws across multiple sites for a standalone IA knee injection, up to 100–120 mL for larger applications (core decompression, combined microfracture) since final concentrate volume is only a fraction (commonly 5–10%) of the starting aspirate.
- **Labs:** screen platelet count and coagulation parameters if BMAC is being combined with another operative procedure; confirm anticoagulants have been appropriately held.
- **Logistics:** confirm the point-of-care centrifuge and kit are set up and calibrated before the patient is prepped — processing time is part of total case time and should not be an afterthought, particularly when BMAC is combined with a time-pressured arthroscopic procedure.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** local anesthesia with or without light sedation (MAC) is standard for a stand-alone harvest-and-inject procedure done in a clinic/procedure room. General or regional (spinal/epidural) anesthesia is used when BMAC is combined with an operative procedure (microfracture, core decompression, open nonunion revision).
- **Position:** prone or lateral decubitus for PSIS harvest (prone is most common — allows bilateral access without repositioning). Supine with the hip in neutral or slight external rotation for anterior iliac crest access. If BMAC is combined with a knee or hip procedure, plan the sequence so the harvest position does not conflict with the surgical position (e.g., harvest PSIS with the patient prone/lateral before final positioning for a supine knee arthroscopy, or harvest AIC supine immediately before draping the ipsilateral extremity).
- **Landmarks:** palpate and mark the PSIS bilaterally (often visible as the "dimples of Venus" in slender patients); mark the planned entry point along the iliac crest, no more than ~10 cm cephalad to the PSIS.
- **Imaging:** fluoroscopic guidance (AP and lateral/oblique pelvis views) or ultrasound is used to confirm trajectory and depth, particularly in obese patients, revision harvests, or patients with abnormal pelvic anatomy (prior trauma, spinal fusion hardware). Experienced operators may use a pure landmark technique for a routine PSIS harvest, but imaging materially widens the safety margin from the sciatic notch and is recommended for anyone early in their experience.
- **Prep/drape:** standard chlorhexidine skin prep; drape the harvest site generously to allow trajectory adjustment and, if bilateral, both PSIS regions. Prep and drape the target injection or surgical site as a separate sterile field — treat harvest and delivery as two distinct sterile procedures even when performed in the same setting.
- **Tourniquet:** not used for the harvest itself; use per standard technique if BMAC is combined with an extremity procedure (e.g., microfracture).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Position the patient prone (or lateral decubitus), prep and drape the PSIS region.
2. Infiltrate local anesthetic to skin, subcutaneous tissue, and periosteum overlying the planned entry point.
3. Make a small stab incision and introduce the aspiration trocar.
4. Direct the trocar toward the PSIS: perpendicular to the PSIS surface, or angled roughly 20–45° caudal from the transverse plane and 30–45° lateral from the parasagittal plane, advancing between the inner and outer tables of the ilium toward the greater trochanter. Keep the entry point no more than ~10 cm cephalad to the PSIS.
5. Advance the trocar with firm, controlled downward pressure until cortical purchase is felt and then a loss-of-resistance sensation as it enters cancellous bone; confirm position with fluoroscopy if used. Entry into marrow typically requires noticeably more downward pressure than a routine soft-tissue injection.
6. Advance to a depth of up to ~7 cm without changing needle direction once cortical purchase is achieved.
7. Attach a syringe pre-loaded with ACD-A anticoagulant and aspirate in small volumes — 2 mL at a time. Stop the draw as soon as flow diminishes or the aspirate visibly thins/becomes more blood-like (a sign of peripheral-blood dilution).
8. Without withdrawing the trocar fully, rotate it 45–90° and/or redirect slightly to sample a fresh trabecular bed at the same or a different depth for the next 2 mL draw, rather than continuing to aspirate from the same spot.
9. If additional volume is needed, withdraw and reposition through the same skin incision to access additional cortical entry points along the crest, combining all aliquots into the anticoagulated collection container until the planned total volume is reached (commonly ~60 mL for a standalone IA injection, up to ~100–120 mL for core decompression or combined microfracture cases).
10. Remove the trocar, hold direct pressure over the harvest site, and apply a dressing.
11. Process the aspirate promptly: transfer to the point-of-care centrifuge system per the kit's protocol (single or double spin) to concentrate mesenchymal stromal cells, hematopoietic progenitors, growth factors, and platelets into a reduced-volume concentrate.
12. Prepare the concentrate for delivery — draw into a delivery syringe; if combining with microfracture, mix with fibrin sealant/scaffold per protocol or apply directly onto the prepared defect base; if combining with core decompression, load for delivery through the decompression cannula.
13. Deliver to the target:
    - **Intra-articular injection** (knee OA): standard sterile IA injection technique, superolateral or anterolateral approach.
    - **Microfracture augmentation:** apply concentrate onto/into the microfractured chondral defect after all chondral preparation and marrow-vent creation is complete, often secured with fibrin glue or a scaffold membrane.
    - **Core decompression augmentation:** deliver through the decompression tract into the necrotic segment of the femoral head.
    - **Nonunion augmentation:** percutaneous delivery into and around the fracture site under fluoroscopic guidance, typically as multiple small aliquots distributed along the nonunion interface (Connolly-technique-style).
14. Confirm hemostasis at the harvest site and dress it. Document total aspirate volume, number of sites/redirections used, final concentrate volume, and estimated cell yield if the kit provides a readout.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Draw only 2 mL per aspiration site — peripheral-blood dilution begins almost immediately past this volume and is the single most important yield-preserving habit in the entire procedure.
- Reposition (rotate or redirect) rather than aspirate longer from one spot; a fresh trabecular bed with each 2 mL draw consistently outperforms a larger single-site draw for progenitor cell yield.
- Pre-load ACD-A anticoagulant syringes before starting so anticoagulation happens the instant marrow enters the syringe — clot formation begins within seconds of aspiration.
- Favor the PSIS over the anterior iliac crest when positioning allows; it offers a more favorable trabecular bone volume for aspiration and keeps you away from the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve territory near the ASIS.
- Bilateral PSIS harvest (or multiple entry points per side) is a better way to reach a larger target volume than over-instrumenting a single cortical window.
- Use fluoroscopy for your first several harvests, and in any patient with abnormal pelvic anatomy (prior trauma, spinal instrumentation, significant obesity) — it meaningfully reduces the risk of losing bony purchase and straying toward the sciatic notch.
- If the trocar loses bony contact, stop and reassess with imaging rather than probing further blindly.
- Process the aspirate as soon as possible after collection; delays measurably reduce viable progenitor cell yield.
- When combining BMAC with microfracture, apply the concentrate only after chondral preparation is complete and the subchondral bed is reasonably hemostatic — brisk bleeding from the microfracture holes dilutes the concentrate sitting on the defect surface.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- A single large-volume aspirate dilutes the marrow with peripheral blood and reduces progenitor cell yield — always use small-volume aspirates at multiple sites/depths instead.
- No single "best" preparation technique is yet established: a 2026 RCT comparing BMAC clot vs BMAC concentrate in grade 3–4 knee OA suggests preparation technique matters and remains an area of active protocol refinement.
- For knee OA, systematic reviews (Han et al., 2024) show BMAC produces clinically meaningful improvement versus baseline and is comparable to PRP in most head-to-head comparisons, without clear superiority over PRP despite higher cost and invasiveness — do not position BMAC as categorically superior to PRP for routine OA.
- Do not promise BMAC as disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy outside its established niche (precollapse osteonecrosis, cartilage-defect augmentation) — evidence supports symptomatic benefit, not guaranteed structural reversal.
- Cost/access reality: reimbursement pathways for BMAC in OA are often not established — counsel patients accordingly before harvest.
- **Neurovascular injury at the harvest site:** the greater sciatic notch and its contents (sciatic nerve, superior/inferior gluteal nerves and vessels) sit close to a too-lateral or too-inferior trajectory, and if the trocar loses contact with bone mid-procedure there is a described risk of straying into surrounding viscera or these neurovascular structures — stop and reassess with imaging rather than continuing blindly if bony purchase is lost.
- A starting entry point that is too lateral and superior on the crest can injure the superior cluneal nerves, producing a patch of numbness or dysesthesia over the buttock — counsel patients this is a recognized, usually self-limited, harvest-site risk.
- Post-harvest complications: local hematoma, and harvest-site pain that is often more prominent (and slower to settle) than the discomfort at the injection/target site — counsel patients this is a two-site procedure with two separate recovery timelines. Rare infection at the harvest site. Deep pelvic injury is exceedingly rare with careful technique but has been reported with poor technique or loss of bony purchase.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Harvest site:** keep the PSIS/AIC dressing dry for 24–48 hours; watch for an expanding hematoma or escalating pain at the harvest site, which should prompt reassessment for bleeding. Expect harvest-site soreness for several days — often more noticeable to the patient than the soreness at the injection/target site.
- **Standalone intra-articular injection for OA:** follow the same general injection-flare precautions as PRP — relative rest 24–48 hours, avoid NSAIDs 3–7 days if feasible, ice for the expected inflammatory flare, resume gentle ROM at 1–2 weeks.
- **Combined with a cartilage procedure (e.g., microfracture augmentation):** follow the primary surgical procedure's weight-bearing protocol, not a generic injection protocol — BMAC in this context is an adjunct, and rehabilitation timelines are dictated by the cartilage repair procedure: typically protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks, progressive return to impact by 3–4 months.
- **Manual labor/heavy lifting:** 1–2 weeks post-harvest/injection to allow the expected inflammatory phase to settle before heavy loading (when not otherwise dictated by a combined surgical procedure).
