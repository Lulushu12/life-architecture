## Indications

Knee OA (moderate, particularly as an adjunct or alternative to PRP in patients who have failed PRP, or combined with microfracture/cartilage procedures). Focal chondral defects as an adjunct to microfracture ("one-step" cartilage repair). Early-stage osteonecrosis of the femoral head — core decompression + BMAC augmentation has reasonable supportive evidence for precollapse Ficat-Arlet stage I–II disease. Nonunion augmentation via percutaneous BMAC injection at the fracture site (historically the Connolly technique, revisited with modern concentration technology). Reserve for failed-PRP or specific cartilage/AVN indications rather than routine first-line OA orthobiologic, given added cost and harvest morbidity versus PRP.

## Positioning & setup

Marrow is harvested from the iliac crest, most commonly the posterior superior iliac spine, occasionally the proximal tibia. Point-of-care BMAC concentration adds significant procedural time and equipment cost versus PRP — confirm access/reimbursement pathway with the patient before proceeding, as this often remains out-of-pocket.

## Steps

1. **Aspirate marrow using a multi-hole/side-port needle**, taking small-volume aspirates (2 mL) at multiple depths and sites rather than a single large-volume draw — large single draws dilute the marrow with peripheral blood and reduce progenitor cell yield.
2. **Anticoagulate the aspirate promptly (ACD-A)** to prevent clotting before processing.
3. **Concentrate by centrifugation** to yield the autologous concentrate of mesenchymal stromal cells, hematopoietic progenitors, growth factors, and platelets.
4. **Deliver to the target**: intra-articular injection for knee OA; combined with microfracture for focal chondral defects; combined with core decompression for precollapse osteonecrosis; percutaneous injection at the fracture site for nonunion augmentation.

## Pitfalls

- A single large-volume aspirate dilutes the marrow with peripheral blood and reduces progenitor cell yield — always use small-volume aspirates at multiple sites/depths instead.
- No single "best" preparation technique is yet established: a 2026 RCT comparing BMAC clot vs BMAC concentrate in grade 3–4 knee OA suggests preparation technique matters and remains an area of active protocol refinement.
- For knee OA, systematic reviews (Han et al., 2024) show BMAC produces clinically meaningful improvement versus baseline and is comparable to PRP in most head-to-head comparisons, without clear superiority over PRP despite higher cost and invasiveness — do not position BMAC as categorically superior to PRP for routine OA.
- Do not promise BMAC as disease-modifying or cartilage-regenerating therapy outside its established niche (precollapse osteonecrosis, cartilage-defect augmentation) — evidence supports symptomatic benefit, not guaranteed structural reversal.
- Cost/access reality: reimbursement pathways for BMAC in OA are often not established — counsel patients accordingly before harvest.

## Post-op protocol

- When BMAC is used as a standalone intra-articular injection for OA, follow the same general injection-flare precautions as PRP: relative rest 24–48 hours, avoid NSAIDs 3–7 days if feasible, ice for expected inflammatory flare, resume gentle ROM at 1–2 weeks.
- When BMAC is combined with a cartilage procedure (e.g., microfracture augmentation), **follow the primary surgical procedure's weight-bearing protocol, not a generic injection protocol** — BMAC in this context is an adjunct, and rehabilitation timelines are dictated by the cartilage repair procedure: typically protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks, progressive return to impact by 3–4 months.
- Manual labor/heavy lifting: 1–2 weeks post-harvest/injection to allow the expected inflammatory phase to settle before heavy loading (when not otherwise dictated by a combined surgical procedure).
