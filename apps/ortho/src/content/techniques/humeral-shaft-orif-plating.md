## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Segmental/comminuted (AO/OTA 12-C) fractures with unacceptable alignment where anatomic reduction and rotational control via direct visualization are paramount.
- Cases where associated radial nerve exploration is independently indicated (open fracture, secondary/post-manipulation palsy, or primary palsy without recovery by 3–4 months).
- Revision surgery for failed nailing or established nonunion.
- Bilateral humeral fractures, floating elbow, vascular injury requiring concurrent repair, and open fractures.
- Fractures unacceptable for functional bracing: >20° varus/valgus, >20° AP angulation, >3 cm shortening, or excessive rotational malalignment.

**Contraindications**:
- Simple closed diaphyseal fractures with acceptable alignment — functional bracing remains first-line (union 90–98% in Sarmiento's series).
- Severely compromised soft-tissue envelope over the planned approach (extensive degloving, burns, or contamination) — consider a bridging external fixator first or a different approach/implant.
- Segmental bone loss/comminution so extensive that plate fixation would require devitalizing stripping better managed by a bridging plate strategy or nailing with adequate locking, depending on the specific pattern.
- Poor bone quality precluding adequate screw purchase without extended plate length or locking technology — plan accordingly rather than abandoning plating outright.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP/lateral humerus radiographs including shoulder and elbow (exclude floating elbow); CT for complex segmental/comminuted patterns or intra-articular extension near the distal humerus to guide plate contouring and screw trajectory.
- **Implant templating**: select plate length to span at least 8–10 cortices (commonly 3 screws or more on each side of a simple fracture, more for comminuted patterns) with a broad, precontoured or contourable locking-compression plate; have both a standard and a longer/narrower plate available in case intraoperative findings (more comminution than expected) require a longer bridging construct.
- **Nerve exploration planning**: if radial nerve palsy is present preoperatively, plan the approach (posterior vs. anterolateral) specifically to allow direct nerve visualization, and document baseline EMG/NCS findings if obtained.
- **Approach selection**: decide anterolateral (Henry-type) versus posterior (triceps-splitting) preoperatively based on fracture location (proximal/middle-third vs. distal-third) and whether nerve exploration is planned.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy (mini C-arm), vessel loops/nerve retractors for radial nerve isolation, bone reduction clamps, and — for comminuted patterns — a full set of interfragmentary and bridging plating options.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, often combined with a regional block for postoperative analgesia (used cautiously if precise postoperative nerve exam is needed).
- **Anterolateral (Henry) approach position**: supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand/arm table, shoulder slightly abducted, allowing access along the anterolateral arm from the deltoid insertion to the antecubital fossa.
- **Posterior (triceps-splitting) approach position**: lateral decubitus or prone with the arm draped over a padded bolster/arm holder, elbow flexed, exposing the full posterior humerus.
- **Table setup**: radiolucent table/arm board mandatory for intraoperative fluoroscopy along the full shaft length; position the C-arm before draping to confirm unobstructed AP/lateral views.
- **Tourniquet**: generally not used given the proximal extent of humeral shaft exposure; meticulous hemostasis and use of bipolar cautery substitute.
- **Draping**: circumferential drape from shoulder to elbow (anterolateral) or scapula to olecranon (posterior), keeping the limb free for intraoperative length/rotation assessment.
- **Landmarks**: mark the deltoid insertion, biceps/brachialis interval (anterolateral) or the triceps midline/olecranon (posterior), and the fracture level as localized on preoperative imaging.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Select the approach by fracture location.** Anterolateral (Henry) for proximal/middle-third fractures; posterior (triceps-splitting) for distal-third fractures or whenever direct radial nerve exploration is independently indicated.
2. **Anterolateral (Henry) exposure.** Incise along the lateral border of the biceps, develop the interval between biceps/brachialis (medial) and brachioradialis/triceps (lateral) proximally, transitioning distally to the interval between brachialis and brachioradialis. Identify the radial nerve as it pierces the lateral intermuscular septum roughly at the junction of the proximal two-thirds and distal one-third of the humerus and follow it distally as it courses anterior between brachialis and brachioradialis — isolate it with a vessel loop and protect it throughout the remainder of the case.
3. **Posterior (triceps-splitting) exposure.** Split the triceps in the midline down to bone, retracting the medial and lateral heads; identify the radial nerve in the spiral groove directly, typically running from posteromedial to posterolateral roughly at the junction of the proximal two-thirds/distal one-third of the shaft — isolate with a vessel loop and mobilize gently for the length needed to protect it during reduction and plate passage.
4. **Expose the fracture.** Elevate the periosteum minimally at the fracture edges only, preserving soft-tissue attachments to comminuted fragments to protect their blood supply.
5. **Isolate and protect the radial nerve throughout.** Keep the nerve under direct vision (or gently retracted with a vessel loop) during every subsequent reduction and plating step — this is the single most important maneuver for avoiding iatrogenic palsy, which the literature reports at rates as high as ~10% with plating even in modern series.
6. **Reduce the fracture.** For simple (12-A) patterns, achieve direct anatomic reduction with pointed reduction clamps and provisional K-wires; for wedge (12-B) or comminuted (12-C) patterns, use indirect reduction techniques (femoral distractor, clamps on the plate, or manual traction) to restore length, alignment, and rotation without forcing anatomic reduction of every fragment.
7. **Apply the plate.** Contour a broad, precontoured or hand-contoured locking-compression plate along the anterolateral (Henry approach) or posterior (triceps-splitting approach) surface of the humerus, spanning the fracture with adequate bicortical purchase on each main fragment (aim for at least 3, often 4+, screws per side depending on bone quality and comminution).
8. **Achieve compression or bridging fixation as appropriate.** For simple transverse/short-oblique patterns, use a dynamic compression technique through the plate; for comminuted/segmental patterns, use bridge-plating technique with screws only in well-vascularized proximal/distal fragments, deliberately leaving comminuted intermediate fragments unstripped.
9. **Confirm reduction, length, rotation, and hardware position fluoroscopically.** Obtain AP and lateral fluoroscopic images along the full plate length, and cross-check rotation clinically against the contralateral limb.
10. **Manage radial nerve palsy per preoperative status.** If a preoperative primary palsy was present, confirm nerve continuity visually and document intraoperative findings; if a laceration or entrapment is found, repair or free the nerve at this time. For open fractures, formally explore and document nerve status at the time of irrigation/debridement regardless of preoperative exam findings.
11. **Irrigate and achieve hemostasis.** Copious irrigation of the wound, particularly around the plate and nerve, and meticulous hemostasis before closure.
12. **Layered closure.** Repair the muscular interval/triceps split, close the fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin; ensure the radial nerve is not left directly abutting hardware without adequate soft-tissue coverage at closure.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Isolate the radial nerve with a vessel loop as early as it is encountered and keep it in continuous view for the remainder of the case — this single habit is the strongest defense against the iatrogenic palsy rates reported with plating (up to ~10% in contemporary multicenter data).
- For comminuted (12-C) fractures, resist the urge to anatomically reduce every fragment; a bridge-plating strategy that restores overall length, alignment, and rotation while preserving fragment vascularity heals more reliably than an over-instrumented, devascularized anatomic reduction.
- Use a femoral distractor or a plate-assisted "push-pull" reduction technique for difficult comminuted reductions rather than aggressive clamp force on osteoporotic or comminuted bone, which risks further fragmentation.
- Always cross-check rotational alignment clinically (comparing forearm/wrist rotation with the contralateral limb, or using a preoperatively marked rotational landmark) — fluoroscopic AP/lateral views alone will not reliably reveal a rotational malreduction.
- When the radial nerve must cross under or adjacent to the plate, ensure a smooth, well-contoured plate surface at that segment and confirm adequate soft-tissue interposition/coverage at closure to avoid chronic nerve irritation against hardware.
- In distal-third fractures approached posteriorly, identify the nerve proximally in the spiral groove first and trace it distally under direct vision rather than starting dissection distally and working proximally into the danger zone blind.
- For open fractures, always formally explore and document radial nerve status at the time of debridement even if the preoperative exam is normal — a normal preoperative exam does not exclude a partial injury that later declares itself.
- Have both compression and bridging plating strategies (and the hardware to execute either) available before starting — fracture comminution is frequently underestimated on preoperative imaging alone.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Iatrogenic radial nerve injury is the key modifiable risk: the HUMMER multicenter cohort (2023) found plating had a 10.5% rate of postoperative (iatrogenic) temporary radial nerve palsy versus 0.6% with nailing. A 2025 systematic review found high iatrogenic palsy rates after plating regardless of surgical approach chosen — meticulous nerve identification and handling technique, not approach selection alone, is the key modifiable factor.
- Some 2025 propensity-matched data show higher nonunion risk with plating versus nailing after matching (and higher radial nerve palsy risk with plating, RR = 2.37), challenging older teaching that plating is uniformly more reliable for union.
- For open fractures with radial nerve palsy, explore at the time of irrigation and debridement — direct visualization is safe and avoids missing a laceration.
- For secondary palsy (new palsy after closed reduction or cast/splint manipulation), explore promptly — high likelihood of nerve entrapment in the fracture site or between fragments.
- For primary palsy without recovery by 3–4 months, obtain EMG/NCS and consider exploration; if fixation is otherwise indicated (nonunion, malalignment), combine nerve exploration with the fixation procedure.
- Over-stripping periosteum around comminuted fragments in pursuit of anatomic reduction devitalizes bone and increases nonunion risk — favor bridge plating and biological fixation for these patterns.
- Nerve entrapment directly against hardware at closure, from inadequate soft-tissue coverage, can cause chronic irritation/neuropathic pain even without an acute laceration.
- Overall humeral shaft nonunion (across fixation types) runs roughly 2–10%, higher in comminuted/segmental patterns, smokers, and NSAID users — a relevant counseling point regardless of which implant is chosen.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Elbow, wrist, hand motion from day 0; shoulder pendulum exercises early.
- Active shoulder/elbow ROM progressed as fracture stability allows.
- Resisted strengthening deferred until union is confirmed (radiographic union expected by 12–16 wk for ORIF/nail).
- Return to heavy lifting/contact sport typically 4–6 months.
