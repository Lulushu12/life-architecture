## Indications

Segmental/comminuted (AO/OTA 12-C) fractures with unacceptable alignment, cases where associated radial nerve exploration is needed, revision of failed nailing or nonunion, and any scenario where precise anatomic reduction and rotational control are paramount.

## Positioning & setup

Anterolateral (Henry-type) approach for proximal/middle-third fractures; posterior (triceps-splitting) approach for distal-third fractures and whenever direct radial nerve exploration is planned. Key structures at risk: the radial nerve as it crosses from the posterior to anterior compartment distally (anterolateral approach) or within the spiral groove (posterior approach — direct identification is recommended); the musculocutaneous nerve.

## Steps

1. Select the approach by fracture location: anterolateral (Henry) for proximal/middle-third fractures; posterior (triceps-splitting) for distal-third fractures or when nerve exploration is independently indicated.
2. Directly visualize and protect the radial nerve throughout the exposure.
3. Achieve anatomic reduction using compression or bridge plating technique, depending on the degree of comminution.
4. If radial nerve palsy is present preoperatively, identify, protect, and — if intraoperative findings suggest entrapment or laceration — explore the nerve at the time of fixation, regardless of whether the palsy is primary or secondary.

## Pitfalls

- Iatrogenic radial nerve injury is the key modifiable risk: the HUMMER multicenter cohort (2023) found plating had a 10.5% rate of postoperative (iatrogenic) temporary radial nerve palsy versus 0.6% with nailing. A 2025 systematic review found high iatrogenic palsy rates after plating regardless of surgical approach chosen — meticulous nerve identification and handling technique, not approach selection alone, is the key modifiable factor.
- Some 2025 propensity-matched data show higher nonunion risk with plating versus nailing after matching (and higher radial nerve palsy risk with plating, RR = 2.37), challenging older teaching that plating is uniformly more reliable for union.
- For open fractures with radial nerve palsy, explore at the time of irrigation and debridement — direct visualization is safe and avoids missing a laceration.
- For secondary palsy (new palsy after closed reduction or cast/splint manipulation), explore promptly — high likelihood of nerve entrapment in the fracture site or between fragments.
- For primary palsy without recovery by 3–4 months, obtain EMG/NCS and consider exploration; if fixation is otherwise indicated (nonunion, malalignment), combine nerve exploration with the fixation procedure.

## Post-op protocol

- Elbow, wrist, hand motion from day 0; shoulder pendulum exercises early.
- Active shoulder/elbow ROM progressed as fracture stability allows.
- Resisted strengthening deferred until union is confirmed (radiographic union expected by 12–16 wk for ORIF/nail).
- Return to heavy lifting/contact sport typically 4–6 months.
