## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- End-stage ankle osteoarthritis (≈70–80% post-traumatic — rotational ankle fracture, chronic instability, pilon fracture — rather than primary/idiopathic) that has failed non-operative management (activity modification, NSAIDs, lace-up/Arizona brace, weight loss, diagnostic/therapeutic intra-articular steroid injection) and is beyond the window for a joint-preserving supramalleolar osteotomy.
- **Arthrodesis** is appropriate across essentially all ages/demand levels as the reliable reference standard, particularly in younger, higher-demand patients, those with avascular necrosis or significant bone loss, and revision settings.
- **Total ankle replacement (TAR)** candidacy: older (>50–55 years), lower-demand patients with preserved/correctable coronal alignment (generally <10–15° deformity, with modern designs able to address more via concurrent osteotomy/ligament balancing), adequate bone stock, and no significant talar avascular necrosis, Charcot, or uncontrolled diabetic neuropathy.
- Choice between the two is individualized by age, activity demand, deformity magnitude, bone quality, and adjacent-joint (subtalar, midfoot) status through shared decision-making, not dictated by a single trial.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- **TAR-specific**: active or recent infection, significant talar avascular necrosis, Charcot neuroarthropathy or uncontrolled diabetic neuropathy, severe uncorrectable coronal deformity (>15–20° in most series, though thresholds vary by implant/surgeon experience), inadequate bone stock, high-demand/high-impact activity expectations (relative), and significant peripheral vascular disease compromising the thin anterior soft-tissue envelope.
- **Arthrodesis-specific**: relatively few absolute contraindications given its reliability across a broad range of hosts, though active infection should be addressed/staged first, and severe combined ankle + adjacent-joint (subtalar/midfoot) disease may warrant a more extensive fusion (TTC/pantalar) rather than isolated tibiotalar fusion.
- Uncorrected hindfoot deformity (PCFD/cavovarus) at the time of either procedure is a relative contraindication to proceeding without concurrent or staged hindfoot correction — an uncorrected hindfoot predisposes to edge-loading/early TAR failure and asymmetric loading after arthrodesis.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: weightbearing AP/lateral/mortise ankle radiographs; **hindfoot alignment (Saltzman) view** to quantify coronal hindfoot alignment; CT for detailed bone stock, deformity, and adjacent-joint assessment in complex/revision planning; assess the subtalar and midfoot joints on the same imaging, since combined ankle + hindfoot arthritis changes the surgical plan (may favor arthrodesis, or an extended TTC construct, over isolated TAR).
- **Deformity assessment**: quantify coronal (varus/valgus) and sagittal plane deformity precisely — this determines whether TAR is feasible with concurrent osteotomy/ligament balancing or whether the deformity magnitude favors arthrodesis; assess ligamentous stability (deltoid, lateral ligament complex) since instability requiring reconstruction changes both the TAR and arthrodesis plan.
- **Implant selection/templating (TAR)**: select a fixed- or mobile-bearing design per surgeon experience and deformity pattern; template component sizing on weightbearing radiographs or CT-based planning software where available; plan for concurrent procedures (supramalleolar osteotomy, ligament reconstruction, hindfoot correction) as a staged or same-day adjunct based on deformity severity.
- **Fixation planning (arthrodesis)**: plan screw configuration (typically 2–3 large cannulated screws in a triangulated or crossed pattern) for open compression arthrodesis; consider plate augmentation for poor bone quality or revision cases; have bone graft available for any bone loss/void encountered at joint preparation.
- **Equipment**: TAR-specific cutting guides/instrumentation matched to the selected implant system; arthroscopic arthrodesis set (for select patients with minimal deformity); fluoroscopy for both procedures; consider patient-specific instrumentation where available, particularly for deformity cases, as this can improve component positioning accuracy.
- **Multidisciplinary/host optimization**: address smoking cessation, glycemic control, and vascular status preoperatively for either procedure, since these materially affect both wound healing (particularly relevant given the thin anterior soft-tissue envelope in TAR) and fusion rates in arthrodesis.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic nerve block (± saphenous block) combined with general or spinal anesthesia/sedation is standard for both procedures; the block provides substantial early postoperative analgesic benefit given the bony work involved.
- **Positioning**: supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for standard anterior TAR approach and for anterior/lateral arthrodesis approaches; the leg is prepped free to the upper thigh to allow intraoperative manipulation and, for arthrodesis, positioning checks of the foot relative to the leg (neutral dorsiflexion, 5–10° of hindfoot valgus, and slight external rotation matching the contralateral limb) before final fixation.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet, exsanguinated before inflation; monitor tourniquet time closely in TAR given the precision bone cuts required and the frequently longer case duration when concurrent deformity correction is performed.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned for AP, lateral, and mortise views without repositioning the leg — used extensively throughout TAR for cutting guide/component positioning confirmation, and for arthrodesis to confirm joint preparation, provisional reduction, and final screw/plate position and trajectory.
- **Landmarks**: mark the anterior midline incision (between tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus tendons) for standard anterior TAR and anterior arthrodesis approaches; mark the lateral transfibular approach landmarks (fibula, peroneal tendons) if a lateral arthrodesis approach is planned; mark planned concurrent procedure incisions (supramalleolar osteotomy, hindfoot osteotomy) before prepping.
- **Draping**: free-drape the leg circumferentially to allow full ankle/hindfoot manipulation for alignment checks — critical in both procedures, but especially in TAR where dynamic ligament balancing and trial range-of-motion assessment occur intraoperatively.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Arthrodesis (open compression screw fixation)**
1. Approach anteriorly (between tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus) or via a lateral transfibular approach (with fibular osteotomy, useful when concurrent deformity correction or fibular pathology coexists), exposing the tibiotalar joint.
2. Remove residual cartilage and sclerotic subchondral bone from both the tibial plafond and talar dome using osteotomes, curettes, and/or a saw, preserving overall bone stock and joint contour while creating congruent, bleeding cancellous surfaces.
3. Perform multiple drill-hole perforations ("fish-scaling") of the subchondral surfaces to enhance vascularity and fusion potential if not already adequately exposing cancellous bone.
4. Reduce the ankle into the target position — neutral dorsiflexion, 5–10° of hindfoot valgus, and external rotation matched to the contralateral limb — and provisionally pin; confirm position clinically (comparing to the contralateral limb) and on fluoroscopy before committing to final fixation.
5. Place definitive fixation, typically 2–3 large cannulated screws in a triangulated or crossed configuration achieving interfragmentary compression across the joint; augment with a plate in cases of poor bone quality, revision surgery, or when screw fixation alone provides inadequate stability.
6. Bone graft any residual defects/voids, then close in layers; if a fibular osteotomy was used for exposure, it can be used as local autograft and/or fixed back in an anatomic or lateral-buttress position depending on surgeon preference.

**Total ankle replacement**
1. Approach through a standard anterior midline incision between tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus, carrying dissection down to the anterior ankle capsule while protecting the neurovascular bundle (deep peroneal nerve/anterior tibial vessels) medially and superficial peroneal nerve branches laterally.
2. Perform a capsulotomy and expose the tibiotalar joint, removing anterior tibial and talar neck osteophytes to improve visualization and allow accurate cutting-guide seating.
3. Assess and address coronal/sagittal deformity concurrently — via ligament releases/balancing for mild deformity, or concurrent supramalleolar/malleolar osteotomy for more significant malalignment — rather than relying on the implant alone to accommodate malalignment; uncorrected deformity is a leading cause of edge-loading and early failure.
4. Apply the tibial and talar cutting guides per the selected implant system, confirming alignment with fluoroscopy (and/or an external alignment jig referencing the tibial shaft axis) before making bone cuts; perform the talar resection with the foot held in a neutral position, a key technical determinant of accurate component orientation.
5. Make the tibial and talar bone cuts, trial the components, and assess range of motion, ligamentous balance, and coronal alignment through the trial range of motion — adjust soft-tissue balancing or bone cuts as needed before committing to final implants.
6. Impact or cement the final tibial and talar components (technique-dependent on implant design — press-fit/porous ingrowth vs. cemented), and insert the polyethylene bearing (fixed or mobile, per design), confirming full seating and appropriate soft-tissue tension throughout range of motion.
7. Address hindfoot deformity (PCFD/cavovarus) concurrently or as a planned staged procedure when present — an uncorrected hindfoot is a leading cause of edge-loading and early TAR failure and should not be deferred indefinitely after the index arthroplasty.
8. Close the capsule and skin meticulously given the thin anterior soft-tissue envelope, which is a recognized site of wound healing complications in TAR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Achieve full seating of both implant fixation surfaces on bone — surgical technique (not implant design alone) is the dominant determinant of minimizing early micromotion, loosening, and failure in TAR; incomplete seating from residual osteophyte or cartilage debris under the component is an avoidable technical error.
- Perform the talar resection with the foot held in a neutral, visually confirmed position — even small malpositioning of the talar component has an outsized effect on subsequent motion and contact pressure distribution.
- Correct coronal/sagittal deformity concurrently with osteotomy or ligament balancing rather than relying on the implant to accommodate malalignment — this is one of the most consistently emphasized technical principles in modern TAR technique and directly affects implant longevity.
- Address hindfoot deformity (PCFD/cavovarus) before or simultaneously with TAR rather than leaving it for a later date — deferred hindfoot correction after an uncorrected TAR risks edge-loading damage to the polyethylene/components that may not be fully reversible even after subsequent hindfoot surgery.
- In arthrodesis, take the time to accurately reproduce the contralateral limb's rotational alignment before final fixation — malrotation (particularly excessive external rotation) is a subtle but consequential technical error that affects gait and shoe wear long-term.
- Preserve bone stock during arthrodesis joint preparation — over-aggressive resection to "guarantee" bleeding bone can shorten the limb and complicate future revision options if fusion fails.
- Protect the anterior neurovascular bundle (deep peroneal nerve, anterior tibial vessels) meticulously throughout the anterior TAR approach — the thin anterior soft-tissue envelope and proximity of these structures make this a genuinely high-stakes exposure, not a routine one.
- Use intraoperative trial range of motion in TAR to directly assess soft-tissue balance and coronal alignment before committing to final components — this is the best real-time check available and should not be skipped even when preoperative planning suggested straightforward alignment.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Uncorrected coronal/sagittal deformity is a major driver of TAR edge-loading and early failure — correct it concurrently, not after the fact.
- Arthrodesis nonunion is **~5–10%** for isolated tibiotalar fusion in healthy hosts, rising substantially (**15–30%**) in smokers, avascular necrosis, Charcot/neuropathic patients, or TTC/pantalar constructs.
- Progressive adjacent-joint (subtalar, midfoot) arthritis over 10–20 years is near-universal after arthrodesis and is the principal long-term trade-off vs. TAR.
- TAR-specific complications: periprosthetic fracture, component subsidence/loosening, syndesmotic nonunion (designs requiring syndesmotic fixation), wound healing complications from the thin anterior soft-tissue envelope, deep infection (**~1–2%**, with higher revision-surgery morbidity than nonunion after failed fusion), and eventual revision (bearing exchange or conversion to fusion) in a meaningful minority by 10–15 years.
- Malrotation or malalignment at arthrodesis fixation is a subtle technical error — always confirm position against the contralateral limb clinically as well as on fluoroscopy before final screw placement.
- Incomplete seating of TAR components on bone (residual osteophyte/debris) predisposes to early micromotion and loosening independent of implant design quality.
- The **TARVA trial** (Goldberg et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2022; HTA report 2023) — a UK multicenter RCT of ~303 patients aged 50–85 randomized to TAR vs. arthrodesis — found both groups improved substantially and similarly on the MOXFQ at 3 years, but with different complication and reoperation profiles between arms; the trial is explicitly described as inconclusive regarding superiority of either procedure, so neither option should be presented to patients as categorically better.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Arthrodesis**: 2 weeks splint NWB; 6 weeks NWB in cast (non-Charcot) — for Charcot/neuropathic patients extend NWB to 8–12+ weeks given impaired bone healing biology; serial radiographs (± CT if union uncertain) before progressing; gradual weightbearing progression over 4–6 weeks once union is confirmed; brace/orthotic support and PT for 3–6 months; return to sedentary work ~2–3 months, physical work/sport typically 6–9 months and only after solid union.

**Total ankle replacement**: protocol varies by implant/approach, but a common pathway is splint NWB 2 weeks, then transition to a CAM boot with progressive weightbearing beginning around 2–4 weeks (faster than arthrodesis since press-fit/cemented fixation does not require bony union across a joint); early ROM exercises started once the wound is stable; formal PT emphasizing ankle ROM, gait training, and proprioception from 4–6 weeks; return to low-impact activity 3–4 months; avoid high-impact/running activity indefinitely per most surgeons given implant longevity concerns. Modern designs report ~90% implant survivorship at 10 years in registries and selected series.
