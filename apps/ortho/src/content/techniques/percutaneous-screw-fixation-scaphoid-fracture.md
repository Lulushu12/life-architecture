## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Displaced (>1 mm) or unstable (vertical-oblique/Russe III, comminuted) waist fractures.
- Proximal-pole fractures (retrograde vascular supply gives these the highest AVN/nonunion risk of any location, favoring early fixation even with minimal displacement).
- Undisplaced waist fractures in athletes/manual laborers wanting earlier return to function, or after informed discussion of the SWIFFT trial trade-offs (faster union and earlier return to work/sport with surgery, but a higher complication rate than cast treatment — implant-related issues, CRPS, scar sensitivity — with no clinically important PRWE difference at 52 weeks or 5 years for undisplaced bicortical waist fractures).

**Contraindications**
- **Absolute**: active infection at the planned surgical site; fracture pattern requiring open reduction because displacement/comminution cannot be adequately controlled closed (see below).
- **Relative**: significant comminution or displacement needing direct visualization for anatomic reduction — convert to open technique rather than forcing percutaneous fixation; associated perilunate dislocation or other carpal ligament injury requiring open exposure; humpback deformity requiring structural wedge grafting (needs open access); inadequate bone stock for screw purchase (e.g., very small proximal-pole fragment) — consider fragment-specific or headless mini-screw systems and lower threshold for open approach; patient factors precluding CT-based preoperative planning of fracture geometry.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: dedicated scaphoid series (PA, lateral, PA in ulnar deviation, pronated/semi-supinated obliques). CT with images oriented along the true long axis of the scaphoid is recommended before any percutaneous procedure — conventional radiographs do not adequately demonstrate the complete fracture configuration, and CT clarifies displacement, comminution, and fracture obliquity that changes screw trajectory planning.
- **Implant selection/sizing**: headless compression screw — historically the Herbert screw, now commonly a variable/differential-pitch design (Acutrak, OmniFix-type, or equivalent). Template screw length off the CT/fluoroscopic scaphoid length, then select a screw **2–4 mm shorter than the measured length** with appropriate thread engagement — an overly long screw can impinge on the scaphotrapezial joint distally or the radioscaphoid joint proximally and cause iatrogenic arthrosis.
- **Approach decision**: volar-percutaneous for waist/distal-pole fractures; dorsal-percutaneous for proximal-pole fractures, since a true central-axis trajectory cannot be achieved from a volar starting point for proximal fractures.
- **Equipment**: mini C-arm fluoroscopy (essential — the entire procedure is fluoroscopically guided), cannulated headless compression screw set with guidewires, small stab-incision instrument set, positioning towels/bump for wrist flexion (dorsal approach), tourniquet.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Regional block (wrist or axillary) or general anesthesia; supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, tourniquet inflated after exsanguination. The mini C-arm is positioned to allow true orthogonal AP and lateral imaging of the scaphoid throughout screw placement — this is the technical foundation of the entire procedure and should be verified before draping is complete. For the **volar approach**, the wrist rests in slight extension over a bump to bring the volar scaphoid tubercle into view. For the **dorsal approach** (proximal-pole fractures), the wrist is positioned in pronation and flexed 45° or more over a folded towel bump until the proximal and distal poles of the scaphoid align end-on — this brings the scaphoid's true long axis perpendicular to the fluoroscopy beam and is essential for central guidewire placement. Mark the entry landmarks before incision: volarly, over the scaphoid tubercle at the FCR/thenar crease junction; dorsally, over the scapholunate interval just distal to Lister's tubercle.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Select approach by fracture location** — volar-percutaneous for waist/distal fractures, dorsal-percutaneous for proximal-pole fractures.

2. **Volar approach.**
   a. Make a small stab incision at the scaphotrapezial joint level, just radial to the FCR tendon.
   b. Bluntly spread down to bone with a hemostat, protecting the FCR tendon and the superficial palmar branch of the radial artery, both of which lie in direct proximity to the intended guidewire path — cadaver studies report FCR tendon penetration by the guidewire in roughly a quarter of specimens and direct adjacency in a similar proportion, so blunt dissection and a protective cannula/soft-tissue sleeve before wire passage are important.
   c. Insert a guidewire starting at the scaphoid tubercle, aiming proximally and slightly dorsally-ulnarly to run along the true central axis of the scaphoid, confirmed on orthogonal AP and lateral fluoroscopy — a true central axis is difficult to achieve purely from a volar start for proximal fractures, reinforcing the dorsal approach preference in that location.
   d. Do **not** change wrist position after the guidewire is placed and before it is removed — repositioning the wrist with the wire in situ risks wire bending or breakage.

3. **Dorsal approach (proximal-pole fractures).**
   a. With the wrist flexed 45°+ over the towel bump (pronated), identify the central axis of the proximal pole on the AP fluoroscopic image — the entry point sits at the proximal pole immediately adjacent to the scapholunate ligament insertion, at the center of the visualized proximal articular surface.
   b. Make a small (~3 mm) stab incision over this point, typically overlying the scapholunate joint.
   c. A large-bore needle (e.g., 16-gauge) can be used first to establish and hold the true starting point/trajectory before passing the guidewire, particularly in a small or sclerotic proximal fragment.
   d. Advance the guidewire from dorsal to volar along the axis of the first metacarpal shaft with the thumb held in radial abduction, keeping the wrist flexed throughout, and confirm the trajectory is centered and perpendicular to the fracture plane on at least two fluoroscopic planes.

4. **Confirm guidewire position** centrally on both AP and lateral views before proceeding — eccentric wire placement on either single view is the most common technical error in this procedure and should prompt withdrawal and redirection rather than proceeding to screw insertion.

5. **Measure for screw length** over the guidewire using the cannulated depth gauge, then select a screw 2–4 mm shorter than the measured length to avoid articular impingement.

6. **Ream/drill over the guidewire** per the implant system's protocol (many differential-pitch designs use a tapered reamer rather than a drill), maintaining guidewire position and wrist position throughout.

7. **Insert the headless compression screw** over the guidewire under fluoroscopic guidance, seating it just below the articular surface at both ends, and confirm compression across the fracture site.

8. **Final fluoroscopic check** in AP, lateral, and scaphoid (ulnar-deviation) views to confirm central screw position, adequate compression, and that no threads or screw tip are proud of either articular surface.

9. **Convert to open reduction** if at any point in steps 1–4 the fracture cannot be adequately reduced or the trajectory cannot be centralized closed (displaced/comminuted fractures, humpback deformity, or associated perilunate injury) — do not persist percutaneously once fragment alignment cannot be controlled.

10. **Closure.** Close the small stab incision(s) with a single suture or adhesive strip, apply a thumb-spica splint or removable brace, and obtain final radiographs.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Obtain a preoperative CT along the true scaphoid axis whenever a percutaneous procedure is planned — plain radiographs routinely understate comminution and fracture obliquity that change trajectory planning.
- For proximal-pole fractures, commit to the dorsal approach with the wrist flexed over a bump rather than attempting a volar trajectory — a true central-axis screw is very difficult to achieve volarly in this location, and eccentric placement is the dominant driver of fixation failure/nonunion.
- Use a 16-gauge needle to "find" and hold the true central starting point on fluoroscopy before committing the guidewire, especially in small, sclerotic, or osteopenic proximal fragments.
- Protect the FCR tendon and superficial palmar branch of the radial artery with blunt spreading and a soft-tissue protector/cannula before wire passage in the volar approach — both structures lie close enough to the entry point to be penetrated inadvertently.
- Never reposition the wrist while the guidewire is in place and before screw insertion — doing so risks wire bending or breakage inside the bone.
- Confirm central position on **both** AP and lateral fluoroscopy before drilling/reaming — a wire that looks central on one view only is a common trap.
- Undersize the screw length by 2–4 mm relative to the measured length to avoid distal (scaphotrapezial) or proximal (radioscaphoid) articular impingement, which can seed secondary osteoarthritis even after solid union.
- Keep a low threshold to convert to a mini-open or fully open approach if two or three attempts at closed guidewire placement fail to achieve a centralized trajectory — repeated wire passes damage bone stock and waste operative time without improving the outcome.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Eccentric screw placement is the most common technical error and increases nonunion risk — the screw must be centrally placed along the true longitudinal axis on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy, not just one view.
- For proximal-pole fractures, a volar approach gives inferior central purchase compared to a dorsal approach with the wrist flexed — approach selection is not interchangeable for this fracture location.
- Screw prominence/malposition is a recognized hardware complication, and is especially technically demanding to avoid in proximal-pole fixation where central purchase is harder to achieve.
- A screw selected too long can impinge on the scaphotrapezial joint distally (or the radioscaphoid articulation proximally), causing iatrogenic articular damage and secondary osteoarthritis in both bones — undersize by 2–4 mm relative to the measured length.
- The FCR tendon and superficial palmar branch of the radial artery are at risk during the volar approach; cadaver data report guidewire penetration or direct adjacency to the FCR tendon in roughly half of specimens combined — blunt protected dissection reduces this risk.
- Changing wrist position with the guidewire in situ risks wire bending or breakage, which can necessitate open retrieval.
- Open reduction should be reserved for displaced/comminuted fractures needing direct visualization or humpback correction — do not force a percutaneous approach where fragment alignment cannot be adequately controlled closed.

## Postoperative Protocol

Short-term splint/removable brace for comfort 1–2 weeks (the screw provides enough stability for early motion), active range of motion (AROM) starting within the first 1–2 weeks, progressive strengthening from 6 weeks, return to sport/heavy manual work at 8–12 weeks pending radiographic union (union is typically evident by 6–8 weeks with rigid fixation). Return to contact/collision sport or loaded wrist-extension sport (gymnastics, football, climbing, racquet sports) is generally not before 3–4 months and should be confirmed with CT if there is any doubt about union.
