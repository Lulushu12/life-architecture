## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Elective hip and knee arthroplasty, and the trauma analogue applied to hip fracture surgery.
- Goal: standardize a bundle of preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative measures that reduce length of stay, opioid consumption, and complications without increasing readmissions.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications to specific ERAS elements (the pathway as a whole is not absolutely contraindicated in any candidate for the underlying surgery, but individual components should be modified):**
- **Spinal/regional anesthesia** component: contraindicated by coagulopathy, therapeutic anticoagulation not yet within a safe neuraxial window, spinal deformity/prior instrumentation precluding safe access, patient refusal, or hemodynamic instability — general anesthesia is substituted without abandoning the rest of the pathway.
- **Carbohydrate loading**: relatively contraindicated in diabetic patients with poor glycemic control, gastroparesis, or any condition raising aspiration risk — omit or modify per anesthesia guidance.
- **Same-day mobilization**: deferred (not abandoned) in patients with hemodynamic instability, uncontrolled pain despite multimodal analgesia, or a surgical complication (e.g., intraoperative fracture, excessive blood loss) that specifically warrants a period of bed rest.
- **Early discharge**: inappropriate for patients without adequate home support, significant frailty/orthogeriatric needs, or medical instability — ERAS pathways should flex the discharge timeline to the patient rather than force a fixed early discharge target.
- **Fascia iliaca/PENG blocks in trauma**: standard contraindications to peripheral nerve blockade apply (local infection at the injection site, patient refusal, anticoagulation exceeding block-specific safety thresholds).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Patient selection and education:** identify candidates for the elective arthroplasty pathway at the surgical consultation, and begin expectation-setting (anticipated length of stay, mobilization timeline, discharge criteria) well before the day of surgery — this is itself an active ERAS intervention, not just courtesy.
- **Prehabilitation:** where time and pathology allow, arrange strength/mobility conditioning of the operative limb in the weeks before elective surgery; coordinate with physiotherapy for a structured program rather than generic advice to "stay active."
- **Medical optimization:** anemia, glycemic, and nutritional optimization should be addressed in the preoperative window (see the wound-closure/DAIR-prevention protocol for specific thresholds) since these directly affect both the surgical outcome and the patient's ability to participate in same-day mobilization.
- **Anesthesia plan:** confirm suitability for spinal/regional anesthesia at preoperative assessment, including a review of anticoagulation status against ASRA timing windows, so the anesthesia team is not deciding this on the morning of surgery.
- **Fasting/carbohydrate-loading orders:** write specific preoperative fasting instructions (minimizing unnecessary prolonged NPO time) and carbohydrate-loading orders (up to 2 hours before surgery in appropriate patients) as part of the standard preoperative order set, not as an individualized afterthought.
- **Multimodal analgesia and TXA orders:** pre-order scheduled acetaminophen/NSAID, plan the peripheral nerve block or periarticular injection technique, and confirm TXA dosing/route as standard components of the case plan (see the TXA administration protocol for specifics).
- **Discharge planning started preoperatively:** identify home support, mobility aids, and any anticipated need for short-stay rehabilitation at the preoperative visit rather than waiting until postoperative day 1 — this is one of the most common points where ERAS pathways lose their time advantage if left too late.
- **Trauma-specific planning:** for hip fracture, engage orthogeriatric co-management at admission (not at the time of surgical booking), and target surgery within 24–36 hours from admission, reserving delay only for genuinely reversible acute medical issues (e.g., correctable coagulopathy, decompensated heart failure).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Preferred anesthesia:** spinal/regional anesthesia over general anesthesia where suitable, as a core ERAS element — reduces postoperative nausea/vomiting, may support earlier mobilization, and avoids airway-management recovery time, though large trials in hip fracture surgery specifically have found no significant difference in 60-day mortality or functional recovery between spinal and general anesthesia, so this remains an individualized choice rather than a mandate.
- **Premedication:** avoid long-acting sedative premedication, which delays early postoperative mobilization and clouds the delirium assessment picture, particularly in older patients.
- **Regional/peripheral block technique:** build the block or periarticular local infiltration into the anesthesia/surgical time-out plan explicitly — adductor canal block (± IPACK) for TKA, fascia iliaca or PENG block for hip fracture — administered per standard technique for the block chosen, timed so onset aligns with case start or early postoperative period as appropriate.
- **Positioning:** standard positioning for the arthroplasty or fracture-fixation approach being performed; no ERAS-specific positioning requirement, though minimizing unnecessary padding/setup time contributes to the broader efficiency goals of the pathway.
- **Temperature and fluid management setup:** confirm forced-air warming and fluid-warming equipment are set up before draping to support intraoperative normothermia, and plan for goal-directed (rather than liberal) fluid therapy from the start of the case rather than defaulting to a fixed maintenance rate.
- **Drains/catheters:** decide preoperatively to omit routine surgical drains and urinary catheters where not specifically indicated, rather than placing them by habit and removing them later.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Core elements (elective arthroplasty):**

1. Deliver preoperative patient education and expectation-setting; initiate prehabilitation where feasible.
2. Minimize preoperative fasting; administer carbohydrate loading up to 2 hours before surgery in appropriate patients.
3. Avoid long-acting sedative premedication on the day of surgery.
4. Proceed with spinal/regional anesthesia over general anesthesia where suitable, per the preoperative anesthesia plan.
5. Perform the surgical approach with attention to tissue-sparing technique and efficient operative time, since operative duration itself affects the patient's ability to mobilize same day.
6. Build multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia into the case — periarticular local infiltration and/or peripheral nerve block, performed at the appropriate point in the sequence (block preoperatively; periarticular infiltration intraoperatively before closure).
7. Administer TXA as standard blood-conservation practice, per the case's chosen route/timing (see TXA administration protocol).
8. Omit routine surgical drains and urinary catheters where not specifically indicated.
9. Maintain intraoperative normothermia (active warming from case start) and goal-directed (rather than liberal) intravenous fluid therapy throughout.
10. Close in a manner consistent with early mobilization goals (see wound-closure protocol) and complete dressing application.
11. In the immediate postoperative period, mobilize the patient the **same calendar day as surgery** wherever feasible, with weight-bearing as tolerated for cemented/standard constructs.
12. Resume oral intake early and discontinue IV fluids/catheters as soon as clinically appropriate, rather than on a fixed postoperative schedule.
13. Base the discharge decision on objective functional criteria (safe transfers, adequate pain control on oral medication, ability to manage stairs if needed at home) rather than a fixed postoperative day.

**Trauma analogue (hip fracture):**

1. Expedite time to surgery to **<24–36 hours** from admission, reserving delay only for genuinely reversible acute medical issues.
2. Initiate orthogeriatric co-management from the point of admission, not from the time of surgical booking.
3. Avoid unnecessary preoperative fasting delays that push surgery later without medical justification.
4. Administer standardized multimodal analgesia — notably fascia iliaca or PENG blocks — promptly on admission to reduce preoperative opioid need and improve comfort during the wait for surgery.
5. Administer TXA intraoperatively per protocol.
6. Mobilize the patient immediately postoperatively, generally within the same calendar day or the first postoperative day, integrated with orthogeriatric medical optimization running in parallel.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- **Front-load discharge planning to the preoperative visit** — identifying home support and mobility-aid needs before surgery, rather than on postoperative day 1, is one of the highest-yield single interventions for actually realizing an ERAS pathway's length-of-stay benefit rather than losing it to a delayed discharge-planning conversation.
- **Set a visible tourniquet/operative-efficiency mindset without sacrificing technique** — operative duration directly affects same-day mobilization feasibility (anesthesia washout, pain control, patient stamina), so efficient, well-rehearsed technique is itself an ERAS lever, not a separate concern from the pathway.
- **Sequence the peripheral nerve block to be well-established by the time of first mobilization attempt**, but be mindful that a dense motor block (e.g., traditional femoral block) can itself delay safe ambulation — this is why adductor canal block is generally preferred over femoral block for TKA and fascia iliaca/PENG over full femoral block for hip fracture.
- **Use dexamethasone as a dual-purpose adjunct** — its antiemetic effect supports early oral intake, and its modest analgesic-duration extension supports the same-day mobilization goal, at low incremental cost/risk in appropriate patients.
- **Treat "no routine drain/catheter" as a default, not an absolute** — document the specific reason when one is placed (e.g., anticipated high drainage, urinary retention risk in a patient with known BPH) so it is a deliberate exception rather than habit reasserting itself.
- **Coordinate physiotherapy timing to the anesthesia washout window**, particularly after spinal anesthesia — attempting mobilization before motor block has genuinely resolved risks a fall and undermines confidence in the pathway; conversely, waiting too long past resolution wastes the same-day mobilization opportunity.
- **In hip fracture patients, treat the analgesia block as a preoperative-holding-area intervention**, not just an intraoperative one — administering fascia iliaca/PENG block promptly on admission reduces the opioid burden accumulated during the wait for theatre availability, which itself affects postoperative delirium risk.
- **Audit length-of-stay and readmission data locally**, not just trust the aggregate literature — pathway fidelity (are all elements actually being delivered, or has the bundle eroded to just "early mobilization") is the most common reason a unit's real-world ERAS results diverge from the published outcome data.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- ERAS pathway implementation in elective hip/knee arthroplasty is consistently associated with shortened length of stay (commonly from a historical 4–5 days down to 1–2 days, with same-day/next-day discharge achievable in selected outpatient-eligible patients), reduced total opioid consumption, reduced postoperative nausea/vomiting, and earlier physiotherapy milestones — importantly, LOS reduction has not come at the cost of increased 30/90-day readmission in the bulk of the published cohort and registry literature.
- A 2018–2024 systematic review across 7,206 patients (2,148 ERAS vs 5,058 traditional care) reaffirmed significantly shortened LOS and reduced intraoperative blood loss with ERAS, with no significant adverse effect on readmission rates.
- Do not treat spinal/regional anesthesia as a mandated component: large contemporary randomized comparisons in hip fracture surgery specifically found no significant difference in 60-day mortality or functional recovery between spinal and general anesthesia — the choice should be individualized (anticoagulation status, spinal deformity, hemodynamic stability, patient preference) rather than mandated, even though regional retains secondary benefits (analgesia, nausea, delirium signal in some cohorts).
- In trauma, bundled programs built on expedited surgery + orthogeriatric co-management + standardized analgesia + TXA + immediate mobilization (formalized in UK national hip fracture audit "Best Practice Tariff"-style frameworks) are associated with reduced 30-day mortality and shorter length of stay compared with unstructured care — the effect is attributed to the bundle, not any single element in isolation, which means partial implementation should not be expected to deliver the full published benefit.
- **A common implementation failure mode is "cherry-picking" ERAS elements** (e.g., adopting TXA and early mobilization but not front-loading discharge planning or fixing fasting practices) — because the mortality/LOS benefit is attributed to the bundle rather than individual components, partial adoption commonly produces disappointing real-world results relative to the published literature.
- **Pushing same-day mobilization in a patient with inadequately controlled pain or hemodynamic instability** risks a fall or a bad early experience that undermines the patient's confidence in the rest of the pathway — mobilization criteria should be genuinely met, not simply scheduled.
- **Premature catheter/drain omission in a patient with a specific indication** (e.g., known urinary retention risk, anticipated high drainage) can convert an ERAS efficiency gain into a complication (urinary retention requiring in-and-out catheterization, or an undrained hematoma) — apply the "no routine" default with clinical judgment, not rigidly.

## Postoperative Protocol

ERAS discharge and rehabilitation follow the general phased perioperative pathway:

- **Phase 1 (Day 0–POD1):** same-day/POD1 mobilization with weight-bearing as tolerated for standard cemented fixation; early active/active-assisted ROM and VTE prophylaxis initiation.
- **Phase 2 (Week 1–2):** progressive gait training and suture/staple removal at 10–14 days.
- **Phase 3 (Week 2–6):** progressive resistance training, driving resumption at 4–6 weeks, and return to sedentary/desk work at 2–4 weeks.
- **Phase 4 (6 weeks–3 months):** return to manual labor around 3 months and low-impact recreational activity at 3 months.
- **Phase 5 (3–12 months):** individualized return to higher-impact activity at 6–12 months.

Discharge itself is based on objective functional criteria rather than a fixed postoperative day.
