## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Rockwood type IV (posterior displacement, clavicle buttonholed through/into trapezius), type V (CC distance increased >100–300% vs. contralateral, gross superior displacement with marked deltotrapezial stripping), and type VI (rare inferior/subcoracoid displacement) — all operative.
- Selected type III injuries in high-demand overhead athletes, heavy manual laborers, or patients wanting the fastest predictable return to loaded activity — shared decision-making, not a default.
- Chronic/symptomatic AC instability after failed nonoperative management of a high-grade injury.
- Acute reconstruction (within ~3 weeks of injury) is preferred over delayed surgery whenever surgery is indicated, because native CC ligament tissue is still repairable in the acute window.

**Contraindications**:
- Type I–II injuries and most type III injuries — treat nonoperatively (sling, early ROM); routine surgery here has not shown a consistent outcome advantage over nonoperative care in comparative literature.
- Active infection over the operative field.
- Uncontrolled medical comorbidity precluding elective reconstruction (posterior/type IV–VI with neurovascular compromise is the exception — that combination needs more urgent intervention).
- Poor bone quality/comminuted coracoid or distal clavicle that will not hold a tunnel or button — consider an alternative fixation strategy (hook plate, wider suspensory footprint) rather than forcing a standard tunnel technique.
- Patient unable/unwilling to comply with a multi-month brace and restricted-loading rehabilitation protocol — graft/construct failure is strongly linked to premature loading.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: bilateral Zanca views (10–15° cephalic tilt, reduced kV) for side-to-side CC distance comparison; axillary view to assess posterior displacement (type IV) and rule out a subcoracoid (type VI) variant; CT for comminuted distal clavicle or coracoid base fracture that changes fixation strategy; MRI selectively if concomitant rotator cuff or labral pathology is suspected clinically.
- **Graft/implant planning**: decide acute repair + suspensory augmentation (suture-button/cortical button device) versus free tendon graft reconstruction (semitendinosus or gracilis autograft, or allograft) before the day of surgery — chronic (>3 week) presentations should be planned as graft reconstructions since native ligament is not repairable.
- **Implant inventory**: cortical button/suspensory fixation system(s) in more than one size, free tendon graft (harvested or allograft ordered in advance), interference screws or additional suture anchors for distal clavicle/AC augmentation if combining CC and AC repair, and a hook plate as a fallback option for comminuted distal clavicle fragments.
- **Consent discussion**: counsel on loss-of-reduction risk (higher with suspensory devices in chronic reconstructions), the possible need for later hardware removal, residual cosmetic deformity even with a technically sound reduction, and the extended timeline to full sport/labor clearance (4–6 months).
- **Concomitant injury check**: confirm no coexisting distal clavicle fracture or SSSC double disruption pattern that changes the fixation plan.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically combined with an interscalene regional block for postoperative analgesia and intraoperative muscle relaxation (facilitates reduction).
- **Position**: modified beach-chair position with the head stabilized in a headrest, torso elevated 30–60°, and the operative shoulder brought to the edge of the table to allow full arm mobility and posterior access if extension of the incision is needed.
- **Table setup**: a padded arm-positioning device or assistant-held arm support is useful for applying upward/anterior translation during reduction; have a radiolucent table or table extension if fluoroscopy will be used to confirm reduction and tunnel position.
- **Draping**: drape free to allow full shoulder girdle access from the base of the neck to mid-humerus, and to allow manipulation of the arm through a full range of motion during reduction maneuvers.
- **Landmarks**: mark the clavicle, coracoid process (palpable ~2–3 cm inferomedial to the anterior acromial edge, deep to the deltopectoral groove), acromion, and the planned "sabre" incision line running from the posterolateral clavicle obliquely toward the coracoid/axillary fold before prepping.
- **Fluoroscopy**: a mini C-arm positioned for AP and axillary-equivalent views of the shoulder is helpful to confirm anatomic clavicle height and coracoid tunnel trajectory, particularly for suspensory button and tunnel-based techniques.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and superficial exposure.** Make a superior "sabre" (saber-shaped) incision centered over the distal clavicle and coracoid, curving gently to follow Langer's lines. Develop full-thickness flaps in the subcutaneous plane to expose the distal clavicle, AC joint, and deltotrapezial fascia without excessive undermining.
2. **Expose the deltotrapezial interval.** Incise the deltotrapezial fascia longitudinally (or in a cruciate fashion) over the distal clavicle, preserving enough tissue on each side for a robust closure/imbrication later — this fascial repair is a meaningful secondary stabilizer.
3. **Identify the coracoid.** Bluntly dissect through the deltopectoral interval and clavipectoral fascia to expose the base and undersurface of the coracoid process. Stay directly on bone; avoid wide subcoracoid dissection, which risks the musculocutaneous nerve and brachial plexus.
4. **Protect the suprascapular nerve.** The suprascapular nerve passes close to the posterosuperior/medial base of the coracoid (as close as ~2 mm in some anatomic studies, mean ~8 mm) en route to the suprascapular notch. Keep dissection and retractor placement directly on the coracoid's superior and undersurface, use a blunt right-angle or coracoid-specific guide to protect soft tissue during drilling, and avoid posteromedial retraction/plunging past the coracoid base.
5. **Débride and prepare the joint/ligament bed.** Excise interposed CC ligament remnants, hematoma, and any comminuted distal clavicle fragments that cannot be salvaged. If an associated distal clavicle fracture is comminuted or Neer type V, plan combined fixation (superior plate/CC augmentation) rather than an isolated ligament reconstruction.
6. **Establish the coracoid tunnel(s).** Place the guide pin at the base/coronal apex of the coracoid's undersurface, aiming to leave adequate bone stock medially and laterally and to avoid drilling too far posteriorly into the scapular body (risks a coracoid base fracture) or too far anteriorly (risks a fatigue fracture through a thin anterior rim). Confirm trajectory with fluoroscopy or direct palpation of the undersurface before committing to bicortical drilling.
7. **Establish the clavicular tunnel(s).** For anatomic double-tunnel reconstructions, place two clavicular tunnels reproducing the conoid (posteromedial, ~45 mm from the distal clavicle end) and trapezoid (anterolateral, ~25 mm from the distal end) footprints; for single-tunnel/suspensory-button techniques, center the tunnel over the coracoid base in both AP and lateral planes.
8. **Pass and tension the graft or suspensory device.** For free tendon graft reconstruction, pass the graft around or through the coracoid tunnel and up through the clavicular tunnel(s), reduce the clavicle to an anatomic or slightly overreduced position (a small amount of overcorrection is traditionally accepted to offset expected settling), and fix the graft under tension with interference screws, button fixation, or suture anchors. For suture-button/suspensory-device reconstruction, pass the device through the coracoid and clavicle tunnels and tension it with the clavicle held reduced.
9. **Reduce the clavicle and confirm reduction.** With an assistant applying gentle downward pressure on the acromion/upward pressure under the elbow (or direct manual reduction of the clavicle), confirm anatomic or slightly overreduced clavicle height on fluoroscopy before final fixation is locked.
10. **Augment the AC joint if indicated.** For type IV–VI injuries or when AC joint instability persists after CC reconstruction alone, add a formal AC ligament repair or a secondary suture/tape construct across the AC joint itself, and/or temporary AC joint pinning/fixation to protect the horizontal plane stability during early healing (remove any temporary AC fixation before full ROM progression per surgeon protocol).
11. **Repair the deltotrapezial fascia.** Imbricate and repair the fascial sleeve over the reconstruction — this restores a secondary stabilizing layer and improves the cosmetic contour.
12. **Layered closure.** Close the deep fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in standard fashion; a subcuticular closure with the sabre incision gives an acceptable cosmetic result along Langer's lines.
13. **Immobilize.** Apply a sling or abduction brace in the operating room before the patient leaves, matching the postoperative protocol below.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Palpate the coracoid before incising — its position varies more than expected, and centering the incision over a mispredicted coracoid location makes the deep exposure unnecessarily difficult.
- Keep all retraction and drilling directly on coracoid bone; do not "chase" soft tissue posteromedially — this is the single best way to protect the suprascapular nerve, which runs closest to the posterosuperior/medial coracoid base.
- A folded, radiolucent bump or the C-arm's fluoroscopic image (rather than eyeballing) is the most reliable way to confirm the guide pin is centered on the coracoid undersurface before committing to a bicortical tunnel.
- Slight overreduction of the clavicle (a few millimeters above anatomic height) is a widely used and reasonable buffer against the expected early postoperative settling of suspensory and graft constructs.
- When using a free tendon graft, pre-tension the graft on the back table before final fixation — tendon creep during intraoperative tensioning is real and can leave the construct loose if not accounted for.
- Repair the deltotrapezial fascia meticulously; it is an underappreciated secondary stabilizer and its failure contributes to later loss of reduction independent of the CC construct itself.
- For comminuted or very thin distal clavicle bone, consider a wider bone bridge/tunnel spacing or a combined plate-and-suspensory construct rather than relying on a single narrow tunnel, which is prone to fracture through the bone bridge.
- In chronic reconstructions, plan for autograft or allograft tendon from the outset — native ligament is not usable and attempting a primary repair in this setting reliably fails.
- Have a mini C-arm available and use it liberally intraoperatively — confirming coracoid tunnel trajectory and final clavicle height fluoroscopically catches malposition before it becomes a fixed problem.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The suprascapular nerve is at direct risk deep to the coracoid during tunnel drilling — anatomic studies show it can pass within ~2 mm of implanted material at the coracoid base, with an average clearance of only ~8 mm; a tunnel drilled too far posteromedially is the mechanism of injury.
- A coracoid tunnel placed too far anteriorly (too close to the coracoid tip) risks a fatigue fracture through the remaining anterior bone bridge; a tunnel placed too far posteriorly can exit into the scapular body/base rather than the coracoid itself.
- Chronic/delayed reconstructions carry inferior outcomes and higher loss-of-reduction rates than acute reconstruction — delayed high-grade AC injuries requiring surgery should not be deferred indefinitely.
- Loss of reduction is a recognized failure mode of suture-button/suspensory constructs, more common in chronic than acute reconstructions, and is associated with premature loading before graft/construct maturation.
- AC joint post-traumatic arthrosis can develop even after operative reconstruction, in addition to after any grade of the original injury; distal clavicle excision is the salvage option for refractory late symptoms.
- Coracoclavicular tunnel widening on suture-button repairs is a described radiographic finding and can be a marker of construct micromotion, though its clinical significance is still debated.
- Under-tensioning the deltotrapezial fascial repair leaves a persistent cosmetic bump and can contribute to recurrent superior clavicle prominence even with a technically adequate CC construct.
- Postoperative infection, though uncommon, is more consequential here given the proximity of hardware/graft to the coracoid and neurovascular structures — monitor wound closely in the first two postoperative weeks.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Sling/abduction brace 4–6 wk protecting the CC reconstruction.
- Passive/active-assisted ROM within brace limits from 2 wk.
- Full active ROM from 6 wk.
- Resisted strengthening from 8–12 wk.
- Return to contact/overhead sport at 4–6 months, once graft/construct maturation and full strength are confirmed.
