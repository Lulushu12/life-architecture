## Indications

Any displaced articular step-off/gap beyond the accepted threshold (≈2 mm for younger/active patients, more permissive in low-demand elderly), condylar widening, coronal instability on stress exam, or any Schatzker IV–VI/bicondylar pattern. High-energy bicondylar fractures with significant soft-tissue swelling, fracture blisters, or an open wound mandate staged management rather than acute definitive fixation.

## Positioning & setup

Occasionally a direct posterior approach is used for isolated posterior column shear fragments, with the patient prone. Otherwise approach and positioning follow the column(s) being addressed — anterolateral, posteromedial, or posterolateral.

## Steps

1. **Stage 1 (urgent, high-energy/soft-tissue-compromised patterns):** apply a spanning knee-bridging external fixator — pins in the femur and tibial diaphysis, crossing the injured joint, restoring length/alignment via ligamentotaxis.
2. **Serial soft-tissue assessment**, followed by definitive CT with 2–3 mm cuts and coronal/sagittal reconstruction obtained *after* the spanning ex-fix (not before) in staged cases — the ligamentotaxis reduction improves fragment visualization and reduces artifact from gross displacement, enabling accurate Luo column mapping.
3. **Defer definitive ORIF until the wrinkle sign returns** (typically 7–21 days). Acute definitive fixation is feasible in carefully selected bicondylar fractures with a favorable soft-tissue envelope (2024 dual-center comparative study) — the staging decision should be soft-tissue-driven and individualized, not reflexive for every bicondylar pattern.
4. **Select approach and implant per the Luo column analysis:** isolated lateral column injury → anterolateral approach with a lateral locking plate; medial column component → separate posteromedial approach and buttress plate (a single lateral plate cannot resist varus collapse of an unaddressed medial column); posterior column shear fragment → posteromedial or posterolateral approach (occasionally a direct posterior approach, prone) — missing this fragment is the classic technical error leading to loss of reduction.
5. **For posterolateral fragments**, choose among the anterolateral approach with capsular elevation, a posterolateral approach, or fibular head osteotomy/supra-fibular-head approaches. Recent (2024–2025) technique refinements include a modified partial fibular head osteotomy that preserves the biceps femoris attachment, and supra-fibular-head anatomic plating — both aimed at improving posterolateral fragment access while limiting peroneal nerve and lateral collateral ligament morbidity.
6. **Reduce and fix:** use large pointed reduction clamps and provisional K-wires before definitive plating. Restore the joint line first with subchondral raft screws just beneath the articular surface, then buttress the metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation.
7. **Bone graft/void-filler the metaphyseal defect** after elevation of the depressed articular fragment to prevent subsidence; synthetic calcium-phosphate/calcium-sulfate substitutes have largely replaced iliac crest autograft for straightforward voids, with autograft reserved for larger defects or compromised bone quality.
8. **Protect the peroneal nerve** with any lateral or posterolateral dissection, including during fibular head osteotomy.
9. **Consider a circular (Ilizarov/hexapod) frame** as an alternative to open plating in bicondylar fractures with severe medial comminution or compromised soft tissue, particularly in elderly patients with fragile skin or when infection risk is prohibitive.

## Pitfalls

- A posterior column fragment identified only on CT and missed on the Schatzker read is the single most common cause of failed lateral-only fixation of a "Schatzker V/VI" fracture.
- A single lateral plate cannot resist varus collapse of an unaddressed medial column — a medial column component always requires independent medial-side fixation.
- Historic wound complication rates with immediate ORIF of high-energy patterns reached 25–40% in the pre-staged-protocol era — this is the entire rationale for staging rather than fixing acutely through compromised soft tissue.
- Schatzker VI (bicondylar with metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation) carries the highest compartment syndrome risk of the group — maintain vigilance for compartment syndrome throughout staging and postoperatively.
- Arthrofibrosis is a major long-term morbidity from prolonged immobilization or delayed ROM initiation — protect against it with early passive/active-assisted motion once fixation is stable.

## Post-op protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** hinged knee brace locked or limited per fixation stability; strict non-weight-bearing; early passive/active-assisted ROM starting within the first 3–7 days if fixation is stable; ankle pumps, quad sets, straight-leg raises; cryotherapy/elevation for swelling control.
- **2–6 weeks:** progress ROM toward full flexion as tolerated and tissue allows; continue non-weight-bearing to touch-down weight-bearing depending on fixation construct and fracture stability; wound check and suture/staple removal at 2 weeks.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive partial weight-bearing beginning around 6–8 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing, advancing to full weight-bearing by ~10–12 weeks for most stable constructs (bicondylar comminuted patterns or elderly osteoporotic bone may need to wait closer to 12 weeks); closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, gait training.
- **3–6 months:** progressive strengthening, proprioception/balance work, return to low-impact activity; return to sedentary/light-duty work typically feasible around 8–12 weeks, return to manual labor generally 4–6 months depending on physical demands.
- **6–12+ months:** return to impact/pivoting sport individualized to fracture severity, articular congruity achieved, and residual laxity. A 2025 RCT found immediate weight-bearing after stable internal fixation yielded better clinical outcomes with similar radiographic outcomes compared with delayed weight-bearing in selected patients — an evolving area suggesting some current protocols may be more conservative than necessary in well-fixed constructs, though this should be individualized rather than applied blanket-fashion to comminuted or elderly osteoporotic patterns.
