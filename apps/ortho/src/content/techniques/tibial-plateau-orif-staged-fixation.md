## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Any displaced articular step-off/gap beyond the accepted threshold (≈2 mm for younger/active patients, more permissive in low-demand elderly).
- Condylar widening or coronal instability demonstrated on stress exam.
- Any Schatzker IV–VI/bicondylar pattern.
- High-energy bicondylar fractures with significant soft-tissue swelling, fracture blisters, or an open wound — these mandate staged management rather than acute definitive fixation.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Truly non-displaced or minimally displaced (<2–3 mm step-off/gap), stable-through-full-range fractures — classically Schatzker I or III in low-demand patients — are candidates for nonoperative management (hinged brace, protected weight-bearing) rather than ORIF.
- Active soft-tissue infection or cellulitis over the planned incision(s) — treat first; do not fix through infected tissue.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease compromising healing potential — consider a circular external fixator strategy over open plating.
- Medical instability precluding a prolonged anesthetic for definitive multi-incision fixation — temporize with a spanning external fixator and revisit timing once the patient stabilizes.
- Segmental bone loss or severe unreconstructable comminution not amenable to stable plating — consider a circular (Ilizarov/hexapod) frame as the definitive strategy.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** definitive CT (2–3 mm cuts, coronal/sagittal reconstruction) obtained after the spanning external fixator is applied (not before) in staged, high-energy cases — the ligamentotaxis reduction improves fragment visualization and enables accurate Luo column mapping.
- **Column mapping:** perform Luo three-column analysis on the axial CT at the level of the tibial spines before the case — this directly dictates which approach combination (anterolateral, posteromedial, posterolateral) and how many plates will be needed.
- **Implant templating:** lateral locking plate for the lateral column; posteromedial buttress plate for a medial column component; a separate plate/screw construct for a posterior column shear fragment. A single lateral plate cannot resist varus collapse of an unaddressed medial column — plan for independent medial fixation whenever the CT shows a medial column component.
- **Graft/void-filler planning:** synthetic calcium-phosphate/calcium-sulfate substitute for straightforward metaphyseal voids beneath elevated depressed fragments; reserve iliac crest autograft for larger defects or compromised (osteoporotic) bone.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** large fragment set, proximal tibia locking plate systems (lateral, medial, and posteromedial options), mini-fragment set, pointed reduction clamps, femoral distractor or spare external fixator components for provisional distraction, fluoroscopy, tourniquet.
- **Positioning logistics:** determine in advance whether a single supine position will suffice (anterolateral ± posteromedial) or whether the posterolateral/posterior column fragment will require repositioning (prone, or a "floppy lateral" position) — communicate this to the OR and anesthesia team.
- **Vascular review:** repeat neurovascular exam; obtain CT angiography for any pulse asymmetry or a Schatzker IV/knee dislocation-associated pattern.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or spinal/regional anesthesia (femoral + sciatic block is common) — regional block aids postoperative analgesia.
- Supine positioning for anterolateral and anteromedial approaches, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for neutral rotation; the knee should flex to roughly 90° for lateral exposure and fluoroscopic access.
- For the posteromedial approach, external rotation and slight flexion of the hip/knee with a bump under the ipsilateral buttock ("figure-4"-type positioning), the leg supported by an assistant or bolster to access the posteromedial corner.
- For isolated posterior column shear fragments requiring a direct posterior approach, prone positioning with careful bolstering and padding of the chest, pelvis, and genitalia; plan the logistics of any subsequent anterior-stage repositioning in advance.
- Tourniquet placed on the proximal thigh, typically inflated for exposure and reduction, and released before final closure in a prolonged case.
- Fluoroscopy positioned to allow AP, lateral, and both oblique views of the knee without extensive C-arm repositioning; test this before draping.
- Prep and drape the entire leg circumferentially, thigh to toes, to allow intraoperative knee flexion/extension and stress examination.
- Mark surface landmarks: fibular head, Gerdy's tubercle, tibial tubercle, joint line, planned incision(s) for each approach, and the course of the peroneal nerve posterior to the fibular neck.
- If staged, confirm external fixator removal, return of the wrinkle sign, and acceptable skin condition for the planned incisions before proceeding.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Stage 1 (urgent, high-energy/soft-tissue-compromised patterns):** apply a spanning knee-bridging external fixator — pins in the femur and tibial diaphysis, crossing the injured joint — restoring length and alignment via ligamentotaxis.
2. Perform serial soft-tissue assessment, then obtain definitive CT (2–3 mm cuts, coronal/sagittal reconstruction) after the spanning fixator is applied.
3. **Defer definitive ORIF until the wrinkle sign returns** (typically 7–21 days); acute definitive fixation is feasible in carefully selected bicondylar fractures with a favorable soft-tissue envelope, but the staging decision should be soft-tissue-driven and individualized, not reflexive for every bicondylar pattern.
4. Position the patient per the planned approach combination; prep and drape; remove the external fixator if not already done.
5. **Approach the lateral column:** an anterolateral incision curving over Gerdy's tubercle, splitting the iliotibial band, elevating the anterior compartment musculature subperiosteally off the proximal tibia, and creating a submeniscal arthrotomy (incising the coronary ligament) to directly visualize the joint surface.
6. **Approach the medial column** (if present on Luo mapping): a separate posteromedial incision along the posteromedial border of the tibia, working in the interval between the pes anserinus/medial gastrocnemius and the tibia, elevating periosteum minimally, and preserving a generous skin/soft-tissue bridge between the two incisions.
7. **Approach the posterior column** (if present): via a posteromedial extension (deep dissection between the medial head of gastrocnemius and the posteromedial tibia, protecting the popliteal neurovascular bundle) or a posterolateral approach (working lateral to biceps femoris, identifying and protecting the peroneal nerve, and using a modified partial fibular head osteotomy that preserves the biceps femoris attachment, or a supra-fibular-head plating technique, to access the fragment without disrupting the lateral collateral ligament complex).
8. **Reduce depressed/split articular fragments:** elevate the depressed fragment through a metaphyseal cortical window with a bone tamp under direct visualization through the submeniscal arthrotomy and fluoroscopic guidance; confirm joint congruity with a probe or finger through the arthrotomy.
9. Provisionally reduce with pointed reduction clamps and K-wires; restore condylar width by compressing split fragments and correcting any coronal-plane widening.
10. Confirm reduction fluoroscopically in AP, lateral, and oblique views — articular step-off/gap should be under 2 mm.
11. Bone graft/void-fill the metaphyseal defect beneath the elevated articular fragment to prevent later subsidence.
12. Apply the lateral locking plate along the lateral column: place subchondral raft screws just beneath the joint surface first to buttress the articular fragments, then metaphyseal/diaphyseal locking screws.
13. If a medial column component is present, apply an independent posteromedial buttress plate — do not rely on the lateral plate alone to resist varus collapse.
14. If a posterior column fragment is present, fix it with a separate posteromedial or posterolateral plate/screw construct directed to buttress the shear fragment.
15. Perform a final fluoroscopic check in all views, then stress the knee under fluoroscopy (varus/valgus at 0° and 20–30° flexion) to confirm construct stability.
16. Repair the submeniscal arthrotomy/coronary ligament, close the iliotibial band split, and close each incision in layers, preserving skin bridges (ideally ≥7 cm) between separate approaches.
17. Release the tourniquet, achieve hemostasis, and place a drain if extensive dissection was performed.
18. Apply a hinged knee brace and splint in extension or slight flexion per fixation stability.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the submeniscal arthrotomy — rather than a large capsulotomy — to directly visualize the joint surface while elevating depressed fragments; this gives real-time confirmation of reduction rather than relying on fluoroscopy alone.
- A femoral distractor or residual spanning ex-fix pins left in place until final fixation provide excellent ligamentotaxis-assisted reduction of the metaphyseal-diaphyseal component while attention is focused on the articular block.
- When both anterolateral and posteromedial approaches are needed, complete the posteromedial (medial buttress) fixation before final tightening of the lateral plate — this avoids fighting a partially reduced medial column while trying to lock the lateral construct.
- For the posterolateral fragment, a modified partial fibular head osteotomy preserving the biceps femoris attachment improves posterolateral access while limiting peroneal nerve traction and lateral collateral ligament morbidity compared with a full osteotomy.
- Identify and protect the common peroneal nerve early, at the posterior border of biceps femoris, before any posterolateral dissection or fibular head osteotomy.
- Pack the metaphyseal void generously — under-grafted voids beneath elevated articular fragments are a common cause of early subsidence and loss of reduction, especially in osteoporotic bone.
- Stress the knee under fluoroscopy after fixation is complete (not just static views) to confirm the construct actually resists the varus/valgus forces it will see in early rehabilitation.
- In complex three-column injuries, consider a two-incision (and occasionally two-team) approach with planned repositioning to reduce total operative time and tourniquet burden.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- A posterior column fragment identified only on CT and missed on the Schatzker read is the single most common cause of failed lateral-only fixation of a "Schatzker V/VI" fracture.
- A single lateral plate cannot resist varus collapse of an unaddressed medial column — a medial column component always requires independent medial-side fixation.
- Historic wound complication rates with immediate ORIF of high-energy patterns reached 25–40% in the pre-staged-protocol era — this is the entire rationale for staging rather than fixing acutely through compromised soft tissue.
- Schatzker VI (bicondylar with metaphyseal-diaphyseal dissociation) carries the highest compartment syndrome risk of the group — maintain vigilance for compartment syndrome throughout staging and postoperatively.
- Peroneal nerve injury or transient palsy can occur with posterolateral dissection or fibular head osteotomy — identify and protect the nerve routinely; a degree of transient neurapraxia is not uncommon after osteotomy/retraction and should be distinguished from a structural nerve injury.
- Popliteal vessel injury is a risk with aggressive posterior dissection or posterior plate placement — work close to bone and use retractors that protect the neurovascular bundle.
- Loss of reduction can result from undersized or malpositioned subchondral raft screws that fail to support the elevated articular fragment — place them just beneath the joint surface, spaced across the width of the depressed segment.
- Malunion (varus/valgus) results from an inadequately buttressed medial column or an under-recognized posterior fragment.
- Arthrofibrosis is a major long-term morbidity from prolonged immobilization or delayed ROM initiation — protect against it with early passive/active-assisted motion once fixation is stable.
- Deep/septic infection risk rises with combined multi-incision approaches performed through compromised soft tissue — this is a further argument for soft-tissue-driven staging rather than a fixed timeline.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** hinged knee brace locked or limited per fixation stability; strict non-weight-bearing; early passive/active-assisted ROM starting within the first 3–7 days if fixation is stable; ankle pumps, quad sets, straight-leg raises; cryotherapy/elevation for swelling control.
- **2–6 weeks:** progress ROM toward full flexion as tolerated and tissue allows; continue non-weight-bearing to touch-down weight-bearing depending on fixation construct and fracture stability; wound check and suture/staple removal at 2 weeks.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive partial weight-bearing beginning around 6–8 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing, advancing to full weight-bearing by ~10–12 weeks for most stable constructs (bicondylar comminuted patterns or elderly osteoporotic bone may need to wait closer to 12 weeks); closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, gait training.
- **3–6 months:** progressive strengthening, proprioception/balance work, return to low-impact activity; return to sedentary/light-duty work typically feasible around 8–12 weeks, return to manual labor generally 4–6 months depending on physical demands.
- **6–12+ months:** return to impact/pivoting sport individualized to fracture severity, articular congruity achieved, and residual laxity. A 2025 RCT found immediate weight-bearing after stable internal fixation yielded better clinical outcomes with similar radiographic outcomes compared with delayed weight-bearing in selected patients — an evolving area suggesting some current protocols may be more conservative than necessary in well-fixed constructs, though this should be individualized rather than applied blanket-fashion to comminuted or elderly osteoporotic patterns.
