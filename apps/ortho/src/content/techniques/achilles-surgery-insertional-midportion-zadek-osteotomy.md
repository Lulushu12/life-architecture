## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Insertional or midportion Achilles tendinopathy that has failed a structured conservative program (eccentric/heavy-load loading protocols, heel lift, NSAIDs, ESWT) for at least 3–6 months.
- Insertional disease with a symptomatic Haglund deformity and retrocalcaneal bursitis unresponsive to shoe modification and injection.
- MRI or ultrasound evidence of extensive intrasubstance degeneration (generally >50% cross-sectional involvement) favoring augmentation (FHL transfer) over debridement alone.
- Zadek osteotomy specifically: insertional disease with a low-lying calcaneal inclination angle amenable to indirect unloading, in a patient wanting to avoid violating the tendon insertion, or with extensive circumferential insertional disease where detachment-reattachment would risk devascularizing a large tendon segment.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active local infection or skin compromise over the planned incision.
- Peripheral vascular disease/smoking with poor soft-tissue healing potential — counsel on markedly elevated wound complication risk before an open detachment-reattachment approach.
- Uncontrolled diabetes with neuropathy (impaired wound healing and higher risk of Charcot-type bone biology at any calcaneal osteotomy).
- Insufficient trial of conservative management (<3 months) — surgery is not first-line even for radiographically severe tendinosis.
- Zadek osteotomy is relatively contraindicated in patients with substantial retrocalcaneal bursitis/Haglund prominence requiring direct excision, or with a normal-to-low calcaneal pitch where further dorsal rotation would not meaningfully unload the insertion — confirm calcaneal pitch on lateral radiograph before offering Zadek over standard debridement.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: weightbearing lateral radiograph to assess calcaneal pitch and Haglund prominence (posterosuperior calcaneal angle); MRI to quantify percentage of tendon cross-sectional involvement and insertional footprint disease, which drives the debridement-vs-reattachment decision intraoperatively; ultrasound is a reasonable adjunct for dynamic assessment but is operator-dependent and less useful for surgical planning than MRI.
- **Templating**: for Zadek osteotomy, preoperative lateral radiograph is used to plan the wedge apex angle (commonly ~25–35°) and confirm adequate calcaneal body height/bone stock for a dorsal closing wedge without over-shortening the tuberosity.
- **Implant/graft selection**: suture anchors for insertional reattachment (2–4 anchors, single- or double-row construct depending on footprint width and detachment extent); headless compression screws (typically two 4.5–7.0 mm) for Zadek osteotomy fixation; have FHL harvest instrumentation (tendon stripper) available whenever >50% cross-sectional debridement is anticipated so augmentation can proceed in the same setting without a second procedure.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy for Zadek wedge planning and screw trajectory; pneumatic tourniquet; saw or high-speed burr for Haglund resection and wedge osteotomy; suture anchor set; consider a sagittal saw with a calibrated cutting guide if available for reproducible wedge angles.
- **Case-specific planning**: confirm Silfverskiöld test result preoperatively for midportion cases to decide whether gastrocnemius recession should be added; discuss with the patient in advance that intraoperative findings (extent of detachment) may change the plan from debridement alone to reattachment ± FHL transfer.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic nerve block (± saphenous block for medial-sided work) combined with general or spinal anesthesia/sedation is standard; a well-placed popliteal block markedly reduces early postoperative pain and opioid requirement.
- **Positioning**: prone position for standard open insertional and midportion approaches and for FHL harvest, allowing direct posterior access to the Achilles insertion and calcaneal tuberosity; a bolster under the shin/ankle keeps the foot free at the end of the table. Some surgeons perform midportion/percutaneous work supine with the hip externally rotated and knee flexed ("figure-4"), but prone gives the most direct exposure for insertional and Zadek work.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet standard; exsanguinate with Esmarch or elevation before inflation.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned to allow lateral and axial calcaneal views without repositioning the leg — essential for Zadek wedge marking, guidewire/screw trajectory, and confirming adequate Haglund resection.
- **Landmarks**: mark the palpable Haglund prominence, the course of the sural nerve (posterior to the lateral malleolus, running with the short saphenous vein toward the fifth metatarsal base), and the planned incision (central-splitting, medial, or lateral depending on disease pattern) before prepping.
- **Draping**: free-draped leg circumferentially to allow full ankle plantar/dorsiflexion intraoperatively — this is essential for both wedge reduction in Zadek osteotomy and for tensioning checks after reattachment/FHL transfer.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Insertional disease — debridement ± reattachment**
1. Prone position, tourniquet inflated after exsanguination. Make a central-splitting (through the tendon, midline posterior) or medial paratendinous incision centered over the insertion, extending from ~4 cm proximal to the insertion distally over the Haglund prominence.
2. Sharply incise the peritenon and, for a central-splitting approach, split the tendon longitudinally in line with its fibers to expose the deep degenerative insertional tissue and the retrocalcaneal bursa.
3. Debride the retrocalcaneal bursa and all macroscopically degenerative/mucoid tendon tissue back to healthy, glistening fibers. Resect the posterosuperior calcaneal (Haglund) prominence with a saw or burr under fluoroscopic guidance, checking the lateral view to confirm the posterior calcaneal angle has been adequately reduced without over-resecting into the insertion footprint.
4. Quantify the percentage of the tendon insertion detached during debridement. If **>50% of the footprint is detached**, proceed to reattachment: freshen the calcaneal footprint bed to bleeding bone, place 2–4 suture anchors (single- or double-row depending on footprint width) and secure the tendon back down with a locking or mattress stitch technique, checking tension with the ankle at neutral to slight plantarflexion.
5. If extensive debridement (>50% cross-section) or a revision/lower-demand case, harvest the FHL through the same or a separate medial incision, route it through a drill hole in the calcaneal tuberosity or fix it with an interference screw/anchor, tensioning with the ankle in slight plantarflexion and the great toe in neutral.
6. Repair the tendon split (if central-splitting approach) side-to-side with absorbable suture. Close peritenon, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers; avoid excessive tension on the skin closure, which is the single largest driver of wound complications in this approach.

**Zadek osteotomy (dorsal closing-wedge calcaneal osteotomy)**
1. Prone position. A lateral (or, for the percutaneous/MIS variant, a small 5 mm) incision is made over the posterior calcaneal tuberosity, at the apex of the planned wedge — typically 5–8 mm from the plantar cortex, just anterior to the plantar fascia origin.
2. Under biplanar fluoroscopy, place guide pins from above and below the heel converging at the planned wedge apex angle (commonly ~25–35°, individualized to calcaneal pitch and desired correction) to outline the dorsal wedge for resection.
3. Using a saw (open technique) or a high-speed burr with the MIS technique (irrigating continuously and pausing every few seconds to prevent thermal bone necrosis), resect the planned dorsal wedge, leaving the plantar cortex intact as a hinge.
4. Close the osteotomy by maximally dorsiflexing the ankle, which rotates the tuberosity and posterior prominence anteriorly/inferiorly, indirectly unloading the Achilles insertion without touching the tendon itself. Confirm wedge closure and correction on lateral fluoroscopy.
5. Provisionally pin the osteotomy, then place two guidewires from the posterior tuberosity across the osteotomy into the anterior/subchondral calcaneal bone; advance two headless compression screws (commonly 6.5–7.0 mm) over the wires while maintaining maximal dorsiflexion, achieving interfragmentary compression.
6. Check final fluoroscopic position (lateral and axial), confirm no intra-articular screw penetration (subtalar joint), and close in layers. No tendon work is performed — the insertion is left undisturbed.

**Midportion disease**
1. Approach directly over the diseased segment (identified by preoperative MRI/palpation), typically 2–6 cm proximal to the insertion, through a paratendinous longitudinal incision slightly medial to the tendon's midline to avoid the sural nerve laterally.
2. Open or percutaneous longitudinal tenotomy through the degenerative nodule, excising mucoid/fibrotic tissue and performing multiple longitudinal tenotomies to stimulate a healing response if a discrete excisable lesion is not present.
3. If Silfverskiöld testing confirmed isolated gastrocnemius contracture preoperatively, perform a gastrocnemius recession (Strayer-type) through a separate proximal medial incision to unload the tendon; this can be done as an adjunct or, in milder cases, as an alternative to direct tendon debridement.
4. If >50% of the cross-section is excised, or the case is a revision/chronic degenerative tendon, harvest and transfer the FHL as above, tensioned with the ankle in slight plantarflexion.
5. Repair the paratenon and close in layers, tubularizing the remaining tendon if a substantial defect remains after debridement.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Mark the sural nerve course preoperatively with the patient in the position of surgery — its path shifts relative to skin landmarks with prone positioning versus supine.
- For the Zadek osteotomy, keep the plantar cortex intact as a hinge during wedge resection; a complete cut risks losing control of the correction and creating an unstable, displaced fragment.
- Irrigate copiously and pause the burr every 3–4 seconds during MIS Zadek osteotomy to prevent thermal necrosis at the cut margins — a well-documented failure mode of high-speed burring in confined cortical bone.
- Achieve maximal ankle dorsiflexion before final screw fixation in the Zadek technique — under-dorsiflexing at the time of fixation under-corrects the deformity and blunts the intended unloading effect.
- When reattaching the Achilles insertion, tension the repair with the ankle at neutral to slight plantarflexion (not full dorsiflexion) to avoid overtightening, which risks a rerupture or excessive stiffness in early rehab.
- Confirm complete resection of the Haglund prominence with a lateral fluoroscopic shot before closure — residual prominence is a common cause of persistent posterior heel pain and patient dissatisfaction.
- When performing FHL transfer, harvest at the level of the knot of Henry (or more proximally) to obtain adequate tendon length and preserve independent flexion of the lesser toes via the FDL.
- In central-splitting approaches, orient the split precisely in the midline and repair it meticulously — an off-center or poorly repaired split increases the risk of a longitudinal tendon defect and altered force transmission.
- Consider the minimally invasive/endoscopic route for insertional debridement when case volume and experience support it — comparable clinical outcomes to open surgery with fewer wound complications have been reported, but there is a real learning curve; do not adopt it for a complex reattachment case as a first attempt.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Wound dehiscence/infection is notably higher with traditional open detachment-reattachment insertional approaches — a major driver of interest in minimally invasive and Zadek alternatives; minimize skin tension at closure and consider a vertical, not transverse, incision orientation.
- Sural nerve injury is a recognized risk with both midportion (lateral-based dissection) and insertional approaches — stay medial to midline when possible and use blunt dissection once through the subcutaneous layer.
- Re-rupture and persistent insertional pain can result from inadequate debridement, under-tensioned reattachment, or heterotopic bone reformation at the resected Haglund site.
- Thermal osteonecrosis at the osteotomy margins is a specific pitfall of high-speed burr use in the MIS Zadek technique — irrigate continuously and avoid prolonged continuous burring.
- Incomplete wedge closure or under-correction in Zadek osteotomy occurs if maximal dorsiflexion is not maintained through provisional and final fixation — recheck fluoroscopic position immediately before screw insertion, not just after pin placement.
- Screw penetration into the subtalar joint is a recognized technical error with Zadek fixation given the proximity of the posterior facet — confirm trajectory on both lateral and axial fluoroscopic views.
- Over-tensioning an FHL transfer or insertional reattachment can produce excessive ankle/hallux stiffness and delayed rehabilitation; under-tensioning risks weakness and residual gait abnormality.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Insertional detachment-reattachment**: splint/NWB 2 weeks; boot with heel wedge, NWB to partial WB 2–6 weeks (bone-tendon healing at the calcaneal insertion is slower than midsubstance repair); progressive WB 6–10 weeks as wedges are gradually removed; formal PT with progressive ROM and eccentric strengthening from ~8–10 weeks; return to running/sport 4–6 months.

**Zadek osteotomy**: allows comparatively earlier progressive WB (often boot WB from 2 weeks) since the insertion itself is undisturbed, with osteotomy union (~6 weeks) guiding progression.

**Midportion debridement ± FHL transfer/gastrocnemius recession**: follows a similar arc — splint/NWB 1–2 weeks, boot with progressive WB from 2–4 weeks, formal PT with ROM and eccentric strengthening beginning once wound healing is secure (typically 3–4 weeks), return to sport 4–6 months, longer (up to 6 months) when FHL augmentation was required for extensive debridement.
