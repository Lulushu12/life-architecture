## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Severe hallux valgus (HVA >40°, IMA >16–18°), a hypermobile or arthritic 1st TMT joint, ligamentous laxity syndromes, juvenile/adolescent hallux valgus, and recurrent deformity after a prior distal/diaphyseal osteotomy.
- Reserved for these settings alongside — or as an alternative to — proximal metatarsal osteotomy (crescentic or opening/closing wedge), since both are used for the same severe-deformity tier; Lapidus is generally preferred when 1st ray instability (not just angular deformity) is the dominant driver.
- 1st ray sagittal-plane instability associated with pes planovalgus or a plantar-gapped/unstable 1st TMT joint on stress radiographs.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active infection, inadequate bone stock, or Charcot neuroarthropathy of the midfoot.
- Very short 1st metatarsal or a patient in whom further loss of ray length (from joint resection) is not acceptable without structural grafting.
- Smoking and poorly controlled diabetes are strong relative contraindications given the procedure's baseline nonunion risk — optimize or counsel accordingly.
- Adjacent 2nd TMT (Lisfranc) joint instability not addressed at the same setting can undermine the correction — assess the whole medial/middle column, not the 1st TMT in isolation.
- Patients requiring rapid return to unrestricted weight-bearing (occupational or logistic constraints) should be counseled that Lapidus generally requires a longer protected weight-bearing course than a distal/diaphyseal osteotomy, even with rigid modern fixation.

## Preoperative Planning

- Weight-bearing AP, lateral, and sesamoid axial radiographs to confirm severity grade (HVA, IMA), sesamoid position, and 1st TMT joint congruency/obliquity.
- Clinically stress the 1st ray in the sagittal plane for hypermobility (>8–10 mm of dorsal–plantar excursion relative to the 2nd ray is generally considered abnormal, though the test has only moderate reliability) — correlate with the lateral radiograph, looking specifically for **plantar gapping at the 1st TMT joint**, which supports a sagittal-plane instability component that the fusion must correct, not just the coronal-plane IMA.
- Evaluate the lateral talus–1st metatarsal angle for coexisting pes planus, which may modify the desired sagittal correction at the TMT joint.
- Decide fixation construct preoperatively: a **plantar (tension-side) plate** with or without an interfragmentary compression screw provides the most biomechanically favorable construct (plate on the tension side of the joint) and has been associated with earlier protected weight-bearing protocols in some contemporary series; a dorsomedial plate is more familiar/accessible surgically but is on the compression side rather than the tension side of the joint; crossed-screw-only fixation is simpler but more dependent on bone quality and surgeon screw-placement technique.
- Have a plan for a structural graft (autograft or allograft wedge) if plantar gapping is significant and simple compression will not adequately restore sagittal alignment without over-shortening the ray.
- Instrumentation: burr or saw for medial eminence and joint-surface preparation, K-wires for provisional fixation and rotational control, plate/screw sets per chosen construct, and mini C-arm.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Regional (ankle/popliteal block) with sedation, spinal, or general anesthesia; given the longer operative time relative to a distal osteotomy, discuss anesthesia duration and postoperative analgesia plan with the anesthesia team in advance.
- Supine, foot free at the end of the table, ipsilateral hip bump for neutral rotation.
- Thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination.
- Position and drape to allow visualization from the medial 1st TMT joint distally to the 1st MTP joint, since concurrent lateral release/derotation and Akin osteotomy are frequently performed in the same setting.
- Mini C-arm positioned for AP, lateral, and oblique views of the midfoot — confirm the 1st TMT joint is clearly visualized (not obscured by overlapping tarsal bones) before incision.
- Mark the dorsomedial longitudinal incision over the 1st TMT joint, extending as needed toward the 1st MTP if concurrent distal procedures are planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a dorsomedial longitudinal incision centered over the 1st TMT joint, protecting the dorsomedial cutaneous nerve and the extensor hallucis longus tendon.
2. Develop the interval to the 1st TMT joint capsule; incise longitudinally and expose the joint circumferentially.
3. Before resecting the medial first metatarsal eminence (if hallux valgus correction is combined with the fusion), remove fibrous periosteal and ligamentous tissue overlying it, then resect it with a burr in a sweeping motion, or with an oscillating saw from dorsal to plantar, creating a flush proximal surface in line with the metatarsal shaft — confirm with fluoroscopy to avoid lateral over-resection.
4. Denude the articular cartilage from the 1st metatarsal base and medial cuneiform down to bleeding subchondral bone; use a curette, small osteotome, or burr, and perforate the subchondral plate with multiple drill holes to promote a vascular fusion bed.
5. On the lateral radiograph/fluoroscopic view, assess for **plantar gapping** of the 1st TMT joint; if present and dorsiflexion of the medial column is a concern, correct by removing slightly more bone from the plantar aspect of the cuneiform (rather than the dorsal aspect) to close the gap without creating a dorsiflexed malunion, or use a structural allograft wedge if the gap is large enough that simple additional resection would over-shorten the ray.
6. Reduce the 1st metatarsal base onto the cuneiform, correcting the intermetatarsal angle by translating/rotating the metatarsal base laterally while holding the proximal phalanx in a corrected, non-rotated position as a reference.
7. Provisionally fix the reduction with a K-wire — placed so it exits the plantar-medial aspect of the medial cuneiform just proximal to the naviculocuneiform articulation, confirmed with fluoroscopy — before committing to definitive fixation.
8. Confirm correction of IMA, sagittal alignment (no residual plantar gap, no iatrogenic dorsiflexion), and appropriate 1st ray length with fluoroscopy in AP and lateral planes.
9. Place definitive fixation: if using a lag screw technique, **leave the provisional K-wire in place while advancing the compression screw** — this prevents the metatarsal base from rotating around the screw during tightening — then remove the K-wire only for final screw seating/compression. **Countersink the metatarsal cortex adequately** to avoid a stress riser and prominent screw head. If using a plate, position it on the plantar (tension) aspect of the joint when the construct allows adequate exposure, or dorsomedially if plantar plating is not feasible for the chosen approach; drill and place screws into both the cuneiform and metatarsal to span the fusion site.
10. Perform lateral soft-tissue release/derotation of the sesamoid apparatus concurrently through the 1st webspace if sesamoid subluxation is significant (Hardy & Clapham ≥4–5).
11. Add an Akin osteotomy at the proximal phalanx if residual hallux valgus interphalangeus persists after the TMT-level correction.
12. Recheck final alignment and fixation with fluoroscopy, irrigate, close the capsule and soft tissues in layers, and apply a well-padded splint or boot.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Address plantar gapping by removing **plantar** (not dorsal) bone from the cuneiform side of the joint, or by grafting — taking dorsal bone to "close" a plantar gap iatrogenically dorsiflexes the ray and predisposes to transfer metatarsalgia.
- Leave the provisional K-wire in place while tightening the primary compression screw, removing it only for the final compression pass — this single maneuver prevents the common problem of the metatarsal base rotating around the screw axis during tightening, which otherwise silently degrades correction.
- Countersink screw heads adequately in the metatarsal cortex; a proud screw head under the thin dorsal soft-tissue envelope is a common source of late hardware symptoms and unplanned removal.
- A plantar (tension-side) plate gives excellent joint exposure and biomechanically favorable load-sharing, but requires comfort with a slightly different exposure/retraction strategy than the more familiar dorsomedial approach — deliberately practice or plan this exposure if adopting plantar plating.
- Use the corrected, non-rotated proximal phalanx as a visual/positional reference while reducing the metatarsal base — it is easy to achieve an acceptable IMA on imaging while leaving subtle rotational malalignment that becomes apparent only clinically.
- Perforate the subchondral plate generously on both joint surfaces regardless of technique — fusion biology, not just mechanical compression, drives union in this procedure.
- Assess the 2nd TMT joint intraoperatively for instability; addressing only the 1st TMT in a patient with broader midfoot hypermobility can leave a source of recurrent deformity or persistent pain.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Lapidus carries a **nonunion risk of approximately 3–6%** in contemporary series — do not assume union; confirm it radiographically before advancing to unrestricted loading.
- Requires a **longer initial protected weight-bearing period** than a distal osteotomy — communicate this trade-off to patients preoperatively.
- Undercorrection of the intermetatarsal angle or failure to adequately address 1st-ray instability is a driver of recurrence, as with other hallux valgus procedures.
- As with any 1st-ray procedure, avoid shortening or elevating the ray excessively — this redistributes load laterally and risks transfer metatarsalgia under the lesser metatarsal heads.
- Iatrogenic dorsiflexion malunion (from mismanaging plantar gapping by resecting dorsal rather than plantar bone) is a specific, avoidable technical error that predisposes to lesser-metatarsal transfer overload.
- Screw rotation of the metatarsal base during final tightening (if the provisional K-wire is removed too early) can silently lose correction achieved during reduction.
- Prominent, inadequately countersunk hardware is a common reason for later reoperation independent of union status.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: non-weight-bearing or protected touch-down weight-bearing in a splint/boot per fixation construct (rigid plate constructs, particularly plantar plating, increasingly allow earlier protected weight-bearing).
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing in a CAM boot as radiographs allow.
- **Weeks 6–8**: radiographic fusion check; if consolidating, transition out of the boot into supportive shoes.
- **Weeks 8–12+**: continue progressive loading; return to impact activity only after confirmed union (typically **10–12 weeks**, sometimes longer) — given the ~3–6% nonunion risk, union should be radiographically confirmed, not assumed, before full unrestricted return to sport.
- General return-to-sport gating: near-full pain-free active ROM, pain-free single-leg heel-rise/hop, swelling resolved to fit normal athletic footwear, graduated walk → run → sport-specific drills, with contact/pivoting sport typically not before 12–16 weeks.
