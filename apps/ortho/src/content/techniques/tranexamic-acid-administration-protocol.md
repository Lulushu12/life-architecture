## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Elective arthroplasty, revision arthroplasty, and operative hip fracture management where reduction of surgical blood loss and transfusion requirement is desired.
- TXA is the single highest-yield blood-conservation intervention in elective arthroplasty and is supported for routine use in operative hip fracture management barring a genuine contraindication.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- **History of seizure disorder** — TXA, especially at high IV bolus doses or via inadvertent intrathecal exposure, is proconvulsant; use caution with dosing/route in this population.
- **Active arterial thromboembolic event within the preceding ~3 months** (e.g., recent MI, stroke) — individualized discussion, not an automatic bar, given the reassuring aggregate orthopedic safety data, but genuine caution is warranted in the acute/subacute window.
- **Renal impairment** — TXA is renally cleared and requires dose reduction; do not use a standard dose unadjusted in significant renal dysfunction.
- **Markedly elevated non-orthopedic-specific cardiovascular risk** — the reassuring orthopedic-specific safety signal should not be extrapolated uncritically to patients whose overall risk profile more closely resembles the broader noncardiac-surgery population studied in POISE-3, where high-dose IV TXA showed a signal that did not meet formal non-inferiority for a composite vascular event outcome (see Pitfalls).
- **Not contraindications despite historical teaching:** active malignancy and remote (>12 months, stable) VTE history are not considered contraindications in current practice, nor is a prior history of VTE, coronary disease, or stroke at standard orthopedic dosing — the aggregate arthroplasty/hip-fracture literature has not shown increased thromboembolic events in these groups.
- **Absolute avoidance:** never administer TXA intrathecally/via inadvertent spinal route — this is a rare but catastrophic medication-error scenario (proconvulsant effect delivered directly to the CNS) and underscores the importance of clear labeling and separation of TXA from neuraxial medications in the OR.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Route and regimen selection at the case-planning stage:** decide IV, topical/intra-articular, oral, or combined regimen based on the specific case (primary vs. revision, expected blood loss, patient factors) rather than defaulting to a single institutional habit for every case.
- **Screen for contraindications at preoperative assessment**, not on the day of surgery: seizure history, recent arterial thromboembolic event, and renal function should all be reviewed with enough lead time to plan dose adjustment or route change if needed.
- **Renal function check:** obtain or confirm recent renal function for any patient in whom dose reduction might be needed.
- **Equipment/preparation for topical or combined use:** if topical/intra-articular TXA is planned, confirm dilution protocol (1–3g in 50–100mL normal saline) and drain-clamping/dwell-time plan are documented in the case plan so they are not improvised at closure.
- **Coordinate with the broader blood-management and ERAS plan:** TXA dosing/timing should be planned alongside tourniquet strategy, anticipated blood loss, and transfusion threshold policy (restrictive strategy, Hb 7–8g/dL trigger) as one integrated blood-conservation plan rather than siloed decisions.
- **High-blood-loss case flagging:** identify revision arthroplasty, bilateral procedures, and complex trauma cases preoperatively as candidates for combined IV + topical regimens for additive effect, rather than deciding intraoperatively once blood loss is already underway.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

Not applicable in the surgical-positioning sense — TXA is a pharmacologic adjunct given at defined timepoints relative to incision and closure rather than a positioning-dependent procedure. Relevant setup considerations:

- **IV line labeling and separation from neuraxial medications:** in cases using spinal/epidural anesthesia, ensure TXA syringes and lines are clearly labeled and kept physically separate from any neuraxial administration setup — this is the key safeguard against the rare but catastrophic inadvertent intrathecal TXA administration error.
- **Timing coordination with the anesthesia team:** confirm who is responsible for administering the incision-time IV bolus (surgeon-directed vs. anesthesia-administered per protocol) so it is not missed in the transition from anesthesia induction to surgical start.
- **Oral dosing logistics:** if using the oral route, confirm the dose (~1.95–2g) is given approximately 2 hours preoperatively — this requires coordination with the preoperative holding area, since it must happen before the patient is called back, not after.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the patient has been screened for contraindications** (seizure history, recent arterial thromboembolic event, renal function) and that the planned route/dose accounts for any needed adjustment.
2. **Select a route based on clinical context:**
   - **IV:** 1g bolus at incision, optionally repeated as 1g at wound closure (or given as a 3-hour infusion), or weight-based 10–15mg/kg per dose.
   - **Topical/intra-articular:** 1–3g diluted in 50–100mL normal saline instilled into the joint before closure, with a dwell time before drain release if a drain is used — favored when systemic exposure is a specific concern.
   - **Oral:** approximately 1.95–2g given 2 hours preoperatively; comparable efficacy to IV in several arthroplasty trials, with lower cost and no IV-line requirement.
   - **Combined IV + topical:** used in high-blood-loss cases (revision arthroplasty, bilateral procedures, complex trauma) for additive effect.
3. **Administer the IV bolus at incision** if using the IV route, confirming the dose and timing against the surgical time-out.
4. **If a topical/intra-articular dose is planned, instill it into the joint before closure**, allowing the planned dwell time before releasing any drain — releasing the drain too early defeats the purpose of the dwell period by washing the drug out before it has acted locally.
5. **If a repeat/closure IV dose is planned, administer it at wound closure** as scheduled rather than as an afterthought once closure is already underway.
6. **Document route, dose, and timing** in the anesthesia/operative record.
7. **Continue standard perioperative blood-loss and VTE monitoring** postoperatively — TXA administration does not change the restrictive transfusion threshold (Hb <8g/dL symptomatic or <7g/dL asymptomatic) or the standard VTE surveillance protocol.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- **Default to combined IV + topical dosing in revision arthroplasty, bilateral procedures, and complex trauma** — these high-blood-loss scenarios are where the additive effect of combining routes has the clearest practical benefit.
- **Keep TXA physically and visually separate from neuraxial medications** whenever spinal/epidural anesthesia is used in the same case — labeling discipline here is the single most important safeguard against the rare, catastrophic inadvertent intrathecal administration error.
- **Use the oral route deliberately, not just as a fallback**, in patients where avoiding an additional IV push or where cost is a genuine consideration — the efficacy data support it as comparable to IV in several arthroplasty trials, not merely "good enough."
- **Time oral dosing carefully with the preoperative holding-area workflow** (~2 hours before surgery) — a dose given too close to incision loses much of its intended pre-incision fibrinolysis-blocking effect.
- **When using topical/intra-articular TXA with a drain, actually observe the planned dwell time before releasing the clamp** — this is easy to shortcut under time pressure at closure but is central to how the topical route is meant to work.
- **Coordinate explicitly with anesthesia on who gives the incision-time bolus** — in busy turnover, an assumption that "the other team is giving it" is a realistic and avoidable way for the incision-time dose to be missed entirely.
- **Do not let a remote or stable VTE/cardiac history trigger automatic TXA avoidance** — current evidence does not support withholding TXA in these patients, and reflexive avoidance forgoes a substantial blood-conservation benefit without a clear safety justification.
- **Reserve genuine caution (not blanket avoidance) for patients whose overall risk profile resembles the higher-cardiovascular-risk, non-orthopedic-specific population** studied in the broader noncardiac surgery TXA literature — this is a narrower group than "anyone with any cardiovascular history."

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The aggregate arthroplasty and hip-fracture literature — spanning many thousands of randomized patients — has not shown an increased rate of DVT, PE, MI, or stroke with TXA at standard doses, including in patients with a prior history of VTE, coronary disease, or stroke, once considered relative contraindications.
- This safety signal is procedure- and dose-context dependent: a large noncardiac surgery trial (POISE-3) in a broader, higher-cardiovascular-risk population found the composite vascular event outcome occurred in 14.2% (TXA) vs 13.9% (placebo) — the trial failed to formally establish non-inferiority on that outcome (upper CI bound exceeded the margin), a more modest and different signal than "increased risk," but a reminder not to extrapolate reassuring orthopedic-specific safety data uncritically to markedly higher cardiovascular-risk patients or non-orthopedic contexts. TXA did significantly reduce major bleeding in that trial (9.1% vs 11.7%).
- Trauma-specific major hemorrhage trials (non-ortho-specific) established a mortality benefit from early TXA in bleeding trauma patients, and a separate large trial in traumatic brain injury found TXA safe with a signal toward reduced head-injury-related death when given early — supportive context for routine use in hip fracture management, but drawn from a broader trauma population.
- A 2024 EFORT Open Reviews meta-analysis and a separate selection-bias-adjusted Journal of Arthroplasty analysis both reaffirm no increase in DVT/PE/mortality with TXA in TJA patients with a prior VTE history; a 2023 meta-analysis found single-dose and double-dose IV TXA equivalent in efficacy and safety.
- **Inadvertent intrathecal TXA administration** is a rare but well-documented and catastrophic medication error (severe refractory seizures, arrhythmia, potential death) resulting from mistaking a TXA syringe for a local anesthetic/neuraxial medication — the countermeasure is strict physical separation and labeling discipline, not clinical vigilance alone.
- **Missed incision-time bolus due to a handoff assumption** between surgical and anesthesia teams is a common, avoidable gap — explicit role assignment at the time-out prevents it.
- **Premature drain release after topical/intra-articular dosing** washes out the dose before its intended local dwell time has elapsed, undermining the rationale for choosing the topical route in the first place.
- **Underdosing via renal non-adjustment** in a patient with unrecognized renal impairment risks both reduced antifibrinolytic effect (if underdosed reflexively out of excess caution) or accumulation/toxicity (if standard dosing is used without adjustment where reduction was actually indicated) — confirm renal function is genuinely known, not assumed normal, before dosing significant renal impairment out of the decision.

## Postoperative Protocol

No dedicated TXA-specific postoperative monitoring beyond standard perioperative VTE and bleeding surveillance; TXA is folded into the broader blood-management and ERAS pathway rather than followed by its own distinct protocol. Restrictive transfusion threshold (Hb <8g/dL symptomatic or <7g/dL asymptomatic) applies regardless of TXA use.
