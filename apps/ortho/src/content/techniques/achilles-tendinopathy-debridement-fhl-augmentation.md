## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Mid-portion or insertional Achilles tendinopathy that has failed a minimum of 6 months of structured conservative treatment: progressive tendon loading (HSR or eccentric decline-board program), activity modification, and a reasonable trial of adjunctive measures (shockwave, high-volume injection).
- Mid-portion disease with imaging-confirmed degenerative tissue burden sufficient to explain symptoms (fusiform thickening, intrasubstance signal change/partial tearing on MRI or US).
- Insertional disease with a symptomatic Haglund prominence and/or retrocalcaneal bursitis unresponsive to non-operative measures, or footprint detachment requiring reattachment.

**Contraindications**
- Active local or systemic infection, uncontrolled diabetes, active smoking without a cessation plan (all materially raise wound complication risk, especially for the posterior/insertional approach).
- Peripheral vascular disease severe enough to threaten posterior heel wound healing — evaluate with ABI/vascular consult if clinically suspected.
- Inadequate trial of conservative care (<6 months) or a loading program that was never properly instructed/adhered to — repeat a structured, supervised trial before committing to surgery.
- Symptoms poorly correlated with imaging findings (i.e., minimal degenerative burden) — reconsider the diagnosis (paratenonitis, plantaris entrapment, retrocalcaneal impingement without tendinosis) before offering debridement.
- Relative: active systemic inflammatory arthropathy (gout, seronegative spondyloarthropathy) driving insertional symptoms — treat the underlying disease first.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI is the workhorse for surgical planning — quantifies percentage of tendon cross-sectional area involved (drives the FHL-augmentation decision), delineates the proximal-distal extent of degenerative tissue, and confirms/excludes a partial-thickness longitudinal split. Weight-bearing lateral radiograph measures the Haglund prominence (parallel pitch lines / calcaneal pitch) and confirms retrocalcaneal bursitis calcification if present.
- **Decision point to template before the day of surgery:** estimate expected percentage of tendon requiring excision from MRI — if it appears it will exceed ~50% of cross-section, plan for FHL harvest and have the transfer set (interference screw or bio-tenodesis screw sizing set, or bone tunnel drill guide) opened and available; do not decide this purely intraoperatively without having consented the patient for FHL harvest and its associated hallux weakness/numbness risk.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** tendon-splitting retractors, headlamp or loupes for identifying longitudinal split tears, rongeur/burr for Haglund resection, suture anchors (2-4, sized per bone quality — typically 4.5-5.5 mm for calcaneal insertion) for footprint reattachment, FHL harvest set (curved and straight tenotomes, whipstitch suture, interference screw or bone tunnel/button system), tourniquet.
- **Consent conversation:** discuss the ~10-25% rate of persistent pain even after well-performed surgery, and set expectations that symptomatic improvement can take 6-12 months.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** popliteal sciatic block (± saphenous block for tourniquet tolerance) combined with sedation is favored — provides several hours of post-operative analgesia and reduces early narcotic need; general anesthesia is a reasonable alternative or supplement.
- **Positioning:** prone with a bump under the ankles to keep the toes off the table and allow free plantar/dorsiflexion; alternatively, lateral decubitus with the operative leg forward can be used for isolated mid-portion work, but prone gives the best access for combined insertional/Haglund work and for FHL harvest through the same posteromedial or posterior window.
- **Tourniquet:** thigh tourniquet is standard; exsanguinate with an Esmarch or elevation, inflate to standard limb-appropriate pressure. Mark expected tourniquet time (typically well under the 2-hour safe limit for this procedure) and communicate with anesthesia if a mid-case release/re-inflation is anticipated for a long combined case.
- **Landmarks to mark before incision:** palpable Achilles tendon borders, the posterosuperior calcaneal prominence (Haglund), the course of the sural nerve (roughly along the lateral border of the tendon, crossing more superficially distally — mark it if a lateral incision is planned), and the medial malleolus/course of the neurovascular bundle if a posteromedial FHL harvest window is planned.
- **Imaging:** fluoroscopy is not routinely required for pure soft-tissue debridement but is useful to confirm adequate Haglund resection (compare pre- and post-resection lateral views) when bony resection is performed.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Prep and drape the leg free from mid-calf to toes so the ankle can be dynamically plantar/dorsiflexed intraoperatively to assess tendon excursion and confirm adequate Haglund clearance.
2. **Mid-portion approach:** make a longitudinal incision just medial to the palpable tendon border (medial approach reduces sural nerve risk relative to a posterolateral incision) centered over the area of maximal thickening/pathology on pre-op imaging.
3. Incise the paratenon longitudinally and preserve it as a separate layer for closure — paratenon repair is a key step in maintaining tendon vascularity and gliding postoperatively.
4. Inspect the tendon; identify areas of mucoid degeneration, longitudinal splits, and nodularity. Make a longitudinal tenotomy through the area of greatest pathology to expose intrasubstance disease if a split tear is not already visible on the surface.
5. Sharply excise degenerative tissue back to grossly normal, glistening, longitudinally-oriented fibers. Resist the temptation to over-resect "for margin" — the goal is removal of demonstrably diseased tissue, not a wide oncologic margin.
6. Estimate the percentage of cross-sectional area excised. If it exceeds approximately 50%, proceed to FHL harvest and transfer (steps 10-13) before closing the tendon.
7. If the remaining tendon is judged adequate (<50% excised), repair the tendon-splitting defect side-to-side with absorbable suture (e.g., running or interrupted 0 or 2-0 suture) in a tension-free fashion with the ankle in slight plantarflexion.
8. **Insertional approach:** use a posterolateral, posteromedial, or central splitting (transtendinous) approach depending on the extent of the Haglund prominence and the location of insertional disease. A central tendon-splitting approach gives excellent access to both the insertion and the posterosuperior calcaneus when disease is midline and bilateral (medial and lateral) exposure is not otherwise needed.
9. Excise the retrocalcaneal bursa, then subperiosteally expose the posterosuperior calcaneal tuberosity. Resect the Haglund prominence with an osteotome or sagittal saw, working from lateral to medial (or vice versa) under direct vision; confirm adequate resection by passively dorsiflexing the ankle and visually/manually checking for residual impingement against the tendon, and by comparing a lateral fluoroscopic image to the pre-operative parallel pitch lines.
10. Debride the diseased insertional tendon and any calcific enthesophyte. If detachment of the tendon footprint exceeds roughly 50%, proceed to anchor reattachment: place 2-4 suture anchors in the calcaneal footprint (a double-row/suture-bridge construct restores the broadest footprint contact and is preferred when more than half the insertion is taken down) and secure the tendon back to bone with the ankle held in slight plantarflexion to avoid excess tension.
11. **FHL harvest (when indicated):** through a posteromedial window (often extending the mid-portion incision, or a separate incision medial to the Achilles), identify and protect the neurovascular bundle anteriorly, incise the fascia over the FHL in the deep posterior compartment, and harvest the tendon as distally as reasonably accessible (through the master knot of Henry region, or via a separate plantar incision for a longer harvest if maximal length/strength is desired).
12. Whipstitch the harvested FHL tendon end, then pass it through a drill hole/bone tunnel in the calcaneus (drilled from posterior-to-plantar, sized to the tendon diameter) or fix it with an interference screw in a unicortical socket, tensioning with the ankle in slight plantarflexion (roughly 10-20°) and the knee extended to set appropriate resting tension.
13. Confirm secure fixation with manual pull-testing and passive ankle ROM through full available dorsiflexion/plantarflexion before releasing traction sutures.
14. Repair the paratenon as a distinct layer over the tendon repair/reconstruction — this restores the gliding envelope and reduces adhesion and wound-edge tension.
15. Close skin in layers (deep dermal absorbable, then a running or interrupted non-absorbable suture for skin) and apply a well-padded posterior splint/boot with the ankle in the position established intraoperatively (neutral for insertional/anchor repairs to protect the reattachment; slight plantarflexion may be used transiently for extensive debridement/FHL transfer per surgeon protocol).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a medial rather than posterolateral incision for isolated mid-portion work whenever possible — it keeps the wound farther from the sural nerve and away from shoe-counter pressure.
- Mark the location of maximal tendon thickening on the skin using pre-operative US or MRI measurements from a fixed landmark (e.g., "4 cm proximal to insertion") — degenerative tissue is not always where it feels most tender.
- When performing a central tendon-splitting insertional approach, orient the split longitudinally in line with the tendon fibers and keep it centered — this preserves medial and lateral tendon "shoulders" for load-sharing and allows easy re-approximation at closure.
- After Haglund resection, always recheck clearance dynamically (passive ankle dorsiflexion) rather than relying on a single static fluoroscopic shot — residual posterosuperior prominence is a common cause of early reoperation.
- Harvest the FHL as distally as the incision allows (rather than settling for a short proximal harvest) to maximize excursion and functional contribution to push-off strength; a plantar "second incision" harvest, when used, yields a substantially longer tendon graft.
- Tension the FHL transfer and the native tendon repair with the knee extended and ankle at the same fixed plantarflexion angle used throughout the case — tensioning in variable positions between steps produces an inconsistent final resting tone.
- Preserve and repair the paratenon as its own layer; do not simply close skin over the tendon — paratenon integrity is one of the more consistent predictors of good early gliding and lower adhesion-related stiffness.
- In heavy or high-demand patients being treated for extensive mid-portion disease, have a low threshold for FHL augmentation even close to the 50% excision threshold — the downside (mild flexor hallucis weakness) is well tolerated, while under-augmentation risking rupture is not.
- Use a headlamp or loupe magnification when searching for an occult longitudinal split — these can be easily missed on a cursory surface inspection of a nodular tendon.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Failing to augment with FHL transfer when >50% of the tendon cross-section has been excised raises re-rupture risk after debridement.
- Bony Haglund impingement requires resection of the prominence itself — insertional debridement alone is inadequate when a mechanical bony driver is present.
- Persistent pain occurs in an estimated ~10-25% of patients even after surgery — set expectations accordingly, particularly in patients who have not clearly failed a full loading-based conservative trial first.
- Wound healing problems are more common after insertional/Haglund resection than after mid-portion debridement — counsel smokers and diabetics specifically and consider delaying elective surgery until modifiable risk factors are optimized.
- Sural nerve injury is a recognized risk during Achilles tendinopathy surgery, as with rupture repair — most common with posterolateral incisions; a medial approach for mid-portion disease mitigates this.
- Under-resection of the Haglund prominence is a common technical error leading to recurrent impingement and reoperation — confirm resection dynamically, not just by feel.
- Over-tensioning the FHL transfer can limit ankle dorsiflexion post-operatively; under-tensioning fails to provide meaningful push-off augmentation — set tension with the ankle and knee in a consistent, reproducible position.
- Inadequate paratenon repair predisposes to adhesion formation and can compromise the vascular supply to the underlying tendon repair.

## Postoperative Protocol

Expected recovery is guided by the same principles as the general Achilles tendon reconditioning framework — progressive protected loading once the surgical repair/reattachment is secure, followed by graded return to activity — with FHL augmentation specifically intended to allow more confident progressive loading after extensive debridement by protecting against rupture and restoring push-off power.

- **Weeks 0-2:** posterior splint/boot immobilization, non-weight-bearing or touch-down weight-bearing per the extent of the reattachment (insertional anchor repairs are typically protected longer than isolated mid-portion debridement); wound checks.
- **Weeks 2-6:** transition to a removable boot, progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, gentle active ROM introduced once the wound is healed; avoid aggressive dorsiflexion stretching in insertional/FHL-augmented cases until bone-tendon healing is established.
- **Weeks 6-10:** wean from the boot to supportive footwear, begin gait retraining and closed-chain calf loading (double-leg heel raises), initiate gentle FHL/great-toe flexor strengthening if transfer was performed.
- **Weeks 10-16:** progressive single-leg heel raise strengthening, proprioception work, gradual reintroduction of loading-based (HSR) programming.
- **Months 4-6:** sport-specific and plyometric progression once strength symmetry criteria are met; full symptomatic response may take 6-12 months, and this should be communicated up front given the ~10-25% persistent-pain rate.

Reported outcomes: persistent pain in ~10-25% of patients even after surgery; wound healing problems more common with insertional/Haglund resection; re-rupture risk if FHL augmentation is omitted despite extensive debridement.
