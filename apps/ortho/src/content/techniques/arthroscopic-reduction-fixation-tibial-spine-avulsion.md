## Indications

Meyers-McKeever III–IV tibial spine avulsion fractures, and type II fractures with a block to reduction or loss of reduction. Arthroscopy is specifically indicated when a mechanical block — entrapped anterior horn meniscal tissue or the intermeniscal ligament — prevents closed reduction.

## Positioning & setup

Aspiration of a tense knee hemarthrosis is both diagnostic (fat globules confirm intra-articular fracture) and therapeutic (improves comfort and enables clinical extension assessment for closed reduction candidacy) and is typically performed before proceeding to the operating room.

## Steps

1. Arthroscopic visualization to identify and free entrapped meniscal or intermeniscal-ligament tissue — present in a meaningful minority of type III/IV fractures and the most common cause of an irreducible fragment.
2. Anatomic reduction of the fragment, achieved with the knee extended (not flexed) — the intact anterior horns of the menisci and the intercondylar roof push the fragment down into its bed as the knee extends.
3. Fixation with **transosseous/epiphyseal suture** — passed around or through the base of the ACL and tied over an anterior bony bridge or button — which avoids drilling across the physis and is preferred in the least skeletally mature patients.
4. Alternatively, **screw fixation** — a single partially-threaded screw or two divergent screws through the fragment into the tibial metaphysis — providing strong compression but requiring adequate fragment size/bone stock and carrying a physeal-crossing consideration in less mature patients.
5. Comminuted (Zaricznyj IV) fragments are usually not amenable to screw purchase and are managed with suture fixation instead.

## Pitfalls

- Entrapped anterior horn menisci or the intermeniscal ligament are the most common cause of an irreducible fragment — do not force a closed reduction without ruling this out.
- Extension, not flexion, is the reducing position — the opposite of the intuitive "flexion relaxes the ACL" reasoning.
- Screw fixation crossing the physis carries a growth-arrest consideration in immature patients, driving the growing preference for suture fixation over screw fixation in this group.
- Fragment size and comminution, not just displacement, dictate the fixation technique (suture vs. screw).

## Post-op protocol

- Brace/cast immobilization in near-extension for **4–6 weeks** (whether treated nonoperatively or with fixation robust enough to permit a brace rather than a cast), with quadriceps-setting/isometric exercises begun **immediately** to limit atrophy.
- Progressive ROM (working toward full flexion) begins at **4–6 weeks** once the fixation/healing fragment can tolerate load; the single greatest rehabilitation risk is **arthrofibrosis/flexion contracture from prolonged extension immobilization** — most contemporary protocols favor earlier (2–3 week) initiation of protected flexion once fixation is judged stable rather than the traditional full 6-week extension cast.
- Return to pivoting sport is typically deferred to **4–6 months**, later if residual anterior knee laxity or extension deficit persists.
- Formal ACL-injury-prevention/neuromuscular training is reasonable before clearance, given the shared mechanism with ACL tears.
