## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Meyers-McKeever type III–IV tibial spine avulsion fractures.
- Type II fractures with a mechanical block to closed reduction (most commonly entrapped anterior horn meniscal tissue or the intermeniscal ligament), or loss of a previously acceptable reduction on serial radiographs.
- Type II fractures that fail to reduce with extension/hyperextension positioning and gentle manual pressure in the ED or under fluoroscopy in the OR.
- Zaricznyj type IV (comminuted) fragments, which are inherently unstable and not amenable to closed treatment.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Meyers-McKeever type I and reducible type II fractures — these are managed with aspiration and extension casting; arthroscopy adds risk without benefit.
- Active septic arthritis or overlying soft-tissue compromise precluding arthroscopic portal placement (rare in this population).
- Extremely small, comminuted fragments with essentially no bone stock for suture purchase and no meaningful ACL insertion remaining — occasionally better served by fragment excision and ACL footprint debridement with delayed reconstruction if late instability develops, though this is unusual in an acute setting.
- A concurrent injury requiring open exposure (e.g., a displaced tibial plateau fracture extending beyond the eminence) may change the approach from arthroscopic to open/mini-open.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP/lateral knee radiographs to confirm Meyers-McKeever grade; MRI is strongly recommended preoperatively (not just for equivocal cases) to characterize fragment size and comminution, assess for meniscal or intermeniscal-ligament entrapment beneath the fragment, and screen for associated meniscal or collateral ligament injury, which is present in a meaningful minority of these fractures (up to ~40% in some series).
- **Timing**: this is not a middle-of-the-night emergency in most cases — aspirate a tense hemarthrosis in the ED/clinic for comfort and to allow an extension trial, and book the arthroscopy semi-electively (within days) once swelling has been controlled and the family/consent process is complete, unless there is an irreducible fracture with a locked knee.
- **Implant/graft planning**: decide in advance, based on fragment size and comminution on MRI, whether the fixation will be suture-based (transosseous/epiphyseal suture around or through the ACL base, tied over an anterior bony bridge or button) or screw-based (partially threaded cannulated screw(s)). Comminuted (Zaricznyj IV) fragments should be planned for suture fixation from the outset — screws will not gain adequate purchase.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: standard 30° arthroscope and pump, arthroscopic shaver, suture-passing/suture-lasso device (e.g., nitinol-wire-based suture lasso), high-strength nonabsorbable suture or suture tape (~1.8 mm), a small-diameter guide pin and cannulated drill system if planning bone tunnels or button fixation, an EndoButton or similar cortical fixation device if using a button construct over the anterior tibial cortex, cannulated partially-threaded screws (typically 3.5–4.0 mm) with washers as a backup/alternative, a probe and arthroscopic grasper for fragment manipulation, and a mini C-arm or fixed fluoroscopy unit for screw trajectory confirmation if screws are used.
- **Consent discussion**: counsel families on the real possibility of postoperative arthrofibrosis/motion loss requiring focused rehabilitation or, rarely, later manipulation/lysis of adhesions, and on the shared mechanism with ACL injury (residual laxity is possible even with a well-reduced, healed fragment).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard; a femoral nerve or adductor canal block is a reasonable postoperative analgesia adjunct but is not required and some surgeons prefer to preserve early quadriceps activation, so discuss with the anesthesia team if early quad sets are a rehab priority.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard table with a leg holder or lateral post to allow valgus stress for medial compartment visualization and a tourniquet high on the thigh (inflated only if visualization is compromised by bleeding — most cases do not require tourniquet inflation for the entire case).
- **Table setup**: a well-padded thigh tourniquet is applied but often left uninflated initially; the leg should hang free off the end of the table or over a leg holder to allow the knee to be flexed to 90° and beyond for posterior visualization, and extended fully to assess reduction with the physiologic extension mechanism.
- **Draping**: standard arthroscopic knee draping with an impervious stockinette and adhesive drape, leaving the patella, joint line, and tibial tubercle palpable for portal placement.
- **Fluoroscopy**: have a mini C-arm draped into the field and readily available if screw fixation or button-tunnel drilling is anticipated, positioned to allow AP and lateral views of the proximal tibia without breaking the sterile field.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the patellar tendon borders, joint line, and standard anterolateral and anteromedial portal sites before insufflation distorts landmarks; also mark a potential accessory anteromedial (near-vertical) portal site if fragment access or suture-lasso passage is anticipated to require a steeper trajectory.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Examination under anesthesia**: assess Lachman and pivot-shift before prepping — document baseline laxity for comparison to postoperative exam, keeping in mind the endpoint will be soft from the bony avulsion regardless of true ACL integrity.
2. **Portal placement**: establish standard anterolateral (viewing) and anteromedial (working) portals just off the margins of the patellar tendon at the joint line. Insufflate the joint and perform a diagnostic arthroscopy of all compartments before addressing the fracture — evaluate the menisci, femoral condyles, and confirm the fracture bed.
3. **Evacuate the hemarthrosis and clear the fracture bed**: use the shaver (on low suction, not aggressive resection) to clear clot from the intercondylar notch and expose the fracture bed and the undersurface of the elevated fragment. Avoid aggressive shaving of the fragment itself or the ACL fibers attached to it.
4. **Identify and address entrapment**: carefully probe beneath the fragment for entrapped anterior horn meniscal tissue or the intermeniscal (transverse meniscal) ligament — this is the most common cause of an irreducible fragment and must be freed before attempting reduction, using a probe or small elevator to sweep the tissue out from under the fragment rather than forcing the fragment down onto it.
5. **Provisional reduction**: with the entrapment cleared, extend the knee (not flex it) while applying gentle downward pressure over the fragment with a probe or percutaneously with a spinal needle/switching stick — extension tensions the intact anterior horns of the menisci and the intercondylar roof, which push the fragment down into its bed. Confirm reduction arthroscopically by direct visualization of the fracture bed apposition, not by radiograph alone.
6. **Suture fixation technique** (preferred for smaller fragments, comminution, or maximal physeal preservation): pass a suture lasso or suture-passing device through the working portal to shuttle a nitinol wire or suture through the posterior/base fibers of the ACL just proximal to the fragment, in a lateral-to-medial (or medial-to-lateral) direction, capturing substantial ACL substance rather than just periosteum. Shuttle a high-strength nonabsorbable suture or suture tape through this pass. Drill two divergent epiphyseal tunnels (or use an all-epiphyseal transtibial guide) from the anteromedial tibial metaphysis, staying below and around the physis, exiting at the base of the fracture bed on either side of the eminence. Shuttle the suture limbs down through these tunnels and tie them over a bone bridge or button on the anterior tibial cortex with the knee in extension and the fragment held reduced, confirming reduction arthroscopically as the sutures are tensioned.
7. **Screw fixation technique** (for a single large, non-comminuted fragment with adequate bone stock): with the fragment held reduced by a probe or K-wire, place a guide pin from an anteromedial percutaneous start point through the fragment into the tibial metaphysis under arthroscopic and fluoroscopic control, aiming to avoid the physis if skeletal immaturity is significant, or accepting a limited physeal crossing near the periphery in an older/near-mature patient if unavoidable. Confirm pin position on AP and lateral fluoroscopy and arthroscopically before drilling. Place one or two divergent partially-threaded cannulated screws (typically 3.5–4.0 mm) over the guide wire(s), with the threads fully past the fracture line to achieve interfragmentary compression; use a washer if the screw head risks countersinking through thin epiphyseal bone.
8. **Final reduction check**: cycle the knee through a full range of motion arthroscopically and fluoroscopically to confirm the fragment does not displace with flexion, and re-inspect the notch to confirm no residual meniscal entrapment or roof impingement (notch impingement in extension is uncommon but should be checked if extension feels blocked).
9. **Closure**: close portal sites with simple sutures or adhesive strips; a soft dressing and hinged knee brace locked in extension (or near-extension, per surgeon preference) is applied before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use extension, not flexion, as your reducing maneuver at every step — this is counterintuitive if you are used to thinking "flexion relaxes the ACL," but the mechanism here is mechanical (the menisci and intercondylar roof pressing the fragment into its bed), not tension-based.
- When passing the suture lasso through the ACL base, take a generous bite of tissue — a thin bite risks suture pull-through under postoperative load; aim for the thick posterior/base fibers rather than the thin anterior fibers.
- A spinal needle placed percutaneously to manipulate the fragment (as an accessory portal) before committing to a formal accessory incision is a fast, low-morbidity way to test whether the fragment reduces once entrapped tissue is cleared.
- Keep shaver suction low and use it sparingly near the fragment and ACL fibers — it is easy to inadvertently thin or shred the very tissue you need for suture purchase.
- For comminuted (Zaricznyj IV) fragments, consider a hybrid construct: suture fixation of the largest fragment plus debridement of small unsalvageable fragments, rather than forcing screw purchase into fragmented bone.
- Tension sutures or tighten screws with the knee held in full extension by an assistant — a construct tensioned in flexion can leave the knee unable to fully extend once the limb is straightened.
- If using a cortical button/bone-bridge construct, avoid over-tensioning to the point of over-reducing the fragment proud of the native eminence contour — this can create anterior notch impingement in extension.
- Recheck the reduction and stability through a full ROM arc before leaving the OR; a fragment that looks reduced in extension can subtly displace in flexion if fixation is inadequate.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Entrapped anterior horn menisci or the intermeniscal ligament are the most common cause of an irreducible fragment — do not force a closed or arthroscopic reduction without first ruling this out; forcing reduction against entrapped tissue can shred the meniscus or intermeniscal ligament.
- Extension, not flexion, is the reducing position — the opposite of the intuitive "flexion relaxes the ACL" reasoning; attempting reduction in flexion is a common technical error.
- Screw fixation crossing the physis carries a growth-arrest consideration in immature patients, driving the growing preference for suture fixation over screw fixation in this group.
- Fragment size and comminution, not just displacement grade, should dictate the fixation technique (suture vs. screw) — do not default to screws simply because the fragment looks large on plain film; assess true bone stock on MRI/arthroscopically.
- **Arthrofibrosis/flexion contracture from prolonged extension immobilization** is the most common and most consequential complication of this procedure — it is a rehabilitation-protocol problem as much as a surgical-technique problem, and is discussed further under Postoperative Protocol.
- Residual anterior knee laxity (from stretched-out healing, malreduction, or an associated but unrecognized ACL midsubstance injury) can produce functional instability in pivoting athletes and is distinct from a true ACL tear — counsel families that a "positive Lachman with a firm endpoint" postoperatively may be the expected, acceptable result rather than a failure.
- Notch impingement from a fragment fixed proud of the native eminence contour can block terminal extension — recognized by a persistent extension deficit that does not improve with therapy; may require arthroscopic notchplasty/debridement if it does not resolve.
- Missed meniscal or intermeniscal ligament injury at the index procedure can present later as mechanical symptoms — a low threshold to revisit the MRI or re-scope if symptoms are inconsistent with simple arthrofibrosis.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Brace/cast immobilization in near-extension for **4–6 weeks** (whether treated nonoperatively or with fixation robust enough to permit a brace rather than a cast), with quadriceps-setting/isometric exercises begun **immediately** to limit atrophy.
- Progressive ROM (working toward full flexion) begins at **4–6 weeks** once the fixation/healing fragment can tolerate load; the single greatest rehabilitation risk is **arthrofibrosis/flexion contracture from prolonged extension immobilization** — most contemporary protocols favor earlier (2–3 week) initiation of protected flexion once fixation is judged stable rather than the traditional full 6-week extension cast.
- Return to pivoting sport is typically deferred to **4–6 months**, later if residual anterior knee laxity or extension deficit persists.
- Formal ACL-injury-prevention/neuromuscular training is reasonable before clearance, given the shared mechanism with ACL tears.
