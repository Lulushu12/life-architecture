## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: current international consensus (Murray et al. 2024) favors surgical management for most multiligament knee injuries, particularly in younger patients, over non-operative bracing, given the poor natural history of combined instability. Applies across the Schenck KD spectrum, especially where vascular repair, open injury, or gross instability precludes definitive bracing. Bridging external fixation is indicated when vascular repair has been performed (protects the repair/graft from re-injury during early mobilization), the injury is open, there is significant soft-tissue compromise, or the knee remains grossly unstable/unable to be splinted in a reduced position.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications to early definitive ligament reconstruction**:
- Ongoing vascular compromise or an unstable revascularization — definitive ligament work must wait until the vascular repair/limb perfusion is secure and the surgical/vascular teams agree the limb can tolerate additional operative time and tourniquet use (tourniquet use is often avoided altogether after vascular repair).
- Active compartment syndrome or evolving neurovascular deficit — fasciotomy and serial re-examination take priority over any ligament procedure.
- Grossly contaminated or open injury with unaddressed soft-tissue compromise — requires staged debridement and soft-tissue coverage/stabilization (often via external fixation) before graft implantation into a contaminated field.
- A stiff, markedly swollen knee with a poor ROM arc in the first 1–2 weeks — early combined cruciate reconstruction into this environment is a recognized driver of arthrofibrosis; a period of ROM optimization is preferred before definitive reconstruction.
- Medical instability (e.g., unresolved polytrauma issues, uncontrolled coagulopathy) — coordinate timing with trauma/critical care teams.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Vascular clearance is the prerequisite step before any ligament-focused intervention** — document pulses, capillary refill, and ankle-brachial index (ABI) on both legs; ABI <0.9 or any hard vascular sign (absent pulse, expanding hematoma, active bleeding, pulsatile mass) mandates CT angiography and vascular surgery consultation without imaging delay, since popliteal artery injury occurs in ~5–15% of true knee dislocations and intimal tears can present with an initially normal pulse and delayed thrombosis.
- **Imaging**: plain radiographs to confirm reduction and screen for periarticular fracture (Schenck KD-V pattern); MRI once safe to characterize the full pattern of ligament injury across all four structures (ACL, PCL, MCL, PLC), meniscal and chondral status; CTA as above if vascular injury is suspected.
- **Classify the injury** using the Schenck system (KD I–V, with C/N modifiers for vascular/neurologic injury) — this pattern, not the mechanism alone, drives the staged surgical plan and helps anticipate which structures will need repair vs. reconstruction.
- **Implant/graft planning**: plan grafts for every structure requiring reconstruction in the same sitting where possible (e.g., combined cruciate autograft/allograft needs, PLC reconstruction graft, MCL graft if operative) so that graft harvest sites and tunnel trajectories can be sequenced to avoid convergence across multiple simultaneous tunnels.
- **External fixator planning**: select a simple, low-profile unilateral frame; plan half-pin sites in the femur and tibia that stay well clear of the planned future ligament reconstruction tunnels, the vascular repair/anastomosis site, and major neurovascular structures — mini-open pin placement technique (rather than blind percutaneous insertion) is preferred to visualize and protect neurovascular structures directly, particularly around the knee where self-drilling pin tips carry a recognized risk of neurovascular injury.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: vascular set on standby/coordinated with the vascular surgery team, external fixation set (unilateral frame, pins, connecting bars), full arthroscopy tower, and the combined graft harvest/tunnel instrumentation needed for whichever ligaments are being reconstructed in each stage.
- **Multidisciplinary coordination**: confirm vascular surgery, and where relevant plastic surgery (soft-tissue coverage) and trauma teams, are aligned on the staging plan before scheduling definitive reconstruction.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is generally preferred for combined/staged multiligament cases given prolonged operative time and the need for full muscle relaxation during graft passage and provisional reduction maneuvers; regional blocks are used adjunctively for postoperative analgesia once any concern for compartment syndrome or evolving neurologic injury has been resolved (a dense block can mask a developing compartment syndrome or nerve injury and should be timed carefully in discussion with the surgical team).
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table with a leg-holder or lateral post allowing full flexion/extension and both varus and valgus stress access; the well leg is padded in a leg holder if a contralateral autograft is being harvested.
- **Tourniquet**: used selectively — often avoided or used only briefly after a vascular repair, per direct discussion with the vascular surgery team, given concern for repair thrombosis; when used, high-thigh placement with careful limb exsanguination.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm set up and draped in, especially for stages involving external fixator pin placement, tibial inlay work, or any associated fracture fixation.
- **Exam under anesthesia**: full four-ligament stress testing (Lachman, pivot-shift, posterior drawer, varus/valgus at 0° and 30°, dial test at 30°/90°) repeated at each definitive stage to confirm the pattern of residual instability, since the acute exam at presentation may have been limited by guarding, pain, or the presence of an external fixator.
- **Draping**: circumferential prep of the entire limb; if an external fixator is in place from a prior stage, prep around pin sites meticulously and consider pin-site cleaning/redressing as part of the surgical prep.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: joint line, medial and lateral epicondyles, Gerdy's tubercle, fibular head (and course of the peroneal nerve), tibial tubercle, and planned arthroscopic portal sites, adjusted as needed around any existing external fixator pins.

## Step-by-Step Technique

Managed as a representative 4-stage framework:

1. **Stage 0 — acute/ED (hours)**: obtain vascular clearance (pulses, ABI both legs, CTA if indicated); reduce the dislocation with gentle longitudinal traction and direct manipulation as needed; splint the knee in slight flexion (avoid full extension, which can re-tension a compromised popliteal artery over a displaced segment); perform and document a repeat neurovascular exam immediately after reduction, since reduction itself can precipitate or reveal vascular compromise.
2. **Stage 1 — damage control, if needed (day 0–3)**:
   - If a vascular injury is present, vascular repair takes absolute priority over any ligament-related procedure.
   - Apply bridging external fixation if the knee construct is grossly unstable, the injury is open, there is significant soft-tissue compromise, or the limb needs protection following vascular repair. Place pins via a mini-open technique to directly visualize and protect neurovascular structures, keeping pin sites well away from planned future tunnel trajectories and any vascular repair site; a simple unilateral frame spanning femur to tibia in a reduced, slightly flexed position is typical.
   - Address opportunistically any meniscal or cartilage injury that is arthroscopically accessible without stressing the healing soft-tissue envelope or prolonging anesthesia inappropriately in a patient who may have other injuries.
3. **Stage 2 — definitive reconstruction (week 2–6)**:
   - Repeat the four-ligament exam under anesthesia to reconfirm the injury pattern before committing to the reconstruction plan.
   - Address the PLC/posteromedial corner first or concurrently with cruciate work, given its poor healing biology if delayed further — this is the most time-sensitive structure in the combined plan.
   - Reconstruct the cruciate ligament(s) per the individual technique articles (ACL, PCL), sequencing tunnel placement to avoid convergence across the multiple simultaneous tunnels required in a combined case (typically PLC/collateral tunnels are drilled first as they are more peripheral, then cruciate tunnels are drilled and grafts passed last).
   - Fix collateral/corner structures with the knee reduced and cruciate grafts tensioned, generally finishing peripheral (collateral/corner) fixation after the cruciate grafts are provisionally seated so the corner is not fighting a knee that is not yet centered.
   - Address the MCL operatively only if it remains unstable at this reassessment — many MCL injuries, even Grade III, will have improved enough with bracing during the staging interval to be managed non-operatively.
   - Remove the external fixator (if present) at this stage or immediately beforehand, once soft-tissue and bony stability allow definitive bracing to take over.
4. **Stage 3 — rehabilitation (week 6 onward)**: individualized, weakest-structure-driven progressive rehabilitation as detailed below, with close surgeon-physiotherapist communication given the competing demands of arthrofibrosis prevention and protection of multiple simultaneously healing reconstructions.

**Sequencing principles**: vascular repair (if present) takes absolute priority over all ligament work. Earlier surgery (<3 weeks in the acute window) is favored particularly for PLC repair given its poor healing tissue quality if delayed, while very early combined cruciate reconstruction in a stiff, swollen knee risks arthrofibrosis — definitive reconstruction is typically staged or single-stage at 2–6 weeks post-injury once swelling resolves and a reasonable ROM arc is regained.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Treat the neurovascular exam as a repeated, documented event, not a single checkpoint — before reduction, immediately after reduction, and again at each subsequent stage; a normal pulse at presentation does not rule out an evolving intimal injury.
- When placing bridging external fixation, use a mini-open (not blind percutaneous) technique for pin insertion around the knee, specifically to visualize and protect neurovascular structures directly — self-drilling pin tips carry a recognized risk of inadvertent neurovascular injury in this region.
- Plan external fixator pin sites with the *future* definitive reconstruction tunnels already in mind — a pin placed through a planned tunnel trajectory forces a compromise in either the fixator or the reconstruction later.
- Sequence tunnel drilling from peripheral (collateral/corner) structures to central (cruciate) structures within a combined single-stage reconstruction to reduce the risk of tunnel convergence and to allow the cruciate grafts to be tensioned last against an already-stabilized periphery.
- Do not rush combined cruciate reconstruction into a knee that has not regained a reasonable ROM arc — a brief period (1–2 weeks) of dedicated motion-focused prehabilitation before Stage 2 measurably reduces arthrofibrosis risk in a procedure category where arthrofibrosis is already the single most common complication.
- Coordinate directly with the vascular surgery team on tourniquet use for any stage following a vascular repair — many centers avoid the tourniquet altogether after vascular repair rather than risk repair thrombosis, which changes visualization strategy and expected operative time.
- Build in explicit VTE chemoprophylaxis planning from Stage 0 onward given the combination of trauma, prolonged immobilization, and multiple surgical stages — this is easy to overlook amid the ligament-focused planning.
- Reassess all four ligaments under anesthesia at Stage 2, not just the structures presumed injured at initial presentation — the staging interval and initial guarding can obscure the true pattern, and a missed structure left unaddressed is the most common preventable cause of early reconstruction failure in this setting.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Failing to recognize and treat all injured structures in one coordinated plan (staged or combined) — leaving an unaddressed PLC or MCL injury is the single most common cause of early failure of cruciate reconstructions performed in the MLKI setting.
- Operating too early on combined cruciate reconstruction in a stiff, swollen knee risks arthrofibrosis, which is itself the most common complication overall in MLKI surgical series — balancing this against the time-sensitive healing biology of the PLC is a recurring technical tension.
- Missing an occult vascular injury: popliteal artery injury occurs in ~5–15% of true knee dislocations, and intimal tears can present with an initially normal pulse and delayed thrombosis — serial vascular exams (not a single normal pulse check) are mandatory, and ABI <0.9 or hard vascular signs mandate CT angiography/vascular surgery consult without imaging delay.
- Missing peroneal nerve injury (15–40% of PLC-involved dislocations) — document the neurologic exam before and after any reduction maneuver.
- Failing to watch for compartment syndrome, a recognized trigger after reperfusion following prolonged ischemia or in high-energy MLKI — check compartments serially.
- External fixator pin-site complications are common in this setting — pin-site infection is the most frequently reported complication of external fixation generally (rates up to 85% reported in some series for pin-site problems broadly), reinforcing the value of meticulous pin placement, pin-site care instructions, and prompt treatment of early inflammation before it progresses to frank infection or loosening.
- Amputation risk with delayed revascularization is severe — historically cited up to ~86% with warm ischemia beyond 8 hours versus <15% when treated within 8 hours — reinforcing why vascular workup and repair cannot be sequenced behind ligament planning under any circumstances.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Rehabilitation is individualized around the "weakest link" structure (usually the PLC or PCL, given the poorest healing/lowest mechanical tolerance): a protected, delayed-ROM approach for the first 4–6 weeks (partial/non-weight-bearing per the fixation construct), followed by progressive ROM and strengthening from 6–12 weeks.
- **Weight-bearing/ROM milestones**: non-/touch-down weight-bearing per fixation construct at 2 weeks; protected, partial weight-bearing at 6 weeks; ROM individualized to the weakest structure, often <90° by 6 weeks; brace-free ambulation around 8–12 weeks.
- Sport-specific return is generally not before 9–12 months, and often 12+ months, given the cumulative healing demands of multiple reconstructed structures.
- Arthrofibrosis prevention is a central rehab priority — early, closely supervised ROM work balanced against ligament protection is a recurring technical tension requiring close surgeon-physiotherapist communication.
- Consider VTE chemoprophylaxis per institutional protocol given the elevated risk from prolonged immobilization plus trauma.
- **Return to work**: sedentary/desk-based 4–8 weeks; light manual 3–6 months; heavy manual/labor 6–12 months, individualized.
