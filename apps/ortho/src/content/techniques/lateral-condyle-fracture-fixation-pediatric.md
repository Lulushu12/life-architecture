## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Displaced lateral condyle fractures (Jakob/Weiss II–III, generally >2 mm true displacement).
- Rotated or "flipped" fragments, and irreducible fragments blocked by interposed soft tissue.
- Fractures that progress on serial films despite initially being judged "stable" and treated nonoperatively — a meaningful minority of nonoperatively-managed fractures displace in the first 2–3 weeks.
- Fractures with an articular step-off on arthrogram/intraoperative assessment that exceeds acceptable limits even if the visible bony displacement on plain film appeared borderline.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Jakob/Weiss I fractures (<2 mm displacement, intact articular cartilage hinge) — manage with cast immobilization and close serial radiographic follow-up rather than surgery.
- An established, long-standing nonunion with significant deformity in an older child near skeletal maturity — may be better served by a different reconstructive strategy (e.g., osteotomy) than acute fixation techniques; discuss goals of treatment separately from the acute-fracture algorithm.
- Grossly comminuted fragments with inadequate bone stock for screw purchase — favor K-wire fixation or a hybrid construct instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP, lateral, and internal oblique elbow radiographs — the oblique view increases sensitivity for detecting and staging displacement that may be underappreciated on standard views. Because the visible bony fragment substantially underestimates the true (mostly cartilaginous) fracture size in young children, have a low threshold to plan for intraoperative arthrogram regardless of how the plain films look.
- **Implant selection**: plan for both K-wire and cannulated screw options to be available — 2–3 smooth 0.062" (1.6 mm) K-wires for younger children/smaller fragments, or a 3.5–4.0 mm partially-threaded cannulated screw (occasionally two smaller-diameter screws for a larger fragment) for older children/larger fragments where a physis-crossing implant carries acceptable arrest risk or where the screw can be placed largely within the epiphyseal fragment.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: image intensifier, K-wire driver and pin cutter, cannulated screw system with guide wires and appropriately sized screws/washers, and equipment for intraoperative arthrogram (contrast, small-gauge needle, or use of the arthroscope in some centers as an alternative to fluoroscopic arthrogram).
- **Positioning-specific planning**: decide preoperatively whether the approach will be percutaneous-only or will require a formal posterolateral/lateral open exposure, based on displacement grade and rotation on preoperative imaging — highly rotated or Jakob III fragments should be planned as open cases from the outset rather than attempting an extended closed/percutaneous trial.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard.
- **Positioning**: supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, or a lateral decubitus position with the arm draped over a bolster — the lateral position can improve access to the posterolateral approach if open reduction is anticipated; discuss surgeon preference in advance.
- **Table setup**: ensure the C-arm can obtain unobstructed AP, lateral, and oblique views of the elbow without repositioning the limb, since displacement and reduction are assessed across multiple projections.
- **Tourniquet**: a pneumatic tourniquet applied high on the arm is standard, inflated for the exposure/fixation portion of the case.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire arm circumferentially to allow full elbow flexion/extension and forearm rotation for fluoroscopic assessment and, if needed, extension of a percutaneous approach to a formal open exposure.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the lateral epicondyle, radial head, and the planned posterolateral or lateral (Kocher-interval) incision line before prepping, and confirm by palpation that swelling has not obscured the normal bony landmarks.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Attempt closed/percutaneous reduction first** in fractures without significant rotation: apply direct pressure over the fragment with a percutaneous elevator or bone hook under fluoroscopic guidance, correcting displacement and any rotational flip.
2. **Confirm reduction with intraoperative arthrogram or direct arthroscopic assessment** — inject contrast into the joint (or use the arthroscope) to visualize the articular cartilage surface, since the cartilaginous extent of the fracture is not reliably seen on plain fluoroscopy and the bony fragment alone underestimates true fracture size.
3. **Provisional fixation**: once anatomic reduction (including the articular surface) is confirmed, place 2–3 divergent smooth K-wires percutaneously from the lateral epicondyle across the fracture into the medial metaphyseal/condylar bone, spreading the wires to maximize construct stability rather than clustering them in a single trajectory.
4. **Screw fixation alternative**: for older children or larger fragments, place a guide wire across the reduced fragment under fluoroscopic control, confirm position on AP/lateral/oblique views, then place a partially-threaded cannulated screw (with a washer if the fragment is thin) with the threads fully past the fracture line to achieve interfragmentary compression — take care not to over-compress and displace the articular surface.
5. **Open reduction (for displaced, rotated, or irreducible fragments)**: use a posterolateral or lateral (Kocher-interval) approach, incising through the fascia and developing the interval without further stripping. Identify the fragment and fracture bed; critically, **do not strip the posterior soft-tissue attachments of the fragment** — the lateral condyle's blood supply enters posteriorly through these attachments, and posterior stripping is the single most important technical error to avoid in this fracture, predisposing to avascular change and nonunion.
6. **Reduce under direct vision**: clear hematoma and any interposed periosteum/soft tissue from the fracture bed, reduce the fragment anatomically (matching both the metaphyseal and articular surfaces), and hold provisionally with a K-wire joystick or reduction clamp placed to avoid the posterior soft-tissue hinge.
7. **Definitive fixation**: place divergent K-wires or a cannulated screw as above, again confirming reduction of the articular surface (by direct visualization and/or arthrogram) before final tightening/seating.
8. **Final fluoroscopic and clinical check**: obtain AP, lateral, and oblique fluoroscopic views to confirm anatomic reduction and appropriate implant position; assess elbow flexion/extension and forearm rotation intraoperatively to confirm no mechanical block.
9. **Closure**: close the fascial interval and skin in layers if an open approach was used; cut and bend K-wires outside the skin for later removal, or bury screws per surgeon preference.
10. **Splinting**: apply a well-padded long-arm splint or cast at approximately 90° flexion, neutral-to-slight-pronation, before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Get the internal oblique radiograph preoperatively even when the AP/lateral look only mildly displaced — this view frequently reveals displacement that changes the treatment plan from nonoperative to operative.
- Use intraoperative arthrogram (or arthroscopy where available) liberally rather than relying on the fluoroscopic appearance of the bony fragment alone — the articular cartilage surface is the true determinant of an anatomic reduction, and it is invisible on standard fluoroscopy.
- When placing K-wires, start them from slightly different points on the lateral epicondyle and aim for genuine divergence (not just two pins that happen to be a few millimeters apart at the skin) — true divergence is what resists the pull-out and rotational forces on this fragment.
- If attempting a closed/percutaneous reduction, give yourself a defined, limited number of attempts before converting to open — repeated percutaneous manipulation of a fragment that will not reduce risks further devascularizing it, which is counterproductive to the very blood-supply concern that makes this fracture unique.
- During an open approach, use blunt, gentle retraction of the posterior soft tissue rather than sharp dissection or aggressive retractor placement — even seemingly minor posterior stripping can compromise the fragment's already-tenuous blood supply.
- For screw fixation, slightly under-tension the compression on a fragile epiphyseal fragment rather than fully seating a large washer flush against thin bone — over-compression can crack or displace the articular surface.
- In younger children with a largely cartilaginous fragment, K-wire fixation (rather than screw) is often the more forgiving choice, since it does not require precise purchase within a small ossific nucleus.
- Recheck the articular reduction one final time after all fixation is placed and tightened — compression or wire placement can subtly shift a fragment that looked perfectly reduced before final fixation.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Posterior stripping of the fragment risks avascular change and nonunion — the lateral condyle physis has a watershed, largely intra-articular blood supply entering posteriorly.
- Plain radiographs routinely underestimate true cartilaginous displacement relative to arthrographic/intraoperative findings.
- Delayed/interval displacement occurs in a meaningful minority of fractures initially treated nonoperatively as "stable" — weekly radiographic follow-up is the safety net.
- K-wire and cannulated screw fixation have comparable union and complication rates in contemporary series; choose the implant based on fragment size/age rather than assuming one is uniformly superior.
- **Lateral condyle nonunion/delayed union** occurs in a meaningful minority of fractures treated nonoperatively that were initially judged "stable," from interval displacement or inherently tenuous blood supply; nonunion can progress to a symptomatic lateral spur, cubitus valgus, and **tardy ulnar nerve palsy** (a delayed ulnar neuropathy from chronic valgus stretch, occurring years after the index injury) — a classic long-term complication specific to this fracture.
- Early recognition of interval displacement or delayed union (via weekly-film follow-up when treated nonoperatively) allows salvage with percutaneous screw fixation before progression to established nonunion.
- Missed articular incongruity at the time of fixation (from relying on bony appearance alone rather than confirming the cartilaginous surface) is a preventable cause of late elbow stiffness, pain, and premature degenerative change.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Immobilize 4–6 weeks.
- K-wires are typically removed in clinic at 4–6 weeks once union is confirmed; screws may be retained or removed depending on symptoms/surgeon preference.
- Active ROM begins after pin/cast removal; expect a similar recovery trajectory to other elbow-region fractures — motion regained gradually over weeks, with return to contact sports/gymnastics/monkey bars typically permitted at 6–8 weeks once radiographic union is confirmed and functional strength/motion has returned.
- Early recognition of interval displacement or delayed union (via weekly-film follow-up when treated nonoperatively) allows salvage with percutaneous screw fixation before progression to established nonunion.
