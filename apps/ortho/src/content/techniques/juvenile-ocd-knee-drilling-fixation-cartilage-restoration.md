## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Unstable lesion, loose body, or failure of an adequate (3–6 month) non-operative trial in juvenile osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) of the knee (open distal femoral physis).
- Technique is selected by lesion characteristics rather than a single fixed protocol:
  - **Stable lesion, intact overlying cartilage, failed non-op:** transarticular or retroarticular drilling.
  - **Unstable but salvageable fragment with viable subchondral bone:** internal fixation (bioabsorbable pins or headless compression screws).
  - **Unsalvageable fragment or full-thickness cartilage defect:** debridement with microfracture, or OATS/particulated juvenile cartilage.
- Earlier arthroscopic fixation (rather than a prolonged non-operative trial) with bioabsorbable pins has support specifically in high-demand young athletes with low-grade instability, a nuance to the standard "non-operative first" algorithm for competitive athletes.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- A stable lesion in a skeletally immature, minimally symptomatic patient who has not yet completed an adequate non-operative trial — surgery is premature here given juvenile OCD's fundamentally favorable natural history compared with adult OCD.
- Active septic arthritis or overlying skin infection.
- Diffuse, advanced degenerative change in the joint (uncommon in the juvenile population but relevant in late-presenting adolescent-onset lesions) may make cartilage-restoration procedures a poor investment relative to the likely outcome — discuss expectations carefully.
- Extremely small, incidentally discovered, asymptomatic lesions on imaging obtained for another reason generally do not require surgery — treat the patient, not the MRI.
- Poor anticipated compliance with a lengthy postoperative protected weight-bearing/activity-restriction course is a relative contraindication to fixation or restoration procedures, since outcome is compliance-dependent.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP, lateral, and tunnel/notch view radiographs — the tunnel/notch view is essential and is specifically added to standard views because it best visualizes the classic posterolateral medial femoral condyle location, which is easily missed on a standard AP view. MRI for staging: the classic instability criteria are a high-signal T2 rim between fragment and parent bone, cyst formation beneath the fragment, and a breach in the overlying articular cartilage. Note that MRI staging is imperfect — arthroscopic probing at the time of surgery remains the most reliable stability assessment and preoperative counseling should reflect that the surgical plan may change based on intraoperative findings.
- **Lesion mapping:** correlate the notch-view radiograph and MRI to estimate lesion size, depth, and precise location, which drives portal planning (see below) and technique choice (transarticular vs. retroarticular).
- **Graft/implant selection:** for fixation, have both bioabsorbable pins (no hardware removal needed, but may fragment/incite synovitis if they back out or degrade unevenly) and headless compression screws (more secure interfragmentary compression, but typically require a second procedure for removal) available; decide based on fragment size/bone stock and surgeon preference. For cartilage restoration, template OATS plug size/donor site (own knee, typically the superolateral or superomedial trochlear margin — low-weight-bearing donor zones) against the defect size measured on MRI/arthroscopy; if particulated juvenile allograft cartilage is planned, confirm graft availability and size ahead of the case.
- **Bone graft for retroarticular drilling:** if performing retroarticular drilling with bone grafting, plan for an autograft harvest site — the iliac crest is the standard donor site for cancellous autograft cores.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list:** standard knee arthroscopy tower and instruments, a full set of arthroscopic probes for stability testing, small-diameter K-wires (0.045–0.062") for transarticular drilling, an extra-articular drill guide/targeting system for retroarticular drilling (or a freehand fluoroscopic technique), bioabsorbable pin or headless compression screw fixation sets in multiple sizes, and (if planned) an OATS harvesting/delivery system or particulated cartilage implantation kit.
- **Fluoroscopy:** have a C-arm available in the room for retroarticular drilling, where the drill trajectory is planned extra-articularly and confirmed with imaging rather than direct arthroscopic visualization.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia, often combined with a regional block (adductor canal or femoral) for postoperative analgesia; some centers add an intra-articular local anesthetic at closure.
- **Positioning:** supine with a lateral post or leg holder to allow varus/valgus stress for joint-line opening (medial or lateral compartment access depending on lesion location); a well-padded thigh tourniquet is standard. For retroarticular drilling specifically, a radiolucent bolster or leg holder is used to flex the knee to roughly 45°, positioned to allow adequate C-arm visualization of the posterior condyle without obstruction from the table or leg holder.
- **Tourniquet:** thigh tourniquet, inflated after limb exsanguination; document inflation time and keep total tourniquet time in mind, particularly if the case is extended by a cartilage-restoration component.
- **Fluoroscopy setup:** for retroarticular drilling, position the C-arm on the same side as the leg holder allows, oriented to obtain clean AP and lateral images of the femoral condyle and the drill trajectory without repositioning the limb between shots.
- **Arthroscopy portals:** standard anterolateral (viewing) and anteromedial (working) portals; a posteromedial or posterolateral portal may be added for direct visualization/instrumentation of a posterior condylar lesion, and an accessory portal is often needed for the classic posterolateral medial condyle location to allow perpendicular instrument/drill access to the lesion.
- **Landmarks:** mark the joint line, patellar tendon borders, and portal sites before draping; for retroarticular drilling, mark the iliac crest if bone graft harvest is planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### A. Diagnostic arthroscopy and stability assessment (all cases)

1. Establish standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals; perform a systematic diagnostic arthroscopy of all compartments.
2. Identify the OCD lesion (most commonly posterolateral medial femoral condyle) — use additional posteromedial/posterolateral portals or a 70° scope if the lesion is not fully visualized through standard anterior portals.
3. Probe the lesion directly: assess whether the overlying cartilage is intact and firmly adherent (stable), soft/ballotable but still attached ("shark bite" edge, boggy but hinged), or clearly hinged/detached with an unstable fragment or a loose body free in the joint.
4. Base the final technique decision on this intraoperative probing, which supersedes preoperative MRI staging if there is a discrepancy.

### B. Transarticular drilling (stable lesion, intact cartilage)

1. With the lesion visualized arthroscopically, pass a small-diameter K-wire (0.045–0.062") through the overlying intact cartilage and subchondral bone, directly perpendicular to the articular surface at multiple points across the lesion.
2. Space drill holes roughly 3–5 mm apart across the entire lesion to maximize the vascular channels created without excessively weakening the subchondral bone.
3. Confirm bleeding from each drill hole (a "paint-brush" pattern of punctate bleeding) as evidence of penetration into vascularized bone — absence of bleeding may indicate the hole did not reach healthy marrow and should be redone or deepened.
4. Withdraw instruments, confirm hemostasis is adequate for visualization, and close portals.

### C. Retroarticular (extra-articular) drilling ± bone grafting

1. With the knee flexed to roughly 45° on the bolster, make a small (~2 cm) incision at the chosen extra-articular entry point (commonly near the physis-sparing metaphyseal region approached from anterior or from the donor graft site if combined with iliac crest harvest).
2. If harvesting autograft, place a guide pin into the iliac crest under direct vision, tamp a cannulated drill sleeve over the pin, and harvest cancellous bone cores through the sleeve.
3. Using fluoroscopy in two planes, advance a guide pin from the extra-articular entry point toward the lesion, stopping short of breaching the articular cartilage, confirmed by fluoroscopy and (if desired) concurrent arthroscopic visualization of the subchondral bone deforming/blanching without cartilage penetration.
4. Pass a cannulated drill over the guide pin to create the retroarticular drilling channel(s) into the lesion, again confirming trajectory and depth on fluoroscopy without breaching the articular surface.
5. If bone grafting, deliver the harvested cancellous autograft cores through the same channel into the subchondral defect beneath the lesion.
6. Confirm final position on fluoroscopy and, if performed concurrently with arthroscopy, confirm the articular surface remains intact; close the graft harvest site and portal/incision.

### D. Internal fixation of an unstable but salvageable fragment

1. Reduce the fragment anatomically under direct arthroscopic (or mini-arthrotomy, if better access is needed for the fragment's location) visualization.
2. Debride the fibrous/sclerotic base of the crater and the undersurface of the fragment back to bleeding, healthy subchondral bone to promote union — this step is essential and frequently under-emphasized; fixing a fragment onto a sclerotic, non-bleeding base predisposes to non-union regardless of implant choice.
3. If the crater beneath the fragment is deficient, pack it with autograft (local or iliac crest) to restore subchondral bone stock before reduction.
4. Provisionally hold the reduced fragment with a K-wire, then place definitive fixation — bioabsorbable pins placed perpendicular to the articular surface and countersunk flush or slightly recessed, or headless compression screws providing interfragmentary compression across the fragment-parent bone interface.
5. Confirm fragment reduction (congruent articular surface, no step-off) and stable fixation by direct probing and range-of-motion testing through full flexion-extension arc under arthroscopic visualization.
6. Close portals/arthrotomy in layers.

### E. Debridement, microfracture, or OATS/particulated cartilage for an unsalvageable fragment

1. Remove the unsalvageable fragment/loose body and debride the crater edges to a stable, vertical cartilage rim.
2. Measure the defect precisely (arthroscopically or via a limited arthrotomy) to guide the restoration technique.
3. **Microfracture:** perforate the subchondral bone at the base of the defect with an arthroscopic awl at closely spaced intervals (roughly 3–4 mm apart) to a depth of a few millimeters, confirming marrow-element bleeding ("fat droplets" and blood) from each perforation, taking care not to breach into deeper subchondral bone.
4. **OATS (osteochondral autograft transfer):** harvest cylindrical osteochondral plugs from a low-weight-bearing donor site (superolateral/superomedial trochlear margin) sized to match the defect, and press-fit them into recipient sockets prepared in the defect, matching articular contour/height as closely as possible — for larger defects, a mosaicplasty pattern of multiple smaller plugs may be used to fill the area.
5. **Particulated juvenile cartilage allograft:** prepare the defect base, size a fibrin-based carrier/scaffold if used, and implant the particulated allograft cartilage into the defect per the product's specific technique (often fibrin-glue fixation without additional hardware).
6. Confirm graft/repair stability and articular congruity through a full range of motion before closing.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always let intraoperative probing — not the preoperative MRI alone — make the final call on stability; MRI instability criteria (T2 rim, cysts, cartilage breach) correlate imperfectly with true mechanical stability, and surgeons should be prepared to shift technique intraoperatively.
- Use an accessory posteromedial or posterolateral portal early rather than fighting for visualization/instrument angle through the standard anterior portals alone — the classic posterolateral medial femoral condyle lesion is notoriously difficult to access perpendicular for drilling or fixation through anterior portals only.
- For transarticular drilling, confirm punctate bleeding from every hole before finishing — a "dry" hole is a wasted pass and may reflect the drill stopping short of viable marrow.
- For retroarticular drilling, use fluoroscopy in two orthogonal planes at every step of guide-pin advancement — depth misjudgment in only one plane is the most common way to inadvertently breach the articular surface.
- When fixing an unstable fragment, do not skip debriding the crater base to bleeding bone even when tempted to save time — inadequate base preparation is one of the most reproducible causes of non-union after fixation, more so than the specific implant chosen.
- Countersink bioabsorbable pins slightly below the articular surface rather than flush — pins left proud, even minimally, can abrade the opposing tibial articular cartilage through the flexion-extension arc.
- When harvesting OATS plugs, orient the harvester perpendicular to the trochlear surface at the donor site (not perpendicular to the tibial plateau or floor) to preserve normal donor-site contour and minimize donor-site morbidity.
- Counsel athletic families before surgery that the specific procedure performed materially changes the return-to-sport timeline (4–6 months after drilling/fixation versus 9–12 months after OATS) — this affects season planning and is a common source of post-hoc dissatisfaction if not set expectations early.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Progression to instability or loose body** can occur if a non-operative trial is inadequately monitored — repeat imaging at ~3-month intervals is required to confirm healing before advancing activity.
- **Non-union** is a recognized risk after fixation, most reliably prevented by thorough debridement of the fragment base to bleeding bone and bone grafting a deficient crater rather than by implant choice alone.
- **OATS carries donor-site morbidity** — harvest from a genuinely low-weight-bearing zone and avoid over-harvesting multiple plugs from a small donor area.
- **Large or lateral/weight-bearing lesions that fail to heal** risk premature osteoarthritis — set realistic long-term expectations with the family for large lesions regardless of technique.
- **Articular cartilage breach during retroarticular drilling** is avoidable with careful two-plane fluoroscopic guide-pin advancement, stopping short of the subchondral plate and confirming trajectory before final drilling.
- **Hardware-related complications** — bioabsorbable pin fragmentation/synovitis if the implant degrades unevenly, or a retained headless compression screw requiring a planned second procedure for removal — should be discussed at consent.
- Juvenile (open physis) OCD has a fundamentally better prognosis than adult OCD due to ongoing bone remodeling capacity and typically smaller, more peripheral lesions — this favors a genuine non-operative trial before surgery in most stable lesions, though earlier arthroscopic fixation with bioabsorbable pins has been supported specifically in high-demand young athletes with low-grade instability.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Drilling/fixation:** protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks, progressive ROM and quadriceps strengthening; return to sport generally 4–6 months after fixation/drilling once imaging confirms healing.
- **OATS/cartilage restoration:** longer recovery, return to sport typically 9–12 months.
- Non-operative trial (for context/comparison): weeks 0–12 activity/impact restriction with brace or cast in younger/more symptomatic patients; month 3 repeat imaging to assess healing; months 4–6 progressive strengthening and functional loading, with full-impact/pivoting sport only after confirmed radiographic/clinical healing.
