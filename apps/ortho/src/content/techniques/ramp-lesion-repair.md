## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:

- Performed concurrently with ACL reconstruction whenever the ramp tear (a tear/avulsion of the peripheral attachment of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus to the posteromedial capsule and meniscotibial/coronary ligament) is unstable on probing — greater than 2 mm mobility on hook-probe testing through the notch or a posteromedial portal.
- Repair is now considered standard of care when an unstable ramp lesion is identified, because unrepaired lesions left in situ at the time of ACLR are associated with increased residual anteroposterior and rotational laxity, higher graft strain, and inferior second-look healing/stability compared with repaired lesions.
- Ramp lesions affect up to 17–42% of ACL-injured knees (range depends on detection method).
- A "reverse ramp" (meniscotibial ligament avulsion, tibial-sided rather than femoral/capsular-sided) is a distinct, easily missed pattern that also warrants repair once identified, particularly in high-grade-laxity ACL cases.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:

- Stable, non-displaceable lesions (<2 mm mobility on probing) do not require formal suture repair — these may be left alone or simply trephinated/abraded to stimulate healing rather than exposed to the added time/risk of a formal repair construct.
- Isolated ramp lesion without a concurrent ACL tear is uncommon and, when found, should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis or a search for occult instability rather than automatic repair in isolation — evidence for repair benefit is specifically in the concurrent-ACLR context.
- Severely degenerated peripheral tissue unlikely to hold suture — treat as a debridement/abrasion case rather than forcing a repair construct that will not heal.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: standard MRI has only moderate sensitivity for ramp lesions (~55–75%) — posteromedial fluid/edema at the meniscocapsular junction raises suspicion but a "normal" MRI does not exclude a ramp lesion, so plan to probe arthroscopically in every ACL reconstruction case regardless of MRI findings.
- **Plan the diagnostic approach**: decide whether trans-notch viewing (through the intercondylar notch, often after a partial notchplasty or during the ACL tunnel-preparation stage) or a dedicated posteromedial portal will be the primary visualization method, and have both available, since the tear may only become fully apparent from one view.
- **Implant/equipment selection**: have both an all-inside device system (suitable via posteromedial portal or trans-notch approach) and an inside-out suture-passing/cannula set (for a posteromedial incision) available — the choice is frequently made intraoperatively based on tear location (femoral/capsular-sided vs. tibial-sided/meniscotibial "reverse ramp") and accessibility.
- **Sequence with the ACL reconstruction**: decide whether ramp assessment/repair will be performed before or after ACL tunnel drilling — probing and repair are often easier before the tibial tunnel and graft are in place, but some surgeons prefer to complete graft passage first to avoid fluid extravasation through an open posteromedial capsule affecting later steps; plan and communicate this sequence explicitly.
- **Equipment/instrumentation checklist**: 70° arthroscope (improves the trans-notch view of the posterior compartment compared with a standard 30° scope), a hooked probe for stability testing, an 18-gauge spinal needle for posteromedial portal localization by transillumination/palpation, all-inside meniscal repair device(s), and inside-out cannulas/needles with a posteromedial protective retractor if that technique is anticipated.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia, typically with a femoral or adductor canal nerve block as part of the standard ACL reconstruction anesthetic plan.
- **Positioning**: supine with a lateral post or leg holder; probing and repair of the ramp lesion are performed with the knee in a figure-4 position (hip flexed and externally rotated, knee flexed) to relax the posteromedial capsule and open the posteromedial compartment for both visualization and portal/instrument access.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh tourniquet applied and available per standard ACL reconstruction setup.
- **Setup**: standard arthroscopic fluid inflow/outflow system as for ACL reconstruction; have a 70° arthroscope available in the room in addition to the standard 30° scope, since it materially improves the trans-notch view of the posteromedial compartment.
- **Landmarks**: standard anteromedial and anterolateral ACL reconstruction portals; localize the posteromedial portal site by transillumination and needle palpation with the knee in the figure-4 position before making the incision — confirming the needle tip position under direct arthroscopic view first is the safest way to place this portal without injuring the saphenous nerve or MCL.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Probe the posterior horn of the medial meniscus in a figure-4 position during every ACL reconstruction — this step is essential, especially with high-grade preoperative laxity (pivot shift 2+/3+).
2. Visualize via a trans-notch view (switching to or supplementing with a 70° arthroscope through the notch, after partial notchplasty if needed for access) or a posteromedial portal; standard anterior-portal viewing alone roughly halves detection compared with the medial gutter view via notch or posteromedial portal.
3. If using a posteromedial portal, localize it first with an 18-gauge spinal needle under direct arthroscopic visualization (confirming safe entry posterior to the MCL and anterior to the medial gastrocnemius) before making the skin incision, minimizing risk to the saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch.
4. Assess stability: use a hooked probe to test mobility of the posterior horn meniscocapsular junction — greater than 2 mm mobility on hook-probe testing indicates an unstable lesion requiring repair; stable/non-displaceable lesions may be observed or trephinated/abraded with a rasp or shaver to stimulate a healing response without formal suture fixation.
5. For unstable lesions, choose the technique:
   - **All-inside device repair** via posteromedial portal or trans-notch approach — now the most common approach, avoiding a separate posteromedial incision; deploy the device across the tear under direct visualization, taking care to capture both the meniscal rim and the capsular/meniscotibial tissue in the repair construct.
   - **Inside-out repair** from a posteromedial incision — remains an excellent option, particularly for more peripheral meniscocapsular/meniscotibial ligament avulsions ("reverse ramp"/hidden lesions) not well captured by all-inside devices; pass a suture hook through the notch or posteromedial portal, taking the deep (capsular) leaf of the meniscotibial ligament and the anterior portion of the tear under direct visualization, then retrieve and tie the sutures through the protected posteromedial incision.
6. For a suspected "reverse ramp" (tibial-sided meniscotibial ligament avulsion), specifically inspect the tibial-sided attachment separately from the more commonly assessed femoral/capsular-sided tear pattern — this variant is easily missed even with a good trans-notch view and requires deliberate posteromedial portal inspection.
7. Confirm repair stability by re-probing the meniscocapsular junction after suture placement — the previously mobile segment should now be firmly fixed to the capsule/tibial rim.
8. Perform the repair concurrently with the ACL reconstruction, sequencing it relative to tunnel drilling and graft passage per the surgeon's preoperative plan.
9. Irrigate the posteromedial compartment thoroughly to clear debris before completing tunnel preparation or graft passage, and close the posteromedial incision (if used) in layered fashion once the ACL reconstruction is complete.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Make probing the posterior horn of the medial meniscus a fixed, non-negotiable step of every ACL reconstruction, performed early before other instrumentation crowds the notch — a missed ramp lesion is a missed opportunity that cannot be easily corrected later in the case.
- Switch to a 70° arthroscope for the trans-notch view — the improved posterior visualization it provides over a standard 30° scope frequently reveals lesions that were not appreciated with the anterior-portal view alone.
- Localize the posteromedial portal with a spinal needle under direct visualization before incising — this single step meaningfully reduces the risk to the saphenous nerve and confirms the trajectory will reach the meniscocapsular junction without an obstructed approach.
- Use the figure-4 position deliberately — it relaxes the posteromedial capsule and opens working space for both the probe and any instruments passed through the posteromedial portal.
- When passing an inside-out suture hook for a ramp repair, deliberately capture the deep (capsular) leaf of the meniscotibial ligament, not just the superficial meniscal rim — this is what actually restores the meniscocapsular attachment's biomechanical function.
- Specifically look for the tibial-sided "reverse ramp" pattern as a separate step from femoral/capsular-sided assessment — it is a distinct lesion easily missed by an examiner who stops after confirming a femoral-sided ramp is (or is not) present.
- Sequence the ramp repair before final ACL graft tensioning where possible — access to the posteromedial compartment can become more difficult once the graft and fixation hardware are in place.
- Trephination/abrasion of a stable lesion, rather than formal repair, is a reasonable and time-efficient choice — reserve the added complexity of suture repair for lesions that actually demonstrate instability on probing.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Standard MRI sensitivity for ramp lesions is only moderate (~55–75%) — a "normal" MRI does not exclude a ramp lesion; probe arthroscopically in every ACL case.
- A reverse ramp (meniscotibial ligament avulsion) variant is easily missed even with a trans-notch view and requires posteromedial portal inspection, especially in high-grade-laxity cases.
- Leaving an unstable ramp lesion unrepaired increases residual AP and rotational laxity, raises graft strain, and produces inferior second-look healing/stability compared with repair.
- Posteromedial portal placement without needle localization risks injury to the saphenous nerve (infrapatellar branch) or the MCL — always confirm the trajectory under direct visualization first.
- Capturing only the superficial meniscal rim (rather than the deep capsular/meniscotibial leaf) during suture passage produces a repair that looks secure but does not actually restore the meniscocapsular junction's stabilizing function.
- Fluid extravasation into the posteromedial soft tissues during prolonged posteromedial-compartment work can obscure landmarks later in the case — work efficiently once the portal is established and monitor pump pressure/outflow.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not specify a ramp-repair-specific rehabilitation timeline distinct from the concurrent ACL reconstruction protocol. Rehabilitation should follow the 2024 formal EU-US Meniscus Rehabilitation Consensus (ESSKA-AOSSM-AASPT) framework — criteria-based, not purely time-based, progression individualized to tear pattern/location, repair construct, and the concurrent ACLR procedure.
