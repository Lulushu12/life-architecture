## Indications

- Performed concurrently with ACL reconstruction whenever the ramp tear (a tear/avulsion of the peripheral attachment of the posterior horn of the medial meniscus to the posteromedial capsule and meniscotibial/coronary ligament) is unstable on probing — greater than 2 mm mobility on hook-probe testing through the notch or a posteromedial portal.
- Stable, non-displaceable lesions may be left alone or simply trephinated/abraded to stimulate healing.
- Repair is now considered standard of care when an unstable ramp lesion is identified, because unrepaired lesions left in situ at the time of ACLR are associated with increased residual anteroposterior and rotational laxity, higher graft strain, and inferior second-look healing/stability compared with repaired lesions.
- Ramp lesions affect up to 17–42% of ACL-injured knees (range depends on detection method).

## Positioning & setup

Probing is performed with the knee in a figure-4 position. Because the tear lies posterior to the PCL, out of the direct line of sight from a standard anterior arthroscopic portal, visualization requires a trans-notch view (through the intercondylar notch) or a dedicated posteromedial portal.

## Steps

1. Probe the posterior horn of the medial meniscus in a figure-4 position during every ACL reconstruction — this step is essential, especially with high-grade preoperative laxity (pivot shift 2+/3+).
2. Visualize via a trans-notch view or posteromedial portal; standard anterior-portal viewing alone roughly halves detection compared with the medial gutter view via notch or posteromedial portal.
3. Assess stability: >2 mm mobility on hook-probe testing indicates an unstable lesion requiring repair; stable/non-displaceable lesions may be observed or trephinated/abraded to stimulate healing.
4. For unstable lesions, choose the technique:
   - **All-inside device repair** via posteromedial portal or trans-notch approach — now the most common approach, avoiding a separate posteromedial incision.
   - **Inside-out repair** from a posteromedial incision — remains an excellent option, particularly for more peripheral meniscocapsular/meniscotibial ligament avulsions ("reverse ramp"/hidden lesions) not well captured by all-inside devices.
5. Perform the repair concurrently with the ACL reconstruction.

## Pitfalls

- Standard MRI sensitivity for ramp lesions is only moderate (~55–75%) — a "normal" MRI does not exclude a ramp lesion; probe arthroscopically in every ACL case.
- A reverse ramp (meniscotibial ligament avulsion) variant is easily missed even with a trans-notch view and requires posteromedial portal inspection, especially in high-grade-laxity cases.
- Leaving an unstable ramp lesion unrepaired increases residual AP and rotational laxity, raises graft strain, and produces inferior second-look healing/stability compared with repair.

## Post-op protocol

The monograph does not specify a ramp-repair-specific rehabilitation timeline distinct from the concurrent ACL reconstruction protocol. Rehabilitation should follow the 2024 formal EU-US Meniscus Rehabilitation Consensus (ESSKA-AOSSM-AASPT) framework — criteria-based, not purely time-based, progression individualized to tear pattern/location, repair construct, and the concurrent ACLR procedure.
