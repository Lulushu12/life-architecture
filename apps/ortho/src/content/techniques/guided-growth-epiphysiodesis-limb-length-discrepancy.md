## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- **Predicted LLD 2–5/6 cm at maturity:** guided growth (temporary hemiepiphysiodesis, 8-plate) of the longer limb, or permanent epiphysiodesis (PETS or Phemister) timed via the Paley multiplier.
- **Predicted LLD >5–6 cm (up to 20+ cm):** limb lengthening (external fixator or magnetic internal nail) ± contralateral epiphysiodesis, staged for very large discrepancies (e.g., PFFD, severe congenital deficiencies).
- **<2 cm predicted LLD:** not a surgical indication — observation or a shoe lift if symptomatic.
- Guided growth is additionally indicated when a combined angular deformity needs correction alongside length (asymmetric plate placement corrects angulation as it modulates growth).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Insufficient growth remaining to achieve meaningful correction (guided growth and epiphysiodesis both depend on the physis being open long enough for the intervention to act) — verify with a current bone-age assessment before committing to a plan.
- Active or recent infection at the planned physis (increases risk of iatrogenic growth disturbance).
- Unpredictable or highly asymmetric growth patterns (e.g., irradiated physis, prior growth-plate injury with partial arrest, some skeletal dysplasias) make multiplier-based timing unreliable — favor a more conservative, closely monitored guided-growth approach with serial re-measurement over a single-shot permanent epiphysiodesis in these patients.
- Limb lengthening is relatively contraindicated in active smokers (adolescents), poorly controlled diabetes, and patients/families unable to commit to the intensive follow-up and pin-care regimen it requires — a frank discussion of adherence demands is part of informed consent.
- Vascular anomalies or prior vascular injury in the limb should prompt vascular surgery consultation before lengthening, given the risk of relative arterial insufficiency with rapid distraction.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/measurement:** full-length standing EOS/scanogram radiograph is the gold standard for measuring the current discrepancy — obtain serially (roughly annually, more often near the planned intervention) to project growth and confirm the trend matches prediction. CT scanogram is an acceptable alternative when EOS is unavailable, at the cost of higher radiation.
- **Growth prediction:** determine skeletal age (Greulich-Pyle atlas or similar) and apply the **Paley multiplier method** — multiply the current limb-segment length (or LLD) by an age- and sex-specific multiplier to predict the value at maturity. Cross-check against the growth-remaining data for the physis in question (distal femur ~9–10 mm/yr, proximal tibia ~6 mm/yr) since these two physes contribute the great majority of lower-limb longitudinal growth.
- **Timing the procedure:** for permanent epiphysiodesis, back-calculate from the predicted maturity discrepancy so that arresting the longer limb's physis (or a combination of distal femoral/proximal tibial physes) allows the shorter limb to equalize by skeletal maturity — err on the side of slight undercorrection in borderline cases, since a residual small LLD is far easier to manage than an iatrogenic reversal of limb dominance.
- **Implant/technique selection:** choose an 8-plate/tension-band construct (guided growth) versus PETS (percutaneous transphyseal screw) versus open Phemister epiphysiodesis based on age, bone quality, and whether reversibility is wanted — guided growth is reversible (growth resumes after removal); PETS and Phemister are intended as permanent, definitive arrests. For lengthening, decide between an external fixator (Ilizarov ring or monolateral/hexapod frame — allows simultaneous deformity correction, lower device cost, but pin-site burden) versus an internal magnetic motorized nail (e.g., PRECICE — better tolerated, no external pin sites, but requires adequate canal diameter/bone length and is substantially more expensive); confirm canal diameter and planned nail length against the manufacturer's sizing chart preoperatively.
- **Multidisciplinary planning for large discrepancies:** staged combinations (lengthening + contralateral epiphysiodesis) require a written multi-year plan shared with the family, typically mapped out at the first consultation with expected ages for each stage.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list:** fluoroscopy, cannulated screw sets in pediatric sizes, tension-band plate system, PETS-specific cannulated screw/guide system if used, external fixator components or the magnetic nail system and its external remote control unit (bring the manufacturer's targeting device to the OR), and a leg-length block set for intraoperative confirmation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia for all these procedures; regional block (femoral/adductor canal or sciatic) is a useful analgesic adjunct, especially for lengthening surgery or open Phemister epiphysiodesis.
- **Positioning:** supine on a radiolucent table for distal femoral/proximal tibial guided growth, PETS, and most epiphysiodesis procedures, with a bump to neutralize limb rotation; confirm the contralateral limb is accessible for comparison fluoroscopy if bilateral or comparative work is needed.
- **Tourniquet:** optional for percutaneous guided-growth/PETS procedures (often not needed given minimal exposure); a sterile or non-sterile thigh tourniquet is reasonable for open Phemister epiphysiodesis or if combined with an osteotomy.
- **Fluoroscopy:** essential throughout — position the C-arm to obtain unrotated AP and lateral views of the physis of interest (distal femoral, proximal tibial, or proximal femoral, depending on the segment being modulated) without moving the limb between views.
- **Draping:** prep and drape the entire limb free, including the contralateral limb if length comparison or simultaneous contralateral epiphysiodesis is planned, from groin to toes.
- **Landmarks:** mark the joint line and physis location (confirmed with a preoperative fluoroscopic shot before incision) for each plate/screw site; for lengthening, mark the planned osteotomy/corticotomy level and the fixator pin/wire sites according to the preoperative plan.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### A. Guided growth (temporary hemiepiphysiodesis, 8-plate)

1. Confirm the correct limb, correct physis (distal femoral and/or proximal tibial, medial and/or lateral depending on whether length alone or combined angular correction is intended), and side with the team.
2. Under fluoroscopy, identify the target physis on a true AP view.
3. Make a small longitudinal incision directly over the physis; split the overlying fascia/periosteum longitudinally and expose the physis extraperiosteally.
4. Position the plate straddling the physis, one screw hole epiphyseal and one metaphyseal, centered on the physis on both AP and lateral fluoroscopic views.
5. Place the epiphyseal screw first (angled to remain extraphyseal, roughly parallel to the joint line), then the metaphyseal screw.
6. For pure length correction, place the plate centrally (medial and lateral plates, or a single central plate depending on system) to arrest the whole physis symmetrically; for combined angular correction, place the plate eccentrically (e.g., medial-only for a valgus-producing tether) to bias growth.
7. Confirm plate and screw position on final fluoroscopy; close in layers.
8. Document the exact date of surgery — correction rate tracking and the removal-timing decision both depend on knowing precisely how long the tether has been in place.

### B. Permanent epiphysiodesis — percutaneous transphyseal screw (PETS)

1. Confirm the physis and timing against the Paley multiplier calculation performed preoperatively.
2. Under fluoroscopy, place a guide pin across the physis from the metaphysis into the epiphysis, centered in both planes.
3. Confirm pin position on AP and lateral views before proceeding.
4. Insert a single large-diameter cannulated screw over the guide pin, crossing the physis completely — the screw is left in place permanently, mechanically tethering and eventually fusing the physis.
5. Repeat for any additional physes being arrested in the same setting (e.g., simultaneous distal femoral and proximal tibial arrest for a larger discrepancy, or symmetric bilateral arrest for excessive predicted overall stature in some indications).
6. Confirm final screw position and depth on fluoroscopy; close the small stab incision.

### C. Permanent epiphysiodesis — open (Phemister) technique

1. Make a longitudinal incision directly over the physis at the planned site.
2. Develop a small cortical window centered on the physis, exposing a block of bone that includes metaphysis, physis, and epiphysis.
3. Rotate the excised bone block 90° (physis now oriented perpendicular to its native plane) and reinsert it, or curette the physeal cartilage from the window directly, to produce a permanent, localized physeal arrest.
4. Repeat at additional quadrants/sites around the physis if a more complete arrest is desired, or rely on a single well-placed window for a partial, growth-modulating arrest.
5. Close in layers.

### D. Limb lengthening — external fixator

1. Apply the fixator frame (Ilizarov ring or monolateral/hexapod) with reference wires/pins placed under fluoroscopic guidance, parallel to the adjacent joint lines, before performing the osteotomy.
2. Perform a low-energy corticotomy (multiple drill holes connected with an osteotome, preserving periosteal/soft-tissue sleeve) at the planned level, typically metaphyseal bone for its superior healing potential.
3. Confirm frame stability and osteotomy completion on fluoroscopy; close incisions/pin sites.
4. Begin distraction after a latency period (typically 5–7 days) at a standard rate of ~1 mm/day, divided into 3–4 smaller increments per day to optimize regenerate quality and patient tolerance.
5. Follow with serial radiographs (typically every 1–2 weeks) to monitor regenerate bone formation, adjust rate if regenerate is too thin (slow down) or prematurely consolidating (speed up), and to correct any angular/rotational drift with frame adjustments (hexapod struts or ring re-alignment).
6. Once the target length (and any concurrent deformity correction) is achieved, hold the frame in neutral fixation through the consolidation phase until adequate cortical bridging (typically 3 of 4 cortices) is confirmed before frame removal.

### E. Limb lengthening — internal magnetic nail (e.g., PRECICE)

1. Confirm canal diameter and planned nail length against preoperative templating.
2. Ream the canal to the appropriate diameter through a standard antegrade or retrograde entry point depending on the segment (femur or tibia).
3. Perform the osteotomy/corticotomy at the planned level using a low-energy technique to preserve blood supply.
4. Pass the nail across the osteotomy and lock it proximally and distally per the manufacturer's targeting guide, confirming osteotomy alignment on fluoroscopy before final locking.
5. Confirm the magnetic mechanism actuates correctly on the table using the external remote control unit before closing.
6. Begin distraction (after a similar latency period) using the external remote control device at home, typically in small increments multiple times per day to reach the standard ~1 mm/day rate, with scheduled clinic radiographs to monitor progress and adjust rate.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Photograph or explicitly document the surgery date and initial radiographic measurements for guided growth — correction rate (roughly 0.5–1°/month for angular work, and length-specific rates for pure length tethers) is only interpretable against a clear baseline, and families/other providers often need this recalled at follow-up.
- When placing a guided-growth plate for pure length correction, center it as precisely as possible on the physeal midline in both planes — a plate that drifts anterior/posterior or medial/lateral of center risks introducing an unwanted angular deformity while chasing length correction.
- Build in a small margin of conservatism when applying the Paley multiplier — because growth prediction always carries some individual variability, many surgeons deliberately time epiphysiodesis to leave a residual 0–1 cm discrepancy rather than aim for exact equalization, since a small residual LLD is easily tolerated (or lift-managed) while overcorrection reversing limb dominance is harder to accept for patients and families.
- For PETS or Phemister epiphysiodesis performed near-simultaneously at two levels (distal femur and proximal tibia) to accelerate the arrest, stage the screws/windows so intraoperative fluoroscopy time is minimized — plan both sites' fluoroscopic approach before draping.
- In limb lengthening, favor a metaphyseal osteotomy level over diaphyseal when anatomically feasible — the denser cancellous bone and richer blood supply produce more reliable and faster regenerate consolidation.
- Divide the daily lengthening rate (whether fixator or magnetic nail) into multiple small increments (e.g., 4 x 0.25 mm rather than 1 x 1 mm) — this is standard practice to reduce soft-tissue and neurovascular strain and improves patient tolerance and regenerate quality.
- Counsel families up front that guided growth requires a genuine commitment to follow-up every 4–6 months — missed visits are the most common reason for an otherwise well-executed guided-growth case to overshoot or undershoot its target.
- For very large discrepancies requiring staged lengthening, sequence the contralateral epiphysiodesis (if planned) to coincide with a natural point in the multi-year plan rather than as an afterthought — timing it too early forfeits growth that could have contributed to overall final height, too late fails to close the gap.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Mistimed epiphysiodesis** — from multiplier miscalculation or unexpected growth variation — produces overcorrection or undercorrection; serial re-measurement remains the best safeguard.
- **An 8-plate placed asymmetrically or removed too early** can produce iatrogenic angular deformity rather than pure length correction — confirm central, on-physis placement at the time of surgery and track correction with serial films rather than removing on a fixed calendar alone.
- **Pin/screw breakage or migration** is a recognized hardware complication with guided-growth constructs — include this in follow-up screening.
- **Limb lengthening carries pin-tract infection, joint contracture, premature consolidation, and neurovascular injury risk**, particularly with larger or faster corrections — pin-site care education and a structured physiotherapy program (maintaining joint ROM through the distraction phase) mitigate contracture risk; watch closely for signs of nerve traction injury (paresthesia, weakness) with rapid distraction and slow the rate immediately if these appear.
- **<2 cm predicted LLD rarely warrants surgery** — observation or a shoe lift if symptomatic is first-line; surgical correction is reserved for predicted discrepancies above this threshold, and operating on a marginal discrepancy risks converting a trivial problem into an iatrogenic one.
- **Premature regenerate consolidation** during external fixator lengthening (regenerate becomes densely mineralized before the target length is reached) requires either accelerating the rate or, if consolidation is well established, further percutaneous corticotomy/re-osteotomy — recognized on serial radiographs by a thin, poorly visible regenerate column that is failing to elongate proportionally with frame adjustment.
- **Regenerate that is too thin/lucent** ("lengthening too fast for the biology") predisposes to fracture through the regenerate after frame removal — slow the distraction rate and extend the consolidation (neutral fixation) period before removal.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated immediately in most percutaneous techniques (guided growth, PETS).
- Routine follow-up every 4–6 months with standing radiographs to monitor correction rate and avoid overcorrection.
- Implant removal (guided growth) timed to the desired correction endpoint.
