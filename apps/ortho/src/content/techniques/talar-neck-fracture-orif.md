## Indications

Hawkins II–IV talar neck fractures require reduction and ORIF; Hawkins I is generally non-operative unless minimally displaced, in which case percutaneous screws are an option. Any dislocated talar fracture with skin tenting or vascular compromise is a reduction emergency independent of when definitive fixation occurs.

## Positioning & setup

Dual anteromedial and anterolateral approaches are used to visualize both columns of the neck without further devascularizing the body. Body or comminuted fractures may require a medial malleolar osteotomy for adequate visualization.

## Steps

1. **Perform emergent closed reduction** for any dislocated talar fracture with skin compromise: sedation, longitudinal traction with the foot plantarflexed, then correction of deformity. This is a surgical/reduction emergency; if closed reduction fails, proceed to open reduction to relieve skin tension and vascular kink.
2. **Delay definitive ORIF** until soft tissues allow, typically within days — contemporary data show timing to definitive fixation (<24 h vs later) does not clearly change AVN rate once reduction is achieved, so urgent reduction, not necessarily emergent final fixation, is the priority.
3. **Expose via dual anteromedial + anterolateral approaches** to visualize both columns of the neck; add a medial malleolar osteotomy for body/comminuted fractures needing more extensile visualization.
4. **Protect the deltoid branch of the posterior tibial artery** during medial exposure — it is often the sole surviving blood supply after a displaced neck fracture.
5. **Achieve anatomic reduction** — this is the single most important modifiable determinant of outcome, more so than fixation method or timing.
6. **Fix with headless compression screws**, posterior-to-anterior preferred biomechanically, or anterior-to-posterior when a posterior approach is impractical.
7. **Add a medial or lateral neutralization/buttress plate** for comminuted neck fractures to prevent varus malunion, the most common malunion pattern and cause of lateral column overload.

## Pitfalls

- Varus malunion is the classic talar neck complication, causing lateral column overload — use a neutralization/buttress plate in comminuted patterns to prevent it; if symptomatic, it requires corrective osteotomy.
- AVN and post-traumatic arthritis rates remain high even with anatomic reduction, particularly for Hawkins III/IV — counsel patients accordingly.
- Core decompression is not evidence-supported for established talar AVN; manage collapse with staged bracing/protected weight-bearing and, if progressive, tibiotalocalcaneal fusion or talar body allograft/prosthetic replacement.
- Nonunion is a recognized (though less common) risk specifically in comminuted talar neck fractures.

## Post-op protocol

- 0–6 weeks: strict NWB, splint/cast, elevation; watch closely for wound issues given the thin soft-tissue envelope.
- 6–12 weeks: progressive weight-bearing guided by radiographic union — talar fractures unite more slowly than most foot/ankle fractures; begin only once trabecular bridging is evident, since premature WB risks talar body collapse in AVN-prone fractures.
- 3–6 months: continue AVN surveillance with serial radiographs (Hawkins sign timing, subchondral collapse); if AVN develops, protected weight-bearing/bracing until revascularization or collapse dictates salvage surgery.
- Return to sport/heavy labor is generally deferred to 6–12 months given slow union and the AVN risk window. Sedentary work 6–12 weeks.
