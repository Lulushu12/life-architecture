## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Hawkins II–IV talar neck fractures require reduction and ORIF.
- Hawkins I fractures that are minimally displaced or of uncertain stability — percutaneous screw fixation is a reasonable option even though most Hawkins I fractures are treated non-operatively.
- Any dislocated talar fracture with skin tenting or vascular compromise — a reduction emergency independent of when definitive fixation occurs.
- Open talar neck fractures, which are common given the subcutaneous location of the talar body and neck.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Truly nondisplaced Hawkins I fractures — treat with NWB cast/boot immobilization.
- Severely comminuted talar body fractures in a medically unstable or non-ambulatory patient where primary fusion or amputation-level decision-making may be more appropriate than heroic reconstruction — discuss goals of care.
- Grossly contaminated open injuries or active infection — staged treatment (irrigation/debridement, external fixation, delayed definitive fixation) takes priority over primary ORIF.
- Established avascular necrosis with collapse at presentation (missed/delayed injury) — ORIF is not indicated; management shifts to protected weight-bearing/bracing or salvage reconstruction.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: CT (1 mm cuts, with sagittal/coronal/axial reconstructions) is mandatory for surgical planning in any displaced talar neck fracture — plain radiographs (AP, mortise, lateral, and a Canale view for the neck) grossly understate comminution and articular involvement. MRI is reserved for occult fracture or later AVN surveillance, not acute planning.
- **Classification**: confirm the Hawkins type (I–IV, with the Canale-Kelly Type IV modification for associated talonavicular subluxation/dislocation) — this drives urgency of reduction and prognostic counseling, though not necessarily the timing of definitive fixation.
- **Implant selection**: headless compression screws (3.5–4.5 mm range, both fully and partially threaded options available) for simple two-part neck fractures; a low-profile neutralization/buttress plate (medial or lateral, contoured to the talar neck) for comminuted patterns to prevent varus collapse. Have both anterior-to-posterior and posterior-to-anterior screw options planned, since final trajectory often depends on which approach affords the better starting point intraoperatively.
- **Equipment**: small distractor or mini external fixator (calcaneus-to-tibia or talar neck-to-tibia pins) to aid exposure and reduction of a comminuted or impacted fracture, a headlamp for deep visualization, joysticks (K-wires or small Schanz pins in the talar head and body) for manipulating fragments, and a lamina spreader for the joint. Plan for a medial malleolar osteotomy set (oscillating saw, osteotomy guide, and pre-drilled/tapped screws for later fixation of the osteotomy) if body involvement or comminution is anticipated to require more extensile visualization.
- **Case-specific planning**: for any dislocated fracture with skin compromise, plan for emergent closed (or open, if closed fails) reduction in the emergency department or operating room before definitive imaging/fixation is finalized — do not delay reduction awaiting a CT scan. Counsel the patient and family preoperatively about the high likelihood of AVN and post-traumatic arthritis, particularly for Hawkins III/IV, regardless of how well the fixation goes.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia (preferred for the sedation/relaxation needed for reduction maneuvers, particularly in the emergent-reduction setting) with a popliteal sciatic ± saphenous block for postoperative analgesia; emergent closed reduction alone (before definitive fixation) can sometimes be accomplished under procedural sedation if the OR is not immediately available.
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to internally rotate the limb for the anteromedial approach, and the ability to externally rotate for the anterolateral approach without repositioning — both approaches are typically performed from the same supine position through separate incisions.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet, inflated for the exposure and reduction; deflate before final fixation checks/closure to confirm perfusion, particularly important given the talus's precarious blood supply.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm positioned for AP, mortise, lateral, and Canale views (the Canale view — foot maximally plantarflexed with the beam angled 15° cephalad — profiles the talar neck in its long axis and is essential for judging neck reduction and screw trajectory).
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire limb free from above the tourniquet to the toes to allow full manipulation of the foot for traction/reduction maneuvers and to access both anteromedial and anterolateral windows.
- **Landmarks**: mark the tibialis anterior tendon (lateral border of the anteromedial approach), the anterior tibial artery/deep peroneal nerve and extensor tendons (anterolateral approach), the medial malleolus (if an osteotomy is planned), and the sinus tarsi, before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Perform emergent closed reduction** for any dislocated talar fracture with skin compromise: with adequate sedation/anesthesia, apply longitudinal traction with the foot plantarflexed to disengage the fragments, then correct the deformity (typically by reversing the deforming force — plantarflexion and inversion for a medially displaced/dislocated head-neck fragment) and gently dorsiflex once reduced. If closed reduction fails, proceed immediately to open reduction (through whichever approach best addresses the blocking structure) to relieve skin tension and the vascular kink on the posterior tibial vessels/deltoid branch.
2. **Delay definitive ORIF** until soft tissues allow, typically within days — contemporary data show timing to definitive fixation (<24 h vs later) does not clearly change AVN rate once reduction is achieved, so urgent reduction, not necessarily emergent final fixation, is the priority.
3. **Expose via the anteromedial approach** first in most cases: incise just medial to the tibialis anterior tendon, working through the interval to expose the medial talar neck and head while protecting the saphenous vein/nerve. This approach also gives access to the medial talar body if a malleolar osteotomy becomes necessary.
4. **Expose via the anterolateral approach** in the interval lateral to the extensor digitorum longus/peroneus tertius, protecting the intermediate dorsal cutaneous nerve and the anterior tibial artery/deep peroneal nerve neurovascular bundle medially — this approach visualizes the lateral talar neck and the sinus tarsi, and is often where the primary comminution/impaction is best assessed and where the lateral screw or plate is placed.
5. **Add a medial malleolar osteotomy** for body/comminuted fractures needing more extensile visualization: pre-drill and tap the osteotomy site before cutting (so hardware seats predictably at closure), perform a chevron or oblique osteotomy directed toward the plafond under fluoroscopic guidance, and hinge the fragment (with the deltoid ligament attached) distally to preserve its blood supply while exposing the medial talar body/dome.
6. **Protect the deltoid branch of the posterior tibial artery** throughout medial exposure and reduction maneuvers — it is often the sole surviving blood supply after a displaced neck fracture, and injudicious stripping here can convert a salvageable talus into one destined for AVN regardless of anatomic reduction.
7. **Clear the fracture site of interposed periosteum, hematoma, and debris**, then achieve reduction using joysticks (K-wires or small Schanz pins placed in the talar head and body fragments) combined with manual traction — a small distractor or lamina spreader across the ankle/subtalar joints can help disimpact a shortened, comminuted neck before final reduction.
8. **Achieve anatomic reduction** — this is the single most important modifiable determinant of outcome, more so than fixation method or timing. Confirm reduction on both the medial and lateral columns directly (through both windows) and fluoroscopically (Canale view for the neck, mortise/lateral for overall alignment), paying particular attention to rotational and varus/valgus alignment of the head relative to the body, since these are easy to miss on a single view.
9. **Provisionally pin the reduction** with K-wires before committing to definitive screws, and reassess alignment fluoroscopically before proceeding.
10. **Fix with headless compression screws**: posterior-to-anterior trajectory is generally preferred biomechanically (stronger construct, screw head buried in the non-articular posterior talar body) but requires either a separate posterolateral or posteromedial stab incision/percutaneous placement; anterior-to-posterior trajectory (starting on the articular head surface, countersunk) is used when a posterior approach is impractical or the fracture pattern favors it. Avoid a lag-screw (compressive) technique on the medial side in a comminuted pattern, since compressing across comminution there tends to collapse the neck into varus.
11. **Add a medial or lateral neutralization/buttress plate** for comminuted neck fractures to prevent varus malunion, the most common malunion pattern and cause of lateral column overload — the plate resists the collapse that screws alone cannot control across comminuted bone.
12. **Fix the medial malleolar osteotomy** (if performed) with the pre-placed screws/washers, confirming anatomic reduction of the osteotomy fluoroscopically.
13. **Final fluoroscopic and direct visual check**: confirm anatomic alignment of the neck (no varus, no rotational malalignment), screw length without articular penetration into the subtalar or ankle joints, and stable fixation through gentle manual stress of the foot.
14. **Release the tourniquet, confirm perfusion and hemostasis**, and close in layers, protecting the neurovascular structures identified during exposure; apply a well-padded splint with the foot in neutral position, avoiding excessive plantarflexion or dorsiflexion that could stress the fixation.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use joysticks (small Schanz pins or K-wires placed directly in the talar head and body fragments) rather than attempting reduction with pointed clamps alone — the talus's small size and comminution make direct pin manipulation far more controllable.
- A small distractor spanning the tibia to the talar neck or calcaneus is invaluable for disimpacting a shortened, comminuted fracture before attempting reduction — trying to reduce a still-impacted fracture leads to persistent shortening and malalignment.
- When performing a medial malleolar osteotomy, pre-drill and tap the screw holes before making the cut — this is a small step that meaningfully speeds and improves the accuracy of osteotomy fixation at closure, and is standard practice across malleolar osteotomies generally.
- Hinge the medial malleolar osteotomy fragment distally with the deltoid ligament intact rather than fully detaching it — this preserves a blood supply route to the fragment and keeps a soft-tissue tether that aids anatomic reduction at closure.
- Avoid lag-screw compression technique on the medial side of a comminuted neck fracture — compressing across comminution collapses the neck into varus; use a position screw or neutralization plate instead to hold length and alignment.
- Insert the lateral screw starting proximal to the articular surface of the talar head on the lateral side, and countersink medial screws placed through the articular surface, to minimize prominence and cartilage damage.
- A headlamp is genuinely useful, not optional, in this case — the deep, narrow working corridors of both the anteromedial and anterolateral windows make overhead OR lighting alone inadequate for confirming anatomic reduction under direct vision.
- Check rotational alignment of the head relative to the body specifically, not just varus/valgus and length — rotational malreduction is easy to miss on a single fluoroscopic view and is a recognized, under-appreciated contributor to poor outcomes even when the neck "looks" anatomic on lateral imaging.
- Confirm final construct stability with gentle manual stress of the foot before closing — a construct that shifts under gentle stress will not survive early controlled motion, however necessary that motion is to avoid subtalar/ankle stiffness later.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Varus malunion is the classic talar neck complication, causing lateral column overload — use a neutralization/buttress plate in comminuted patterns to prevent it; if symptomatic, it requires corrective osteotomy.
- AVN and post-traumatic arthritis rates remain high even with anatomic reduction, particularly for Hawkins III/IV — counsel patients accordingly, and note that in one contemporary series Hawkins III fractures had significantly higher rates of AVN, infection, and reoperation than Hawkins II, while nonunion rates did not significantly differ between the two types.
- The Hawkins sign (subchondral lucency at 6–8 weeks) is supportive but not definitive for predicting AVN — its absence does not reliably predict AVN in recent cohort data (in one series, 38% of Hawkins-sign-negative tali still avoided AVN), so do not over-reassure or over-alarm patients based on this sign alone.
- Core decompression is not evidence-supported for established talar AVN; manage collapse with staged bracing/protected weight-bearing and, if progressive, tibiotalocalcaneal fusion or talar body allograft/prosthetic replacement.
- Nonunion is a recognized (though less common) risk specifically in comminuted talar neck fractures.
- Devascularization of the talar body from overly aggressive medial soft-tissue stripping (rather than the fracture itself) is an avoidable, iatrogenic contributor to AVN — protect the deltoid branch deliberately throughout the case, not just during the initial approach.
- Rotational malalignment of the head relative to the body is easy to miss and a recognized cause of suboptimal outcome even when length and varus/valgus alignment appear restored — cross-check with both direct visualization and multiple fluoroscopic views.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–6 weeks**: strict NWB, splint/cast, elevation; watch closely for wound issues given the thin soft-tissue envelope.
- **6–12 weeks**: progressive weight-bearing guided by radiographic union — talar fractures unite more slowly than most foot/ankle fractures; begin only once trabecular bridging is evident, since premature WB risks talar body collapse in AVN-prone fractures.
- **3–6 months**: continue AVN surveillance with serial radiographs (Hawkins sign timing, subchondral collapse); if AVN develops, protected weight-bearing/bracing until revascularization or collapse dictates salvage surgery.
- **Return to sport/heavy labor** is generally deferred to 6–12 months given slow union and the AVN risk window. Sedentary work 6–12 weeks.
