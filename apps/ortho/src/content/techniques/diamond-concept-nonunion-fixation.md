## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Established nonunion — no radiographic progression over 3 consecutive months and/or failure to heal by 6–9 months post-injury — particularly oligotrophic and atrophic types where both mechanical stability and biology need to be addressed simultaneously.
- Hypertrophic nonunion is a partial indication: the mechanical (fixation-revision) arm of the Diamond Concept applies, but the biologic-augmentation arm (grafting) is not required, since biology is already adequate in this type.
- Nonunion with a segmental bone deficit up to roughly moderate size, where revision fixation plus grafting is a reasonable primary strategy — very large defects (≥ 5 cm) should be considered for a staged Masquelet or bone-transport strategy layered onto diamond-concept biology optimization rather than revision-and-graft alone.
- Failed prior fixation (broken/loosened implant) at a nonunion site — signals inadequate initial stability and is itself an indication for construct revision, not repeat use of the same construct.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active, untreated infection at the nonunion site — an infected nonunion must be treated first (culture-directed debridement, implant removal/exchange, antibiotic spacer, delayed grafting); grafting an infected nonunion without eradicating infection reliably fails and is a contraindication to proceeding directly to the diamond-concept protocol.
- Segmental defects large enough to behave biologically like bone loss rather than a simple nonunion (≥ 5 cm) — a relative contraindication to revision-and-graft alone, favoring a staged reconstructive strategy instead.
- Uncorrected major modifiable risk factors (active smoking, uncontrolled diabetes, ongoing NSAID use, significant malnutrition) should be addressed as far as possible before committing to a major biologic-augmentation procedure, since these directly impair the biology the graft is meant to restore — not an absolute contraindication, but a planning priority.
- Poor soft-tissue envelope precluding safe exposure and graft coverage — address the soft-tissue problem (local flap, staged coverage) before or concurrently with revision fixation rather than closing a graft under tension over compromised skin.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Confirm nonunion type on imaging**: classify as hypertrophic ("elephant's foot"/"horse's hoof" — abundant callus, flared ends, persistent lucent gap), oligotrophic (minimal callus, ends not resorbed, persistent gap), or atrophic (no callus, rounded/resorbed osteopenic ends, sometimes a fibrous gap) — this classification directly determines whether the mechanical-only or combined mechanical-plus-biologic pathway is appropriate.
- **Exclude infection before finalizing the plan**: check ESR/CRP and WBC; consider aspiration or biopsy with antibiotics held ≥ 2 weeks before sampling to avoid a false-negative culture. Treating an infected nonunion as aseptic is one of the most common and costly errors in this field — do not skip this step even when clinical signs of infection are absent, since indolent low-grade infection can be radiographically and clinically silent.
- **Imaging**: serial plain radiographs to confirm the nonunion diagnosis (no progressive bridging callus over 3 consecutive months); CT with metal-artifact reduction when plain films are equivocal, to confirm or exclude bridging across at least 3 of 4 cortices.
- **Implant/graft planning**: template the revision construct appropriate to the nonunion type and location — compression plating or exchange reamed nailing with a larger-diameter nail for diaphyseal nonunions; dual-plating or blocking-screw configurations for challenging metaphyseal patterns where a single plate cannot achieve adequate stability. Plan graft source and volume: iliac crest (limited volume, well-described morbidity) versus Reamer-Irrigator-Aspirator (RIA) harvest from the contralateral or ipsilateral femur (larger cancellous graft volume with lower donor-site morbidity, but requires an intact femoral canal and specific instrumentation).
- **Biologic adjunct planning**: for atrophic or biologically deficient nonunions, plan for BMP or concentrated bone marrow aspirate (BMAC) augmentation of the graft; have percutaneous iliac crest aspiration equipment and a concentration system available if BMAC is planned.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: implant-removal set matched to the existing hardware, revision plate/nail system, RIA set (if planned), iliac crest bone graft harvest tray (if planned), BMAC harvest/concentration kit (if planned), and a full culture-collection setup for intraoperative deep tissue and implant sonication cultures.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard, often combined with regional block for postoperative analgesia; positioning and draping must accommodate both the nonunion site and the bone graft harvest site in the same operative field.
- **Positioning**: supine for most femoral/tibial nonunion revisions, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for tibial work; lateral decubitus for some femoral or humeral revisions depending on the planned approach and graft harvest site. If iliac crest graft is planned, position and drape to allow access to the iliac crest without recontaminating the primary field (many surgeons prep both fields simultaneously with separate instrument trays).
- **Table/imaging setup**: radiolucent table with image intensifier access for orthogonal views throughout — confirming implant removal, reduction, and final construct position fluoroscopically is essential, and RIA graft harvest (if used) also requires fluoroscopic guidance for canal instrumentation.
- **Draping**: wide prep incorporating the nonunion site, the existing hardware's full length (in case extended exposure for removal is needed), and the planned graft harvest site (iliac crest or contralateral femur for RIA) as a single or clearly separated sterile field.
- **Landmarks**: mark the prior surgical incision (reuse when feasible to avoid a new zone of scarring/vascular compromise), the nonunion site itself (confirmed against preoperative imaging), and the graft harvest site landmarks (anterior/posterior iliac crest, or the femoral entry point for RIA) before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm infection status intraoperatively** even after a negative preoperative workup: send deep tissue cultures (and implant sonication culture if hardware is being removed) before starting systemic antibiotics for the case, and hold definitive grafting pending results if there is any intraoperative suspicion (frank pus, unhealthy granulation tissue, loose implant with surrounding reactive membrane) — proceed to a staged antibiotic-spacer protocol instead if infection is suspected.
2. **Expose the nonunion site**, generally through the prior surgical approach, carefully identifying and protecting neurovascular structures that may be scarred or displaced from prior surgery.
3. **Remove existing hardware** (or the portion requiring exchange) and assess the nonunion site directly — debride any interposed fibrous tissue at the nonunion gap back to bleeding bone on both fragments; for atrophic nonunions this may require decorticating or freshening both bone ends until punctate bleeding is seen.
4. **Reduce the nonunion** to restore length, alignment, and rotation, using the same principles as acute fracture reduction — reference the contralateral limb and preoperative imaging to confirm the corrected position under fluoroscopy.
5. **Apply the revision fixation construct appropriate to the nonunion type**: for hypertrophic nonunion, rigid mechanical stabilization alone (revision plating/nailing with improved working length and screw purchase, exchange reamed nailing to a larger-diameter nail, or dynamization of an existing nail) is generally sufficient — proceed to closure without grafting. For oligotrophic/atrophic nonunion, combine rigid fixation (compression plating, exchange nailing, or dual-plating/blocking-screw constructs for metaphyseal patterns) with biologic augmentation as below.
6. **Harvest bone graft**: iliac crest (open harvest, taking care to protect the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve laterally and the ilioinguinal nerve medially, and to avoid violating the sciatic notch posteriorly) or RIA harvest (through a validated femoral or tibial entry point, with continuous irrigation/aspiration to minimize thermal injury and to collect graft material directly from the aspirate filter).
7. **Augment the graft for biologically deficient (atrophic) nonunions**: add BMP or concentrated bone marrow aspirate to the autograft at the nonunion site as indicated by the severity of the biologic deficiency and case-specific factors (defect size, prior failed grafting, patient risk factors).
8. **Pack graft material circumferentially around the freshened nonunion site**, ensuring good contact with both bleeding bone ends and adequate soft-tissue coverage over the graft to protect it and support vascular ingrowth.
9. **Confirm final construct stability and alignment** on fluoroscopy in orthogonal planes before closure — check screw purchase, plate contouring, or nail interlock position, and confirm no residual gap, malalignment, or rotational error remains uncorrected.
10. **Close in layers**, with a drain if graft-site or surgical-site hematoma risk is significant, and dress the graft harvest site separately per standard technique for the harvest method used.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Match the fixation revision to the actual mechanical deficiency of the prior construct, not simply to "more hardware" — a nonunion under an inadequate working-length plate needs a longer plate/greater screw spread, not necessarily a bigger plate; an undersized nail needs exchange to a larger diameter with better canal fill, not just repeat nailing with the same implant size.
- When exchange nailing a diaphyseal nonunion, reaming to a larger diameter itself provides autogenous bone graft (reamings) delivered directly to the nonunion site — this "auto-grafting" effect is a genuine biologic benefit of exchange reaming, not just a mechanical one.
- Use RIA harvest preferentially over iliac crest when a large cancellous graft volume is needed and the ipsilateral or contralateral femoral canal is available and uninstrumented — reported donor-site pain and morbidity are generally lower than open iliac crest harvest for comparable graft volumes.
- Decorticate atrophic nonunion bone ends until genuine punctate bleeding is visible before grafting — inadequate freshening of sclerotic, avascular bone ends is a common reason grafting fails even when the mechanical construct is excellent.
- Consider blocking (Poller) screws when exchange nailing a metaphyseal or wide-canal nonunion to control canal position and prevent recurrent malalignment through the nail — this is a small technical addition with a large stability payoff in the metaphyseal zone.
- Hold antibiotics for at least 2 weeks before any preoperative aspiration/biopsy for culture, and repeat deep intraoperative cultures even if the preoperative workup was negative — a single negative culture set does not reliably exclude a low-grade infected nonunion.
- Sequence BMAC or BMP addition as the last step before closure, mixing with the autograft material immediately before implantation rather than allowing prolonged exposure time, to preserve growth-factor and cell viability.
- Document the reason for the prior fixation failure explicitly in the operative note (inadequate stability vs biology vs infection) — this drives not just the current revision but also informs rehabilitation weight-bearing progression and follow-up imaging intervals.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Grafting an infected nonunion without first eradicating infection reliably fails** — this sequencing error is one of the most common and costly mistakes in nonunion management.
- **Very large defects (≥ 5 cm) treated under the Diamond Concept alone achieve union in only ~64% within 2 years** — these behave more like segmental bone loss than a simple nonunion and often need a staged Masquelet or bone-transport strategy layered onto the diamond-concept biology optimization, rather than revision fixation and grafting alone.
- **Adjunct biologics (BMAC, PRP, BMP) have variable evidence quality** — moderate evidence for BMAC combined with adequate mechanical stability, weak evidence for BMAC alone; PRP evidence is low-to-moderate and largely from case series in refractory nonunion; none of these adjuncts substitutes for correcting mechanical instability when that is the primary problem.
- **Donor-site morbidity from iliac crest bone graft harvest** (chronic pain, sensory nerve injury from the lateral femoral cutaneous or ilioinguinal nerve, hematoma) is a real trade-off in treatment planning and should factor into the harvest-method decision.
- **Persistent instability despite revision** if the new construct does not meaningfully improve on the mechanical deficiency of the failed construct — reassess working length, screw purchase, and implant sizing critically rather than assuming any revision is sufficient.
- **Wound/graft-site complications** — hematoma, seroma, or wound-healing problems at either the nonunion site or the graft harvest site, more likely in patients with the same risk factors (smoking, diabetes, malnutrition) that predisposed to the nonunion in the first place.
- **Implant-related mechanical failure** (screw/plate loosening or breakage) at a nonunion site signals inadequate initial stability and should prompt genuine construct revision rather than a repeat attempt with the same type of implant.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Follow-up with serial radiographs at fixed intervals (6, 12, then every 4–6 weeks) to assess bridging callus/progression; use CT with metal-artifact reduction to confirm bridging across ≥ 3 of 4 cortices when plain films are equivocal.
- Weight-bearing progression is governed by the stability of the revised construct — the same strain-theory principle (absolute vs. relative stability) that determines healing pathway more broadly in this cluster.
- Continue correction of modifiable systemic risk factors (smoking cessation, glycemic control, avoiding NSAIDs, vitamin D repletion, nutrition optimization) through the healing period, since these directly impair the biology the graft and fixation are trying to restore.
- Implant-related mechanical failure (screw/plate loosening or breakage) at a nonunion site signals inadequate initial stability and should prompt revision rather than a repeat attempt with the same construct.
