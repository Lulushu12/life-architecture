## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Degenerative spondylolisthesis with symptomatic stenosis where decompression alone is deemed insufficient because of instability. Per AO Spine 2025 recommendations, favor **decompression alone** for stable slips (no significant motion on flexion-extension, no prior same-level decompression, minimal slip progression, generally Meyerding I–II). Reserve **fusion** for radiographic instability, facet sagittalization/effusion suggesting a hypermobile segment, revision surgery at the same level, or predominant axial back pain out of proportion to leg symptoms. The trial landscape is genuinely mixed (SLIP favored fusion; Försth and NORDSTEN-DS at 5 years found no advantage for fusion in stable cases), making this a paradigm case for structured shared decision-making with the patient.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Active systemic or local infection, and uncontrolled medical comorbidity precluding safe general anaesthesia, are contraindications to elective fusion.
- Isolated axial back pain without concordant stenosis, radiculopathy, or demonstrated instability is a poor indication for fusion in this population — investigate other pain generators and exhaust conservative care first.
- Anterior/lateral approaches (ALIF/OLIF/LLIF) are relatively contraindicated when direct central or bilateral lateral recess decompression is required, since these approaches achieve only indirect decompression — a patient with severe bony central stenosis or a large disc fragment needing direct visualisation is better served by a posterior approach (with or without supplemental anterior/lateral interbody support).
- LLIF/XLIF is relatively contraindicated at L5-S1 (iliac crest blocks the transpsoas corridor in most patients) and should be used cautiously at L4-5 in patients with an anteriorly positioned lumbar plexus or a high-riding psoas, both of which raise femoral/lumbar plexus injury risk.
- Severe osteoporosis without a bone-health plan is a relative contraindication to a long instrumented construct without adjuncts (cement augmentation, larger-footprint interbody devices, or pre-operative anti-osteoporotic therapy such as teriparatide).
- Active smoking is a relative contraindication warranting structured cessation counselling before elective fusion, given its strong, well-documented association with pseudarthrosis.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** standing lateral radiographs and dedicated flexion-extension radiographs are mandatory before committing to a decompression-alone versus fusion plan — static MRI cannot demonstrate dynamic instability, and a slip that looks stable on supine MRI may translate substantially on upright flexion-extension films. MRI defines the stenosis pattern (central, lateral recess, foraminal), disc height, and facet effusion/sagittalization. CT is useful for pedicle morphology, bone quality assessment, and pre-operative screw trajectory planning, particularly in patients with prior surgery or unusual anatomy.
- **Instability criteria to document explicitly:** >4 mm or >10–15° of translation/angulation on flexion-extension films, facet effusion or sagittalized facet orientation on MRI, and any history of prior decompression at the same level — these are the findings that tip the plan toward fusion per AO Spine 2025 guidance.
- **Implant/graft selection:** decide the fusion technique and levels before the day of surgery (posterolateral only vs TLIF/PLIF vs ALIF/OLIF vs LLIF/XLIF, see the approach-selection table below), and template interbody cage height/lordosis and screw length/diameter off the pre-operative CT/MRI. For anterior/lateral approaches, review the pre-operative axial imaging specifically for great vessel position, psoas/lumbar plexus anatomy, and the position of the iliac vessels relative to the disc space.
- **Bone health optimisation:** screen with DEXA/FRAX in postmenopausal women and older men being considered for fusion; consider pre-operative anti-osteoporotic therapy when surgery can be safely delayed, and plan for cement augmentation or larger-footprint interbody devices when it cannot.
- **Neuromonitoring:** book intraoperative neuromonitoring (EMG at minimum, ideally combined with MEP) for any transpsoas (LLIF/XLIF) approach, given the well-documented risk to the lumbar plexus during transpsoas dissection.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** posterior pedicle screw and rod instrumentation set spanning the planned levels; interbody cage system appropriate to the chosen approach (TLIF/PLIF, ALIF, or lateral cages) with trial and inserter sets; retractor system specific to the approach (tubular/expandable retractor for MIS TLIF, table-mounted retractor for LLIF/XLIF, vascular retractor set for ALIF/OLIF with vascular surgery availability as needed); high-speed burr; operating microscope or loupes/headlight.
- **Multidisciplinary planning for anterior approaches:** for ALIF/OLIF, confirm access surgeon (general/vascular surgery) availability for the retroperitoneal exposure per institutional practice.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anaesthesia:** general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation for all approaches. For LLIF/XLIF, coordinate with anaesthesia regarding neuromuscular blockade — paralysis must be avoided or reversed during the transpsoas dissection and neuromonitoring-guided retractor advancement, since EMG monitoring requires an unblocked neuromuscular junction to detect nerve irritation.
- **Positioning — posterior approaches (decompression, posterolateral fusion, TLIF/PLIF):** prone on a radiolucent spinal frame (Jackson table, Wilson frame, or Andrews frame) with the abdomen free to reduce epidural venous pressure; hips flexed to open the interlaminar spaces for decompression, though less flexion is needed if a large interbody footprint/lordosis restoration is prioritised. Pad all pressure points; protect eyes, chest, iliac crests, and peroneal nerves.
- **Positioning — ALIF/OLIF:** supine (ALIF) or true lateral/slightly oblique decubitus (OLIF) on a radiolucent table, arms positioned to keep the operative field clear of fluoroscopy; break or flex the table at the level of the disc space to open the interspace and improve access.
- **Positioning — LLIF/XLIF:** true lateral decubitus with the table broken/flexed at the greater trochanter to open the space between the iliac crest and rib cage, which is often the limiting factor for L4-5 access; secure the patient firmly with tape or a bean bag, as any intraoperative table flexion adjustments require the patient to remain fixed relative to the table. Confirm true lateral positioning fluoroscopically (superimposed pedicles on AP, superimposed posterior vertebral body lines on lateral) before prep, since rotation compromises both the safety of the transpsoas trajectory and the accuracy of trajectory-dependent neuromonitoring.
- **Table setup:** confirm unobstructed AP and lateral fluoroscopy across all planned levels for whichever approach is chosen; for combined anterior-posterior cases, plan the sequence and any repositioning in advance so the team is not improvising mid-case.
- **Draping and imaging setup:** wide sterile prep appropriate to the approach (posterior midline; flank for lateral approaches; lower abdominal for ALIF/OLIF); mark planned incision sites relative to fluoroscopically confirmed disc space levels before draping.
- **Surface landmarks:** iliac crests (approximate L4-5 disc level posteriorly and the L4-5 lateral working corridor), posterior superior iliac spines, and the umbilicus/costal margin (anterior/lateral approach planning) should be marked before prep, then confirmed radiographically once positioned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Time-out and level confirmation.** Confirm patient, procedure, and level(s) with fluoroscopic/spot radiograph localisation before incision, using the pre-operative flexion-extension films and MRI as the reference.
2. **Confirm the instability plan intraoperatively.** Before committing instrumentation, re-confirm the pre-operative decision (decompression alone versus fusion) against the exposed anatomy — facet effusion, gross hypermobility on manual palpation of the exposed segment, or unexpectedly extensive facetectomy required for decompression can shift an initially "decompression-alone" plan toward fusion; have instrumentation available even for cases planned as decompression-only when instability criteria are borderline.
3. **If decompression alone is chosen:**
   a. Expose the lamina and facets subperiosteally, preserving the facet capsules and pars interarticularis as much as possible.
   b. Perform a bilateral-via-unilateral laminotomy ("over-the-top" technique): approach from the more symptomatic or more stenotic side, undercut the base of the spinous process and contralateral lamina with a burr, then decompress the contralateral lateral recess by angling instruments across the midline under direct or microscope visualisation — this preserves the midline posterior tension band (spinous process, interspinous/supraspinous ligaments) and the contralateral facet, reducing iatrogenic instability compared to a formal bilateral laminectomy.
   c. Confirm both traversing roots and the thecal sac are decompressed circumferentially before closure.
4. **If fusion is indicated, expose for instrumentation.** Subperiosteal dissection off the spinous processes, lamina, facets, and transverse processes bilaterally at all instrumented levels; place pedicle screws under fluoroscopic (or navigated) guidance with AP and lateral confirmation of trajectory and depth before final seating.
5. **Perform the decompression** (laminectomy or bilateral-via-unilateral technique) to address central, lateral recess, and foraminal stenosis at the symptomatic level(s), now less constrained by iatrogenic-instability concerns since instrumentation will stabilise the segment.
6. **Select and perform the interbody technique** if planned:
   - **PLIF:** complete bilateral facetectomy/laminectomy already performed provides posterior access; retract the thecal sac and traversing roots (gently, bilaterally, one side at a time) to access the disc space, perform discectomy and endplate preparation, and place bilateral cages.
   - **TLIF:** perform a unilateral facetectomy on the more symptomatic/stenotic side, retract the thecal sac medially with the exiting and traversing roots protected, perform discectomy and endplate preparation through this unilateral corridor, and place a single (occasionally two smaller) cage obliquely across the disc space — angle the cage insertion to reach as far anteriorly and contralaterally as possible to maximise footprint and avoid under-restoring contralateral foraminal height.
   - **ALIF/OLIF:** via the anterior or antero-oblique retroperitoneal corridor (with access surgeon assistance as needed), mobilise the great vessels off the anterior disc space, perform discectomy and thorough endplate preparation under direct vision, and place a large-footprint cage restoring disc height and segmental lordosis; this is typically combined with posterior percutaneous pedicle screw fixation placed in the same or a staged setting.
   - **LLIF/XLIF:** dock a sequential dilator system through the retroperitoneal fat and psoas under continuous EMG neuromonitoring, confirming a safe trajectory (monitoring thresholds and directional stimulation help gauge proximity to lumbar plexus fibres as the dilators advance); place the working retractor, perform discectomy and endplate preparation, and insert a large lateral cage; supplement with posterior percutaneous or lateral-plate fixation per surgeon preference.
7. **Apply the practical default for approach selection:**

   | Approach | Access | Advantages | Trade-offs / selection caveats |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | PLIF | Posterior, bilateral facet/nerve root retraction | Direct decompression + fusion in one posterior exposure | Greater nerve root/thecal sac retraction, higher dural tear risk than TLIF |
   | TLIF | Posterior, unilateral facetectomy | Less neural retraction than PLIF; workhorse for single/two-level degenerative disease with stenosis | Unilateral cage placement can under-restore contralateral foraminal height if not carefully executed |
   | OLIF/ALIF | Anterior or antero-oblique, retroperitoneal, no direct canal exposure | Large-footprint cage restores disc height/lordosis well, indirect decompression, avoids posterior muscle/facet disruption | Vascular injury risk (great vessels); typically combined with posterior percutaneous fixation; not suitable when direct central/lateral recess decompression is required |
   | LLIF/XLIF | Transpsoas lateral, retroperitoneal | Large cage, strong indirect decompression and deformity correction capacity, muscle-sparing posteriorly | Lumbar plexus/femoral nerve injury risk (higher at L4-5); limited access at L5-S1 due to iliac crest; transient thigh numbness/weakness in most |

   TLIF is the workhorse when direct decompression and fusion are both needed at one or two levels; ALIF/OLIF/LLIF are favored when indirect decompression suffices and greater segmental lordosis/disc height restoration is prioritized (e.g., adjacent segment disease revision, higher-grade slips needing more correction), often as part of a combined anterior-posterior construct.
8. **Provisional and final fixation.** After interbody placement, apply compression across the cage with the pedicle screw-rod construct, confirm segmental lordosis and disc height on fluoroscopy, then finalise rod-screw locking. Obtain final AP and lateral images confirming screw position, cage position, and overall alignment before leaving theatre.
9. **Bone grafting.** Decorticate the posterolateral gutters (transverse processes, lateral facets) and pack with local autograft supplemented by allograft/bone graft substitute as needed, in addition to the interbody graft material.
10. **Closure.** Achieve meticulous haemostasis, consider a subfascial drain for larger or multilevel exposures, and close fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers. For combined anterior-posterior or staged procedures, confirm haemostasis and retroperitoneal closure separately per the anterior/lateral approach before repositioning.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Re-examine instability intraoperatively, not just on pre-operative films — palpable segmental hypermobility or an unexpectedly large facetectomy needed for adequate decompression should prompt intraoperative reconsideration of a decompression-alone plan, so keep instrumentation available even for borderline cases.
- When performing the bilateral-via-unilateral ("over-the-top") technique, undercut the spinous process base generously with a burr before attempting to pass instruments across the midline — an inadequately undercut base forces excessive angulation and risks incomplete contralateral decompression.
- In TLIF, angling the cage trajectory as obliquely and anteriorly as possible (rather than inserting it straight in) maximises footprint on the stronger apophyseal ring and reduces subsidence risk, while also improving contralateral foraminal height restoration.
- For LLIF/XLIF, use directional (four-electrode) EMG probes on the dilators where available — these localise the nerve relative to the trajectory (anterior/posterior/superior/inferior), not just proximity, which lets you actively steer the docking point away from at-risk plexus fibres rather than simply stopping when a threshold is crossed.
- Confirm true lateral position fluoroscopically before starting the transpsoas dissection in LLIF/XLIF — even modest rotation shifts the apparent safe corridor and increases risk to the lumbar plexus, which sits more anteriorly at L4-5 than at more cranial levels.
- For ALIF/OLIF, develop the retroperitoneal plane bluntly and stay directly on the anterior longitudinal ligament/disc surface — straying laterally off the disc risks the ureter and sympathetic chain, while straying too far cranially risks the great vessels and their segmental branches.
- After any interbody placement, restore compression across the disc space before final rod locking (rather than locking the rod first and compressing after) to maximise graft-endplate contact and segmental lordosis.
- When treating a multilevel construct with a caudal fusion ending at L5, consider whether extending to S1 (or adding iliac fixation) is warranted if there is meaningful degeneration or instability at L5-S1 — stopping short of a degenerated distal segment is a recognised driver of early adjacent-segment revision.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Vascular injury (great vessels) is specific to anterior/lateral approaches (ALIF/OLIF/LLIF); segmental vessel/lumbar plexus injury occurs during transpsoas access — mitigate with careful approach-surgeon co-operation, neuromonitoring during transpsoas access, and thorough pre-operative vascular anatomy review.
- Unilateral TLIF cage placement can under-restore contralateral foraminal height if not carefully executed.
- Adjacent segment degeneration is radiographically common (up to ~30% at long-term follow-up) though clinically symptomatic ASD requiring reoperation is less frequent (~2–3% per year cumulative) — a key argument favoring fusion avoidance when decompression alone is a reasonable option.
- Pseudarthrosis risk rises with construct flexibility, multilevel constructs, poor bone quality, and smoking; document smoking-cessation counseling as a standard pre-operative step.
- Instrumentation failure (screw loosening/pullout, rod fracture) is more frequent in osteoporotic bone, long constructs, and at the caudal-most level of long fusions — consider cement augmentation or expandable screws and pre-operative bone health optimization (DEXA/FRAX screening, teriparatide when surgery can be delayed) in osteoporotic patients.
- Surgical site infection is higher for instrumented fusion (up to ~5%) than decompression alone, particularly with diabetes, obesity, smoking, and prolonged operative time.
- Transient thigh numbness, weakness, or anterior thigh/groin pain occurs in a substantial minority of LLIF/XLIF patients due to psoas/lumbar plexus manipulation — this is usually self-limited over weeks to a few months but should be discussed pre-operatively as an expected possibility rather than framed purely as a complication.
- Sympathetic chain injury during anterior/lateral retroperitoneal dissection can cause a warm, dry leg or mild vasomotor changes post-operatively — usually self-limited, recognised by asymmetric limb temperature without motor/sensory deficit.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2:** early mobilization day 1; brace per surgeon preference (many use a soft lumbar corset primarily for proprioceptive feedback/pain control rather than mechanical necessity with rigid pedicle screw fixation); avoid bending, lifting >4–5 kg, and twisting.
- **Weeks 2–6:** walking program advanced, gentle isometric core activation, no formal resistance training; radiographic check at 6 weeks.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progressive core/paraspinal strengthening once early fusion mass consolidation is presumed underway; sedentary return to work often 4–6 weeks, manual labor typically deferred to 3–4 months.
- **Months 3–6:** advance to functional strengthening and conditioning; return to high-demand manual labor or axial-loading sport generally not before 4–6 months, guided by clinical exam and radiographic evidence of fusion progression (bridging bone, absence of screw lucency/instrumentation failure); obtain flexion-extension films or CT at 6–12 months if fusion status is uncertain, especially before clearing heavy labor/contact sport.
- Union rates for instrumented posterolateral/interbody fusion in degenerative lumbar disease are reported in the 90–97% range in modern series with pedicle screw instrumentation and interbody support; smoking, diabetes, osteoporosis, and multilevel constructs reduce union rates and should prompt more conservative return-to-work/return-to-sport timelines.
- Routine imaging: AP/lateral radiographs at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6–12 months; flexion-extension or CT if fusion status is uncertain before high-demand clearance; earlier imaging for hardware-related pain or suspected pseudarthrosis/instrumentation failure.
