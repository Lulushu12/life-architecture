## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: extra-articular or simple intra-articular fractures, especially in younger patients (<65 years, per DRAFFT2 eligibility) with dorsally displaced patterns amenable to closed or limited-open reduction. Useful where a volar locking plate (VLP) is not readily available, or as definitive treatment when reduction proves stable after pinning. Classic candidates for intrafocal (Kapandji) pinning specifically are displaced extra-articular fractures with a large, minimally comminuted distal fragment and reasonable bone quality.

**Contraindications**:
- Significant metaphyseal comminution or osteoporotic bone unable to provide purchase for a buttressing wire — the intrafocal technique depends on cortical bone to resist the pin's leverage; comminuted or very soft bone lets the wire cut out.
- Volarly displaced/unstable (Smith, volar Barton) patterns — inherently shear-unstable and not reliably controlled by K-wires; these need buttress plating.
- Complex intra-articular (die-punch, AO/OTA 23-C3) fractures with multiple articular fragments — wires cannot achieve fragment-specific articular control the way a plate or combined construct can.
- Patients >65 years are not an absolute contraindication (DRAFFT2's eligibility cutoff was a trial design choice, not a biological limit), but comminution and bone quality should be weighed more heavily in this group.
- Grossly unstable DRUJ or significant radial shortening that will not be adequately restored by pin-assisted reduction alone.
- Inability to tolerate the wrist immobilization required with pins in place (unlike a VLP, K-wire constructs generally require blocking wrist ROM until wire removal).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: PA, lateral, and oblique wrist radiographs to confirm the fracture is extra-articular or simply intra-articular, characterize the direction and degree of displacement, and assess bone quality/comminution. CT if there is any suspicion of a more complex articular pattern that plain films underestimate.
- **Wire selection**: 1.6 mm (0.062") smooth K-wires are the standard for adult distal radius pinning; 1.1 mm (0.045") wires are an alternative in smaller patients or where a thinner wire is preferred, at some cost to construct stiffness. Have both interfragmentary (crossed, Willenegger-Guggenbühl style) and intrafocal (Kapandji) technique equipment available, since the fracture pattern encountered intraoperatively may favor one over the other.
- **Equipment list**: mini fluoroscopy (C-arm), power wire driver, wire cutter, sterile finger traps and traction tower/weight for reduction, sugar-tong or short-arm splint/cast material for post-fixation immobilization, marking pen for skin entry points.
- **Case-specific planning**: decide provisionally whether the pattern favors an interfragmentary (oblique wires crossing the fracture line, anchored in the far cortex) or intrafocal (wires inserted at the fracture site itself and used as a lever/buttress) configuration, or a combination — dorsally displaced fractures with a reasonably sized distal fragment are the classic intrafocal candidate, while smaller/more comminuted distal fragments may pin better with crossed interfragmentary wires. Counsel the patient using the DRAFFT2 evidence: no 12-month PRWE difference versus casting, but a substantially lower redisplacement/rescue-fixation rate with pinning.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or regional (axillary/supraclavicular block) anesthesia is typical; a hematoma block plus sedation can suffice for a straightforward extra-articular pattern if institutional pathways support it, but most percutaneous pinning is done under a formal block or GA given the need for stable reduction during pin placement.
- **Position**: supine, arm on a radiolucent hand table, shoulder abducted ~90°.
- **Tourniquet**: pneumatic upper-arm tourniquet, inflated for the procedure; can often be released before dressing if hemostasis is adequate, since this is typically a brief case.
- **Traction/reduction setup**: finger traps on the index and middle (± ring) fingers with weighted countertraction over the elbow, as for closed reduction, to achieve and maintain length while wires are placed.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm to allow PA and lateral views without moving the arm between shots — plan wire trajectories on both projections before the first pin goes in, since percutaneous pinning is entirely fluoroscopy-guided.
- **Landmarks**: mark Lister's tubercle, the radial styloid, and the dorsal fracture line/step-off; palpate and mark the course of the superficial radial nerve branches near the radial styloid entry site, and the extensor tendon compartments dorsally, since wire entry points must avoid both.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Achieve closed (or limited-open, if closed reduction is imperfect) reduction of the displaced fracture using finger-trap traction and the manual reduction maneuver appropriate to the displacement pattern (see Closed Reduction & Casting technique).
2. Confirm acceptable alignment on PA and lateral fluoroscopy before placing any wires — pins fix the reduction achieved; they do not improve a poor reduction.
3. Choose the wire configuration based on fracture pattern:
   - **Interfragmentary (crossed) technique**: insert wires obliquely through the distal fragment (typically via the radial styloid and, for the second wire, a dorsal-ulnar entry point) crossing the fracture line and anchoring bicortically in the proximal fragment, in a crossed configuration.
   - **Intrafocal (Kapandji) technique**: insert wires directly at the fracture site itself rather than through the distal fragment, then use the wire as a lever/buttress — pivot the wire roughly 45° and advance it proximally through the near and far cortex, using it to hold the reduction rather than simply transfixing bone-to-bone. To correct residual dorsal angulation, insert an intrafocal pin from the dorsal-ulnar side to re-establish volar tilt; to correct loss of radial inclination, direct a pin along the radial cortex, entered from the volar-radial side.
4. Place 2–3 wires in total (commonly one radial-styloid wire plus one or two dorsal wires), positioning them to buttress against redisplacement in the direction the fracture is prone to fail (dorsal for the classic dorsally angulated pattern).
5. Confirm each wire is extra-articular and has not entered the radiocarpal or DRUJ joint on both PA and lateral fluoroscopy before final seating.
6. Confirm final alignment (inclination, tilt, height, step/gap) on multiple fluoroscopic views once all wires are placed.
7. If a concomitant DRUJ instability is suspected, perform intraoperative fluoroscopic pronation/supination stress testing before leaving the OR (see DRUJ Stabilization technique).
8. Bend and cut the wires outside the skin, leaving enough length for later extraction in clinic; cap the ends and apply a small sterile dressing at each pin site.
9. Apply a well-padded splint or cast with the wrist in neutral-to-slight flexion, protecting the pin sites from direct pressure.
10. Obtain a final set of post-fixation radiographs before the patient leaves the operating room/recovery area, to serve as the baseline for subsequent surveillance films.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Achieve the best possible closed reduction before placing wires — pins are excellent at maintaining a reduction and poor at correcting one; do not rely on the wires themselves to "walk" a fragment into position.
- For the intrafocal (Kapandji) technique, remember the wire is being used as a lever at the fracture site, not simply as a transfixing pin — insert it into the fracture gap first, then pivot and advance, rather than trying to drive it straight across like an interfragmentary wire.
- Direct the correcting intrafocal pin from the side opposite the direction of deformity (dorsal-ulnar entry to correct dorsal tilt; volar-radial entry to correct lost radial inclination) — placing it on the wrong side will not generate the buttressing effect intended.
- Keep wires strictly extracapsular/extra-articular and outside the dorsal extensor compartments and superficial radial nerve branches near the radial styloid — confirm trajectory on fluoroscopy before final seating, not just after.
- A crossed (interfragmentary) and an intrafocal wire can be combined in the same case when the fracture pattern calls for both length/rotation control and a specific angular correction — do not feel obligated to use a single technique throughout.
- Bend wires so the tips sit just proud of the skin, angled away from areas of pressure (e.g., not directly under a splint strap), to reduce pin-site irritation during the immobilization period.
- Recheck wire position on a final orthogonal fluoroscopy pair after all pins are placed and bent/cut — the act of bending can subtly change wire depth/position.
- Since wrist ROM is deferred until pin removal, counsel the patient up front that digit and elbow/shoulder motion should start immediately and be maintained throughout the pinning period to prevent secondary stiffness elsewhere.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- DRAFFT2 (multicentre UK RCT, 500 adults) showed no significant PRWE difference between cast and K-wire fixation at 12 months (mean 21.2 vs 20.7, adjusted difference −0.34, p=0.87), but 13% of the cast group required rescue surgical fixation for loss of position versus essentially none in the K-wire group — K-wire fixation trades an upfront procedure for a substantially lower redisplacement/rescue-surgery rate, an important shared-decision-making point.
- Pin-site infection is generally superficial and managed with local care ± oral antibiotics; deep pin-tract infection is uncommon but should prompt early wire removal and culture-directed treatment if suspected.
- Wrist ROM must be deferred until pin removal — premature loading risks loss of fixation before soft-tissue/bony stability is established.
- Wires placed too close to the radial styloid risk injury to the superficial branch of the radial nerve, producing dysesthesia over the dorsoradial hand — a well-recognized, largely avoidable complication with careful entry-point planning and blunt dissection to bone before wire insertion.
- Intra-articular wire placement (missed on inadequate fluoroscopic views) can cause cartilage damage and pin migration into the joint — always confirm extra-articular position on both PA and lateral views.
- Loss of reduction can still occur despite pinning, particularly in comminuted or osteoporotic bone where the wires lack adequate cortical purchase — this is the biomechanical rationale for excluding significantly comminuted patterns from the indications above.
- Wire migration or bacterial tracking along the pin can occur with prolonged retention; wires are generally not left in situ beyond 6 weeks for this reason.
- Malunion remains possible even with pin fixation if the initial reduction was suboptimal — pins maintain, they do not correct, alignment.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: immobilized; digit AROM from day 1; protect pin sites.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: pins remain in place to week 4–6; active digit motion continues; wrist ROM deferred until pin removal.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: pins removed ~week 4–6, then progressive wrist ROM; light functional ADLs.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: progressive resistive strengthening, proprioception, occupational/sport-specific reconditioning, as per the general rehabilitation pathway.
- **Imaging**: post-op, 2 wk, 4–6 wk (pin removal decision), then as clinically indicated.
- **Return to work/sport**: manual labor/heavy lifting typically deferred until ~12 weeks with radiographic union and functional grip strength; return to contact/collision sport generally 3–4 months, individualized to fracture pattern and fixation stability.
