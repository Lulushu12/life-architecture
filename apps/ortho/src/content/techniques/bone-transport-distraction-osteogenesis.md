## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Segmental diaphyseal bone defects not limited by size — the primary advantage over Masquelet, which becomes unreliable above roughly 6 cm (≈62 mm cited as an approximate critical threshold in some series).
- Very large (> 6 cm) or combined bone-and-soft-tissue defects, where the gradual transport process simultaneously resurfaces the soft-tissue envelope through the associated tissue stretch ("bone transport-associated soft-tissue regeneration").
- Infected nonunion/osteomyelitis with segmental resection required for eradication — the frame allows staged debridement, dead-space management, and gradual filling without a large acute bone graft that could itself become infected.
- Centres and surgeons with Ilizarov/circular- or hexapod-frame expertise — this is not a technique to improvise; case volume matters for both the surgeon and the supporting nursing/physiotherapy team.
- Combined limb-length discrepancy or angular deformity with the bone defect — transport can correct length and alignment simultaneously with defect closure.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Patient unable to tolerate a prolonged external fixation course (cognitively unable to manage pin-site care, unable/unwilling to attend frequent follow-up for frame adjustments, or with a psychosocial situation precluding many months in a frame).
- Severe peripheral vascular disease compromising the regenerate's blood supply.
- Active, uncontrolled systemic sepsis — get source control first; the frame does not substitute for debridement.
- Very short residual bone stock at the proposed corticotomy site insufficient to accept a fixation ring/wire construct with adequate purchase.
- Patient factors that severely impair regenerate bone formation (uncontrolled diabetes, heavy smoking, malnutrition) should be optimized preoperatively where possible, though they are not absolute contraindications.
- Defects easily managed by simpler means (< 4–5 cm, well-vascularized bed, soft tissue already closed) are relative contraindications to transport in favor of Masquelet or acute shortening, given transport's much higher complication and time burden for an equivalent result.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length orthogonal radiographs of the involved bone with a radiopaque ruler/scale marker to measure true defect length; long-leg standing (mechanical axis) films if any coexisting deformity or limb-length discrepancy needs to be corrected in the same frame; CT to characterize bone quality at the proposed corticotomy site and to rule out occult sequestrum/residual infection.
- **Defect measurement and transport plan**: measure the exact defect length and plan the corticotomy site — ideally a well-vascularized metaphyseal location (proximal tibial metaphysis for distal-third tibial defects, distal metaphysis for proximal defects) at least one ring's width away from both the defect and any joint, to preserve adequate bone stock for wire/half-pin fixation and to leave a docking segment long enough to be biomechanically stable.
- **Frame selection and sizing**: choose ring diameter to allow 2 finger-breadths of soft-tissue clearance circumferentially for swelling; select a monofocal (single corticotomy) construct for shorter defects, bifocal (two transport sites converging on the defect from both ends) for very long defects to halve treatment time, or a trifocal construct in select very-large-defect cases. Hexapod (Taylor Spatial Frame/TrueLok-type computer-assisted) frames are preferred when concurrent multiplanar deformity correction is needed; simple monolateral rail systems are an option for straightforward unifocal transport in a cooperative patient, at the cost of less versatility for angular correction.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: full ring fixator set (rings sized to the limb with soft-tissue clearance, threaded rods, wire/half-pin fixation hardware, wire tensioner), Gigli saw or oscillating saw with osteotomes for a low-energy corticotomy, image intensifier, drill with cooled irrigation for wire/pin insertion, and — if docking-site grafting is anticipated at index surgery — bone graft harvest instrumentation (RIA set or iliac crest tray).
- **Culture and infection workup**: if there is any history of open fracture, prior surgery, or clinical suspicion of infection, obtain deep tissue cultures and inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP) preoperatively; plan for culture-directed antibiotics and, if infected, a staged debridement-first protocol before committing to the transport construct.
- **Patient education**: dedicate real preoperative counselling time to the expected treatment length (frame worn for many months), pin-site care responsibilities, the self-directed distraction schedule, and realistic expectations for docking-site problems — patient buy-in is as important to outcome as the mechanical construct.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia or combined general/regional (spinal/epidural or peripheral nerve block) depending on limb and anticipated duration; a working popliteal or femoral catheter can be left for postoperative analgesia after tibial frame application, but avoid dense blocks that would mask a developing compartment syndrome in the early postoperative period.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table for tibial/femoral transport, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip if needed to neutralize external rotation; a well-padded thigh tourniquet is used for the resection/corticotomy portion but released before final wire tensioning to avoid tourniquet-related muscle/nerve injury during prolonged frame application.
- **Table/imaging setup**: image intensifier brought in from the contralateral side (or foot of table) with orthogonal AP/lateral views confirmed and unobstructed before draping; mark out the mechanical axis and the planned corticotomy/ring levels on the skin before prepping.
- **Draping**: circumferential limb prep and drape (the whole limb must be free to rotate and be manipulated for frame assembly and fluoroscopy in multiple planes) — a stockinette or U-drape technique that leaves the entire limb accessible is standard; the foot is often left exposed distally to allow visual/pulse assessment.
- **Landmarks**: mark the fibular head/peroneal nerve course, the subcutaneous border of the tibia, the planned corticotomy level, and safe-corridor zones for wire/half-pin placement (standard Ilizarov safe-corridor diagrams, respecting neurovascular structures at each ring level) before the first incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Apply the reference (proximal) ring block first**, away from the zone of injury/defect, using orthogonal wires and/or half-pins per the safe corridor for that level; tension wires to standard tension (~90–130 kg) with a calibrated tensioner.
2. **Apply the distal ring block** in the same fashion, ensuring the overall frame is coaxial with the mechanical axis of the limb — check this under fluoroscopy in both planes before final tightening.
3. **Debride the defect itself** if not already done at a prior stage: excise all non-viable, infected, or fibrous tissue back to healthy bleeding bone (paprika sign) and healthy soft tissue; culture any suspicious tissue.
4. **Perform the corticotomy** at the pre-planned metaphyseal site, remote from the defect: make multiple drill-hole perforations circumferentially through the cortex (leaving the medullary contents and surrounding soft-tissue/periosteal sleeve as undisturbed as possible — a true "corticotomy," not a full osteotomy, preserves the endosteal and periosteal blood supply that drives regenerate bone formation), then complete the cut with a thin osteotome and controlled manual fracture (or a low-heat oscillating saw with copious irrigation) rather than a high-speed saw, to minimize thermal and vascular injury.
5. **Insert the transport segment's own ring/wire fixation** immediately adjacent to the corticotomy so the transported fragment can be independently controlled by the transport rods.
6. **Confirm frame stability and mechanical alignment** with fluoroscopy — check that rings are parallel, the limb is not left in residual angulation/translation, and all wire/pin fixation points are secure before leaving theatre.
7. **Close soft tissues over the corticotomy site** with minimal tension; leave the defect site itself open or lightly dressed if a flap/NPWT plan is already in place.
8. **Postoperative latency period**: allow 5–7 days (shorter in children, longer — up to 10–14 days — in older or medically complicated adults or after very proximal/distal corticotomies) before starting distraction, to allow initial fracture-healing (soft callus) response to begin at the corticotomy site without disrupting it.
9. **Begin gradual distraction** at the standard rate of ~1 mm/day, delivered in small increments (typically 0.25 mm four times daily, or 0.5 mm twice daily) rather than as a single daily turn — frequent small increments produce more uniform, better-quality regenerate bone than infrequent large increments.
10. **Monitor the regenerate radiographically** every 2–4 weeks during active transport: the classic appearance is a central lucent "growth zone" flanked by columnar, longitudinally oriented new bone ("fir-tree" pattern) — a widening lucent gap with poor columnar organization signals a rate too fast for the biology (slow the rate); dense, premature bridging signals a rate too slow (increase modestly) or premature consolidation risk.
11. **Continue transport until the leading edge of the transported segment reaches the docking site** (the native bone end at the far side of the original defect).
12. **Manage the docking site actively rather than expecting spontaneous union**: at docking, freshen both bone ends (small "refresh" via percutaneous or open débridement of the abutting surfaces if there is any interposed fibrous tissue), compress the docking site under the frame, and strongly consider primary docking-site autograft (percutaneous or open) at the time of docking rather than waiting to see if it unites spontaneously — a substantial proportion of docking sites do not unite without this active step.
13. **Once the regenerate has consolidated** (assessed by clinical stability — no pain/motion on manual stress of the regenerate with the frame's connecting rods loosened — and radiographic cortical bridging on at least 3 of 4 cortices, or a validated consolidation index), begin frame dynamization (progressive loosening of one or more rods) to load-share and stimulate final cortical maturation before frame removal.
14. **Remove the frame** once consolidation is confirmed; consider protective bracing or a period of protected weight-bearing/prophylactic intramedullary nail or plate ("frame-to-nail" or "lengthen-and-plate" conversion) in patients at higher risk of post-frame fracture through the immature regenerate, particularly in adults with long transport distances.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Keep the corticotomy truly percutaneous/low-energy and preserve the medullary and periosteal blood supply — this is the single biggest determinant of regenerate quality; a full osteotomy that strips the endosteal supply behaves more like a standard fracture and regenerates poorly.
- Site the corticotomy at a metaphyseal location with generous cancellous bone and blood supply (proximal tibial metaphysis is the classic favorable site) rather than mid-diaphyseal bone whenever the deformity/defect location allows a choice.
- Use a bifocal (two simultaneous corticotomy sites transporting toward each other) construct for defects longer than roughly 6–8 cm to substantially shorten overall frame time compared with a single long unifocal transport.
- Distract in small, frequent increments (4×/day) rather than once daily — this produces measurably better regenerate bone quality and is one of the most consistently reproducible technical lessons from the Ilizarov literature.
- Encourage active weight-bearing through the frame as early as comfort allows once the construct is stable — axial micromotion/loading stimulates regenerate mineralization (mechanical loading is a biologic stimulus here, not just a functional milestone).
- Plan for docking-site grafting proactively rather than reactively — many surgeons now graft the docking site electively at the time of docking (or very shortly after) rather than waiting the traditional several months to diagnose nonunion, since delayed union and nonunion at the docking site remain the single most common reason for a secondary procedure after otherwise successful transport.
- Track the "healing index" as you go (roughly 1–1.5 months of total frame time per centimeter of defect transported, i.e., the external fixation index) — use it to set realistic patient expectations and to flag a construct that is falling noticeably behind schedule as one to actively investigate.
- Have the patient/family or a home-health nurse trained early in pin-site care and in reading and executing the distraction schedule correctly — errors in the patient-driven distraction rate (skipped days, doubled-up turns) are a common source of both premature consolidation and excessive gap formation.
- Consider hexapod (six-strut, computer-programmed) frames over classic Ilizarov ring-and-rod constructs when any element of angular, rotational, or translational correction is needed alongside transport — the software-driven correction schedule reduces the error rate compared with manual multi-planar adjustment.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Pin-site infection** is the most frequent complication of bone transport and is the leading reason patients/surgeons favor Masquelet when defect size allows either option — meticulous pin-site hygiene, tension-free skin at each wire/pin, and prompt treatment of superficial infection (oral antibiotics, dressing changes) before it progresses to septic loosening are essential.
- **Docking-site nonunion/delayed union** is common: reported delayed union at the docking site in roughly a fifth of cases in modern series, with axial deviation and soft-tissue interposition at the docking site as other recognized complications; when soft tissue becomes interposed at the docking site, formal freshening of both bone ends is required for union — simple compression without addressing interposed tissue will not succeed. Risk is higher with longer bone defects, longer overall external fixation time, more prior operations, and distal-third defect location.
- **Regenerate deformity or fracture** — angulation of the regenerate during transport (from asymmetric frame tension, muscle pull, or premature/uncontrolled weight-bearing) or fracture through an immature regenerate after premature frame removal; avoid by checking regenerate alignment at every follow-up visit and confirming solid consolidation (not just "some" callus) before frame removal.
- **Joint contracture and stiffness** — prolonged frame wear, especially spanning or near a joint, predisposes to knee/ankle stiffness; proactive physiotherapy and, where feasible, frame constructs that do not immobilize adjacent joints unnecessarily reduce this risk.
- **Neurovascular injury from wire/pin placement** — always place wires/half-pins through the validated safe corridors for each ring level; the peroneal nerve at the fibular neck and the neurovascular bundle in the deep posterior compartment are at particular risk with proximal tibial ring placement.
- **Prolonged external fixation time and complication burden** are the major overall burden of transport — the frame is often worn many months, with an external fixation index of roughly 1–1.5 months per centimeter transported; this is the central trade-off against Masquelet.
- **Premature consolidation** if distraction is started too late, too slowly, or interrupted — recognized by a densely ossifying, non-lucent gap on serial films; treat by increasing distraction rate promptly or, if fully consolidated, may require re-osteotomy.
- **Excessive gap/poor regenerate ("hourglass" or lucent, poorly organized regenerate)** if distraction proceeds too fast for the biology — recognized by a widening lucent zone with minimal columnar bone formation; treat by slowing or temporarily halting the rate, and consider compression-distraction ("accordion") maneuvers to stimulate a sluggish regenerate.
- **Overall comparative data**: meta-analysis of transport versus Masquelet shows a higher union rate for transport (~95% vs ~86% for Masquelet) but at the cost of a higher complication burden and longer external fixation time; functional outcomes are otherwise broadly comparable — selection should be individualized to defect size, soft-tissue status, and patient tolerance for prolonged external fixation rather than surgeon habit.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Post-frame placement**: non- to touch-weight-bearing initially, ROM of adjacent joints maintained, pin-site care education.
- **Distraction phase**: typically starts 5–7 days post-corticotomy at ~1 mm/day; weight-bearing as tolerated through the frame is often encouraged, since axial loading stimulates regenerate bone formation, unless contraindicated by the fixation type.
- **Post-docking**: protected weight-bearing progressing over 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic incorporation at the docking site; full weight-bearing typically not before 3–4 months and often later for large-defect reconstructions.
- **Return to work/sport** is defined case-by-case and typically delayed 9–18 months for large segmental reconstructions given the prolonged biological remodeling timeline.
