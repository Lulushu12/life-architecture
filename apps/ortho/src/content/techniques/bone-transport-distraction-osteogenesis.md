## Indications

Segmental bone defects not limited by size — favored for very large (> 6 cm) or combined bone-and-soft-tissue defects, where transport can simultaneously resurface soft tissue via the associated stretch. Also favored at centres with Ilizarov/circular-frame expertise, and as an option when large-volume graft harvest for Masquelet is undesirable.

## Positioning & setup

A circular or hexapod external fixator (Ilizarov-type frame) is applied to span the defect and the corticotomy site, which is placed at a separate metaphyseal location from the defect itself.

## Steps

1. Perform acute or gradual bone resection at the defect margins (radical debridement to bleeding bone).
2. Create a corticotomy at a separate metaphyseal site, away from the defect.
3. Begin gradual distraction typically 5–7 days post-corticotomy, at ~1 mm/day, delivered in 0.25 mm increments 4×/day.
4. Transport the mobilized bone segment across to fill the defect, forming a "docking site" where the transported segment meets the far native bone end.
5. Plan for docking-site management — this frequently requires secondary bone grafting or exchange nailing to achieve union at the docking site even after successful transport.
6. For very long diaphyseal defects (> 8–10 cm), especially in the humerus/forearm or where local biology is severely compromised (post-osteomyelitis), consider a free or pedicled vascularized fibular graft as an alternative or adjunct.

## Pitfalls

- Prolonged external fixation time is the major burden — frames are often worn for many months, with an external fixation index of roughly 1–1.5 months per centimeter of defect transported.
- Pin-site infection burden is significant and is the most common reason patients/surgeons favor Masquelet when defect size allows either option.
- Docking-site nonunion is common and frequently requires secondary bone grafting or exchange nailing even after otherwise successful transport.
- Bone transport carries greater overall patient burden and complexity than Masquelet, and should be selected for defect size/soft-tissue reasons rather than by habit.
- Comparative meta-analysis data show bone transport achieves a higher union rate (~95%) than Masquelet (~86%) but at the cost of a higher complication burden and longer external fixation time — functional outcomes are otherwise comparable.

## Post-op protocol

**Nonunion/segmental defect reconstruction (bone transport)**
- Post-frame placement: non- to touch-weight-bearing initially, ROM of adjacent joints maintained, pin-site care education.
- Distraction phase: typically starts 5–7 days post-corticotomy at ~1 mm/day; weight-bearing as tolerated through the frame is often encouraged, since axial loading stimulates regenerate bone formation, unless contraindicated by the fixation type.
- Post-docking: protected weight-bearing progressing over 6–12 weeks guided by radiographic incorporation at the docking site; full weight-bearing typically not before 3–4 months and often later for large-defect reconstructions.
- Return to work/sport is defined case-by-case and typically delayed 9–18 months for large segmental reconstructions given the prolonged biological remodeling timeline.
