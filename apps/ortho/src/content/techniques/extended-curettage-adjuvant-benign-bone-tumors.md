## Indications

- Giant cell tumor of bone (GCT), Campanacci I–II routinely, and many Campanacci III lesions when a joint-sparing plane is achievable (± neoadjuvant denosumab).
- Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC), after biopsy/sampling of any solid component to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma or secondary ABC arising within another lesion.
- Unicameral bone cyst (UBC), for recurrent or high fracture-risk lesions (proximal femur especially), typically after steroid injection has failed or is not appropriate.
- Chondrosarcoma grade I / atypical cartilaginous tumor (ACT) of the appendicular skeleton — an increasingly favored alternative to wide resection for these low-grade lesions, supported by equivalent oncologic outcomes with better function.
- Enchondroma with pain or pathologic fracture, when imaging cannot confidently exclude low-grade chondrosarcoma (endosteal scalloping >2/3 cortical thickness, cortical breach, soft tissue mass, or progressive growth should instead prompt biopsy before presumptive curettage).
- Fibrous dysplasia requiring structural reconstruction after curettage.

## Positioning & setup

Positioning follows the anatomic site of the lesion. A headlight is used to allow direct visualization into the depth of the cavity. Instrumentation includes a full set of curettes and a high-speed burr, plus the chosen chemical/thermal adjuvant (phenol, liquid nitrogen cryotherapy, or argon beam coagulation) and reconstruction material (PMMA cement or bone graft/substitute).

## Steps

1. Confirm diagnosis and margin plan (Enneking stage, Campanacci grade for GCT) before proceeding; for ABC, sample any solid/soft-tissue component first to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma or secondary ABC.
2. Create a cortical window large enough to access the full cavity — an inadequately sized window is the most common reason for incomplete curettage and early recurrence.
3. Perform mechanical curettage of the entire cavity with curettes, then extend the high-speed burr 1–2 mm into grossly normal-appearing bone circumferentially before applying any adjuvant — mechanical technique remains the dominant determinant of local control.
4. Apply the chosen local adjuvant (phenol, liquid nitrogen cryotherapy, or argon beam coagulation) to extend the thermal/chemical kill zone beyond the curetted margin.
5. Reconstruct the cavity with PMMA cement (preferred by many for immediate mechanical support, ability to load-bear sooner, and easier radiographic detection of local recurrence against cement than against incorporated graft) or bone graft/substitute.
6. For UBC with high fracture risk (proximal femur especially), consider adding intramedullary decompression (cannulated screws or an elastic stable intramedullary nail placed across the cyst) for durable decompression and mechanical support.
7. For fibrous dysplasia, use cortical allograft rather than cancellous autograft — autograft placed into dysplastic bone tends to be resorbed and remodeled into further dysplastic bone, giving poor durability; add an intramedullary rod for the proximal femur to prevent/treat progressive varus "shepherd's crook" deformity in polyostotic/McCune-Albright disease.
8. For Campanacci III GCT with joint destruction not amenable to joint-sparing curettage, or for recurrent disease after failed curettage, convert to wide resection with endoprosthetic or allograft reconstruction instead.

## Pitfalls

- An inadequately sized cortical window is the most common technical cause of incomplete curettage and early recurrence — do not undersize the access window to minimize cosmetic morbidity at the expense of complete cavity access.
- Cement generates measurable exothermic thermal necrosis that contributes to the adjuvant effect, but this should not be relied on as the sole adjuvant — mechanical curettage technique and a dedicated chemical/thermal adjuvant remain the dominant determinants of local control.
- Neoadjuvant denosumab to downstage a Campanacci III GCT is a genuine tradeoff: it induces new bone formation and fibrosis around residual tumor that can make the curettage plane harder to define, and is associated with higher local recurrence in several retrospective series compared with curettage alone in resectable disease — discuss this tradeoff with the patient rather than treating it as a free downstaging strategy.
- Local recurrence rates vary by lesion and adjuvant use: GCT curettage alone ~25–50% vs ~10–15% with adjuvant + cement; ABC ~10–20% even with adjuvant curettage; chondrosarcoma grade I after extended curettage with adjuvant has recurrence comparable to historical wide resection series in modern cohorts (roughly 7.5–11%), but higher than for a truly wide margin.
- Chondrosarcoma grade I curettage requires confidence in the grade — endosteal scalloping >2/3 cortical thickness, cortical breach, associated soft tissue mass, or progressive growth on serial imaging should prompt biopsy rather than presumptive curettage, as these features suggest a higher grade requiring wide resection instead.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Protection | 0–2 weeks | Curettage + cement: protected partial WB from day 1–2. Grafted cavity/large defect: non-WB | Passive ROM of adjacent joints as construct allows | Wound care, drain management, extended DVT prophylaxis, pain control |
| 2 — Early mobilization | 2–6 weeks | Curettage/cement: progress to full WB if radiographically stable. Graft-only: remains protected | Active-assisted → active ROM; gentle isometrics | Early gait training |
| 3 — Progressive loading | 6–12 weeks | Graft/curettage cavities: full WB once incorporation confirmed | Full active ROM goal | Closed-chain strengthening initiated, gait normalization, home exercise program |
| 4 — Strengthening & return to function | 3–6 months | Full WB established | Full ROM maintenance | Proprioceptive/balance training, progressive resistance |

Return to work is typically sedentary at 6–12 weeks once wound-healed and pain-controlled, with physical labor delayed 4–6 months or longer depending on reconstruction. For benign lesions treated with curettage alone (small/moderate cavity, good bone quality restored), return to full activity including sport is reasonable by 3–6 months once radiographic healing/incorporation is confirmed and the bone is judged to have regained adequate structural strength.
