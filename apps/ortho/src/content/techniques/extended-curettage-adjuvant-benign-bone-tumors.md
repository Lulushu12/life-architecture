## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Giant cell tumor of bone (GCT), Campanacci I–II routinely, and many Campanacci III lesions when a joint-sparing plane is achievable (± neoadjuvant denosumab).
- Aneurysmal bone cyst (ABC), after biopsy/sampling of any solid component to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma or secondary ABC arising within another lesion.
- Unicameral bone cyst (UBC), for recurrent or high fracture-risk lesions (proximal femur especially), typically after steroid injection has failed or is not appropriate.
- Chondrosarcoma grade I / atypical cartilaginous tumor (ACT) of the appendicular skeleton — an increasingly favored alternative to wide resection for these low-grade lesions, supported by equivalent oncologic outcomes with better function.
- Enchondroma with pain or pathologic fracture, when imaging cannot confidently exclude low-grade chondrosarcoma (endosteal scalloping >2/3 cortical thickness, cortical breach, soft tissue mass, or progressive growth should instead prompt biopsy before presumptive curettage).
- Fibrous dysplasia requiring structural reconstruction after curettage.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Any lesion with radiographic or MRI features suggesting a higher-grade process (wide zone of transition, cortical breach with soft tissue mass, aggressive periosteal reaction) — biopsy first at a treating center rather than proceeding to presumptive curettage.
- Campanacci III GCT with joint destruction not amenable to a joint-sparing curettage plane, or recurrent disease after a prior failed curettage — convert to wide resection with endoprosthetic or allograft reconstruction instead.
- Suspected secondary ABC overlying an unsampled solid/soft-tissue component — biopsy the solid component first; do not curette blind.
- Chondrosarcoma with any of: endosteal scalloping >2/3 cortical thickness, cortical breach, associated soft tissue mass, or progressive growth on serial imaging — these features suggest grade II/III disease requiring wide resection, not curettage.
- Axial/pelvic location for any of the above — wide margins are harder to achieve and curettage carries a higher relative morbidity there; a more aggressive approach is generally warranted regardless of grade.
- Poor bone stock or an unreconstructable defect after planned curettage (e.g., extensive cortical loss threatening structural integrity) without a clear reconstruction plan.

## Preoperative Planning

- Plain radiographs (orthogonal views of the full bone) plus MRI to define the extent of the lesion, cortical integrity, and any soft tissue component; CT is useful for assessing cortical thinning/breach and for surgical planning of the access window.
- Confirm Enneking stage (benign lesions) and, for GCT, Campanacci grade — this determines whether curettage alone is appropriate or whether neoadjuvant denosumab or primary wide resection should be discussed.
- For any lesion where the diagnosis is not secure (particularly ABC with a solid component, or cartilage lesions near the grade I/II borderline), arrange image-guided or open biopsy at/with the treating center before committing to curettage.
- Template the expected defect size against available reconstruction options: PMMA cement volume/mixing system, structural or morselized allograft, cortical allograft (fibrous dysplasia specifically), or bone graft substitute.
- For UBC with high fracture risk, plan concurrently for intramedullary decompression hardware — cannulated screws or an elastic stable intramedullary nail sized to the canal.
- For fibrous dysplasia of the proximal femur, plan for an intramedullary rod (not just curettage/grafting) to prevent or treat a progressive varus "shepherd's crook" deformity.
- Instrumentation checklist: full curette set (multiple sizes, straight and angled/curved for reaching recesses), high-speed burr with assorted burr heads, headlight or magnification loupes, the chosen chemical/thermal adjuvant (phenol, liquid nitrogen cryotherapy system, or argon beam coagulator), hydrogen-peroxide-soaked sponges/mops for soft-tissue isolation, pulsatile lavage, PMMA cement and mixing/delivery system or bone graft/substitute, and fluoroscopy.
- If neoadjuvant denosumab is being considered to downstage a Campanacci III lesion, discuss the tradeoff explicitly with the patient before starting therapy (see Pitfalls) and set a defined window (typically a limited number of monthly doses) before proceeding to surgery, since prolonged courses make the curettage plane progressively harder to define.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or regional (spinal/epidural for lower-extremity lesions) anesthesia per site and patient factors.
- Position according to the anatomic site of the lesion (supine for most femur/tibia/humerus lesions, lateral or beach-chair for proximal humerus, etc.) with a radiolucent table to allow intraoperative fluoroscopy.
- Apply a pneumatic tourniquet for extremity lesions when the anatomy allows, to keep the cavity dry for adequate visualization during curettage and adjuvant application; exsanguinate before inflation.
- Prep and drape widely enough to extend the incision proximally/distally should the plan need to convert to wide resection intraoperatively (frozen section surprise, unexpectedly soft/friable margin).
- Mark surface landmarks (joint line, palpable subcutaneous border of the bone, prior biopsy tract) before incision; the biopsy tract, if present, must be excised en bloc with the specimen/incorporated into the approach.
- Set up fluoroscopy in the field to confirm cortical window location and depth of curettage/burring, and to check final implant/graft/cement position.
- Have a headlight or loupe magnification donned before opening the cortical window — visualization into the depth of the cavity is the single biggest determinant of a complete curettage.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the diagnosis and margin plan (Enneking stage, Campanacci grade for GCT) before proceeding; for ABC, sample any solid/soft-tissue component first to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma or secondary ABC.
2. Approach directly onto the bone through the plane dictated by the lesion's location, incorporating any prior biopsy tract into the incision.
3. Create a cortical window large enough to access the full cavity. Plan the window's size and position under fluoroscopy before cutting — an inadequately sized window is the most common reason for incomplete curettage and early recurrence; do not undersize it to minimize cosmetic morbidity at the expense of complete access.
4. Isolate the surrounding soft tissues with hydrogen-peroxide-soaked sponges/mops before opening the cavity, to reduce the risk of local tumor cell contamination/seeding of the wound.
5. Perform mechanical curettage of the entire cavity with a full set of curettes — start with larger curettes for bulk removal, then progress to smaller, angled, or flexible curettes to reach recesses and corners the straight curette cannot access. This "scooping" mechanical debulking is the dominant determinant of local control, more so than any adjuvant.
6. Extend the high-speed burr 1–2 mm into grossly normal-appearing bone circumferentially ("burr-down" technique) before applying any adjuvant — this removes microscopic tumor extension beyond the visibly curetted margin. Use a headlight/magnification to confirm a uniformly bleeding, healthy-appearing cancellous surface circumferentially.
7. Irrigate copiously with pulsatile lavage to clear debris and assess hemostasis before applying the adjuvant.
8. Apply the chosen local adjuvant to extend the thermal/chemical kill zone beyond the curetted margin, taking care to avoid excessive contact with surrounding soft tissue (protect with the peroxide-soaked mops placed in step 4):
   - **Phenol**: applied with cotton-tipped applicators for a defined dwell time, then neutralized/washed with alcohol and saline.
   - **Liquid nitrogen cryotherapy**: direct pour or spray technique, typically 1–2 freeze-thaw cycles, with attention to protecting adjacent soft tissue and articular cartilage from cryo-injury.
   - **Argon beam coagulation**: applied circumferentially over the cavity surface for a controlled depth of thermal necrosis with less soft-tissue toxicity than phenol.
9. Reconstruct the cavity with PMMA cement (preferred by many for immediate mechanical support, ability to load-bear sooner, and easier radiographic detection of local recurrence against cement than against incorporated graft) or bone graft/substitute. When using cement near a subchondral surface, build up a few millimeters of subchondral bone graft first rather than cementing directly against the joint, to avoid a hard cement-on-cartilage interface and to better distribute load.
10. For UBC with high fracture risk (proximal femur especially), add intramedullary decompression (cannulated screws or an elastic stable intramedullary nail placed across the cyst) at the time of curettage for durable decompression and mechanical support.
11. For fibrous dysplasia, use cortical allograft rather than cancellous autograft to fill the defect — autograft placed into dysplastic bone tends to be resorbed and remodeled into further dysplastic bone, giving poor durability — and place an intramedullary rod for the proximal femur to prevent/treat progressive varus "shepherd's crook" deformity in polyostotic/McCune-Albright disease.
12. Confirm final cavity fill, cement/graft position, and any adjunctive hardware position under fluoroscopy in two planes before closure.
13. Close in layers with attention to hemostasis (cavities can ooze after cementing/burring); place a drain if the cavity was large or bleeding was significant.
14. For Campanacci III GCT with joint destruction not amenable to joint-sparing curettage, or for recurrent disease after failed curettage, abandon the curettage plan intraoperatively (if not already decided preoperatively) and convert to wide resection with endoprosthetic or allograft reconstruction instead.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Plan the cortical window generously and centrally over the lesion's longest axis — a window that is too small forces you to curette "around corners" blind, which is the single most correctable cause of incomplete clearance.
- Use angled and flexible curettes routinely, not just straight ones — most residual disease after curettage is found in recesses that a straight curette cannot reach.
- Headlight or loupe magnification into the cavity depth measurably improves the ability to distinguish tumor from normal cancellous bone at the margin.
- Isolate the wound with hydrogen-peroxide-soaked mops before opening the cavity to reduce the theoretical risk of local seeding during curettage and adjuvant application.
- Use the high-speed burr methodically and circumferentially (the "burr-down" technique) rather than only in areas that look grossly abnormal — microscopic extension is, by definition, not visible.
- Keep the field dry (tourniquet where feasible) during curettage and adjuvant application — blood in the cavity dilutes phenol and impairs visualization of residual tumor.
- Build up a few millimeters of subchondral bone graft before filling with cement when the cavity approaches a joint surface, to avoid direct cement-cartilage contact and to cushion the subchondral bone.
- Confirm cavity access and final reconstruction fluoroscopically in two planes — do not rely on direct vision alone for depth/completeness in a deep or irregularly shaped cavity.
- When neoadjuvant denosumab has been used, expect the tumor-bone interface to be harder to define by both feel and appearance; curette more conservatively but more thoroughly, and have a low threshold to extend the burr margin further given the anticipated loss of a clean visual/tactile plane.
- Keep the incision extensile and the field draped widely enough to convert to wide resection without needing to re-prep if intraoperative findings (frozen section, unexpectedly soft/friable tissue) suggest higher-grade disease than anticipated.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- An inadequately sized cortical window is the most common technical cause of incomplete curettage and early recurrence — do not undersize the access window to minimize cosmetic morbidity at the expense of complete cavity access.
- Cement generates measurable exothermic thermal necrosis that contributes to the adjuvant effect, but this should not be relied on as the sole adjuvant — mechanical curettage technique and a dedicated chemical/thermal adjuvant remain the dominant determinants of local control.
- Neoadjuvant denosumab to downstage a Campanacci III GCT is a genuine tradeoff: it induces new bone formation and fibrosis around residual tumor that can make the curettage plane harder to define, and is associated with higher local recurrence in several retrospective series compared with curettage alone in resectable disease — discuss this tradeoff with the patient rather than treating it as a free downstaging strategy.
- Local recurrence rates vary by lesion and adjuvant use: GCT curettage alone ~25–50% vs ~10–15% with adjuvant + cement (one series combining scooping curettage, burr-down technique, phenolization, and cement reported recurrence as low as ~7.5%); ABC ~10–20% even with adjuvant curettage; chondrosarcoma grade I after extended curettage with adjuvant has recurrence comparable to historical wide resection series in modern cohorts (roughly 7.5–11%), but higher than for a truly wide margin.
- Chondrosarcoma grade I curettage requires confidence in the grade — endosteal scalloping >2/3 cortical thickness, cortical breach, associated soft tissue mass, or progressive growth on serial imaging should prompt biopsy rather than presumptive curettage, as these features suggest a higher grade requiring wide resection instead.
- Excessive phenol or cryotherapy contact with unprotected surrounding soft tissue can cause local soft-tissue necrosis, skin slough (if the window is too superficial), or injury to adjacent neurovascular structures — always isolate the field with protective mops before applying the adjuvant.
- Iatrogenic fracture through the curetted cavity can occur if a large defect is not adequately protected postoperatively before healing/incorporation — match the postoperative weight-bearing restriction to the size of the residual defect and quality of reconstruction, not to a fixed timeline.
- Postoperative hematoma/wound drainage is more common after large-cavity curettage with extensive burring — maintain a drain and appropriate compression dressing when bleeding is brisk at closure.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Protection | 0–2 weeks | Curettage + cement: protected partial WB from day 1–2. Grafted cavity/large defect: non-WB | Passive ROM of adjacent joints as construct allows | Wound care, drain management, extended DVT prophylaxis, pain control |
| 2 — Early mobilization | 2–6 weeks | Curettage/cement: progress to full WB if radiographically stable. Graft-only: remains protected | Active-assisted → active ROM; gentle isometrics | Early gait training |
| 3 — Progressive loading | 6–12 weeks | Graft/curettage cavities: full WB once incorporation confirmed | Full active ROM goal | Closed-chain strengthening initiated, gait normalization, home exercise program |
| 4 — Strengthening & return to function | 3–6 months | Full WB established | Full ROM maintenance | Proprioceptive/balance training, progressive resistance |

Return to work is typically sedentary at 6–12 weeks once wound-healed and pain-controlled, with physical labor delayed 4–6 months or longer depending on reconstruction. For benign lesions treated with curettage alone (small/moderate cavity, good bone quality restored), return to full activity including sport is reasonable by 3–6 months once radiographic healing/incorporation is confirmed and the bone is judged to have regained adequate structural strength.
