## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Surgical decompression is indicated for cubital tunnel syndrome that fails a trial of non-operative management (McGowan I-IIa) or presents with measurable intrinsic weakness or advanced disease (McGowan IIb-III). McGowan grade drives the operative threshold, and specific anatomic factors — not grade alone — drive the choice between in situ decompression and anterior transposition: nerve subluxation/snapping over the medial epicondyle on dynamic exam, recurrent/revision surgery, significant cubitus valgus or bony deformity, and inadequate glide after in situ release all favor transposition over simple decompression.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Mild, purely subjective sensory symptoms (McGowan I) without objective findings should exhaust a structured non-operative trial (splinting, activity/positional modification, nerve gliding exercises) before surgery is offered.
- Severe, fixed intrinsic wasting/clawing of very long duration is not a contraindication to surgery but warrants counseling that motor recovery may be incomplete regardless of technique — surgery is still indicated to halt further deterioration and address pain/sensory symptoms.
- Active infection over the medial elbow.
- Submuscular transposition in particular should be used cautiously in patients who cannot tolerate a longer period of relative elbow immobilization/protected motion (occupational or medical factors), given its greater early wound and stiffness morbidity relative to in situ release or subcutaneous transposition.
- A normal or only mildly abnormal preoperative nerve conduction study in a patient with an atypical history should prompt re-evaluation for a cervical or more proximal process (thoracic outlet, C8/T1 radiculopathy) before committing to surgery.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm McGowan grade clinically (elbow flexion-compression test, Tinel's at the cubital tunnel, Wartenberg's and Froment's signs, intrinsic wasting/clawing) and with NCS/EMG (across-elbow conduction velocity <50 m/s or a drop >10 m/s versus the forearm segment is diagnostic; EMG documents denervation in advanced disease).
- Examine specifically for dynamic nerve subluxation/snapping with active elbow flexion-extension — this single finding is often the deciding factor for transposition over in situ decompression and should be documented pre-operatively, not discovered intraoperatively.
- Assess for cubitus valgus, prior elbow fracture/deformity, or a palpable mass (ganglion, anconeus epitrochlearis, heterotopic bone) that would favor an open, wider exposure and possibly transposition.
- Plan revision strategy if this is a repeat operation: anticipate scar, a possibly adherent or already-transposed nerve, and be prepared to extend the exposure proximally/distally.
- No implants; equipment is a standard hand/upper-extremity soft tissue set with bipolar cautery for perineural vessels, plus loupe magnification.
- Discuss and mark handedness/laterality; confirm no ipsilateral shoulder or cervical spine pathology that could confound postoperative assessment of recovery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or regional block (supraclavicular/axillary) with sedation; WALANT (local tumescent lidocaine-epinephrine without tourniquet) is increasingly used for in situ decompression and even for transposition in selected patients, allowing intraoperative active elbow flexion to confirm the nerve does not sublux or kink after transposition.
- **Positioning:** supine with the arm abducted on a hand table, elbow flexed and the medial epicondyle facing up; alternatively a bump/rolled towel under the shoulder can improve access to the medial elbow. Some surgeons prefer a lateral position with the arm draped over a bolster for improved medial exposure in larger patients.
- **Tourniquet:** a well-padded arm tourniquet is standard if not using WALANT; exsanguinate with Esmarch and inflate to standard upper-arm pressure. With WALANT, no tourniquet is used, which has the added benefit of allowing live intraoperative excursion testing of the transposed nerve.
- **Landmarks marked before incision:** the medial epicondyle, the course of the ulnar nerve palpated/subluxated preoperatively if possible, and the incision line curving gently posterior to the epicondyle (avoid a straight incision directly over the epicondyle, which crosses the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve branches at a right angle and increases neuroma risk).
- **Draping:** standard sterile extremity drape exposing the entire elbow circumferentially from mid-humerus to mid-forearm to allow full exposure and, if transposition is chosen, room to develop the flexor-pronator fascial sling.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**A. In situ decompression (first-line for most McGowan I-IIb without instability)**

1. Make a longitudinal or gently curved incision centered over the cubital tunnel, roughly 6-8 cm long, curving slightly posterior to the medial epicondyle to avoid crossing directly over it.
2. Carefully identify and protect branches of the medial antebrachial cutaneous (MABC) nerve in the subcutaneous layer before deepening the dissection — these are superficial and easily transected if the dissection is rushed.
3. Identify the ulnar nerve proximal to the cubital tunnel (proximal to the arcade of Struthers) and trace it distally.
4. Release Osborne's ligament (the roof of the cubital tunnel, the fibrous arcuate band spanning the two heads of FCU) sharply under direct vision, then continue the release distally through the deep flexor-pronator fascia between the humeral and ulnar heads of FCU for several centimeters until the nerve is free of any constriction.
5. Release the intermuscular septum proximally if it is compressing the nerve or if a proximal arcade of Struthers is contributing; avoid devascularizing the nerve by preserving its segmental blood supply and minimizing circumferential stripping.
6. Take the elbow through a full range of flexion-extension and directly observe/palpate the nerve for subluxation over the medial epicondyle. If the nerve consistently subluxates anteriorly with flexion, convert to transposition rather than leaving an unstable nerve in situ.
7. Achieve hemostasis, close the fascia/subcutaneous layer loosely (do not reconstruct the roof of the tunnel tightly), and close skin.

**B. Anterior subcutaneous transposition**

1. Perform the same exposure and decompression as above (release Osborne's ligament, deep flexor-pronator fascia, and intermuscular septum), fully mobilizing the nerve from just proximal to the arcade of Struthers to several centimeters distal to the medial epicondyle, including ligating/coagulating the small vascular leash that crosses the nerve near the epicondyle if needed for mobility.
2. Excise or release the distal intermuscular septum completely — a retained septal edge is a common cause of a new, iatrogenic compression point at the site the nerve now crosses after being moved anteriorly.
3. Elevate an anterior soft-tissue/skin flap and transpose the nerve anterior to the medial epicondyle, ensuring a straight, tension-free, kink-free course without any residual sharp angulation proximally or distally.
4. Create a stabilizing sling to prevent the nerve from re-subluxating posteriorly: a common technique is a fascial sling raised from the flexor-pronator fascia (e.g., two or three parallel incisions in the fascia to create hinged flaps that are sutured to each other over the transposed nerve, leaving a loose, non-constricting hammock) or a sling fashioned from Osborne's ligament itself; avoid suturing subcutaneous fat directly over the nerve under tension.
5. Take the elbow through full flexion-extension again to confirm the nerve now stays anterior to the epicondyle throughout the arc and glides freely without kinking at either end of the sling.
6. Close the fascial sling loosely (never tightly, which recreates a compressive tunnel), then subcutaneous tissue and skin.

**C. Submuscular transposition**

1. Perform the same nerve mobilization as above, then additionally elevate the flexor-pronator muscle origin from the medial epicondyle (a Z-lengthening or step-cut of the common flexor-pronator origin is commonly used) to allow the nerve to lie directly on the anterior surface of the deep flexor musculature/brachialis, deep to the muscle bulk.
2. Lay the nerve in this submuscular bed in a straight, tension-free course, then repair the flexor-pronator origin back down (lengthened, to avoid recreating tension across the newly transposed nerve) over the nerve.
3. Confirm free excursion with elbow flexion-extension before completing the muscle repair.
4. This technique is reserved for revision cases, cases with significant cubitus valgus/bony deformity, or when subcutaneous transposition has failed or is judged inadequate, given its greater soft-tissue morbidity and longer required protection.

**D. Medial epicondylectomy** (less commonly used alternative): partial excision of the medial epicondyle removes the bony prominence the nerve subluxates over without formally transposing it; requires careful subperiosteal technique to avoid destabilizing the medial collateral ligament origin, and is generally reserved for surgeons experienced with the technique or specific anatomic scenarios.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify and protect the MABC nerve branches early, in the subcutaneous layer, before deep dissection — this is the single most common avoidable nerve injury in cubital tunnel surgery and causes a painful, disabling neuroma if transected.
- Trace the ulnar nerve proximal to the arcade of Struthers and distal past the two heads of FCU into the forearm — a release that stops at Osborne's ligament alone without extending into the deep flexor-pronator fascia is a common cause of incomplete decompression.
- When performing transposition, explicitly excise the distal intermuscular septum; leaving it in place is one of the most common causes of a new, more distal point of compression after the nerve has been moved.
- Preserve the nerve's segmental vascular supply by avoiding long-segment circumferential stripping; coagulate only the specific vessels that tether the nerve and prevent transposition.
- Build the fascial sling loose, not tight — its job is to prevent posterior re-subluxation, not to compress the nerve; test excursion with active/passive elbow flexion before closing every layer.
- If using WALANT, take advantage of awake, active elbow flexion-extension testing intraoperatively to directly confirm the transposed nerve tracks anteriorly without kinking — this is far more reliable than passive testing under anesthesia.
- In situ decompression should still include a directed assessment for subluxation at the end of the case; a stable-appearing nerve preoperatively can still prove dynamically unstable once decompressed, and this changes the operative plan on the table.
- For revision cases, plan the incision to allow full exposure of the previously transposed segment and be prepared for adhesions and an unpredictable course of the nerve — proceed cautiously and identify normal nerve proximally and distally before addressing the scarred segment.
- Counsel patients preoperatively, especially McGowan III patients, that intrinsic motor recovery lags sensory recovery by months and may remain incomplete — this avoids postoperative dissatisfaction with an otherwise technically successful decompression.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Nerve subluxation after in situ release favors converting to (or primarily choosing) transposition in predisposed anatomy.
- Wound complications are more common with submuscular transposition than with subcutaneous transposition or in situ release.
- Medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve (MABC) injury causes a painful neuroma if the nerve is not identified and protected during exposure — a common avoidable complication.
- Persistent intrinsic weakness in advanced (McGowan III) cases can occur regardless of technique — counsel patients pre-operatively that severe, longstanding disease may not fully recover.
- Incomplete decompression and new/worsened ulnar nerve symptoms are recognized complications independent of which technique is chosen.
- A retained distal intermuscular septal edge or an inadequately mobilized nerve creates a new iatrogenic compression point after transposition — this is a technical error, not an inherent limitation of the procedure.
- Devascularization of the nerve from overly aggressive circumferential stripping during mobilization can worsen outcomes rather than improve them.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **In situ decompression:** early motion with brief splint immobilization (days) mainly for comfort, progressive activity as tolerated; light duty in 1-2 weeks, heavier labor at 4-6 weeks.
- **Transposition (especially submuscular):** longer immobilization (1-3 weeks in a posterior splint/sling protecting the transposed segment), then progressive elbow ROM, with heavier lifting/resistance restricted 6-8 weeks.
- Sensory recovery precedes motor recovery after either technique; intrinsic recovery in McGowan III cases may remain incomplete regardless of technique or timing.
