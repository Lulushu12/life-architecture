## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- **Tension-band wiring (TBW):** simple transverse fractures at the level of the trochlear notch in low-demand patients with good bone quality (Mayo IIA).
- **Plate fixation:** comminuted, oblique, or distally-extending fracture lines, and osteoporotic bone (Mayo IIB/III) — plating provides angularly stable fixation independent of an intact anterior cortex.
- **Intramedullary screw (± suture-tension band augmentation):** simple transverse fractures where a lower-profile construct is desired to reduce hardware prominence.
- **Transolecranon fracture-dislocation (Monteggia/olecranon variant, Mayo III):** requires plate fixation with restoration of the trochlear notch and treatment of any associated coronoid or radial head injury.
- Displacement threshold for surgery: **>2 mm** articular step-off or extensor mechanism disruption (including a positive resisted-extension lag even with minimal displacement on static imaging — a functionally unstable fracture requires fixation regardless of the millimeters measured on a static film).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Non-displaced, stable fractures (Mayo IA/IB) with intact extensor mechanism on active resisted testing — treat non-operatively with splint/brace and serial radiographs.
- Active local skin infection or heavily contaminated open wound not yet debrided — manage per open-fracture protocol before definitive internal fixation.
- Very low-demand, medically frail patients with minimally displaced fractures and preserved (even if weak) active extension — individualize toward non-operative management given the added risk of a second procedure for hardware removal.
- TBW is relatively contraindicated (in favor of plating) in any oblique, comminuted, or distally-extending fracture line, since the construct depends on an intact anterior cortical hinge that these patterns lack.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP/lateral elbow radiographs are usually sufficient for simple patterns; obtain CT for comminuted fractures, transolecranon fracture-dislocations, or when preoperative planning of the trochlear notch reconstruction is uncertain from plain films alone.
- **Skin assessment:** assess skin integrity circumferentially before proceeding — olecranon and proximal ulna fractures are frequently open due to the subcutaneous location; treat per the BOAST open fracture standard (debridement <24h for low-grade injuries, IV antibiotics within 1h of arrival).
- **Implant selection:** for TBW, plan two parallel 1.6mm K-wires plus 18-gauge stainless wire (or a cannulated 4.0–4.5mm screw plus tension-band wire); for plating, select a pre-contoured locking olecranon (proximal ulna) plate sized to the patient's bone, with proximal screws configured to capture the small proximal fragment securely; for a hybrid construct, a 6.5mm (or similarly sized) intramedullary screw with suture- or wire-tension band augmentation.
- **Case-specific planning:** examine triceps continuity actively (resisted extension against gravity) preoperatively and document — a positive extensor lag changes the urgency/indication discussion even with modest displacement on imaging. For suspected transolecranon fracture-dislocation, plan for possible associated coronoid or radial head injury and have small-fragment/headless screw sets available.
- **Equipment checklist:** small fragment and mini fragment sets, 1.6mm K-wires, 18-gauge wire (or suture-tape tension band system), cannulated screw set with washer, pre-contoured locking olecranon plate options in two sizes, pointed reduction clamps, wire tensioner/twister, mini C-arm.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or regional (supraclavicular/axillary brachial plexus) block — regional block is well suited to this extremity-only procedure and provides useful postoperative analgesia.
- **Position:** lateral decubitus with the arm draped over a padded bolster, or supine with the arm across the chest/on a hand table with the elbow flexed — lateral decubitus gives the most direct posterior access to the olecranon and is generally preferred for comminuted or plate cases.
- **Tourniquet:** pneumatic tourniquet on the upper arm, exsanguinate, inflate to standard limb pressure; olecranon fixation is usually a shorter case, but document tourniquet time regardless.
- **Fluoroscopy:** mini C-arm positioned to allow true AP and lateral views of the elbow without repositioning the arm — used to confirm articular reduction, K-wire/screw trajectory (avoiding joint penetration), and final hardware position.
- **Draping:** standard posterior elbow prep from above the tourniquet to the fingertips; note that olecranon fractures are frequently associated with an open wound at the fracture site itself given the subcutaneous location — extend the prep/drape generously to incorporate any traumatic wound into the surgical field for formal debridement.
- **Landmarks marked:** palpate and mark the olecranon tip and the subcutaneous border of the ulna before incision, planning a slightly curved (not straight-over-the-tip) posterior midline incision to reduce painful scar contact with the point of the elbow.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a posterior midline incision curving slightly around the olecranon tip; if a traumatic wound is already present at the fracture site, incorporate it into the incision after formal debridement rather than making a separate clean incision.
2. Expose the fracture subperiosteally, minimizing further soft-tissue stripping; irrigate out hematoma and clot from the fracture site and joint.
3. Reduce the fracture anatomically under direct vision, confirming restoration of the trochlear (semilunar) notch articular surface — use a pointed reduction clamp across the fracture and check articular congruity by direct palpation/visualization through the fracture gap before committing to fixation.

**Tension-band wiring (simple transverse pattern, Mayo IIA):**

4. Place two parallel 1.6mm K-wires from the tip of the olecranon, across the fracture, engaging the anterior cortex of the ulnar diaphysis distally — keep the wires as parallel as possible and avoid excessive divergence, which is a recognized technical error that compromises construct stability.
5. Drill a transverse hole through the ulna 2–3cm distal to the fracture, pass an 18-gauge wire through it in a figure-eight pattern crossing posterior to the K-wires, and tighten by twisting two ends together (or using a commercial tensioning device) until the wire is snug — the figure-eight configuration converts tensile (extensor) load into interfragmentary compression at the articular surface during flexion.
6. Bend the proximal ends of the K-wires into a hook and impact them into the bone, or bend and cut them short and bury them beneath the triceps insertion — leaving wires too long or improperly seated is a major driver of later hardware irritation.
7. Confirm on fluoroscopy that no K-wire has penetrated the trochlear notch articular surface, that the wires are reasonably parallel, and that the tension-band wire loop is snug (not loose) — a loose figure-eight configuration is a recognized technical imperfection that leads to early loss of fixation.

**Plate fixation (comminuted, oblique, or distally-extending pattern, or osteoporotic bone, Mayo IIB/III):**

4. After anatomic reduction of the articular surface (bridging comminuted fragments as needed with an indirect/bridging technique to restore the trochlear notch contour without necessarily anatomically reducing every small fragment), apply a pre-contoured locking olecranon plate along the subcutaneous (posterior) border of the proximal ulna.
5. Place locking screws in the proximal fragment to capture as much of the tip as possible (many designs allow multiple divergent screws into the small proximal fragment), and place bicortical screws distally along the ulnar shaft, achieving standard working-length fixation.
6. For comminuted fragments not amenable to direct fixation, use the plate itself as a buttress/bridge, restoring overall length and the trochlear notch contour rather than forcing anatomic reduction of every fragment.
7. Confirm on fluoroscopy that the trochlear notch is congruent, no screw has penetrated the joint, and plate position does not impinge on the proximal radioulnar joint or produce a prominence that will be symptomatic under the skin (the subcutaneous location of the olecranon makes even well-placed plates more likely to be palpable/symptomatic than plates elsewhere).

**Intramedullary screw ± suture-tension band (hybrid, simple transverse pattern):**

4. After reduction, pass a guide pin down the intramedullary canal of the ulna from the olecranon tip, confirm position on fluoroscopy, and place a cannulated screw of appropriate length and diameter over the guide pin, achieving compression across the fracture.
5. Augment with a suture-tape or wire tension band passed through a distal transverse drill hole and around/through the screw head or a proximal drill hole, tensioned in a figure-eight pattern, to protect against extensor pull-off, particularly during early motion.

**Transolecranon fracture-dislocation (any technique above, plus):**

4. Reduce and fix the olecranon component anatomically first to restore the trochlear notch — this indirectly reduces the ulnohumeral joint, since the dislocation in this pattern follows the olecranon fragment.
5. Assess for and address any associated coronoid or radial head injury per terrible-triad principles once the olecranon fixation is secure.

**Closure (all techniques):**

8. Take the elbow through a gentle passive range of motion to confirm stable fixation and no mechanical block or hardware impingement.
9. Irrigate, close the triceps expansion/fascia over the fixation, close subcutaneous tissue and skin, and apply a well-padded posterior splint with the elbow at 45–90° flexion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm articular (trochlear notch) congruity by direct visualization through the fracture gap before final fixation — a fracture that "looks reduced" externally can still have a step-off in the notch that is only visible from inside the joint.
- Keep tension-band K-wires as parallel as possible and avoid intramedullary passage into soft cancellous bone alone — engaging the anterior cortex distally gives more reliable purchase and construct stability.
- Bury or carefully contour the proximal ends of TBW K-wires beneath the triceps insertion — this is the single most effective step in reducing the very high reported rates of symptomatic hardware after TBW.
- In plating, orient proximal locking screws divergently within the small proximal fragment to maximize purchase — most contemporary olecranon plate systems are designed specifically to allow this "fan" pattern of fixed-angle screws into limited proximal bone stock.
- For comminuted fragments, prioritize restoring the overall trochlear notch contour and length with a bridging/buttress plate technique over forcing anatomic reduction of every small piece — this preserves fragment vascularity and is more forgiving in osteoporotic bone.
- Consider an all-suture or suture-tape tension band construct instead of metal wire for simple transverse fractures in patients highly motivated to avoid a second surgery for hardware removal — contemporary comparative data suggest equivalent or superior biomechanical performance to wire with reduced hardware prominence.
- Always incorporate a preexisting traumatic wound at the fracture site into the surgical incision (after formal debridement) rather than creating an additional separate incision — this reduces the total zone of soft-tissue compromise around the olecranon.
- Take the elbow through a gentle passive arc intraoperatively before closing to confirm the fixation does not block motion and that hardware does not impinge the proximal radioulnar joint during rotation.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- TBW is a dynamic compression construct that depends on an intact anterior cortical hinge under axial (extensor) load — it fails in oblique, comminuted, or distally-extending fracture lines; use plating instead in these patterns.
- Reported hardware-irritation/removal rates after TBW are high (roughly 9–80% across series, with some series reporting up to 75% prominent painful hardware and hardware removal indicated in 65% of patients) — counsel patients that a second surgery for wire removal is common.
- Recognized technical imperfections that compromise TBW constructs include: non-parallel K-wires, wires that are too long or extend radially outward, intramedullary-only wire passage without anterior cortex purchase, joint-surface perforation, a single (rather than double/secured) wire knot, a jutting/prominent wire knot, a loose figure-eight configuration, and incomplete/incorrect repositioning of the fracture before wiring — each is independently avoidable with careful technique.
- Because olecranon fractures are frequently open, treat per standard open-fracture principles (early debridement, antibiotics, staged fixation if grossly contaminated) to minimize infection risk.
- Nonunion risk is elevated after inadequate fixation of comminuted/oblique patterns treated with TBW.
- Plate prominence under the subcutaneously-located olecranon skin remains a recognized source of symptomatic hardware even with locking plate constructs — contour carefully and counsel patients that removal is occasionally needed even after plating.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–1 week:** splint immobilization for comfort/soft-tissue rest.
- **1–2 weeks:** transition to a hinged brace, begin gentle AROM (flexion typically well-tolerated early; extension against gravity/resisted triceps activity delayed).
- **6 weeks:** avoid resisted elbow extension (triceps loading against the fixation) until this point to protect the tension-band/plate construct from extensor-mechanism pull-off, then progress resisted strengthening.
- **3 months:** full strengthening; return to contact/manual labor once radiographically healed and strength restored.
