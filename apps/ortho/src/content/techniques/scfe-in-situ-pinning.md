## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- In-situ single-screw fixation is the standard of care for the great majority of slips, both stable and most unstable.
- There is no accepted non-operative pathway once SCFE is confirmed — even a mildly symptomatic, stable, minimally displaced slip is treated surgically because of the risk of progression.
- Stable slips (Loder) can generally be scheduled semi-urgently (within 24–48h); unstable slips are a surgical urgency (same-day/urgent).
- Prophylactic contralateral fixation is a related, individualized indication (see below).

**Contraindications / when to consider an alternative technique instead**
- Severe chronic slips with a large Southwick head-shaft angle and anticipated significant residual femoroacetabular impingement may be considered for a corrective procedure (modified Dunn, or a staged/subsequent corrective osteotomy) rather than simple in-situ fixation, at centers experienced with those techniques — see the modified Dunn procedure guide for the comparative discussion.
- Active septic arthritis of the hip must be excluded/treated before elective-appearing pinning proceeds, since it changes the surgical plan.
- Forceful closed reduction of an unstable slip is not an acceptable alternative technique — gentle positional reduction only is used (see Step-by-Step Technique).

## Preoperative Planning

- Obtain AP pelvis and a true lateral or cross-table lateral of the affected hip (avoid forcing a frog-leg lateral in a suspected unstable slip, since positioning itself can displace the epiphysis further); measure the Southwick head-shaft angle and assess Klein's line.
- Determine Loder stability (ability to weight-bear with or without crutches) — this is the single most important variable driving urgency and expected AVN risk, and should be documented explicitly.
- Screen the contralateral hip on the same films at the time of diagnosis, and again at every subsequent visit — early/subtle contralateral slips are easily missed.
- Select cannulated screw system and size in advance (screw diameter appropriate to the child's femoral neck size; have more than one length available). A single screw is favored over multiple screws in most contemporary practice.
- Decide whether prophylactic contralateral fixation will be offered at the same anesthetic — discuss with family beforehand given the individualized nature of this decision (see below).
- If atypical features are present (age <10 or >16, weight below 50th percentile, short stature, bilateral synchronous presentation), coordinate preoperative endocrine/metabolic work-up, though this should not delay urgent fixation of an unstable slip.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia (regional adjunct as tolerated).
- Fracture table with fluoroscopy, or a radiolucent flat table with a bump, depending on surgeon preference and stability of the slip — a fracture table facilitates controlled, gentle positioning for unstable slips.
- For unstable slips, only gentle positional reduction is used — achieved simply by positioning the leg (mild traction, slight internal rotation/extension as tolerated) without forceful manipulation; aggressive closed reduction maneuvers are avoided given evidence they increase AVN risk by further compromising the tenuous remaining blood supply.
- Prep and drape the hip and proximal thigh widely enough to allow the C-arm full AP and true lateral access.
- Position the C-arm to obtain orthogonal (90°) AP and lateral views of the femoral neck and epiphysis without moving the leg between views where possible — this speeds the case and reduces the temptation to force positioning for imaging.
- Mark the anterolateral thigh starting point (proximal to the lesser trochanter, distal to the intertrochanteric line) before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **For unstable slips, achieve gentle positional reduction** on the fracture table by positioning the leg only (mild traction, gentle internal rotation/extension as tolerated by the soft tissues) — do not attempt forceful reduction maneuvers; accept whatever position is achieved without force.
2. **Confirm the starting point** on the anterolateral femur — proximal to the lesser trochanter (to minimize the risk of a subtrochanteric stress fracture through the guidewire/screw hole) and distal to the intertrochanteric line (to avoid the screw head impinging anteriorly). Make a small (~1 cm) longitudinal incision at this point.
3. **Pass the guidewire under fluoroscopic guidance**, directed toward the central third of the epiphysis, perpendicular to the physis on both AP and lateral views. Aim for a "center-center" position in the epiphysis to minimize the risk of inadvertent joint penetration and to maximize purchase.
4. **Check wire position on orthogonal fluoroscopic views** before proceeding — a wire that looks centrally placed on the AP view but is actually eccentric or peripheral on the lateral view (or vice versa) is a common technical error that undermines fixation.
5. **Confirm the depth** — the wire/screw tip should stop approximately 3–5 mm short of the subchondral bone; measure this directly off the fluoroscopic image on both views rather than relying on a fixed preoperative screw-length estimate alone.
6. **Measure for screw length**, ream/tap as needed per the implant system, and **place a single cannulated screw** over the guidewire, aiming for at least 5 threads across the physis for adequate fixation.
7. **Take the hip through a full range of internal-external rotation under live fluoroscopy** after screw placement to confirm the screw has not violated the joint cavity — this dynamic check is more sensitive for occult penetration than static AP/lateral images alone.
8. **Favor a single screw over multiple screws** in most contemporary practice — it achieves adequate stabilization with lower reported rates of penetration-related chondrolysis and is technically simpler to revise if needed.
9. **Irrigate and close** the small incision in layers (fascia, subcutaneous tissue, skin).
10. **Consider prophylactic contralateral fixation** at the same anesthetic, individualized rather than routine — favored in patients with open triradiate cartilage, young age at presentation (<10–12y), modifiable endocrinopathy, obesity, or where reliable follow-up cannot be assured; if performed, repeat the same technique on the contralateral hip.
11. **Obtain final fluoroscopic images** (and postoperative radiographs) documenting screw position, depth, and thread count across the physis for the medical record.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Choose the anterolateral starting point carefully: too proximal (near or below the lesser trochanter) risks a stress riser and subtrochanteric fracture; too distal/anterior risks screw-head impingement against the anterior neck/intertrochanteric region.
- Confirm central screw/wire position on both AP and true lateral views at every step, not just before final seating — catching an eccentric trajectory early, while it is still just a guidewire, is far easier to correct than after the cannulated screw is seated.
- Stop 3–5 mm short of the subchondral bone rather than aiming for maximum depth — chasing extra purchase by advancing closer to the joint surface materially raises the risk of penetration and chondrolysis without a proportionate gain in fixation strength.
- Use the dynamic range-of-motion fluoroscopic check after screw placement — internal/external rotation of the hip under live imaging is the most sensitive intraoperative test for occult intra-articular screw penetration.
- Aim for 5 or more threads across the physis, and center the screw within the epiphysis on both views to spread the load evenly and reduce the risk of screw cut-out or continued slip progression.
- Do not force a frog-leg lateral for imaging in a suspected unstable slip preoperatively or intraoperatively — a true or cross-table lateral avoids the risk of displacing the epiphysis further through positioning alone.
- Screen and document the contralateral hip on the same operative fluoroscopic run if feasible, and revisit the prophylactic fixation discussion explicitly with the family before the child leaves the OR, since this decision is often easier to make proactively than to revisit at a later, separate anesthetic.
- Keep the incision small and the dissection minimal — this is intended to be a percutaneous or mini-open procedure; extensive soft-tissue stripping is unnecessary and adds morbidity without benefit.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Confirm central screw position on both AP and true lateral fluoroscopic views before final seating — a screw that appears epiphysis-centered on AP but is actually eccentric/peripheral on the lateral risks inadequate purchase and slip progression.
- Stop 2–5 mm short of subchondral bone, confirmed on both views, to avoid intra-articular penetration, historically implicated in chondrolysis.
- Do not attempt forceful closed reduction of an unstable slip beyond simple, gentle positioning — pressure-driven reduction maneuvers have been specifically associated with higher AVN rates in several series.
- Screen the contralateral hip at every visit — early/subtle contralateral slips are easily missed, and 20–40% of unilateral presenters go on to a contralateral slip if not prophylactically fixed, highest in the youngest patients.
- Subtrochanteric stress fracture through an overly distal/aggressive starting point is a recognized, avoidable technical complication — respect the "proximal to lesser trochanter" boundary.
- AVN, particularly in unstable slips, remains the dominant driver of long-term disability regardless of technique; it is a disease-related risk more than a technique-related one once gentle positioning (rather than forced reduction) is used.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: protected weight-bearing (touch-down/toe-touch to partial per surgeon protocol) on crutches for stable slips fixed promptly; unstable slips kept more strictly protected (touch-down only) for a longer initial period given higher AVN risk and theoretical benefit of unloading the compromised epiphysis.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing as tolerated, guided by pain and radiographic screw position; gentle active ROM, avoiding forced internal rotation/flexion that stresses the fixation.
- **Weeks 6–12**: full weight-bearing typically achieved; physiotherapy for hip and core strengthening, gait normalization, and gradual return to low-impact recreational activity.
- **Months 3–6**: return to sport/full activity individualized — earlier for uncomplicated stable slips with good screw position and no AVN signs on interval imaging (often by 3–4 months), later and more cautiously for unstable slips or any radiographic AVN concern.
- **AVN surveillance**: any unstable slip or one with delayed presentation warrants interval clinical and radiographic (± MRI) follow-up through at least the first postoperative year, as radiographic AVN changes may not be apparent for several months.
- **Contralateral surveillance**: continued clinical/radiographic monitoring of the unaffected hip through skeletal maturity in patients not treated with prophylactic fixation.
- **Return to school**: 1–2 weeks for stable slips (crutch-assisted return acceptable); 2–4 weeks, protected, for unstable slips.
