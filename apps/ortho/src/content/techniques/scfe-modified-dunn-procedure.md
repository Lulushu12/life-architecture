## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Proposed primarily for unstable/severe slips at high-volume centers with surgeons experienced in the technique, on the rationale that anatomic realignment reduces long-term femoroacetabular impingement and may reduce chondrolysis risk compared with fixation of a residually deformed hip.
- Severe chronic slips (Southwick head-shaft angle >50°) where in-situ fixation would leave substantial residual deformity and anticipated impingement.

**Contraindications / considerations**
- This remains one of the most actively contested questions in pediatric orthopedics and should be regarded as unresolved rather than settled — in-situ pinning remains the default for the large majority of slips (stable, and most unstable slips managed promptly).
- Not recommended outside high-volume centers with specific experience in the Ganz surgical hip dislocation technique — the dissection itself risks the retinacular vessels in less experienced hands.
- Referral to a high-volume center for modified Dunn consideration is reasonable for severe, unstable slips, particularly when there will be unavoidable delay to fixation, but is not mandatory given the absence of definitive comparative superiority data.
- Not indicated for stable, mild-to-moderate slips, where simple in-situ pinning has equivalent outcomes with lower technical demand and morbidity.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm Loder stability status and Southwick head-shaft angle on AP pelvis and cross-table lateral imaging (avoid forcing a frog-leg lateral in an unstable slip).
- Plan for a lateral decubitus position and the instrumentation needed for a Ganz surgical hip dislocation: a digastric trochanteric osteotomy set, capsulotomy instruments, and standard epiphyseal fixation (cannulated screws or smooth pins, per surgeon preference) plus osteochondroplasty burrs for the anticipated concurrent cam resection.
- Discuss and consent explicitly for the trochanteric osteotomy component (with its own fixation, typically screws, and its own healing/complication profile) as part of the overall procedure.
- Given the urgency of unstable slips, balance thorough preoperative planning against the time-sensitive nature of the case — this procedure is generally reserved for centers where the full team and instrumentation are reliably available without meaningful delay.
- Consider intraoperative fluoroscopy and, where available, a means of assessing femoral head perfusion (e.g. intraosseous pressure monitoring or Doppler) as an adjunct to direct visual assessment of the retinacular vessels.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia.
- Lateral decubitus position on a standard or radiolucent table, with the operative hip up, positioned and padded as for a standard Ganz surgical hip dislocation approach; secure the pelvis firmly to allow controlled hip manipulation during the dislocation and reduction maneuvers.
- Prep and drape the entire limb free, from the iliac crest to below the knee, to allow full intraoperative range of motion and dislocation of the hip.
- Mark the greater trochanter, the planned longitudinal lateral incision centered over it, and the Gibson interval (between tensor fascia lata and gluteus maximus) before incision.
- Position fluoroscopy for AP and lateral pelvis/hip views, used intermittently to confirm epiphyseal reduction and fixation position.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach** — make a longitudinal lateral incision centered over the greater trochanter, carrying sharp dissection down to the fascia lata, and develop the Gibson interval between tensor fascia lata and gluteus maximus.
2. **Perform a digastric trochanteric (flip) osteotomy**, roughly 1 cm in depth, to mobilize the trochanteric fragment anteriorly with the gluteus medius and vastus lateralis attached, while leaving a cuff (roughly 15 mm) of posterior gluteus medius fibers behind and freeing the vastus lateralis from the underlying bone — this preserves the soft-tissue sleeve that protects the retinacular vessels as the fragment is mobilized.
3. **Perform a Z-shaped capsulotomy** — the long arm running along the length of the femoral neck, a horizontal limb along the physis, and a final limb curving anteriorly toward the lesser trochanter — to expose the femoral neck and head while controlling the capsule for later repair.
4. **Dislocate the hip anteriorly** with gentle external rotation and flexion, following release of the ligamentum teres, to bring the femoral head out of the acetabulum and fully expose the head-neck junction and the epiphysis.
5. **Prepare a retinacular soft-tissue flap**, carefully protecting the posterosuperior retinacular vessels running along the posterior femoral neck to the epiphysis — this is the single most important step in the entire procedure, since these vessels are the epiphysis's remaining blood supply.
6. **Remove the remaining physis and any abundant posterior callus/metaphyseal bone** at the slip site, sculpting the neck to allow the epiphysis to be brought down onto the metaphysis without impinging or over-tensioning the retinacular flap.
7. **Achieve controlled reduction of the slipped epiphysis onto the metaphysis**, gently derotating and reducing the head into an anatomic or near-anatomic position relative to the neck, under direct vision of the retinacular vessels throughout — reduction should be achieved without tension or kinking of the flap.
8. **Provisionally fix the epiphysis** to the metaphysis (smooth pins or cannulated screws, per surgeon preference), confirming position on fluoroscopy in both AP and lateral planes.
9. **Assess retinacular vessel patency/head perfusion visually** (and via intraosseous pressure monitoring or Doppler if used) before committing to final fixation — do not proceed if there is visible compromise of the flap.
10. **Complete definitive epiphyseal fixation.**
11. **Perform concurrent femoral neck osteochondroplasty**, resecting the residual cam-type prominence at the head-neck junction to reduce anticipated femoroacetabular impingement, while continuing to protect the retinacular vessels during this step.
12. **Reduce the hip** and take it through a range of motion to confirm stable, impingement-free motion and a well-seated femoral head.
13. **Repair the capsule**, then reduce and fix the trochanteric osteotomy fragment (typically with screws), restoring the abductor lever arm.
14. **Close in layers** — fascia, subcutaneous tissue, skin, with a drain per surgeon preference given the extensive dissection.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Treat the retinacular flap dissection as the rate-limiting, highest-stakes step of the entire case — go slowly, use magnification if available, and be willing to accept a less-than-perfect anatomic reduction rather than risk the blood supply for the last few degrees of correction.
- Leave an adequate posterior cuff of gluteus medius (roughly 15 mm) attached during the digastric trochanteric osteotomy — this cuff carries part of the soft-tissue protection for the retinacular vessels as the fragment is mobilized anteriorly.
- Free the vastus lateralis from the trochanteric fragment before flipping it anteriorly — this allows the fragment to move without tethering and reduces traction on the surrounding soft tissues.
- Confirm epiphyseal reduction on both AP and lateral fluoroscopic views before final fixation, exactly as in isolated in-situ pinning — the same central, perpendicular screw principles apply once the head-neck relationship is corrected.
- Assess head perfusion by direct visual inspection of the retinacular vessels (bleeding from a drill hole in the epiphysis is a classic intraoperative perfusion check) before finalizing fixation, and consider intraosseous pressure monitoring or Doppler as an adjunct where available.
- Sequence the osteochondroplasty after epiphyseal fixation is secure, and continue to work around (not through) the retinacular flap during head-neck junction resection.
- Do not underestimate the trochanteric osteotomy repair — secure fixation of the flipped fragment is necessary to restore abductor function and avoid a postoperative Trendelenburg gait or nonunion of the trochanteric fragment itself.
- This is not a procedure to learn on an urgent unstable slip in the middle of the night without prior experience — case volume and familiarity with the Ganz approach are directly tied to the vascular safety of the dissection.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The more technically demanding modified Dunn dissection itself risks compromising the retinacular vessels in less experienced hands, potentially causing the very AVN it aims to prevent — this is the central argument against routine/non-expert-center use.
- Comparative literature remains dominated by case series and case-control studies rather than randomized trials: a 2024 case-control study restricted to stable/moderate SCFE found no significant difference in outcomes between in-situ pinning and modified Dunn, questioning routine use of the more invasive procedure outside unstable/severe slips.
- Long-term (7-year) prospective case-series data on the modified Dunn in moderate-to-severe SCFE continue to report favorable functional and radiographic outcomes, but specifically in expert hands — outcomes at lower-volume centers are not well represented in this literature.
- Historical AVN rates in unstable slips, while elevated, are not clearly improved by the modified Dunn approach in several comparative series, so it should not be assumed to be categorically safer than in-situ pinning for a given center's actual experience level.
- Trochanteric osteotomy nonunion or the need for later hardware removal is a procedure-specific complication not present with simple in-situ pinning, given the additional osteotomy required for surgical hip dislocation.
- Persistent or recurrent femoroacetabular impingement can still occur despite osteochondroplasty if resection is incomplete or if head-neck offset remains suboptimal after reduction.

## Postoperative Protocol

Because the modified Dunn procedure is reserved for unstable/severe slips, post-operative management follows the unstable-slip pathway: strictly protected touch-down-only weight-bearing for a longer initial period (given the higher baseline AVN risk of this population and the theoretical benefit of unloading the compromised epiphysis), progressive weight-bearing as tolerated by weeks 2–6, and full weight-bearing typically by weeks 6–12 with hip/core physiotherapy and gait normalization. Trochanteric osteotomy healing is followed in parallel with the epiphyseal fixation, and abductor strengthening is emphasized once bony healing allows. AVN surveillance (clinical and radiographic ± MRI) is required through at least the first postoperative year, since radiographic AVN changes may not appear for several months. Return to sport is deferred, often 6+ months, pending serial MRI/radiographic reassurance. Contralateral hip surveillance continues through skeletal maturity if prophylactic fixation was not performed.
