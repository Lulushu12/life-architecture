## Indications

Proposed primarily for unstable/severe slips at high-volume centers with surgeons experienced in the technique, on the rationale that anatomic realignment reduces long-term femoroacetabular impingement and may reduce chondrolysis risk compared with fixation of a residually deformed hip. This remains one of the most actively contested questions in pediatric orthopedics and should be regarded as unresolved rather than settled — in-situ pinning remains the default for the large majority of slips (stable, and most unstable slips managed promptly), with referral to a high-volume center for modified Dunn consideration reasonable for severe, unstable slips, particularly when there will be unavoidable delay to fixation, but not mandatory given the absence of definitive comparative superiority data.

## Positioning & setup

Performed via a Ganz surgical hip dislocation approach, which allows direct visualization of the head-neck deformity and retinacular blood supply.

## Steps

1. **Perform a Ganz surgical hip dislocation** to directly visualize the head-neck deformity.
2. **Confirm the retinacular blood supply intact throughout** — a key theoretical advantage over closed methods, where blood supply status is inferred rather than seen.
3. **Achieve controlled reduction of the slipped epiphysis onto the metaphysis.**
4. **Fix the epiphysis** in the corrected position.
5. **Add concurrent femoral neck osteochondroplasty**, often performed, to address the residual cam deformity.

## Pitfalls

- The more technically demanding modified Dunn dissection itself risks compromising the retinacular vessels in less experienced hands, potentially causing the very AVN it aims to prevent — this is the central argument against routine/non-expert-center use.
- Comparative literature remains dominated by case series and case-control studies rather than randomized trials: a 2024 case-control study restricted to stable/moderate SCFE found no significant difference in outcomes between in-situ pinning and modified Dunn, questioning routine use of the more invasive procedure outside unstable/severe slips.
- Long-term (7-year) prospective case-series data on the modified Dunn in moderate-to-severe SCFE continue to report favorable functional and radiographic outcomes, but specifically in expert hands — outcomes at lower-volume centers are not well represented in this literature.
- Historical AVN rates in unstable slips, while elevated, are not clearly improved by the modified Dunn approach in several comparative series, so it should not be assumed to be categorically safer than in-situ pinning for a given center's actual experience level.

## Post-op protocol

Because the modified Dunn procedure is reserved for unstable/severe slips, post-operative management follows the unstable-slip pathway: strictly protected touch-down-only weight-bearing for a longer initial period (given the higher baseline AVN risk of this population and the theoretical benefit of unloading the compromised epiphysis), progressive weight-bearing as tolerated by weeks 2–6, and full weight-bearing typically by weeks 6–12 with hip/core physiotherapy and gait normalization. AVN surveillance (clinical and radiographic ± MRI) is required through at least the first postoperative year, since radiographic AVN changes may not appear for several months. Return to sport is deferred, often 6+ months, pending serial MRI/radiographic reassurance. Contralateral hip surveillance continues through skeletal maturity if prophylactic fixation was not performed.
