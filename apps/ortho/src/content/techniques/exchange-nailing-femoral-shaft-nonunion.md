## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- First-line surgical salvage when a diaphyseal fracture fails to progress radiographically by 6 months (or shows no interval change on two consecutive visits 6-8 weeks apart), for a well-aligned, hypertrophic/oligotrophic nonunion with infection excluded.
- Nonunion following an unreamed index nailing, or a reamed nailing with an undersized nail relative to canal diameter, where a larger-diameter reamed nail is expected to substantially improve mechanical stability and endosteal biology.
- Broken or failed interlocking screws with ongoing motion at the nonunion site in an otherwise well-aligned construct.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Rotationally or angularly malaligned nonunion — exchange nailing alone will not correct deformity and instead requires deformity correction (osteotomy, staged correction, or a fixation strategy capable of controlling alignment, such as a plate or combined construct) at the time of revision.
- Infected nonunion — proceeding with exchange nailing in the presence of unrecognized or inadequately treated infection risks seeding a new, larger-diameter implant into a septic canal; infection must be excluded (or eradicated first, staged with an antibiotic spacer) before exchange nailing.
- Atrophic nonunion with segmental bone loss — the biologic deficit in atrophic nonunion is not reliably corrected by reaming and a larger nail alone; these cases need adjunctive bone grafting (autograft, RIA-harvested graft, or vascularized fibula for large segmental defects) and often a different fixation strategy altogether.
- Very short residual proximal or distal segment precluding adequate new interlock purchase — consider a combined construct or plate augmentation instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: current AP/lateral full-length femur radiographs to characterize nonunion morphology (hypertrophic "elephant's foot," oligotrophic, or atrophic), assess alignment in both planes, and confirm hardware status (intact vs broken interlocking screws, nail integrity). CT with metal-artifact reduction protocols can help characterize bridging bone and rule out subtle deformity not apparent on plain films, and is particularly useful when rotational malalignment is suspected clinically but not obvious radiographically.
- **Infection workup**: inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) at minimum; if clinical suspicion exists (prior wound problems, drainage, elevated markers, systemic symptoms), obtain preoperative aspiration/culture or plan intraoperative tissue cultures and frozen section before committing to single-stage exchange. Do not proceed with a definitive exchange nail if infection is not confidently excluded.
- **Implant planning**: confirm the existing nail's manufacturer/system if possible (operative report, implant card) to plan extraction instrumentation (extraction post/device compatible with the indwelling nail); template the anticipated new nail diameter (typically 1-2 mm larger than the existing nail) and length, and confirm reaming equipment capable of reaching the larger diameter is available.
- **Equipment**: nail extraction set matched to the existing implant, broken-screw removal instruments (trephines, screw-removal sets) in case interlocking screws have failed or are difficult to back out, a full reaming set able to progress well beyond the prior nail's diameter, and bone graft materials/RIA harvesting system on standby in case intraoperative findings (unexpectedly atrophic bone, bone loss) require a change in plan mid-procedure.
- **Case-specific planning**: address modifiable nonunion risk factors preoperatively where possible — counsel on smoking cessation, discontinue NSAIDs, optimize glycemic control in diabetic patients, and plan early postoperative weight bearing as part of the revision strategy, since these factors (higher BMI, diabetes, NSAID use, unreamed index nailing, delayed weight-bearing initiation, smoking, open fracture) are all associated with nonunion risk and, where modifiable, should be corrected to reduce the risk of a repeat nonunion after exchange.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, as for primary antegrade nailing; regional block adjuncts (fascia iliaca/femoral nerve block) can be used for postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent fracture table with traction, or lateral decubitus on a flat table, following the same setup as primary antegrade femoral nailing — the procedure is performed as a revision through the existing nail's entry point, so positioning should allow full fluoroscopic access to the original entry site, the nonunion zone, and the distal interlocking region.
- **Draping**: wide prep and drape from the iliac crest to the knee, exposing the existing proximal entry incision/scar and any existing interlocking incisions so they can be reopened rather than creating unnecessary new incisions.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: confirm full-length imaging access before draping, with particular attention to clear visualization of the nonunion site itself (to assess reduction/alignment during extraction and reinsertion) and both proximal and distal interlocking regions.
- **Landmarks**: mark the existing entry portal and prior interlocking incision sites, identified from the preoperative imaging and, where available, prior operative records.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm eligibility intraoperatively** before committing to exchange: re-verify alignment is acceptable and, if any doubt about infection remains, send frozen section and/or intraoperative cultures before proceeding to definitive reaming and new nail placement — a positive intraoperative finding should convert the case to a staged infected-nonunion protocol rather than proceeding with single-stage exchange.
2. **Reopen the existing proximal entry incision** and expose the proximal end of the indwelling nail.
3. **Remove any proximal interlocking screws**, using a broken-screw removal set if a screw is stripped, broken, or otherwise difficult to extract through standard means.
4. **Attach the extraction device/post** matched to the existing nail system and extract the nail, applying steady controlled traction; if the nail is well-incorporated or bent, extraction can require substantial force — have a backup plan (e.g., a stronger slap-hammer/extraction device, or in rare cases, an open approach to the nonunion site) if standard extraction fails.
5. **Remove the distal interlocking screws** through the existing distal incisions once the nail has been sufficiently withdrawn proximally to allow this, or before final withdrawal, depending on the sequence dictated by the specific extraction system.
6. **Pass a new guidewire** down the existing canal, confirming it crosses the nonunion site centrally on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy.
7. **Ream sequentially, 1-2 mm larger than the prior nail diameter**, to maximize endosteal stimulation (the "internal bone graft" effect of reaming) and mechanical canal fill — this larger-diameter reaming is the central biologic and mechanical rationale for exchange nailing's effectiveness in an aligned, non-infected nonunion.
8. **Assess the nonunion site directly** during reaming and guidewire passage for gross malalignment not previously appreciated; if unexpected instability or deformity is found, be prepared to convert to an open reduction or blocking-screw-assisted correction before final nail passage.
9. **Pass the new, larger-diameter nail** across the nonunion site to the same general depth/positioning principles as a primary nailing.
10. **Lock the nail statically** (proximal and distal) through new or reused interlocking holes as dictated by the new nail's hole pattern relative to the prior incisions.
11. **Confirm rotation and length** against the contralateral limb intraoperatively, exactly as in primary nailing — exchange nailing does not exempt the surgeon from this check, and pre-existing rotational deformity from the index procedure should be actively corrected at this step if present.
12. **Obtain final fluoroscopic confirmation** of nail position, interlocking screw placement, and overall alignment before closing.
13. **Close incisions in layers**, irrigating the entry and interlocking sites; consider deep tissue/bone cultures sent even if infection was not clinically suspected, per surgeon/institutional preference, given the consequences of an unrecognized low-grade infection.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Have a broken-screw removal set immediately available before starting — interlocking screws in a nonunion construct have frequently been in place long enough to be difficult to extract by standard means, and this is one of the most common sources of unexpected delay in exchange nailing.
- Confirm the existing nail manufacturer/system preoperatively whenever possible; a mismatched or unavailable extraction post is a preventable case-delaying problem.
- Ream progressively and re-check fluoroscopic tracking through the nonunion site at each pass — the nonunion itself can act as a pivot point where the reamer skives off-axis more easily than through healthy diaphyseal bone.
- Use the reaming pass itself as an opportunity to directly visualize (fluoroscopically) whether the nonunion gap is closing with reamed debris/endosteal contact, which offers real-time reassurance (or a warning) about the adequacy of the biologic stimulus being provided.
- If extraction proves unexpectedly difficult, do not escalate force indiscriminately — confirm no residual interlocking screw or nail deformity (bent nail) is mechanically blocking withdrawal before applying maximal extraction force, since forced extraction of a mechanically blocked nail risks fracturing the femur at the nonunion site.
- Correct any pre-existing rotational malalignment from the index nailing during the exchange — this is a genuine opportunity to fix a problem that may itself have contributed to the nonunion (malrotation alters loading across the fracture site).
- Counsel and document modifiable risk-factor optimization (smoking cessation, NSAID avoidance, glycemic control) at the time of surgical planning, since these factors meaningfully affect the success rate of the exchange procedure itself, not just future injury risk.
- Consider bone graft augmentation (even in an aligned, non-infected nonunion) if intraoperative findings suggest more atrophic biology than the preoperative imaging indicated — have graft material or an RIA harvesting setup available as a contingency rather than as a routine requirement.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Exchange nailing alone is appropriate only for an aligned, non-infected nonunion. A rotationally or angularly malaligned nonunion, an infected nonunion, or an atrophic nonunion with bone loss instead requires deformity correction, infection eradication (staged with an antibiotic spacer if septic), and often adjunctive bone grafting (autograft, RIA-harvested graft, or vascularized fibula for large segmental defects) rather than exchange nailing alone.
- Known nonunion risk factors — higher BMI, diabetes, NSAID use, unreamed index nailing, delayed weight-bearing initiation, smoking, open fracture — should be addressed where modifiable (e.g., NSAID avoidance, early weight bearing) to reduce the risk of a repeat nonunion after exchange.
- Difficult or failed nail/screw extraction can prolong operative time substantially and, if forced inappropriately, risks fracturing the femur at the nonunion site — have contingency extraction instrumentation and a plan for conversion to an open approach if standard extraction fails.
- Under-recognized low-grade infection proceeding to single-stage exchange risks converting a treatable aseptic nonunion into an infected nonunion around a larger, harder-to-remove implant — maintain a low threshold for intraoperative cultures/frozen section when any preoperative signal (elevated inflammatory markers, prior wound problems) is present.
- Failure to correct rotational malalignment at the time of exchange perpetuates the biomechanical problem that may have contributed to the original nonunion.
- Repeat nonunion after exchange nailing, though less common than after the index procedure when technique and risk factors are optimized, still occurs — plan close radiographic follow-up given the prior failure to unite, and have a bone-grafting or construct-change contingency in mind if healing again stalls.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Reported union rates after exchange nailing for femoral shaft nonunion are favorable (commonly cited in the 80-90% range) when the nonunion is aligned and infection has been excluded.
- Follow the standard femoral shaft nailing rehabilitation pathway thereafter (early mobilization, weight bearing per construct stability, progressive strengthening), with close radiographic follow-up to confirm healing given the prior failure to unite.
- Reassess at 6-8 week intervals with serial radiographs to confirm progressive callus formation; a repeat lack of radiographic progression by 3-6 months post-exchange should prompt reassessment for an unrecognized biologic or mechanical contributor (infection, inadequate reduction, insufficient fixation stability) rather than simply extending the observation period further.
