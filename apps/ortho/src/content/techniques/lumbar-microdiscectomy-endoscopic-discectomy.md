## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Persistent, disabling radiculopathy despite 6–12 weeks of conservative care (activity modification, NSAIDs, structured PT); progressive or severe motor deficit; or cauda equina syndrome (emergent — see the dedicated CES technique guide). MRI should confirm level, side, and herniation zone (see Lee classification) before proceeding — imaging is not routinely obtained at first presentation of uncomplicated sciatica, only once conservative care has failed or a progressive deficit is present.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Predominant axial back pain without a concordant, dermatomal radicular pattern is a poor indication for discectomy — imaging findings (disc bulge, mild protrusion) are near-ubiquitous with age and must be clinically concordant, not simply present.
- Active systemic or local infection at the planned surgical site is a contraindication to elective discectomy.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy is a relative contraindication given the epidural venous bleeding risk in a confined canal.
- For endoscopic technique specifically: highly migrated fragments (significantly cranial behind the pedicle, or far caudal) and significant bony lateral recess stenosis are relative contraindications in lower-volume/less-experienced hands, given the steeper learning curve and more limited working corridor compared with open microdiscectomy — case selection should be more conservative early in a surgeon's endoscopic experience.
- A large central disc extrusion causing bilateral symptoms should raise suspicion for evolving cauda equina syndrome — screen explicitly (bladder function, perianal sensation) before proceeding with a routine, non-emergent discectomy plan.
- Severe, longstanding motor deficit (e.g., a fixed, dense foot drop present for many months) without ongoing pain may still be offered decompression to arrest further loss, but expectations for motor recovery should be set conservatively and discussed explicitly pre-operatively.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI to confirm level, side, and herniation zone (Lee classification: Zone 1 central, Zone 2 subarticular/lateral recess, Zone 3 foraminal, Zone 4 extraforaminal/far-lateral) — this directly determines the surgical trajectory and approach, since a standard interlaminar window will miss a Zone 3–4 fragment entirely. Note the sagittal migration pattern (cranial behind the pedicle vs caudal) as well, since significant migration changes the extent and direction of bony resection needed and is a recognised cause of a missed fragment at revision surgery.
- **Approach selection based on zone and migration:** plan the operative approach (standard interlaminar micro/endoscopic for Zone 1–2; more lateral trajectory or transforaminal endoscopic/extended facetectomy for Zone 3; Wiltse paraspinal or extraforaminal endoscopic technique for Zone 4) before the day of surgery, not as an intraoperative improvisation.
- **Technique selection:** decide between open/tubular microdiscectomy and full-endoscopic (interlaminar or transforaminal/PELD) technique based on herniation zone, surgeon experience/case volume, and patient factors — the transforaminal endoscopic route is particularly well suited to foraminal and far-lateral (Zone 3–4) herniations that are technically awkward via a standard interlaminar corridor.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** standard microdiscectomy set (Kerrison rongeurs, curettes, pituitary rongeurs of varying angles and sizes, nerve root retractor, bipolar cautery) with an operating microscope, loupes with headlight, or a tubular retractor system; for endoscopic technique, the full-endoscopic system (working cannula, endoscope, radiofrequency probe/bipolar, endoscopic grasper/rongeur set, continuous irrigation setup) specific to interlaminar or transforaminal technique. Have a dural repair set (fine non-absorbable suture, fibrin sealant/dural patch) available regardless of planned technique.
- **Neuromonitoring/EMG for transforaminal endoscopic technique:** consider a radiofrequency nerve stimulator/continuous EMG probe as an adjunct for confirming safe distance from the exiting nerve root during transforaminal portal placement, particularly earlier in a surgeon's experience with the technique.
- **Fluoroscopy:** confirm C-arm/image intensifier availability and plan for both AP and lateral fluoroscopic access, essential for accurate portal/trajectory placement in transforaminal endoscopic technique and for level confirmation in any approach.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anaesthesia:** general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation is standard for open/tubular microdiscectomy. Endoscopic discectomy can be performed under general, spinal, or local anaesthesia with conscious sedation depending on technique (transforaminal technique is more commonly performed awake/sedated in some practices, which allows real-time patient feedback — e.g., reproduction of leg pain — during nerve root manipulation as an additional safety signal, though many centres use general anaesthesia routinely).
- **Positioning:** prone on a radiolucent spinal frame (Jackson table, Wilson frame, or Andrews frame) with the abdomen free to reduce epidural venous pressure and bleeding; hips flexed to open the interlaminar space for the interlaminar (open, tubular, or endoscopic) approach. For transforaminal endoscopic technique, positioning is similar but the working trajectory is planned obliquely from a more lateral starting point rather than directly posterior.
- **Table setup:** pad all pressure points; protect the eyes and free the abdomen and genitalia; confirm fluoroscopic access will not be obstructed at the working level.
- **Draping and imaging setup:** obtain a lateral (and, for transforaminal technique, an AP) fluoroscopic image after positioning to confirm the level and to plan the skin entry point and trajectory before prepping. For transforaminal endoscopic technique, draw a line from the site of disc pathology through the superior articular process to the skin, as ventral on the superior articular process as anatomically appropriate, to estimate how far off the midline the skin entry point should be.
- **Surface landmarks:** mark the iliac crests (approximate L4-5 level), midline spinous process line, and — for transforaminal technique — the planned paramedian entry point (typically 8–12 cm off midline depending on level and body habitus, individualised from the pre-operative axial imaging) before prep.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Time-out and level confirmation.** Confirm patient, procedure, level, and side with fluoroscopic/spot radiograph localisation before incision.
2. **Select the approach by Lee zone.** Central/subarticular (Zone 1–2): standard interlaminar micro/endoscopic approach. Foraminal (Zone 3): more lateral trajectory — consider transforaminal endoscopic technique or extended facetectomy. Far-lateral/extraforaminal (Zone 4): Wiltse paraspinal approach or extraforaminal endoscopic technique. Standard midline laminotomy will miss Zone 3–4 herniations.
3. **Open/tubular microdiscectomy:**
   a. Make a small paramedian or midline incision centred over the interlaminar space at the confirmed level; for tubular technique, dock a graduated dilator/tubular retractor under fluoroscopic guidance.
   b. Create a partial hemilaminotomy/flavectomy through the interlaminar window using a Kerrison rongeur and/or high-speed burr, exposing the underlying epidural space and traversing nerve root.
   c. Add a medial facetectomy as needed for lateral recess exposure, particularly for a subarticular (Zone 2) fragment.
   d. Identify and gently retract the traversing nerve root medially with a nerve root retractor, protecting it throughout; identify the herniated fragment beneath/lateral to the root.
   e. Excise the herniated fragment with pituitary rongeurs, working systematically to remove free/sequestered material first, then assess the disc space for additional loose material.
4. **Decide extent of discectomy.** Fragmentectomy-only (excise the free/loose fragment without complete nuclear evacuation) reduces disc height loss and theoretically slows degeneration but carries a somewhat higher same-level recurrence risk than more aggressive annular/nucleus removal. Most surgeons perform fragment excision plus removal of loose/free intradiscal material without complete nuclear evacuation, balancing these trade-offs.
5. **Endoscopic discectomy (transforaminal/PELD) — portal placement and access:**
   a. Under fluoroscopic guidance, place a spinal needle along the pre-planned trajectory toward Kambin's triangle — the working corridor bounded by the superior endplate of the inferior vertebral body (base), the superior articular process (height/posterior wall), and the exiting nerve root (hypotenuse/roof).
   b. "Walk" the needle progressively more ventral along the superior articular process until it "falls" into the triangle and enters the disc space, confirmed on AP (needle tip at the medial pedicle line) and lateral (needle tip at the posterior vertebral body line) fluoroscopy.
   c. Pass a guidewire, then sequentially dilate and, if needed, perform foraminoplasty with a high-speed bur or specialised reamer to widen the working corridor at the base of Kambin's triangle, before inserting the working cannula and endoscope.
   d. Because the exiting nerve root forms the roof of this narrow corridor, the most severe potential complication of this step is exiting-root injury from moving the needle, guidewire, or sequential dilators/reamers too superior or too ventral within the foramen — deliberate, image-confirmed, incremental advancement is essential rather than working by feel alone.
6. **Endoscopic discectomy — fragment removal.** Under continuous irrigation and direct endoscopic visualisation, identify and remove the herniated fragment with endoscopic graspers/rongeurs, using a radiofrequency probe for haemostasis and for shrinking/mobilising annular tissue as needed; assess the foramen and lateral recess endoscopically for residual compression before withdrawing.
7. **Interlaminar endoscopic technique (alternative for Zone 1–2 herniations, particularly at L5-S1 where the interlaminar window is often large):** dock the working cannula through a small interlaminar window analogous to the tubular approach, then proceed with endoscopic visualisation and instrumentation in a manner directly analogous to steps 3b–3e but through the endoscopic system.
8. **Consider endoscopic discectomy** as an alternative to open/tubular microdiscectomy where the zone and surgeon experience support it. Current comparative data show endoscopic approaches are non-inferior for leg pain, ODI, and JOA outcomes, with shorter length of stay (often <2 days vs standard admission), faster return to work, and comparable or lower complication/dural tear/reoperation rates in experienced hands.
9. **Account for the learning curve** when adopting endoscopic technique: outcomes in early-adopter/low-volume series lag behind high-volume centers. In low-volume settings, avoid highly migrated fragments and significant bony lateral recess stenosis as endoscopic candidates until experience is established.
10. **Confirm decompression.** Regardless of technique, probe the traversing and exiting nerve roots to confirm they are free of residual compression, and inspect the disc space and epidural space for any remaining loose fragments before closing.
11. **Haemostasis and closure.** Achieve haemostasis with bipolar cautery (or the endoscopic radiofrequency probe); for open/tubular technique, close the fascia and skin in layers; for endoscopic technique, a single skin suture or adhesive closure is often sufficient given the small portal size.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Retract the nerve root gently and only as much as needed to visualise the fragment — persistent or forceful retraction is a more common cause of post-operative dysesthesia than most surgeons appreciate, and intermittent release of retraction during the case reduces this risk.
- When removing a fragment, work systematically from a clearly identified free edge rather than pulling on tissue that has not been mobilised — blind traction on adherent annular/disc material risks avulsing a nerve root or under-resecting a fragment that then fractures, leaving a piece behind.
- After apparent complete fragment removal, actively probe cranially and caudally along the posterior longitudinal ligament and into the axilla of the nerve root for a second, unretrieved fragment — this is one of the most common causes of an early "failed" microdiscectomy.
- For transforaminal endoscopic portal placement, draw the planned trajectory line on the pre-operative axial MRI, marking how ventral on the superior articular process the needle should aim — walking the needle progressively more ventral (rather than starting too medial/posterior) is the key manoeuvre for reliably falling into Kambin's triangle rather than striking the facet or the exiting root.
- Foraminoplasty (burring the ventral/caudal margin of the superior articular process) to widen the base of Kambin's triangle meaningfully improves endoscopic access and reduces the forceful cannula manipulation that can otherwise irritate the exiting root — use it liberally rather than forcing dilators through an unenlarged corridor.
- If an incidental durotomy occurs, obtain clear visualisation before attempting repair — this may require additional selective bony resection, loupe/microscope magnification, and good haemostasis (bipolar, gelatin sponge, cottonoids) so the defect is not obscured by ongoing bleeding.
- Repair a durotomy primarily with fine (6-0 or 7-0) non-absorbable monofilament suture under magnification where the defect and access allow it; reinforce with fibrin sealant, and if the closure remains tenuous, buttress with a small locally harvested muscle graft.
- Always perform a sustained Valsalva manoeuvre (coordinate with anaesthesia) after completing a dural repair, before the microscope/loupes leave the field, to confirm a watertight closure — do not rely on visual inspection alone, and add further sutures, sealant, or a patch if any leak is seen.
- For endoscopic technique, smaller, less severe dural tears with a competent annulus can sometimes be managed expectantly given the different fluid dynamics of continuous low-pressure irrigation compared with an open field — but have a clear, pre-agreed threshold with the team for when to convert to open repair if there is any doubt about adequacy of containment.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Missed fragment — especially a migrated fragment or one in Zone 3–4 — is a recognized failure mode; approach selection must match the zone and sagittal migration pattern.
- Dural tear/CSF leak occurs in roughly 2–3% of open discectomy, generally reported lower in endoscopic series though this is technique-dependent; manage with primary repair or fibrin sealant, or accept small CSF leaks with endoscopic technique given different fluid dynamics.
- Exiting nerve root injury during transforaminal endoscopic portal placement is a specific and recognised risk of the technique — the corridor between the exiting root and the superior articular process in Kambin's triangle is anatomically narrow, and injury typically occurs when the needle, guidewire, dilators, or reamers move too superior or too ventral within the foramen during initial access; deliberate, fluoroscopically-confirmed, incremental instrument advancement is the primary mitigation.
- Same-level recurrent disc herniation carries a lifetime risk of roughly 5–15%, higher with larger annular defects; revision discectomy is generally effective but carries a modestly higher complication profile than primary surgery.
- Epidural hematoma is rare but a leading cause of early post-operative neurological deterioration — maintain a low threshold for emergent MRI and re-exploration if a new or progressive deficit develops in the immediate post-operative period.
- Wrong-level surgery is mitigated by intraoperative localization imaging (fluoroscopy/spot film) and a formal surgical time-out with level confirmation — particularly important in obese patients and those with transitional anatomy.
- Post-operative dysesthesia along the affected nerve root distribution can follow retraction or endoscopic manipulation of the root; usually self-limited over weeks, but persistent cases warrant reassessment for a missed structural cause before attributing symptoms to transient neurapraxia.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2:** wound care; avoid prolonged sitting (>30 min) and bending/lifting >2–4 kg; walking encouraged from day 1; no formal brace typically required.
- **Weeks 2–6:** gradual increase in walking tolerance; initiate core-stabilization/lumbar stabilization exercises avoiding end-range flexion under load; driving typically permitted once off opioids and reaction time/comfort allow (~1–2 weeks); desk-based work return often possible by 2–4 weeks.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progressive strengthening, return to low-impact aerobic conditioning; manual/heavy labor return typically 6–12 weeks depending on job demands and surgeon assessment; return to non-contact sport generally 6–8 weeks; contact/axial-loading sport (weightlifting, contact sports) is individualized, often around 3 months.
- Endoscopic patients often track faster through early milestones (walking, sitting tolerance, return to sedentary work) given the smaller access corridor, but strengthening progression timelines converge with open microdiscectomy by 6–12 weeks.
- Routine post-op imaging is not needed if the clinical course is favorable; obtain imaging for new/worsening neurological deficit or suspected recurrent herniation/epidural hematoma.
