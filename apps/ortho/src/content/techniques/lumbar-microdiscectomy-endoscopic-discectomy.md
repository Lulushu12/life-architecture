## Indications

Persistent, disabling radiculopathy despite 6–12 weeks of conservative care (activity modification, NSAIDs, structured PT); progressive or severe motor deficit; or cauda equina syndrome (emergent). MRI should confirm level, side, and herniation zone (see Lee classification) before proceeding — imaging is not routinely obtained at first presentation of uncomplicated sciatica, only once conservative care has failed or a progressive deficit is present.

## Positioning & setup

Standard open or tubular microdiscectomy is performed via a posterior interlaminar window. Endoscopic discectomy (full-endoscopic interlaminar or transforaminal / percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy, PELD) uses a smaller access corridor; the transforaminal route is particularly suited to foraminal/far-lateral herniations.

## Steps

1. **Select the approach by Lee zone.** Central/subarticular (Zone 1–2): standard interlaminar micro/endoscopic approach. Foraminal (Zone 3): more lateral trajectory — consider transforaminal endoscopic technique or extended facetectomy. Far-lateral/extraforaminal (Zone 4): Wiltse paraspinal approach or extraforaminal endoscopic technique. Standard midline laminotomy will miss Zone 3–4 herniations.
2. **Open/tubular microdiscectomy:** create a partial hemilaminotomy/flavectomy through the interlaminar window, add medial facetectomy as needed for lateral recess exposure, retract the nerve root, and excise the herniated fragment.
3. **Decide extent of discectomy.** Fragmentectomy-only (excise the free/loose fragment without complete nuclear evacuation) reduces disc height loss and theoretically slows degeneration but carries a somewhat higher same-level recurrence risk than more aggressive annular/nucleus removal. Most surgeons perform fragment excision plus removal of loose/free intradiscal material without complete nuclear evacuation, balancing these trade-offs.
4. **Consider endoscopic discectomy** (full-endoscopic interlaminar or transforaminal/PELD) as an alternative to open/tubular microdiscectomy. Current comparative data show endoscopic approaches are non-inferior for leg pain, ODI, and JOA outcomes, with shorter length of stay (often <2 days vs standard admission), faster return to work, and comparable or lower complication/dural tear/reoperation rates in experienced hands.
5. **Account for the learning curve** when adopting endoscopic technique: outcomes in early-adopter/low-volume series lag behind high-volume centers. In low-volume settings, avoid highly migrated fragments and significant bony lateral recess stenosis as endoscopic candidates until experience is established.

## Pitfalls

- Missed fragment — especially a migrated fragment or one in Zone 3–4 — is a recognized failure mode; approach selection must match the zone and sagittal migration pattern.
- Dural tear/CSF leak occurs in roughly 2–3% of open discectomy, generally reported lower in endoscopic series though this is technique-dependent; manage with primary repair or fibrin sealant, or accept small CSF leaks with endoscopic technique given different fluid dynamics.
- Same-level recurrent disc herniation carries a lifetime risk of roughly 5–15%, higher with larger annular defects; revision discectomy is generally effective but carries a modestly higher complication profile than primary surgery.
- Epidural hematoma is rare but a leading cause of early post-operative neurological deterioration — maintain a low threshold for emergent MRI and re-exploration if a new or progressive deficit develops in the immediate post-operative period.
- Wrong-level surgery is mitigated by intraoperative localization imaging (fluoroscopy/spot film) and a formal surgical time-out with level confirmation — particularly important in obese patients and those with transitional anatomy.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2:** wound care; avoid prolonged sitting (>30 min) and bending/lifting >2–4 kg; walking encouraged from day 1; no formal brace typically required.
- **Weeks 2–6:** gradual increase in walking tolerance; initiate core-stabilization/lumbar stabilization exercises avoiding end-range flexion under load; driving typically permitted once off opioids and reaction time/comfort allow (~1–2 weeks); desk-based work return often possible by 2–4 weeks.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progressive strengthening, return to low-impact aerobic conditioning; manual/heavy labor return typically 6–12 weeks depending on job demands and surgeon assessment; return to non-contact sport generally 6–8 weeks; contact/axial-loading sport (weightlifting, contact sports) is individualized, often around 3 months.
- Endoscopic patients often track faster through early milestones (walking, sitting tolerance, return to sedentary work) given the smaller access corridor, but strengthening progression timelines converge with open microdiscectomy by 6–12 weeks.
- Routine post-op imaging is not needed if the clinical course is favorable; obtain imaging for new/worsening neurological deficit or suspected recurrent herniation/epidural hematoma.
