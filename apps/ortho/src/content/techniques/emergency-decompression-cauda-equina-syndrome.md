## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Confirmed cauda equina syndrome (CES) on emergent MRI — any of saddle/perianal anaesthesia, new bladder dysfunction (retention with overflow incontinence is the most sensitive sign; loss of urge to void is an earlier, subtler clue), faecal incontinence/loss of anal tone, bilateral leg pain/weakness, or sexual dysfunction — mandates decompression as soon as it can be safely arranged. Screen for these red flags at **every** visit for a patient with low back and/or leg pain, not only the first presentation — CES can develop in a patient previously managed conservatively for a known herniation or stenosis, and delayed recognition on a return visit is a recurring theme in medicolegal case reviews.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- There is no absolute contraindication to decompression once radiologically confirmed CES with a correctable compressive lesion is present — this is one of the few true spine emergencies, and medical optimization should run in parallel with (not instead of) getting the patient to theatre.
- Relative contraindications that modify but rarely cancel the plan: uncorrected coagulopathy or supratherapeutic anticoagulation (correct emergently rather than delay surgery indefinitely — involve haematology), active systemic sepsis from a source that should be addressed simultaneously (e.g., epidural abscess — this changes the operative plan toward debridement/culture but does not defer surgery), and unstable cardiopulmonary status requiring optimisation before any general anaesthetic (loop in anaesthesia/ICU early rather than delaying the whole pathway).
- CES caused by a non-surgical or slowly evolving process (e.g., early/mild compressive change without a clear surgical target, or a process better treated medically such as an infiltrative tumour on emergent MRI) should prompt a rapid multidisciplinary discussion (oncology/radiotherapy, infectious disease) rather than automatic surgery — but this is the exception, not the rule, and should not be used to justify delay when a mechanically decompressible lesion (extruded disc, epidural haematoma, abscess) is identified.
- Pre-existing complete, longstanding bladder/bowel dysfunction with no acute change is not an indication for emergency surgery in itself — confirm this represents a new, acute deterioration before activating the emergency pathway.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** whole-spine MRI is the study of choice and should be obtained **within 4 hours of request** once CES is suspected (UK GIRFT National CES Pathway 2023) — "whole-spine" is deliberate, since a proportion of apparent single-level CES has a second, unsuspected lesion (e.g., a synchronous more cranial stenotic segment or a rare intradural mass) that changes the operative plan. Review the MRI personally before going to theatre; do not rely solely on the written report given the time pressure.
- **Level and laterality planning:** identify the causative pathology (most commonly a large central/paracentral disc extrusion, but also spinal stenosis, epidural haematoma, epidural abscess, or tumour) and its cranio-caudal extent — a migrated fragment or a haematoma spanning more than one level changes the planned laminectomy length.
- **Labs and correction:** coagulation profile, full blood count, group and save/crossmatch as indicated; correct any anticoagulation or coagulopathy in parallel with theatre booking rather than sequentially.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** standard posterior lumbar decompression set (self-retaining retractor, Kerrison rongeurs of multiple sizes, curettes, nerve root retractors, high-speed burr), bipolar cautery, operating microscope or loupes with a good headlight, Valsalva-capable anaesthesia (controlled positive-pressure manoeuvre at closure), dural repair set (fine non-absorbable suture, fibrin sealant/dural patch) on standby even when a straightforward discectomy is anticipated, given the higher tissue friability and time pressure of these cases.
- **Theatre logistics:** this is an emergency case — engage the emergency theatre pathway immediately on MRI confirmation rather than waiting for the next elective list; document the time of symptom onset, time of imaging request, time of imaging completion, and time of skin incision, since this timeline is central to both clinical decision-making and later medicolegal review.
- **Consent:** full, meticulous informed consent documentation regarding the risk of residual bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction despite timely surgery is essential given the recognised high medicolegal profile of this diagnosis — this should be a distinct, explicit conversation, not folded into a generic discectomy consent.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anaesthesia:** general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation is standard — these cases proceed emergently and are not suitable for regional/local technique given the need for a controlled airway, potential for a longer or more extensive decompression than initially planned, and the value of a reliable Valsalva manoeuvre at closure.
- **Positioning:** prone on a radiolucent spinal frame (e.g., Wilson frame, Jackson table, or Andrews frame per surgeon/institution preference) with the abdomen hanging free to reduce epidural venous pressure and bleeding; hips flexed to reduce lumbar lordosis and open the interlaminar spaces. Pad all pressure points (chest rolls, iliac crests, knees, ankles), free the abdomen and genitalia, and protect the eyes.
- **Table setup:** arms positioned forward on arm boards ("superman" position) or tucked, below 90° of shoulder abduction to avoid brachial plexus traction; confirm fluoroscopy access (C-arm or image intensifier) will not be obstructed by the frame.
- **Draping and imaging setup:** standard sterile prep and drape of the lumbar spine; obtain a lateral fluoroscopic or spot radiograph after positioning and before incision to localise the level(s), marking the skin over the planned incision — wrong-level surgery is a recognised and avoidable pitfall even (especially) under time pressure.
- **Surface landmarks:** palpate and mark the iliac crests (approximate L4-L5 disc level), posterior superior iliac spines, and spinous processes corresponding to the planned levels before draping, then confirm radiographically once positioned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Time-out and level confirmation.** Perform a formal surgical time-out (patient identity, procedure, side/level) and confirm the operative level with fluoroscopy or a spot radiograph before incision, even under time pressure — this is not a step to abbreviate in an emergency.
2. **Exposure.** Make a midline posterior incision centred over the causative level(s) as defined on the pre-operative MRI, extending it generously enough to cover the full craniocaudal extent of the pathology (err wide rather than narrow given the diffuse, often multi-root nature of CES compression). Incise the lumbodorsal fascia and subperiosteally elevate the paraspinal (erector spinae) muscles off the spinous processes and lamina bilaterally with a Cobb elevator, then place a self-retaining retractor.
3. **Bony decompression.** Because CES compression is typically broader than a single-root radiculopathy, plan for a wide laminectomy (often bilateral, occasionally extending one level cranial and caudal to the imaged lesion if the fragment or haematoma is tracking beyond the disc space) rather than the more limited unilateral laminotomy used for routine radiculopathy. Remove the ligamentum flavum and lamina with Kerrison rongeurs and/or a high-speed burr, working from a known normal area toward the pathology to stay oriented. Extend a medial facetectomy as needed to fully visualise the lateral recess and confirm both traversing nerve roots are decompressed.
4. **Address the causative pathology.**
   - *Disc extrusion:* incise the annulus over the extrusion, remove the extruded/sequestered fragment with pituitary rongeurs, and gently probe the epidural space cranially, caudally, and across the midline for migrated fragments — under-resection is the single most common technical error in this setting.
   - *Epidural haematoma:* evacuate with irrigation and gentle suction; identify and control the bleeding source (often epidural venous plexus) with bipolar cautery, haemostatic agents, or gentle tamponade — avoid aggressive blind cautery near the dura.
   - *Epidural abscess:* debride purulent/granulation material, send tissue and fluid for microbiology (culture and Gram stain) before starting or adjusting antibiotics, and irrigate copiously; involve infectious disease early for antibiotic duration planning.
   - *Tumour:* decompress the canal and obtain diagnostic tissue; definitive oncologic management is typically staged and multidisciplinary.
5. **Confirm circumferential decompression.** After removing the compressive lesion, palpate and visually inspect the thecal sac and traversing/exiting roots across the full length of the exposure to confirm they are free and pulsatile — do not close on the assumption that the "main" fragment removal was sufficient without checking the full extent.
6. **Haemostasis.** Achieve meticulous haemostasis with bipolar cautery, haemostatic matrix/gelatin sponge, and patience before closure — a bleeding source hidden by epidural fat or bone dust is a common cause of post-operative haematoma re-exploration.
7. **Dural inspection and repair if needed.** If a durotomy occurred during exposure or fragment removal, repair primarily with fine non-absorbable suture under magnification, reinforce with fibrin sealant, and confirm a watertight closure with a Valsalva manoeuvre from anaesthesia before proceeding to closure (see Technical Tips).
8. **Layered closure.** Close the fascia in a watertight fashion (interrupted or running non-absorbable/slowly absorbable suture), followed by subcutaneous and subcuticular/skin closure per surgeon preference. Consider a subfascial drain if haemostasis was imperfect or the exposure was extensive, aware that a drain does not substitute for achieving haemostasis intraoperatively.
9. **Document the operative timeline** explicitly in the operative note (time of MRI, time of decision to operate, time of incision, time of closure) given the medicolegal significance of timing in this diagnosis.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Personally review the MRI (not just the report) before incision — confirm the level, the laterality, and whether the compressive lesion extends beyond a single level, since this changes the planned exposure.
- Plan the laminectomy wider than you think you need; CES pathology is frequently more extensive than a single-level, unilateral radiculopathy, and a too-limited exposure risks leaving a portion of the compressive lesion (or a second, unsuspected lesion) untreated.
- Work from known, normal anatomy toward the pathology when opening a canal distorted by a large extrusion or haematoma — this keeps you oriented and reduces the risk of straying laterally into the nerve root or facet.
- Keep the field dry with continuous, patient bipolar haemostasis as you go rather than trying to control diffuse epidural bleeding after the fact — epidural venous bleeding obscures the field precisely when visualisation matters most.
- After fragment/haematoma removal, actively probe cranially, caudally, and across the midline with a blunt nerve hook or small curette for missed material — a residual fragment found on early post-operative MRI after "complete" decompression is a recurring and avoidable pattern.
- Perform a Valsalva manoeuvre (coordinate with anaesthesia for a sustained positive-pressure breath-hold) at the end of any dural repair, and before the microscope/loupes leave the field, to confirm a watertight closure — do not rely on visual inspection alone.
- Have fibrin sealant and a small autologous fat or muscle graft available as an adjunct for a marginal dural repair; a buttressed repair with sealant is preferable to leaving a tenuous primary closure unreinforced.
- Engage urology (or document a clear plan for post-void residual monitoring and intermittent self-catheterisation teaching) before the patient leaves the recovery area — this should not be an afterthought arranged days later.
- Involve the patient (and family, where appropriate) in an explicit conversation about residual bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction risk before surgery, and document it — both for genuine informed consent and because this diagnosis carries a disproportionate medicolegal burden.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Delayed recognition on a return visit** — in a patient previously managed conservatively for a known herniation or stenosis — is a recurring theme in medicolegal case reviews; re-screen for red flags at every encounter, not just the first.
- **Fixed time-window mentality:** do not default to waiting for an arbitrary 24- or 48-hour cutoff — decompress as soon as safely possible once CES is confirmed. Earlier decompression trends toward better bladder/bowel/sexual recovery, even though the historical 24–48h cutoff specifically for motor/sensory recovery is not sharply supported by modern series.
- **Missed fragment or incomplete decompression:** the most common technical error is under-resection — a fragment left cranial/caudal to the main exposure, or on the contralateral side of a central extrusion, undermines the point of emergency surgery; probe widely before closing.
- **Dural tear/CSF leak:** occurs at a broadly similar rate to other lumbar decompressions (~2–3%), arguably with higher tissue friability given the underlying pathology (large extrusion, haematoma, tumour) distorting normal planes; primary repair or sealant, flat bed rest 24–48h if repaired, and watch for pseudomeningocele or post-dural-puncture-type headache.
- **Epidural haematoma (post-operative):** rare but a leading cause of early post-operative neurological deterioration in any lumbar decompression; maintain a low threshold for emergent MRI and re-exploration if a new or progressive deficit develops after surgery — do not attribute a new deficit to "expected" post-operative swelling without imaging.
- **Nerve root injury:** direct retraction/traction injury during aggressive fragment or haematoma removal near a swollen, oedematous thecal sac; work gently and avoid excessive traction on already-compromised roots.
- **Wrong-level surgery:** mitigate with intraoperative localisation imaging and a formal time-out, particularly important in obese patients, transitional lumbosacral anatomy, and under the time pressure of an emergency case — pressure to move quickly is exactly when this error occurs.
- **Persistent/permanent bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction:** a meaningful minority of patients retain some degree of permanent dysfunction despite technically successful, appropriately timed surgery — set expectations and document this explicitly before surgery rather than after.
- **Surgical site infection:** similar baseline risk to other lumbar decompressions (roughly 1–3%), though emergency cases performed outside normal theatre workflow warrant the same attention to prophylactic antibiotic timing and sterile technique as elective cases.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Bladder/bowel function recovery is often the slowest domain and may lag motor/sensory recovery by months; early urology involvement (post-void residual monitoring, intermittent self-catheterisation teaching if needed) is standard and should begin in the immediate post-operative period, not on outpatient follow-up.
- Physiotherapy follows the same general decompression timeline as non-emergent decompression (early mobilisation, progressive walking program, core stabilisation, strengthening progression), but return-to-work/return-to-sport timing is individualised entirely around neurological and sphincter recovery rather than a fixed schedule.
- Counsel from the outset that a meaningful minority of patients have permanent bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction despite timely surgery, and reflect this in rehabilitation goal-setting and the pace of follow-up (closer, more frequent review than a routine discectomy given the higher stakes and slower recovery trajectory of the autonomic domains).
