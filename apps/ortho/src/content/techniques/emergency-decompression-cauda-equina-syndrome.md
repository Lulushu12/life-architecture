## Indications

Confirmed cauda equina syndrome — any of saddle/perianal anaesthesia, new bladder dysfunction (retention with overflow incontinence is the most sensitive sign; loss of urge to void), faecal incontinence/loss of anal tone, bilateral leg pain/weakness, or sexual dysfunction — mandates emergency whole-spine MRI within 4 hours of request (UK GIRFT National CES Pathway 2023) and decompression as soon as safely possible. Screen for these red flags at **every** visit for a patient with low back and/or leg pain, not only the first presentation, since CES can develop in a patient previously managed conservatively for a known herniation or stenosis.

## Positioning & setup

Standard posterior spinal exposure, positioned and planned around the causative pathology identified on emergent MRI — most commonly a large central/paracentral disc extrusion, but also spinal stenosis, epidural hematoma/abscess, or tumor.

## Steps

1. **Screen and confirm.** Perianal sensation, digital rectal tone/exam, and a post-void residual bladder scan are mandatory in any patient with bilateral leg symptoms or reported bladder change — ask directly about bladder/bowel/sexual symptoms rather than relying on the patient to volunteer them, as embarrassment commonly suppresses spontaneous reporting.
2. **Obtain emergency whole-spine MRI within 4 hours of request** once CES is suspected (GIRFT 2023 standard).
3. **Decompress the causative pathology** (laminectomy/discectomy addressing the compressive lesion) as soon as safely and logistically possible after diagnosis is confirmed. Do not wait for a fixed 24/48-hour "window" — earlier decompression trends toward better bladder/bowel/sexual recovery, even though the historical 24–48h cutoff specifically for motor/sensory recovery is not sharply supported by modern series.
4. **Document informed consent thoroughly**, specifically addressing the risk of residual bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction, given the high medicolegal profile of this diagnosis.

## Pitfalls

- Delayed recognition on a return visit — in a patient previously managed conservatively for a known herniation or stenosis — is a recurring theme in medicolegal case reviews; re-screen for red flags at every encounter, not just the first.
- Do not default to a fixed time-window mentality; decompress as soon as safely possible once CES is confirmed rather than waiting for an arbitrary cutoff.
- A meaningful minority of patients retain permanent bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction despite timely surgery — set expectations and document this risk explicitly before surgery.

## Post-op protocol

- Bladder/bowel function recovery is often the slowest domain and may lag motor/sensory recovery by months; early urology involvement (post-void residual monitoring, intermittent self-catheterization teaching if needed) is standard.
- Physiotherapy follows the same general decompression timeline as non-emergent decompression (early mobilization, progressive walking program, core stabilization, strengthening progression), but return-to-work/return-to-sport timing is individualized entirely around neurological and sphincter recovery rather than a fixed schedule.
- Counsel from the outset that a meaningful minority of patients have permanent bladder, bowel, or sexual dysfunction despite timely surgery, and reflect this in rehabilitation goal-setting.
