## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Physiologically younger patients with reconstructable fracture patterns (2-/3-part, and 4-part with intact medial hinge/calcar per Hertel criteria).
- Displacement >1 cm or angulation >45° at any of the four segments, head-split fractures amenable to reconstruction, articular displacement >25–40%, glenohumeral dislocation, vascular injury, or open fracture per the general surgical indication set for proximal humerus fractures.
- Isolated displaced greater tuberosity fractures (>5 mm in overhead athletes/younger patients, >1 cm generally) at risk of subacromial impingement if left displaced.

**Contraindications**:
- Elderly, low-demand patients with displaced fractures where nonoperative management or arthroplasty (for unreconstructable patterns) is preferred — the PROFHER trial found no clinically important functional difference between ORIF and nonoperative treatment at 2 and 5 years in this population.
- Head-splitting fractures — poor candidate for ORIF; favor arthroplasty.
- 4-part fractures with disrupted medial hinge/calcar (Hertel-negative predictors) and severe osteoporosis, where screw cut-out and AVN risk are high and arthroplasty is often more reliable.
- Active infection, or a soft-tissue envelope unable to tolerate a deltopectoral exposure.
- Non-reconstructable comminution of the tuberosities in an older patient — arthroplasty avoids relying on tuberosity healing for a functional result.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: true AP (Grashey), scapular-Y, and axillary lateral views (mandatory — do not accept a trauma series without an adequate axillary view); CT with 2D/3D reconstruction routinely for displaced 3-/4-part patterns to assess Hertel medial hinge/calcar length (>8 mm favorable), head-split extent, and tuberosity fragment size/comminution.
- **Implant templating**: select a proximal humerus locking plate system with an adequate range of proximal locking screw trajectories (including dedicated inferomedial "calcar screw" holes) and confirm plate length/contour against the injury films; have both standard and long-plate options available in case the fracture line extends further down the shaft than initially appreciated.
- **Suture planning**: plan for heavy non-absorbable suture (typically 2–3 sutures per tuberosity) for tendon-bone cerclage augmentation of the greater and lesser tuberosities — order/confirm availability before the case.
- **AVN risk stratification**: use Hertel's criteria (medial hinge displacement, metaphyseal head extension length, anatomic-neck fracture line) preoperatively to counsel the patient on AVN risk and to decide whether ORIF is realistic versus arthroplasty is a better primary choice.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy capable of true AP and axillary views plus dynamic rotation views intraoperatively; a full set of provisional K-wires/reduction pins for tuberosity and head fragment control.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, frequently combined with an interscalene block for postoperative analgesia and to reduce intraoperative muscle tone during reduction.
- **Position**: modified beach-chair position, torso elevated 30–60°, head secured in a headrest, operative shoulder at the edge of the table to allow full arm excursion for reduction maneuvers and axillary fluoroscopic views.
- **Table setup**: radiolucent table or table extension permitting true AP and axillary-equivalent fluoroscopy without repositioning the patient; an assistant or mechanical arm holder to support the limb during reduction.
- **Draping**: prep and drape widely from the base of the neck to mid-humerus, including enough of the anterior chest to identify the deltopectoral interval and enough distally for plate length and, if needed, extension of the approach.
- **Landmarks**: mark the coracoid process, clavicle, acromion, and the deltopectoral groove (a faint fat stripe often visible/palpable) before incision; mark the planned incision from just below the coracoid extending distally along the deltopectoral interval.
- **Fluoroscopy sequencing**: obtain baseline true AP and axillary images after positioning to confirm the fracture pattern matches preoperative planning before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Approach.** Make a deltopectoral incision from just inferior to the coracoid extending distally along the interval between deltoid and pectoralis major, identified by the cephalic vein running in the groove — retract the vein either medially (with the pectoralis) or laterally (with the deltoid), consistently, to avoid repeated crossing injury. An anterolateral deltoid-splitting approach is an alternative for more posterolateral tuberosity fragments, but respect the axillary nerve's excursion — do not extend the split more than 5 cm below the acromion.
2. **Deep exposure.** Develop the interval, retract the deltoid laterally and the conjoint tendon/pectoralis medially, and identify the fracture hematoma and rotator interval; ligate or cauterize the ascending branch of the anterior circumflex humeral vessels as encountered, taking care given the "arcuate artery" vascular contribution classically described here (contemporary vascular studies show the posterior circumflex humeral artery is the dominant supply to the head via the posteromedial calcar, reinforcing why preserving the medial hinge matters more than protecting this anterior vessel alone).
3. **Tag the tuberosities.** Place heavy non-absorbable traction sutures through the rotator cuff tendon-bone junction of the greater tuberosity (supraspinatus/infraspinatus) and lesser tuberosity (subscapularis) — not through tuberosity bone alone, which is often osteoporotic and comminuted and will not hold a suture reliably. These sutures serve as the primary reduction and fixation tool for the tuberosity fragments throughout the case.
4. **Reduce the head fragment.** Using the tagged sutures for the tuberosities and a bone hook, K-wire joystick, or elevator for the head fragment, reduce the head onto the shaft, restoring head height and version — use the bicipital groove as a rotational landmark (it should sit roughly 5–10 mm posterior to the plate's leading edge once the plate is applied) and restore the normal ~130–140° neck-shaft angle.
5. **Provisional fixation.** Secure the reduction provisionally with K-wires (taking care not to place wires in a trajectory that will conflict with planned screw holes) before applying the plate.
6. **Apply the plate.** Position the plate 5–8 mm below the top of the greater tuberosity and posterior to the bicipital groove — placing it too proximal causes subacromial impingement, and too anterior risks biceps tendon irritation. Provisionally pin the plate to the shaft.
7. **Place the calcar screws.** Direct inferomedial "calcar screws" from the plate into the inferomedial head fragment, aiming toward the calcar/medial cortex just above the medial hinge — this is the single most consistent predictor against varus collapse and fixation failure in the literature, and should be prioritized even if fewer total proximal screws are used elsewhere.
8. **Complete proximal screw fixation.** Place the remaining proximal locking screws in a divergent, multiplanar pattern to maximize head fragment purchase, confirming on fluoroscopy that no screw is within a few millimeters of the subchondral bone (to avoid intra-articular penetration as the head can settle slightly after fixation).
9. **Fix the shaft.** Place bicortical (or locking, per system) screws distally in the shaft, achieving a stable plate-to-bone construct along the full length of the plate.
10. **Augment tuberosity fixation.** Tension and tie the previously placed tuberosity sutures through or around the plate/screws, restoring the tuberosities anatomically against the head and shaft — this suture cerclage augmentation is critical in osteoporotic bone where bone-to-bone screw fixation of the tuberosities alone is unreliable.
11. **Dynamic fluoroscopic check.** Obtain intraoperative fluoroscopy with dynamic internal/external rotation views (not just static AP/axillary) to confirm no screw penetrates the articular surface — static views alone reliably under-detect penetration, especially posteriorly.
12. **Assess rotator cuff and stability.** Passively range the shoulder through internal/external rotation and forward flexion to confirm stable fixation without impingement or hardware prominence.
13. **Irrigate and close.** Irrigate the wound, achieve hemostasis, and close the deltopectoral interval, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers; consider a drain if hemostasis is imperfect given the vascular nature of this region.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Tag both tuberosities with heavy suture through the tendon-bone junction before attempting head reduction — these sutures become the primary joystick for reduction as well as the backbone of the final fixation construct, especially in osteoporotic, comminuted bone that will not hold screws in the tuberosities reliably on its own.
- Use the bicipital groove as a consistent intraoperative landmark for both rotational alignment and plate position — it should end up roughly 5–10 mm posterior to the plate's lateral edge once properly positioned.
- Prioritize calcar screw placement even under time pressure — it is the single most consistent predictor against varus collapse in the literature, more so than the total number of proximal screws placed.
- Provisional K-wire fixation should avoid trajectories that will later conflict with planned locking screw holes — plan the wire positions with the final screw map in mind, not just for immediate provisional stability.
- Always obtain dynamic rotation fluoroscopy (internal and external rotation) before leaving the operating room — static AP and axillary views alone under-detect screw penetration, particularly of posterior calcar/head screws.
- In markedly osteoporotic bone, consider augmenting screw purchase with cement, a fibular strut allograft in the medullary canal, or additional divergent screw trajectories rather than relying on locking technology alone to compensate for poor bone density.
- Restore the normal neck-shaft angle (~130–140°) before final plate fixation — a plate applied to a fracture reduced in varus will lock the deformity in rather than correct it.
- If, on final inspection, the tuberosities cannot be reduced to within a few millimeters of anatomic position around the head, reconsider whether arthroplasty (converting intraoperatively) is a better option than accepting a suboptimal ORIF reduction, particularly in a lower-demand patient.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Varus malreduction and screw cut-out/penetration in osteoporotic bone are the dominant ORIF-specific failure modes — the calcar screw is the single most preventable technical safeguard against this.
- AVN risk is higher with anatomic-neck involvement, a disrupted medial hinge, or head-splitting patterns — these may not manifest until months to years later, so surveil at follow-up beyond the initial healing window in high-risk patterns.
- Static AP/axillary fluoroscopy alone under-detects screw penetration — always obtain dynamic rotation views intraoperatively.
- Rotator cuff dysfunction and tuberosity malunion/nonunion are major drivers of poor functional outcome independent of whether the fracture itself unites.
- Plate placed too proximal (above the recommended 5–8 mm below the top of the greater tuberosity) causes subacromial impingement; placed too anterior risks irritating the biceps tendon in the bicipital groove.
- Suture fixation of tuberosities through bone alone (rather than the tendon-bone junction) frequently fails in osteoporotic, comminuted tuberosity fragments — tendon-based cerclage is more reliable.
- Axillary nerve injury is possible with either the deltopectoral (retraction-related) or anterolateral deltoid-splitting (direct injury if the split extends beyond ~5 cm below the acromion) approach.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Passive ROM often started day 1–2 (surgeon-dependent, informed by fixation stability and tuberosity repair integrity).
- Active-assisted ROM from 2–4 wk.
- Resisted strengthening deferred to 8–12 wk to protect tuberosity healing.
- Full unrestricted activity/sport typically at 3–6 months once strength and ROM are near-symmetric and there is no residual instability.
- The PROFHER trial found no clinically important difference in Oxford Shoulder Score between locked-plate ORIF and nonoperative treatment of displaced fractures in older adults (mean age ~66) at 2 and 5 years — reinforces individualized selection, particularly in lower-demand elderly patients.
