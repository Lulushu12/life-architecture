## Indications

Carpal tunnel release is indicated for carpal tunnel syndrome that has failed or is unsuited to non-operative management (night splinting, activity modification, corticosteroid injection). Electrodiagnostic studies (NCS/EMG) remain the reference standard for confirming diagnosis and grading severity, and are recommended before surgery in atypical presentations or when the diagnosis is uncertain. Corticosteroid injection response is a useful prognostic indicator of likely benefit from surgery; a 2025 meta-analysis of RCTs confirms surgical release produces superior and more durable symptom and function outcomes than injection at intermediate/long-term follow-up, positioning injection as a bridge/diagnostic step rather than a long-term strategy in moderate-severe disease.

## Positioning & setup

WALANT (lidocaine + epinephrine, no tourniquet) is now widely adopted for both open and endoscopic CTR, avoiding tourniquet pain/sedation and allowing intra-operative active motion confirmation before closure.

## Steps

1. **Open carpal tunnel release (OCTR):** longitudinal incision releasing the transverse carpal ligament under direct vision. This remains the workhorse technique, with cure/major-improvement rates >90%.
2. **Endoscopic release** (single- or dual-portal): offers faster return to work and less pin-tenderness (grip strength and 2-point discrimination recover a few weeks faster on average) with equivalent long-term outcomes and symptom relief to open release in most meta-analyses.
3. **WALANT confirmation:** with lidocaine + epinephrine and no tourniquet, ask the patient to actively flex/extend the fingers and wrist intra-operatively to confirm complete release and tendon gliding before skin closure.
4. **Minimally invasive alternatives:** ultrasound-guided/percutaneous release techniques (mini-open, thread/knife-based) are increasingly reported with good early results, though with less long-term comparative data than open/endoscopic release.

## Pitfalls

- Endoscopic release carries a slightly higher rate of incomplete release/reoperation than open release, along with a learning curve and transient nerve injury risk (recurrent motor branch, ulnar neurovascular bundle) reported in early-experience series.
- Incomplete release is more common with limited/endoscopic exposure or missed accessory ligaments — confirm full division of the transverse carpal ligament before closing.
- Watch for injury to the recurrent motor branch and palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve; either causes a painful neuroma or numbness if injured.
- Pillar pain is a common but usually self-limited complication (resolving over months) and should be counseled pre-operatively rather than mistaken for a technical failure.
- Recurrent CTS can result from scar tethering or incomplete release; infection and complex regional pain syndrome are uncommon/rare complications.

## Post-op protocol

- Soft dressing/removable splint for comfort 3-7 days; splinting is not mandatory beyond this for either open or endoscopic release.
- Early active finger/wrist motion is encouraged from day 1-3 to limit stiffness/adhesion; scar massage begins around 2 weeks once the incision is sealed.
- Light activities of daily living are permitted immediately; return to light desk work is often possible within days (WALANT particularly facilitates this).
- Heavier manual labor/lifting is typically restricted 4-6 weeks.
- Grip strength recovery continues over 3 months, with full recovery of strength and resolution of pillar pain up to 3-6 months.
