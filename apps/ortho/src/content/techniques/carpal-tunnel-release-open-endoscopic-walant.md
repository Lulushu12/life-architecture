## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Carpal tunnel release is indicated for carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) that has failed or is unsuited to non-operative management (night splinting, activity modification, corticosteroid injection). Electrodiagnostic studies (NCS/EMG) remain the reference standard for confirming diagnosis and grading severity, and are recommended before surgery in atypical presentations or when the diagnosis is uncertain. Corticosteroid injection response is a useful prognostic indicator of likely benefit from surgery; a 2025 meta-analysis of RCTs confirms surgical release produces superior and more durable symptom and function outcomes than injection at intermediate/long-term follow-up, positioning injection as a bridge/diagnostic step rather than a long-term strategy in moderate-severe disease. Thenar atrophy, constant (non-intermittent) numbness, and severe electrodiagnostic findings (absent sensory response, severe motor latency prolongation) all favor prompt surgery over further non-operative trials.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Active local infection or cellulitis overlying the planned incision.
- Uncertain diagnosis without a discriminating exam and, in atypical cases, without electrodiagnostic confirmation — proceeding on clinical suspicion alone risks operating on a cervical radiculopathy or double-crush pattern that will not resolve with CTR.
- Endoscopic release specifically is relatively contraindicated in: revision surgery, prior wrist trauma/fracture distorting anatomy, space-occupying lesions in the canal (ganglion, lipoma, flexor tenosynovitis with proliferative synovium), rheumatoid disease with tenosynovial hypertrophy, and an inability to hyperextend the wrist on the traction tower — any of these should push toward an open approach with direct visualization.
- Severe, fixed thenar atrophy with long-standing constant numbness does not contraindicate surgery but should be accompanied by counseling that motor/sensory recovery may be incomplete regardless of technique.
- Uncorrected coagulopathy or anticoagulation that cannot be safely bridged (relative — most CTR can be done without interrupting standard antiplatelet therapy given the epinephrine-assisted hemostasis of WALANT).

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm diagnosis with focused history/exam (Phalen's, Durkan compression test, Tinel's) and, where indicated, NCS/EMG severity grading; correlate laterality and severity with symptoms before consenting for surgery.
- Decide open vs endoscopic vs mini-open technique based on: patient anatomy (wrist crease mobility, ability to hyperextend for endoscopic tower), surgeon experience/case volume with endoscopy, presence of a space-occupying lesion (favors open), and patient priorities (endoscopic gives a faster return to pillar-pain-free grip in most series).
- Plan anesthesia: WALANT (local lidocaine + epinephrine, no tourniquet, no sedation) is now the default for most primary CTR in appropriately selected, cooperative patients; confirm no contraindication to epinephrine-containing local (severe peripheral vascular disease, digit-specific concerns not applicable to palm/wrist) and review the patient's ability to tolerate an awake procedure.
- Equipment: basic hand set, headlight or loupes for open release, dedicated endoscopic CTR tower/cannula system (Chow 2-portal or Agee/MicroAire 1-portal, per surgeon's system) if performing endoscopic release, fluoroscopy is not routinely needed.
- Review prior wrist surgery/imaging if revision case — anticipate scar and possible incomplete prior release, and plan for open exposure in virtually all revision cases.
- Discuss with the patient in advance that pillar pain and slow grip-strength recovery over months are expected, not failures, to set realistic expectations.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** WALANT (1% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine, buffered with sodium bicarbonate to reduce injection burning, ~10-15 mL infiltrated along the planned incision and into the deep palmar/carpal tunnel plane) is standard for both open and endoscopic release in cooperative adults; wait at least 20-30 minutes after injection for full epinephrine-mediated hemostasis before incising. Monitored sedation or axillary/Bier block is reserved for anxious patients, revision cases, or surgeon preference; general anesthesia is rarely needed.
- **Positioning:** Supine on the operating table with the arm extended on a hand table, palm up. No tourniquet is used with WALANT; if a tourniquet-based technique is chosen instead, prep and inflate after Esmarch exsanguination to standard forearm pressure.
- **Endoscopic-specific setup:** the wrist must be able to hyperextend on the endoscopic traction/hand-holder device to open the volar approach to the ligament — test this passively before draping; inability to comfortably hyperextend the wrist is a reason to convert the plan to open release.
- **Draping:** standard sterile hand draping exposing the palm, wrist, and distal forearm; keep the antecubital fossa accessible if conversion to regional block is anticipated.
- **Landmarks marked before incision:** Kaplan's cardinal line (from the apex of the first web space thumb abduction crease, parallel to the proximal palmar crease) approximates the deep motor branch takeoff; the radial border of the ring finger in line proximally marks a safe zone avoiding the superficial palmar arch and recurrent motor branch; the distal wrist crease and the "safe zone" between the thenar crease (radially) and the hook of hamate axis (ulnarly) are marked for the open incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**A. Open carpal tunnel release (OCTR)**

1. Mark a longitudinal incision in line with the radial border of the ring finger, beginning just distal to Kaplan's cardinal line/distal wrist crease and extending proximally roughly 2-3 cm into the palm, staying ulnar to the thenar crease to avoid the recurrent motor branch and radial to the hook of hamate to avoid the ulnar neurovascular bundle.
2. Incise skin and subcutaneous fat; identify and preserve the palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve (runs radial/superficial in the proximal wound) and palmar fascia.
3. Incise the palmar fascia in line with the skin incision, then identify the distal edge of the transverse carpal ligament (TCL).
4. Under direct vision, incise the TCL longitudinally along its ulnar half (protects the recurrent motor branch, which usually arises from the radial-volar aspect of the nerve) from distal to proximal, using a blunt-tipped instrument or narrow retractor beneath the ligament to protect the median nerve as you cut.
5. Extend the release proximally across the entire ligament and the distal antebrachial (volar) forearm fascia until the nerve is visibly free and a right-angle or blunt probe passes easily proximal and distal to the confirmed release plane.
6. Retract the median nerve gently and inspect the canal for a space-occupying lesion, bifid median nerve, persistent median artery, or synovitis; address incidental findings (e.g., partial synovectomy) as indicated.
7. With WALANT, ask the patient to actively flex and extend the fingers and wrist to confirm free tendon and nerve gliding before closure.
8. Achieve hemostasis (epinephrine typically suffices); close skin only (the TCL and fascia are left open — do not attempt to repair the ligament) with simple interrupted nylon or a running subcuticular suture, and apply a soft dressing.

**B. Endoscopic carpal tunnel release (ECTR)**

1. Position the wrist in hyperextension on the endoscopic traction tower; make a small transverse or oblique incision just proximal to the distal wrist crease, ulnar to the palmaris longus tendon (or at the standard portal site for the chosen system — 1-portal Agee/MicroAire vs 2-portal Chow).
2. Bluntly dissect down to and incise the antebrachial fascia, then develop a plane on the volar surface of the TCL, sweeping the contents of the canal dorsally away from the ligament with a blunt probe/dilator sequence (progressively larger dilators/obturators) to create a clear working channel.
3. Insert the cannula/slotted device with the endoscope; visualize the undersurface of the TCL and confirm the characteristic transverse fibers before introducing the retractable blade.
4. Under direct endoscopic visualization, divide the TCL from distal to proximal (or proximal to distal depending on system) in a controlled fashion, watching continuously for the median nerve and its branches to stay clear of the blade path; for 2-portal (Chow) technique, a second distal portal in the palm is used to pass the device and confirm/complete the cut under bidirectional visualization.
5. Confirm complete transection by visualizing fat prolapsing through the divided ligament and by palpating loss of the distinct ligament edge; if any doubt exists about completeness of release or if bleeding obscures the view, convert to open release without hesitation.
6. Remove the endoscope/cannula, confirm hemostasis, and close the small portal incision(s) with a single suture or adhesive strips.
7. Under WALANT, have the patient actively move the fingers/wrist to confirm gliding before dressing.

**C. Minimally invasive / mini-open and ultrasound-guided percutaneous techniques** are increasingly reported (thread- or knife-based release under ultrasound guidance, mini-open through a 1-1.5 cm incision) with good early results but less long-term comparative data than open/endoscopic release; reserve for surgeons with specific training/experience in these techniques rather than adopting ad hoc.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Inject WALANT local anesthetic at least 20-30 minutes before incision and consider injecting at the start of the OR day/in the pre-op holding area to maximize the epinephrine hemostatic window by the time you incise.
- Buffer lidocaine-epinephrine with sodium bicarbonate (roughly 1 part bicarb to 9-10 parts lidocaine) to markedly reduce injection discomfort — this improves patient tolerance of the awake technique.
- For open release, keep the incision ulnar to the thenar crease and radial to a line drawn from the ring-finger axis — this "safe zone" avoids both the recurrent motor branch and the ulnar neurovascular bundle in the vast majority of patients.
- Use a blunt nerve/ligament protector (e.g., a Freer or dedicated carpal tunnel knife guide) under the TCL before every cut, whether open or endoscopic, so the blade always cuts against a protected surface.
- Always release the distal forearm fascia proximally and confirm the ligament is divided all the way to its distal margin at the level of the superficial palmar arch — incomplete proximal or distal release is a common cause of persistent symptoms.
- In endoscopic cases, if visualization is ever compromised by bleeding, synovium, or an unclear anatomic plane, convert to open rather than persisting blindly — this is a judgment call, not a complication.
- Actively test finger and wrist motion intraoperatively under WALANT before closing; this both confirms complete release and lets you demonstrate to the patient in real time that their tingling has resolved, which is a powerful reassurance tool.
- Inspect for a bifid median nerve or persistent median artery before working near the nerve — these anatomic variants are not rare and change the risk profile of aggressive dissection.
- For revision or endoscopic-appropriate-but-anatomically-borderline cases, have a low threshold to start (or convert to) open technique; a partially completed endoscopic release converted to open still benefits from having already developed the working plane.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Endoscopic release carries a slightly higher rate of incomplete release/reoperation than open release, along with a learning curve and transient nerve injury risk (recurrent motor branch, ulnar neurovascular bundle) reported in early-experience series.
- Incomplete release is more common with limited/endoscopic exposure or missed accessory ligaments — confirm full division of the transverse carpal ligament, including its distal margin and the distal antebrachial fascia proximally, before closing.
- Watch for injury to the recurrent motor branch and palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve; either causes a painful neuroma or numbness if injured. Anatomic variants of the recurrent motor branch (subligamentous, transligamentous, or ulnar-to-radial course) make this the most feared avoidable injury in CTR.
- Pillar pain is a common but usually self-limited complication (resolving over months) and should be counseled pre-operatively rather than mistaken for a technical failure.
- Recurrent CTS can result from scar tethering or incomplete release; infection and complex regional pain syndrome are uncommon/rare complications.
- Endoscopic technique is relatively contraindicated (or should be converted to open) if the wrist cannot be hyperextended on the tower, if proliferative tenosynovitis is suspected, or if there is prior surgery/trauma distorting the canal anatomy — proceeding endoscopically in these settings raises nerve injury risk.
- Bleeding that obscures the endoscopic field should prompt conversion to open rather than continued blind cutting.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Soft dressing/removable splint for comfort 3-7 days; splinting is not mandatory beyond this for either open or endoscopic release.
- Early active finger/wrist motion is encouraged from day 1-3 to limit stiffness/adhesion; scar massage begins around 2 weeks once the incision is sealed.
- Light activities of daily living are permitted immediately; return to light desk work is often possible within days (WALANT particularly facilitates this).
- Heavier manual labor/lifting is typically restricted 4-6 weeks.
- Grip strength recovery continues over 3 months, with full recovery of strength and resolution of pillar pain up to 3-6 months.
