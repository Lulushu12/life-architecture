## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Posterior ankle impingement (bony — os trigonum, prominent Stieda process of the lateral talar tubercle — or soft-tissue — posterior capsule/synovium, FHL tenosynovitis) that has failed activity modification, NSAIDs, PT (FHL flexibility, posterior capsule mobility), and/or ultrasound-guided FHL sheath or posterior joint injection.
- A diagnostic US-guided local anesthetic injection into the posterior recess is both diagnostic and prognostic for scope success and should be used to help confirm the indication before committing to surgery, particularly in atypical presentations.
- Concurrent FHL tenosynovitis requiring release/debridement in the same setting.
- Selected posterior talar process fracture fragments (small, displaced) amenable to endoscopic excision or fixation.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- An acute-on-chronic presentation with a palpable Achilles gap — rule out an acute Achilles rupture through chronic tendinopathy before committing to elective posterior hindfoot work, since the clinical pictures can overlap and missing an acute rupture has significant consequences.
- Large fracture fragments, revision cases with extensive scarring, or concurrent procedures not amenable to endoscopic access — these are better served by an open posteromedial or posterolateral approach.
- Active infection or significant local soft-tissue compromise over the planned portal sites.
- Inadequate non-operative trial (<3 months) without a diagnostic injection confirming the posterior recess as the pain generator — proceeding without this confirmation raises the risk of operating on the wrong pain source.
- Surgeon inexperience with the technique in complex/revision cases — the neurovascular corridor is narrow and technically demanding; a lower threshold for open conversion or referral is appropriate early in a surgeon's experience with the procedure.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: weightbearing lateral radiograph to confirm the presence and size of an os trigonum or prominent Stieda process; MRI to characterize soft-tissue impingement, FHL tenosynovitis, and to exclude alternative diagnoses (Achilles partial tear, osteochondral lesion, occult fracture); CT selectively for detailed bony anatomy in revision or fracture-fragment cases.
- **Diagnostic confirmation**: review the response to a prior US-guided posterior recess or FHL sheath injection, if performed — a strong, if temporary, response supports the indication and predicts a favorable surgical outcome.
- **Equipment**: standard 2.7 mm (or 4.0 mm, per surgeon preference) 30° arthroscope, full radius shaver and small joint burr for bone resection, arthroscopic graspers/curettes, and a set of blunt trocars/cannulas for safe portal establishment; have an open instrument set available in case of conversion to an open posteromedial/posterolateral approach.
- **Case-specific planning**: confirm preoperatively whether FHL release/debridement is anticipated based on MRI findings, and plan the case duration accordingly; in athletes, discuss realistic return-to-sport timelines tied to the specific demands of their sport before surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic nerve block combined with general anesthesia or sedation is standard; the block provides substantial early postoperative analgesia and facilitates rapid same-day discharge, which is typical for this procedure.
- **Positioning**: **prone position** with a bolster under the shin/ankle so the foot hangs free off the end of the table, allowing direct posterior access for both portals and unobstructed instrument trajectory — this is the defining positioning feature of the van Dijk two-portal technique and distinguishes it from anterior ankle arthroscopy.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet is optional and used per surgeon preference — many surgeons perform this procedure without a tourniquet or with it available but not routinely inflated, since fluid distension and gravity-assisted drainage in the prone position often provide adequate visualization; have it ready regardless.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned for lateral views to confirm adequate bony resection (os trigonum/Stieda process) before completing the case, and to assist with fragment localization in fracture cases.
- **Landmarks**: with the ankle in a neutral to slightly plantarflexed position, mark the posteromedial and posterolateral portals **just anterior to the respective borders of the Achilles tendon, along a line at the level of the tip of the fibula (intermalleolar line)**; mark the course of the sural nerve laterally and the neurovascular bundle (posterior tibial artery/nerve, FHL) medially; some surgeons also mark the lateral border of the third metatarsal on the plantar foot to help orient instrument trajectory away from the medial neurovascular bundle.
- **Draping**: free-drape the leg with the foot accessible from the end of the table; ensure the drapes do not obstruct fluoroscopic imaging from the lateral projection.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Position the patient prone with the foot free off the end of the table; mark portal sites and the sural nerve/neurovascular bundle courses as above.
2. Make the **posterolateral portal** first, just anterior to the lateral border of the Achilles tendon at the intermalleolar line; use a blunt trocar directed toward the interdigital web space between the first and second toes (aiming roughly toward the front of the ankle) to avoid the sural nerve and short saphenous vein, which run close to this portal.
3. Introduce the arthroscope through the posterolateral portal and establish the **posteromedial portal** under direct visualization (inside-out or outside-in technique, per surgeon preference), just anterior to the medial border of the Achilles tendon at the same level — use blunt subcutaneous dissection with a mosquito clamp before introducing the working cannula/trocar to protect the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle and FHL, which lie just medial to this portal.
4. Perform a systematic diagnostic survey of the posterior ankle and subtalar recesses, identifying the os trigonum or prominent Stieda process, the posterior capsule/synovium, and the FHL tendon in its groove between the medial and lateral talar tubercles (the FHL serves as a critical medial-boundary landmark protecting the neurovascular bundle throughout the case).
5. Debride hypertrophic posterior capsule/synovium with a shaver to improve visualization and directly address soft-tissue impingement.
6. Excise the os trigonum or resect the prominent Stieda process using a combination of curettes/graspers (to free soft-tissue attachments) and a small burr or osteotome, working from lateral to medial while keeping instruments oriented away from the FHL and posterior neurovascular bundle.
7. If concurrent FHL tenosynovitis is present, open the FHL sheath (retinaculum) longitudinally with a shaver or hook knife just medial to the tendon, releasing constriction and debriding synovitis, taking care to stay directly on the tendon sheath to avoid the adjacent neurovascular bundle.
8. Confirm adequate bony resection on fluoroscopic lateral view and by direct arthroscopic assessment of plantarflexion range of motion (impingement should no longer be reproducible with forced plantarflexion under direct visualization).
9. For large fracture fragments, revision cases, or concurrent procedures not amenable to endoscopy, convert to or primarily perform an open posteromedial or posterolateral approach instead.
10. Close the small portal incisions with simple sutures or adhesive strips; apply a soft compressive dressing.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Establish the posterolateral portal first and use it to visualize posteromedial portal placement under direct vision — this "inside-out" sequencing meaningfully reduces the risk of neurovascular injury compared with blindly establishing both portals from the outside.
- Use the FHL tendon as your critical intraoperative landmark throughout the case — everything medial to it is neurovascular structure, and staying lateral to or directly on the FHL is the single most reliable way to keep instruments safely oriented.
- Use blunt, not sharp, dissection once through the skin and subcutaneous layer for both portals — a mosquito clamp spread technique minimizes the risk of direct nerve laceration compared with continued sharp dissection.
- Keep the ankle in neutral to slight plantarflexion during portal establishment — this relaxes the posterior neurovascular structures slightly and widens the working corridor.
- Direct the initial posterolateral trocar toward the first web space (not straight anterior) to stay clear of the sural nerve and short saphenous vein, which are more posterior/superficial than a naive straight-in trajectory would suggest.
- Confirm complete resection of the Stieda process or os trigonum with a final fluoroscopic lateral view before closing — residual bony impingement is a common, avoidable cause of persistent symptoms and is easy to underestimate arthroscopically without a radiographic check.
- Have a low threshold to convert to a limited open approach if visualization is inadequate or the fragment is larger than anticipated on preoperative imaging — this is a safety valve, not a failure, particularly for surgeons early in their experience with the technique.
- When releasing the FHL sheath, incise directly on the tendon's dorsal or medial sheath surface under clear visualization rather than working blindly toward presumed anatomy — the neurovascular bundle's proximity leaves little margin for a misdirected release.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Sural nerve or posterior tibial neurovascular bundle/FHL injury is a recognized risk given the technically demanding, narrow working corridor of prone two-portal posterior endoscopy — careful portal marking, blunt dissection, and using the FHL as a landmark are the primary mitigants.
- Recurrent impingement can result from incomplete resection of the os trigonum/Stieda process or incomplete debridement of hypertrophic posterior soft tissue — confirm adequacy on final fluoroscopic and arthroscopic assessment before closing rather than assuming resection is complete.
- An acute-on-chronic presentation with a palpable Achilles gap should prompt ruling out an acute Achilles rupture through chronic tendinopathy before committing to elective posterior hindfoot work — the two conditions can coexist and a missed rupture has major functional consequences if unaddressed.
- Portal placement error (too posterior, too far from the intermalleolar line) increases neurovascular risk on both the medial and lateral sides — adhere closely to established surface landmarks rather than estimating freehand.
- Inadequate FHL sheath release, or an overly aggressive release extending too far proximally/distally, can leave residual tenosynovitis symptoms or risk injury to the neurovascular bundle at the margins of the working field.

## Postoperative Protocol

Weightbearing as tolerated in a supportive boot or shoe within days; early ROM; return to sport typically 4–8 weeks depending on sport demands and whether concurrent FHL or ligament work was performed. Contemporary reviews confirm two-portal posterior hindfoot endoscopy as the standard of care, with high satisfaction and rapid return to sport in athletes.
