## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Tenosynovitis/partial longitudinal tear (<50% cross-section) refractory to non-operative management (immobilization/bracing, PT, NSAIDs).
- Extensive tear (>50% cross-section) or an irreparable tendon.
- Complete non-functional tendon (chronic rupture with retraction/degeneration beyond primary repair).
- Peroneal subluxation/dislocation from superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) incompetence, symptomatic and refractory to bracing.
- Concomitant hindfoot varus or chronic lateral ankle instability contributing to recurrent peroneal pathology — plan to address at the same setting.

**Contraindications**
- Active local infection or skin compromise about the lateral hindfoot.
- Asymptomatic incidental tendinosis/split tearing found on imaging without correlating clinical symptoms — imaging findings alone are not an operative indication.
- Inadequately trialed non-operative management for tenosynovitis/subluxation without frank instability — complete a genuine trial of bracing, activity modification, and NSAIDs first.
- Severe peripheral neuropathy or vascular insufficiency in the operative limb that would compromise wound healing and postoperative protected weight-bearing compliance — optimize or reconsider surgical candidacy.
- Uncorrected, unaddressed hindfoot varus or chronic ankle instability when planning isolated tendon surgery — proceeding without a plan to correct the underlying driver predisposes to recurrence regardless of how well the tendon itself is repaired.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI is the imaging standard for characterizing tear location, length, and percentage of cross-sectional involvement, and for assessing the SPR and fibular groove morphology (flat or convex grooves predispose to subluxation). Dynamic ultrasound can catch dynamic subluxation missed on static MRI and is a useful adjunct when the diagnosis is equivocal. Weight-bearing hindfoot alignment radiographs (or weight-bearing CT) assess for varus malalignment that must be addressed concurrently.
- **Procedure selection to template in advance:** based on percentage of cross-sectional tear, decide among debridement + tubularization (<50%), tenodesis to the adjacent intact tendon (>50% or one tendon irreparable), or FHL/FDL tendon transfer (complete non-functional tendon) — confirm this before the day of surgery so the appropriate transfer set is available if needed, even though intraoperative tendon quality can still shift the final decision.
- **Groove-deepening decision:** assess fibular groove morphology on axial MRI pre-operatively — a flat or convex (rather than concave) groove predisposes to subluxation and supports planning for groove deepening in addition to SPR repair; a normal concave groove with isolated retinacular incompetence may be adequately treated with SPR repair/reconstruction alone.
- **Equipment:** tenotomes and whipstitch suture for tubularization/tenodesis/transfer; SPR repair typically uses suture anchors (2-4, small caliber, 2.4-3.5 mm) along the posterior fibular ridge; groove-deepening instrumentation (curved osteotome or thin sagittal saw, and/or a cannulated reaming system) if bony deepening is planned; have a calcaneal osteotomy set available if concomitant hindfoot varus correction is planned.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** popliteal sciatic block (± saphenous block) with sedation, or general anesthesia; regional block provides valuable post-operative analgesia.
- **Positioning:** lateral decubitus with the operative leg up (bolstered) gives the most direct posterior-to-anterior access to the retromalleolar groove and tendons, though supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip is also used, particularly when a concomitant medial-sided or calcaneal osteotomy procedure is planned in the same setting.
- **Tourniquet:** thigh or calf tourniquet, exsanguinated and inflated; a bloodless field is important for distinguishing tendon substance from surrounding synovium/scar and for precise SPR and groove work.
- **Landmarks to mark:** the posterior border of the fibula, the palpable/visible course of the peroneal tendons (have the patient actively evert pre-operatively if awake/regional block to mark subluxation dynamically if relevant), and the course of the sural nerve, which runs close to the peroneal tendon sheath posteriorly and is at risk throughout this exposure.
- **Imaging:** fluoroscopy is useful when a concomitant calcaneal osteotomy or fibular osteotomy is performed, and can help confirm adequate groove depth/contour after deepening.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Prep and drape the leg free to allow intraoperative dynamic testing (active/passive ankle eversion and dorsiflexion) to assess tendon tracking and subluxation before and after the repair.
2. Make a curvilinear incision posterior to the fibula, centered over the retromalleolar groove, extending proximally toward the peroneal muscle bellies and distally toward the peroneal tubercle/base of the fifth metatarsal as needed for tear extent.
3. Identify and protect the sural nerve, which runs in this field close to the tendon sheath — isolate it early and retract gently rather than working blindly around it.
4. Incise the peroneal tendon sheath longitudinally (posterior to the SPR insertion if an SPR-preserving approach is planned, or through/adjacent to the retinaculum if SPR reconstruction is also needed) and perform a synovectomy of any hypertrophic/inflamed synovium obscuring tendon inspection.
5. Systematically inspect both the peroneus brevis (typically longitudinally split against the sharp posterior fibular edge — attritional pathology) and peroneus longus (more often torn at the os peroneum or in the cuboid tunnel distally) along their full length within the field.
6. **Tenosynovitis/partial longitudinal tear (<50% cross-section):** debride frayed/degenerative tissue, then tubularize the remaining tendon by running a whipstitch or simple running suture along the split to restore a round, functional cross-section rather than leaving a flattened, splayed tendon.
7. **Extensive tear (>50% cross-section) or one tendon irreparable:** perform a side-to-side tenodesis of the irreparable/deficient tendon to the adjacent intact peroneal tendon, proximal and distal to the zone of injury, ensuring the intact tendon is not overloaded or overtensioned by the added tissue.
8. **Complete non-functional tendon:** proceed to FHL or FDL tendon transfer — harvest the donor tendon through a separate posteromedial incision, whipstitch it, route it to the peroneal insertion (typically the base of the fifth metatarsal for brevis reconstruction), and fix with an interference screw or through a bone tunnel, tensioning with the foot in slight eversion/neutral dorsiflexion.
9. **SPR incompetence/subluxation:** assess the SPR quality — if attenuated but present, advance and imbricate it back to the posterior fibular ridge with suture anchors (2-4, placed along the ridge); if the SPR is absent or of very poor quality, reconstruct it using a local periosteal flap or a strip of adjacent Achilles/peroneal fascia secured with anchors.
10. **Fibular groove deepening (when indicated by a flat/convex groove on pre-operative imaging or by direct intraoperative assessment):** elevate a thin osteoperiosteal flap from the posterior fibula while carefully preserving the fibrocartilaginous lining of the groove itself (do not strip or violate the native fibrocartilage — this reduces post-operative shear/compressive irritation of the relocated tendons); deepen the underlying cancellous bone with sequential curettes/reaming or a thin curved saw/osteotome (a thin, narrowly curved saw requires less force on the distal fibula than an osteotome and reduces fracture risk), then re-impact the osteoperiosteal flap down into the deepened trough to restore a smooth, concave gliding surface.
11. Re-tension and secure the SPR repair/reconstruction over the deepened groove and relocated tendons, then dynamically test tracking by taking the ankle through active/passive eversion and dorsiflexion — the tendons should track smoothly within the groove without subluxing over the posterior fibular rim.
12. If hindfoot varus is present and contributing, perform the planned calcaneal osteotomy (or address it in a staged fashion per surgeon preference) — untreated malalignment predisposes to recurrent peroneal pathology even after a technically sound tendon procedure.
13. Release the tourniquet, achieve hemostasis, and close the tendon sheath (loosely, to avoid constricting the repaired/transferred tendons), subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers.
14. Apply a well-padded splint or boot with the foot held in slight eversion/neutral position to protect the SPR repair and reduce tension on the tendon repair.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Identify and protect the sural nerve early in the exposure — it runs close to the peroneal sheath throughout this field and is easily injured with blind retraction.
- Inspect both peroneal tendons along their entire visible length, not just the area of clinical suspicion — brevis splits are often longer than they appear on a limited exposure, and longus pathology at the os peroneum/cuboid tunnel can be missed if the distal extent of dissection is too conservative.
- When tubularizing a split tendon, aim to restore a genuinely round cross-section rather than simply tacking the split closed — a flattened, incompletely tubularized tendon remains mechanically abnormal and prone to re-tearing.
- When performing groove deepening, preserve the fibrocartilaginous lining of the native groove on the elevated osteoperiosteal flap — keeping this layer intact meaningfully reduces post-operative shear and compressive irritation of the relocated tendons compared with deepening directly into raw cancellous bone.
- Favor a thin, narrowly curved saw over an osteotome for the bony deepening cut when feasible — it requires less force against the distal fibula and reduces the risk of an inadvertent fibular fracture.
- Test tendon tracking dynamically (active/passive eversion and dorsiflexion) after SPR repair/reconstruction and groove deepening, before closing — this is the only reliable way to confirm the construct actually prevents subluxation, rather than assuming stability from the repair's appearance alone.
- SPR repair alone (without bony groove deepening) achieves good results in appropriately selected patients with a normal concave groove and isolated retinacular incompetence — reserve groove deepening for genuinely flat/convex grooves rather than performing it routinely.
- Always examine and address underlying hindfoot varus or coexisting lateral ankle instability at the time of peroneal surgery — treating the tendon pathology in isolation while ignoring a driving alignment or instability problem is a common cause of recurrence.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Peroneal disease and lateral ankle instability frequently coexist — always examine the peroneals during any chronic ankle instability work-up, and address both together when both are present.
- Underlying hindfoot varus, if unaddressed, predisposes to recurrent peroneal pathology after an otherwise technically sound repair.
- Sural nerve injury is a recognized risk during peroneal tendon surgery — stays close to the working field throughout the exposure.
- Reported pooled complication rates as high as ~38% in older systematic review (predominantly minor, e.g., mild residual pain) versus near-zero complication series more recently with modern SPR-focused technique — technique refinement over time has materially changed the risk profile.
- Recurrent subluxation can occur if SPR reconstruction is insufficient — verify groove depth and retinacular tension intra-operatively with dynamic testing rather than relying on the repair's static appearance.
- Violating the native fibrocartilaginous groove lining during deepening (rather than preserving it on the osteoperiosteal flap) can increase post-operative shear irritation of the relocated tendons.
- Overtensioning a tenodesis to the adjacent intact tendon can overload and secondarily injure the previously healthy tendon — tension neutrally, not tightly, and confirm smooth excursion through full ankle motion before closure.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0-4:** non-weight-bearing to protected weight-bearing in boot/cast (position often slight eversion/neutral to protect repair, depending on procedure).
- **Weeks 4-8:** progressive weight-bearing, ROM, gentle strengthening.
- **Weeks 8-12+:** progressive peroneal strengthening, proprioception, gradual return to activity.
- **Sport-specific return:** typically 4-6 months.

Outcomes: AOFAS improves ~70→89 post-operatively; complication rate up to ~20-39% (mostly minor) across series, with more modern SPR-focused series trending toward near-zero major complications.
