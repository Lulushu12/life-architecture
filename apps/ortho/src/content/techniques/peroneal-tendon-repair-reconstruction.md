## Indications

Tenosynovitis/partial longitudinal tear (<50% cross-section), extensive tear (>50% cross-section) or an irreparable tendon, complete non-functional tendon, or peroneal subluxation/dislocation from superior peroneal retinaculum (SPR) incompetence — after non-operative management (immobilization/bracing, PT, NSAIDs) has failed for tendinosis/subluxation without instability.

## Positioning & setup

Exposure is centered posterior to the lateral malleolus and the fibular groove; concurrent examination for coexisting lateral ankle instability and hindfoot varus is standard, since peroneal disease and lateral ankle instability frequently coexist.

## Steps

1. **Tenosynovitis/partial longitudinal tears (<50% cross-section):** debridement + tubularization — repair the tendon into a round/functional cross-section.
2. **Extensive tears (>50% cross-section) or one tendon irreparable:** tenodesis of the torn tendon to the adjacent intact peroneal tendon.
3. **Complete non-functional tendon:** FHL or FDL tendon transfer.
4. **Peroneal subluxation/dislocation (SPR incompetence):** SPR repair/reconstruction ± fibular groove deepening (for a shallow/flat retromalleolar groove) ± fibular osteotomy in select recurrent cases — SPR repair alone (without bony procedures) achieves good results in recent case series.
5. Address underlying hindfoot varus with calcaneal osteotomy, or concurrent chronic ankle instability, when present — untreated malalignment/instability predisposes to recurrent peroneal pathology.

## Pitfalls

- Peroneal disease and lateral ankle instability frequently coexist — always examine the peroneals during any chronic ankle instability work-up, and address both together when both are present.
- Underlying hindfoot varus, if unaddressed, predisposes to recurrent peroneal pathology after an otherwise technically sound repair.
- Sural nerve injury is a recognized risk during peroneal tendon surgery.
- Reported pooled complication rates as high as ~38% in older systematic review (predominantly minor, e.g., mild residual pain) versus near-zero complication series more recently with modern SPR-focused technique — technique refinement over time has materially changed the risk profile.
- Recurrent subluxation can occur if SPR reconstruction is insufficient — verify groove depth and retinacular tension intra-operatively.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0-4:** non-weight-bearing to protected weight-bearing in boot/cast (position often slight eversion/neutral to protect repair, depending on procedure).
- **Weeks 4-8:** progressive weight-bearing, ROM, gentle strengthening.
- **Weeks 8-12+:** progressive peroneal strengthening, proprioception, gradual return to activity.
- **Sport-specific return:** typically 4-6 months.
- Outcomes: AOFAS improves ~70→89 post-operatively; complication rate up to ~20-39% (mostly minor) across series, with more modern SPR-focused series trending toward near-zero major complications.
