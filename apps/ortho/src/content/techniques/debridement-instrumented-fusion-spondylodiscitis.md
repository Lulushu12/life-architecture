## Indications & Contraindications

**Surgical triggers for spondylodiscitis**: neurologic deficit/cord compression, mechanical instability or significant vertebral body destruction/collapse, failed medical management (persistent bacteremia/sepsis or progressive bone destruction despite appropriate antibiotics), a significant epidural or paraspinal abscess with mass effect, progressive kyphotic deformity, or need for open tissue diagnosis when percutaneous biopsy is non-diagnostic.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:
- No surgical trigger present — asymptomatic radiographic changes, stable alignment, and a patient responding appropriately to antibiotics should be managed medically, not operated on because imaging "looks bad."
- Patient too physiologically unstable for formal open debridement (uncontrolled septic shock) — consider damage-control percutaneous stabilization alone, or medical stabilization first, rather than a prolonged combined procedure.
- Diagnosis achievable and disease controllable with a percutaneous approach (CT-guided biopsy plus antibiotics, or percutaneous drainage) without a surgical trigger — formal open debridement adds risk without added benefit in this group.
- Severe, uncorrected coagulopathy or medical comorbidity precluding safe anesthesia, unless the neurologic emergency overrides this (evolving deficit).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: contrast-enhanced whole-spine MRI (gold standard — endplate destruction, T2-hyperintense disc, paraspinal/epidural enhancement, and screening for skip lesions); CT for bony destruction detail and templating corpectomy/cage size or planned screw trajectories in destroyed bone.
- **Microbiology**: obtain 2–3 sets of blood cultures *before* starting antibiotics (positive in 50–70% of hematogenous cases). If the patient is hemodynamically stable and cultures are negative, consider holding empiric antibiotics 48–72 hours to allow a CT-guided biopsy, which meaningfully improves microbiologic yield.
- **Regional epidemiology**: in endemic regions (Central/Eastern Europe, Mediterranean, including Romania), plan Brucella serology and TB-specific workup (interferon-gamma release assay, AFB culture, biopsy histology for caseating granulomas) for atypical or slow-indolent presentations, especially with a large paraspinal/psoas abscess and relatively preserved disc space.
- **Source workup**: consider echocardiography if *S. aureus* bacteremia is present (endocarditis as a seeding source changes antibiotic duration and cardiology involvement).
- **Infectious disease consultation** to plan empiric-then-culture-directed antibiotic strategy before and after surgery.
- **Approach selection**: choose anterior corpectomy, posterior-only, lateral extracavitary, or a combined approach based on the level involved and the location of the abscess/destruction — template accordingly.
- **Implant planning**: size a titanium mesh cage or structural allograft/autograft strut off CT for the anterior column defect; select a posterior segmental pedicle screw system, planning to span additional levels (often 2 above/2 below) if bone quality is poor.
- **General optimization**: nutritional status, glycemic control in diabetics, and correction of any coagulopathy before surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia**; positioning depends on the chosen approach.
- **Anterior/lateral approach** (retroperitoneal or transthoracic): lateral decubitus, often with a table break to open the flank/intercostal space; single-lung ventilation (double-lumen tube) if a transthoracic corridor is needed.
- **Posterior approach**: prone on a radiolucent frame (Jackson/Wilson) with standard prone-spine padding (chest/iliac bolsters, abdomen free, arms <90°, face/eyes protected).
- Arterial line and large-bore IV access for cases with anticipated significant blood loss (destroyed, hypervascular infected bone bleeds more than routine degenerative surgery).
- **Cell salvage is relatively contraindicated in the presence of frank purulence** — discuss with anesthesia and generally avoid autotransfusion from a grossly infected field; use allogeneic blood instead if transfusion is needed.
- Fluoroscopy for level localization and instrumentation placement; mark the planned incision(s) under fluoroscopy before prepping.
- Confirm antibiotic timing with anesthesia — many surgeons hold an additional preoperative dose until intraoperative cultures are obtained, then proceed with dosing per protocol.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm a surgical trigger is present** (see Indications) rather than proceeding on imaging findings alone.
2. **Position and prep** per the chosen approach; obtain a timeout confirming level and laterality.
3. **Obtain intraoperative cultures first** — multiple separate specimens (bone, disc, abscess fluid, and tissue for histopathology to look for granulomas/TB or malignancy mimics) before any irrigation or antibiotic-soaked instruments contaminate the field.
4. **Decompress any epidural abscess or canal compromise first** if a neurologic deficit is present — laminectomy and abscess evacuation take priority over anterior column work.
5. **Debride all grossly infected and necrotic bone and disc** back to bleeding, healthy-appearing tissue, circumferentially. Historically performed via anterior corpectomy; now frequently posterior-only (transpedicular/costotransversectomy corridor) or a combined approach depending on level and abscess location.
6. **Irrigate copiously** (pulse lavage; dilute betadine or antibiotic irrigation per surgeon preference).
7. **Reconstruct the anterior column defect** with a sized titanium mesh cage or structural allograft/autograft strut, restoring vertebral height and segmental lordosis.
8. **Add posterior segmental instrumentation for stability**, spanning enough levels above and below the debrided segment (often 2 above/2 below, more with poor bone quality) — instrumenting in the setting of active infection is now widely accepted practice provided adequate debridement and appropriate antibiotic coverage, contrary to older teaching that avoided hardware in infected fields.
9. **Consider percutaneous posterior stabilization alone** (without formal debridement) as a damage-control option in frail, high-risk patients who cannot tolerate a formal debridement procedure; formal debridement can be staged later if the patient improves.
10. **Achieve meticulous hemostasis**, place a drain if indicated, and close in layers.
11. **Continue the antibiotic course**: empiric-then-culture-directed IV antibiotics, standard duration 6–8 weeks guided by serial CRP trend (6 weeks is non-inferior to 12 weeks for most pyogenic cases per Bernard et al., *Lancet* 2015), with extension for undrained abscess, endocarditis, or slow clinical response.
12. **Manage a concurrent spinal epidural abscess if present** — combine debridement/stabilization with formal abscess drainage, since the two frequently coexist.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Send multiple separate specimens (bone, disc, abscess fluid, and dedicated histopathology) rather than a single swab — histology looking for caseating granulomas is often the fastest way to raise suspicion for TB when culture will take weeks.
- Debride circumferentially to bleeding bone — inadequate debridement is the single most common technical cause of relapse.
- In endemic regions, keep a high index of suspicion for TB/Brucella when the disc space is relatively preserved despite a large paraspinal or psoas abscess — request AFB smear/culture and dedicated histology proactively rather than waiting on routine culture results.
- When instrumenting infected or osteoporotic bone, extend fixation further than you would for degenerative disease (often 2 levels above/below the debrided segment) — a short construct in weak bone is a common cause of early hardware failure.
- A posterior-only "eggshell" corpectomy through a transpedicular or costotransversectomy corridor avoids the added morbidity of an anterior thoracotomy or retroperitoneal approach in medically frail patients, and allows combining decompression, debridement, reconstruction, and instrumentation through a single position.
- In an unstable or septic patient who cannot tolerate a long combined procedure, stage the surgery — posterior stabilization first, anterior reconstruction second once the patient is more stable.
- Coordinate closely with infectious disease before and after surgery — surgery treats the mechanical/structural problem, it does not substitute for an adequate antibiotic course.
- Use local irrigation additives (dilute betadine, vancomycin powder) per institutional protocol; evidence specifically in frank infected fields is mixed, so base the decision on surgeon and institutional experience rather than assuming uniform benefit.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Delayed diagnosis remains the leading modifiable driver of poor outcomes — do not defer surgical evaluation while awaiting a slow medical response.
- Relapse/chronic infection, progression to epidural abscess, and vertebral collapse with kyphotic deformity are recognized failure modes of undertreated or inadequately debrided disease.
- A CRP that fails to trend down after 3–4 weeks of appropriate antibiotics should prompt reimaging and reconsideration of organism/source control, not simple continuation of the same regimen.
- In endemic regions (Central/Eastern Europe, Mediterranean, including Romania), Brucella and TB (Pott's) spondylitis are meaningfully more prevalent — consider Brucella serology and TB-specific workup (interferon-gamma release assay, AFB culture, biopsy histology for caseating granulomas) for atypical or slow-indolent presentations, especially with a large paraspinal/psoas abscess and relatively preserved disc space.
- Sepsis and clinically meaningful mortality in elderly/comorbid populations are recognized risks of spondylodiscitis generally, reinforcing the importance of timely surgical trigger recognition.
- **Instrumentation failure** in destroyed or osteoporotic bone if the construct is not extended sufficiently beyond the debrided segment.
- **Wound complications** from a contaminated/infected surgical field — meticulous hemostasis and irrigation reduce but do not eliminate this risk.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Early mobilization within pain limits once medically stable is prioritized over prolonged bed rest, which primarily adds deconditioning risk without proven infection-control benefit.
- TLSO bracing for pain and provisional stability, typically 6–12 weeks in non-instrumented cases, weaned as CRP normalizes and imaging shows healing.
- Outpatient parenteral antibiotic therapy (OPAT) is standard once the patient is clinically stable, allowing rehabilitation to proceed outside the acute hospital setting.
- In patients with a preceding neurologic deficit, formal inpatient or outpatient neurorehabilitation (gait training, strengthening) proceeds in parallel with the antibiotic course.
