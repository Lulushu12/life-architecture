## Indications

Surgical triggers for spondylodiscitis: neurologic deficit/cord compression, mechanical instability or significant vertebral body destruction/collapse, failed medical management (persistent bacteremia/sepsis or progressive bone destruction despite appropriate antibiotics), a significant epidural or paraspinal abscess with mass effect, progressive kyphotic deformity, or need for open tissue diagnosis when percutaneous biopsy is non-diagnostic.

## Positioning & setup

Approach (anterior corpectomy versus posterior-only versus combined) is selected based on the level involved and the location of any abscess; the monograph does not specify further positioning detail beyond this approach-selection principle.

## Steps

1. **Confirm a surgical trigger is present** (see Indications) rather than proceeding on imaging findings alone.
2. **Debride infected/necrotic bone and disc.** Historically performed via anterior corpectomy; now frequently posterior-only or a combined approach depending on level and abscess location.
3. **Reconstruct the resulting structural defect** with a titanium cage or allograft.
4. **Add posterior instrumentation for stability.** Instrumenting in the setting of active infection is now widely accepted practice — provided adequate debridement and appropriate antibiotic coverage — contrary to older teaching that avoided hardware in infected fields.
5. **Consider percutaneous posterior stabilization alone (without formal debridement)** as a damage-control option in frail, high-risk patients who cannot tolerate formal debridement.
6. **Coordinate with the antibiotic course**: continue empiric-then-culture-directed IV antibiotics, standard duration 6–8 weeks guided by serial CRP trend (6 weeks is non-inferior to 12 weeks for most pyogenic cases per Bernard et al., Lancet 2015), with extension for undrained abscess, endocarditis, or slow clinical response.
7. **Manage concurrent spinal epidural abscess if present** — combine debridement/stabilization with abscess drainage, since the two frequently coexist.

## Pitfalls

- Delayed diagnosis remains the leading modifiable driver of poor outcomes — do not defer surgical evaluation while awaiting a slow medical response.
- Relapse/chronic infection, progression to epidural abscess, and vertebral collapse with kyphotic deformity are recognized failure modes of undertreated or inadequately debrided disease.
- A CRP that fails to trend down after 3–4 weeks of appropriate antibiotics should prompt reimaging and reconsideration of organism/source control, not simple continuation of the same regimen.
- In endemic regions (Central/Eastern Europe, Mediterranean, including Romania), Brucella and TB (Pott's) spondylitis are meaningfully more prevalent — consider Brucella serology and TB-specific workup (interferon-gamma release assay, AFB culture, biopsy histology for caseating granulomas) for atypical or slow-indolent presentations, especially with a large paraspinal/psoas abscess and relatively preserved disc space.
- Sepsis and clinically meaningful mortality in elderly/comorbid populations are recognized risks of spondylodiscitis generally, reinforcing the importance of timely surgical trigger recognition.

## Post-op protocol

- Early mobilization within pain limits once medically stable is prioritized over prolonged bed rest, which primarily adds deconditioning risk without proven infection-control benefit.
- TLSO bracing for pain and provisional stability, typically 6–12 weeks in non-instrumented cases, weaned as CRP normalizes and imaging shows healing.
- Outpatient parenteral antibiotic therapy (OPAT) is standard once the patient is clinically stable, allowing rehabilitation to proceed outside the acute hospital setting.
- In patients with a preceding neurologic deficit, formal inpatient or outpatient neurorehabilitation (gait training, strengthening) proceeds in parallel with the antibiotic course.
