## Indications

Chronic lateral ankle instability that has failed a minimum of 3 months of structured proprioceptive/peroneal strengthening rehabilitation. Suture-tape augmentation is considered primarily for revision cases, high-demand athletes, generalized ligamentous laxity, or poor-quality remnant tissue. Free tendon graft reconstruction is reserved for revision surgery, absent/attenuated native ligament tissue, or high-demand athletes with a failed prior repair.

## Positioning & setup

Can be performed open or arthroscopically. Arthroscopic technique allows concurrent treatment of intra-articular pathology, reduces complication rate, and speeds recovery versus open technique in comparative series.

## Steps

1. **Modified Broström-Gould repair:** imbricate/directly repair the ATFL ± CFL, then reinforce the repair with the extensor retinaculum (the "Gould modification"). This remains the gold-standard anatomic procedure.
2. **Suture-tape internal brace augmentation** (when indicated by risk factors): secure a synthetic tape from the fibula to the talus/calcaneus alongside the anatomic repair. It functions as a protective internal splint during ligament healing, not a permanent structural replacement.
3. **Free tendon graft reconstruction** (when native tissue is inadequate or in revision settings): use a semitendinosus, peroneus longus half-graft, or allograft, anatomically routed through fibular and talar/calcaneal tunnels to restore the ligament complex — higher morbidity and longer rehab, but a more robust reconstruction.
4. Address concomitant intra-articular pathology (osteochondral lesions, anterolateral impingement, syndesmotic instability) at the time of ligament surgery.

## Pitfalls

- Failing to address concomitant intra-articular pathology at the time of ligament surgery is a major driver of otherwise-unexplained post-operative dissatisfaction — always evaluate and treat concurrently.
- Suture-tape augmentation's principal proven benefit is faster return to sport at similar mid-term clinical outcome scores, not necessarily lower failure rate — frame this accurately with patients/athletes rather than overselling reduced recurrence.
- Superficial peroneal nerve injury/neuroma is the most common nerve complication; sural nerve injury is a risk with open lateral approaches.
- Suture-tape-specific risks include hardware prominence/irritation and, rarely, tunnel-related fracture.
- Arthroscopic technique reduces complication rate and speeds recovery compared with open technique in comparative series — favor it when the surgeon's expertise and case complexity allow.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0-2:** non-weight-bearing to protected weight-bearing in a boot/splint per surgeon protocol (suture-tape augmentation may allow slightly earlier protected WB given the internal splinting effect).
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive weight-bearing in boot, gentle inversion/eversion-protected ROM initiated around 2-4 weeks.
- **Weeks 6-8:** transition to supportive footwear/brace, begin proprioceptive and peroneal strengthening.
- **Weeks 8-12:** progressive strengthening, agility introduction.
- **Months 3-4 (isolated repair) to as early as months 2-3 (suture-tape augmented):** return to pivoting/cutting sport, criterion-based (strength/proprioceptive symmetry, sport-specific agility testing); the suture-tape cohort achieves faster return to sport at similar clinical outcome scores in recent comparative studies.
- Complications: superficial peroneal nerve injury/neuroma (most common), sural nerve injury (open lateral approaches), residual/recurrent instability (failure rate variably reported, generally low single digits to low teens depending on series and risk factors), stiffness, wound complications.
