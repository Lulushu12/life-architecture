## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Chronic lateral ankle instability (mechanical laxity, functional instability, or both) that has failed a minimum of 3 months of structured proprioceptive/peroneal strengthening rehabilitation.
- Adequate native ATFL ± CFL remnant tissue present on exam/imaging suitable for direct repair/imbrication.
- Suture-tape augmentation is considered primarily for revision cases, high-demand athletes, generalized ligamentous laxity, or poor-quality remnant tissue.
- Free tendon graft reconstruction is reserved for revision surgery, absent/attenuated native ligament tissue, or high-demand athletes with a failed prior repair.

**Contraindications**
- Active infection or significant soft-tissue compromise about the lateral ankle.
- Inadequately trialed conservative management (<3 months of structured, supervised proprioceptive/strengthening rehab) — repeat or extend the non-operative program before proceeding.
- Uncorrected substantial hindfoot varus malalignment left unaddressed — proceeding with isolated soft-tissue repair without correcting a driving varus deformity predisposes to failure; plan concomitant or staged calcaneal osteotomy in these patients.
- Generalized connective tissue disorder with markedly attenuated tissue where even augmented repair is unlikely to hold — favor free tendon graft reconstruction over isolated repair (even augmented) in this group.
- Active untreated systemic or local infection, or medical unfitness for elective surgery.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** weight-bearing radiographs (assess hindfoot alignment/varus), stress radiographs or stress ultrasound if mechanical laxity needs objective quantification, and MRI to assess ATFL/CFL tissue quality, identify concomitant intra-articular pathology (osteochondral lesions, anterolateral impingement/synovitis, peroneal tendon tears, syndesmotic injury — found in a substantial proportion of chronic cases at arthroscopy), and screen for peroneal tendon pathology. Weight-bearing CT is increasingly used in revision or complex cases to fully characterize hindfoot alignment before committing to a technique.
- **Technique selection to template in advance:** based on tissue quality and risk-factor assessment (Beighton score for generalized laxity, BMI, revision status, remnant tissue quality on MRI/exam), decide among isolated modified Broström-Gould, Broström-Gould with suture-tape augmentation, or free tendon graft reconstruction — this should be a pre-operative decision informed by imaging, not purely an intraoperative judgment call, though intraoperative remnant quality can still shift the plan.
- **Graft selection (if reconstruction planned):** semitendinosus autograft, peroneus longus half-graft (harvested from the same limb, accepting some donor-site morbidity), or allograft — confirm availability/consent in advance if allograft is anticipated.
- **Equipment:** suture anchors (2-4, typically 2.4-3.5 mm bioabsorbable or PEEK anchors) for the fibular ATFL/CFL footprint, suture-tape augmentation implant system if planned, ankle arthroscopy tower/instrumentation if an arthroscopic or arthroscopic-assisted approach is chosen, and interference screws/tunnel drill guides if free graft reconstruction is planned.
- **Concomitant procedure planning:** if arthroscopic evaluation is planned, anticipate and be prepared to treat anterolateral impingement (debridement), osteochondral lesions (microfracture/other), or syndesmotic pathology found at the time of surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** popliteal sciatic nerve block (± saphenous block) with sedation, or general anesthesia; regional block provides substantial post-operative analgesia for this typically same-day procedure.
- **Positioning:** supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip/buttock to internally rotate the leg and bring the lateral ankle into a more accessible position, or with the leg in a well-leg holder for arthroscopic portions of the case; ensure the foot extends past the end of the table (or use a radiolucent table extension) for free ankle motion and fluoroscopy if needed.
- **Tourniquet:** thigh or calf tourniquet, exsanguinated and inflated per protocol; a bloodless field materially aids identification of the ATFL/CFL remnant and the superficial peroneal nerve.
- **Landmarks to mark:** the distal fibula, the ATFL/CFL footprints, the course of the superficial peroneal nerve (crosses the anterolateral ankle roughly 2-3 finger-breadths proximal to the tip of the fibula in a variable but describable location — identify it by plantarflexing/inverting the ankle with the toes flexed, which tenses the nerve subcutaneously and makes it visible/palpable through the skin) and the sural nerve if a more posterior open exposure is planned.
- **Imaging:** mini C-arm fluoroscopy is useful when placing suture anchors or tunnels for graft reconstruction to confirm appropriate fibular/talar/calcaneal trajectory and depth, and to avoid physeal injury in skeletally immature patients.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Prep and drape the leg free with the foot accessible for full plantarflexion/dorsiflexion and inversion/eversion stress testing intraoperatively.
2. Perform an examination under anesthesia (anterior drawer, talar tilt) to confirm and quantify mechanical laxity before incision, comparing to the contralateral side if not already done pre-operatively.
3. If an arthroscopic-assisted or all-arthroscopic approach is used, establish anteromedial and anterolateral portals, perform a diagnostic survey, and treat identified intra-articular pathology (loose bodies, anterolateral impingement/synovitis debridement, osteochondral lesion management) before proceeding to the ligament work — an accessory anterior portal near the fibular tip is often used to access and debride the ATFL footprint directly.
4. **Open exposure (or open completion of an arthroscopic-assisted case):** make a curvilinear incision centered over the anterolateral ankle, from just anterior to the fibular tip curving distally toward the sinus tarsi, staying mindful of the superficial peroneal nerve's marked course.
5. Identify and protect the superficial peroneal nerve branches within the subcutaneous layer before deepening dissection; retract gently rather than placing fixed retractors directly on the nerve.
6. Incise the extensor retinaculum/inferior extensor retinaculum in continuity with (or just proximal to) the attenuated ATFL capsule-ligament complex, preserving it as a distinct flap for the later Gould reinforcement step.
7. Identify the attenuated/elongated ATFL (and CFL if involved) — typically visualized as a thinned, redundant capsular-ligamentous sheet rather than a discrete cord in chronic cases. Freshen the fibular footprint with a rongeur or shaver to a bleeding bony bed.
8. **Modified Broström repair:** imbricate the ATFL (and CFL, if incompetent) in a pants-over-vest fashion, overlapping and advancing the ligament proximally onto the freshened fibular footprint; secure with either transosseous sutures through fibular drill holes or, more commonly in current practice, 1-2 suture anchors placed in the mid-portion of the fibular footprint. Tension the repair with the ankle held in neutral dorsiflexion and slight eversion (avoid inversion, which would set the repair too loose) before final tying.
9. **Gould modification:** advance the inferior extensor retinaculum flap proximally and suture it over the completed ATFL/CFL repair to the fibula/periosteum, adding a secondary layer of reinforcement and effectively re-tensioning the anterolateral capsule.
10. **Suture-tape augmentation (if indicated):** place a small-diameter anchor or tunnel in the fibula at the ATFL origin and a second fixation point in the talar neck (and/or calcaneus, for combined ATFL+CFL augmentation), then route a synthetic tape between them alongside — not in place of — the completed biologic repair, tensioning it with the ankle in neutral position so it acts as an internal splint without over-constraining the joint; confirm a normal (not overly restricted) range of inversion/plantarflexion after tensioning.
11. **Free tendon graft reconstruction (if indicated instead of repair):** prepare the graft (semitendinosus, peroneus longus half-graft, or allograft) with whipstitched ends sized to the planned tunnel diameter, then drill anatomically positioned tunnels in the fibula and talus (and calcaneus for a combined ATFL+CFL reconstruction) under fluoroscopic guidance, pass the graft, and fix with interference screws or suture anchors, tensioning with the ankle in neutral dorsiflexion and slight eversion as with the biologic repair.
12. Take the ankle through a full range of motion and repeat anterior drawer/talar tilt stress testing to confirm restored stability without over-constraint (the ankle should still dorsiflex/plantarflex through a functional range).
13. Close the extensor retinaculum/capsule layer, then subcutaneous tissue and skin in standard fashion; apply a well-padded posterior splint or removable boot with the ankle held in neutral to slight eversion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Tension the ATFL/CFL imbrication with the ankle in neutral dorsiflexion and slight eversion, never in inversion — repairing in an inverted position creates a repair that is too tight and restricts normal inversion range post-operatively.
- Identify the superficial peroneal nerve early by plantarflexing and inverting the ankle with the toes flexed before incision — this maneuver tenses the nerve subcutaneously and makes it visible/palpable, letting you plan the incision and retraction around it.
- Freshen the fibular footprint to bleeding bone before anchor placement or imbrication — a smooth, non-bleeding cortical surface is a poor healing bed for the repaired ligament.
- Preserve the inferior extensor retinaculum as a distinct, separately handled flap from the moment of the initial incision — trying to identify and mobilize it after the ATFL repair is already tied down is needlessly difficult.
- When placing a suture-tape augmentation, tension it as a true internal splint (protecting, not replacing, the biologic repair) — over-tensioning the tape restricts physiologic ankle motion and can produce a stiff, unnatural-feeling ankle even though objective stability testing looks excellent.
- Address concomitant intra-articular pathology (osteochondral lesions, anterolateral impingement, syndesmotic instability) at the time of ligament surgery — this is not optional add-on work; untreated intra-articular pathology is one of the most common causes of a technically sound repair that still leaves the patient dissatisfied.
- Favor an arthroscopic or arthroscopic-assisted approach when the surgeon's expertise and case complexity allow — comparative series show it reduces complication rate and speeds recovery relative to a fully open approach, largely by minimizing soft-tissue dissection around the superficial peroneal nerve and by enabling simultaneous intra-articular treatment.
- In revision cases or where remnant tissue is clearly inadequate, do not force an isolated repair — proceed directly to augmentation or free graft reconstruction rather than repairing tissue unlikely to hold, which only sets up a second revision.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Failing to address concomitant intra-articular pathology at the time of ligament surgery is a major driver of otherwise-unexplained post-operative dissatisfaction — always evaluate and treat concurrently.
- Suture-tape augmentation's principal proven benefit is faster return to sport at similar mid-term clinical outcome scores, not necessarily lower failure rate — frame this accurately with patients/athletes rather than overselling reduced recurrence.
- Superficial peroneal nerve injury/neuroma is the most common nerve complication; sural nerve injury is a risk with open lateral approaches — meticulous identification and gentle (not fixed) retraction reduce this.
- Suture-tape-specific risks include hardware prominence/irritation and, rarely, tunnel-related fracture — avoid oversized tunnels relative to bone stock, particularly in smaller or osteopenic patients.
- Repairing/tensioning the ATFL with the ankle held in inversion is a common technical error that over-constrains the joint and leads to stiffness and restricted inversion range.
- Unaddressed hindfoot varus malalignment predisposes to recurrent instability after an otherwise technically sound ligament procedure — assess alignment pre-operatively and plan concomitant calcaneal osteotomy when indicated.
- Arthroscopic technique reduces complication rate and speeds recovery compared with open technique in comparative series — favor it when the surgeon's expertise and case complexity allow.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0-2:** non-weight-bearing to protected weight-bearing in a boot/splint per surgeon protocol (suture-tape augmentation may allow slightly earlier protected WB given the internal splinting effect).
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive weight-bearing in boot, gentle inversion/eversion-protected ROM initiated around 2-4 weeks.
- **Weeks 6-8:** transition to supportive footwear/brace, begin proprioceptive and peroneal strengthening.
- **Weeks 8-12:** progressive strengthening, agility introduction.
- **Months 3-4 (isolated repair) to as early as months 2-3 (suture-tape augmented):** return to pivoting/cutting sport, criterion-based (strength/proprioceptive symmetry, sport-specific agility testing); the suture-tape cohort achieves faster return to sport at similar clinical outcome scores in recent comparative studies.

Complications: superficial peroneal nerve injury/neuroma (most common), sural nerve injury (open lateral approaches), residual/recurrent instability (failure rate variably reported, generally low single digits to low teens depending on series and risk factors), stiffness, wound complications.
