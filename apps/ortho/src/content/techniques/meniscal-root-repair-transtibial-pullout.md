## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:

- Preferred technique for most LaPrade Type 2 (complete radial tear within 9 mm of the root attachment) tears — the most common root-tear pattern.
- Type 3 (bucket-handle with complete root detachment): repair the bucket-handle component and the root simultaneously.
- Type 4 (complex oblique/longitudinal tear extending into the root): repair if tissue quality allows; often technically demanding.
- Type 5 (bony root avulsion): suture or screw fixation of the bony fragment rather than a soft-tissue pull-out technique — generally excellent healing.
- Best candidates are acute, traumatic tears with minimal extrusion and low background osteoarthritis. Repair candidacy is further modified by Kellgren-Lawrence grade ≤2, meniscal extrusion ≤3 mm, mechanical axis within 5° of neutral, age generally <60–65 (relative), and absence of diffuse cartilage loss in the affected compartment.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:

- Chronic tears with Kellgren-Lawrence ≥3, extrusion >3 mm, and varus >5° have poor repair outcomes — favor partial meniscectomy, non-operative care, or the arthroplasty pathway (± HTO) instead.
- Diffuse advanced cartilage loss in the affected compartment (independent of KL grade on plain films) — repair is unlikely to alter the trajectory of an already end-stage compartment.
- Uncorrected malalignment >5° without a plan for concurrent or staged osteotomy — proceeding with isolated root repair in this setting predicts inferior survivorship.
- LaPrade Type 1 (partial, stable) tears generally do not require repair and are managed non-operatively/observed.
- Active infection or an unaddressed concurrent ligamentous instability that would compromise the repair environment.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI is the key diagnostic step — confirm the "ghost sign" (absence of meniscal tissue on sagittal images through the normal root location), a vertical linear defect near the root on coronal images, and measure meniscal extrusion (>3 mm on coronal MRI, measured from the tibial plateau margin) explicitly, since it is easy to mistake for simple degenerative change if not measured directly. Standing long-leg (hip-knee-ankle) alignment films are mandatory whenever malalignment is suspected clinically or radiographically — this determines whether concurrent/staged HTO must be planned.
- **Assess repair candidacy modifiers** systematically before the day of surgery: Kellgren-Lawrence grade, extrusion magnitude, mechanical axis, patient age, and cartilage status in the affected compartment — document each, since they jointly determine whether repair, meniscectomy, or an osteotomy-combined pathway is appropriate.
- **Implant selection**: choose and have available both a transtibial pull-out system (guide/aiming device, cannulated drill, suture-passing shuttle, and a fixation button/post construct — including newer adjustable-tension subcortical button systems) and an all-inside suture-anchor system for the footprint, since posterolateral root tears in particular may be more amenable to anchor fixation given a more difficult tunnel trajectory.
- **Equipment/instrumentation checklist**: root-repair-specific tibial aiming guide, guide pins (with a technique for confirming pin depth — a low-cost intraoperative method to gauge guide-pin depth accurately has been described and is useful when fluoroscopy is not used), cannulated reamers sized to the suture-passing device, suture-passing instruments (curved suture hooks or shuttle relay devices) for the root stump, high-strength suture, and a button/post fixation construct for the anteromedial tibia.
- **Consider fluoroscopy availability** intraoperatively, particularly for less experienced surgeons or difficult trajectories, since precise tunnel depth and guide pin position are difficult to judge by feel alone; anatomic tunnel placement within roughly 5–6 mm of the true footprint center is associated with meaningfully better healing and outcomes at 1 year.
- **Plan concurrent HTO** (instrumentation, implant, and OR time) if standing alignment films show >5° varus — either as a staged first-stage procedure or in the same anesthetic episode per surgeon preference and construct considerations.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia; a femoral or adductor canal nerve block is commonly added for postoperative analgesia given the more demanding weight-bearing restrictions of this procedure.
- **Positioning**: supine with a lateral post or leg holder that allows full flexion beyond 120° and a figure-4/externally-rotated position for posterior compartment access — root repair requires excellent visualization and instrumentation of the posterior root footprint, which is only achievable with deep, well-controlled flexion.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh tourniquet placed and available; many surgeons keep it deflated for most of the case to allow physiologic bleeding cues and inflate selectively if visualization is compromised.
- **Fluoroscopy**: a sterile C-arm should be draped into the field or immediately available if the surgeon's technique uses fluoroscopic confirmation of guide-pin position and tunnel depth, particularly for less-routine trajectories (posterolateral root, revision cases).
- **Setup**: standard knee arthroscopy fluid management system; mark the projected anteromedial tibial tunnel exit site (roughly at or just distal to the pes anserinus, medial to the tibial tubercle) before draping, since this is where the button/post fixation will ultimately sit and where a small skin incision will be made.
- **Landmarks**: standard anteromedial/anterolateral portals plus, if the technique calls for it, an accessory posteromedial or posterolateral portal for direct posterior visualization/instrumentation depending on which root is being repaired.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the diagnosis and type on MRI (ghost sign on sagittal images, vertical linear defect near the root on coronal images, extrusion >3 mm on coronal MRI) and assess repair candidacy modifiers (Kellgren-Lawrence grade, extrusion, mechanical alignment, age, cartilage status). Obtain standing long-leg alignment films if malalignment is suspected clinically or radiographically.
2. Perform diagnostic arthroscopy through standard portals; confirm the root tear pattern and LaPrade type, and probe the remainder of both menisci to exclude a concurrent ramp or additional tear.
3. Prepare the root footprint stump — debride the torn root edge and the native footprint on the tibial plateau back to a bleeding, healthy bony bed using a shaver or curette, taking care not to over-resect meniscal tissue needed for suture purchase.
4. Pass two limbs of suture (simple or locking cinch stitch, e.g., a modified Mason-Allen or two independent simple sutures) through the root stump using a suture-passing device or curved suture hook, securing solid purchase in the meniscal substance near the tear edge.
5. Using the tibial aiming guide, drill a guide pin to the anatomic root footprint on the tibial plateau, confirming trajectory and depth (fluoroscopically or with a validated depth-gauging technique) before over-reaming — anatomic tunnel placement is critical, as a malpositioned tunnel under-restores hoop-stress mechanics; when two tunnels are used (e.g., a double-tunnel technique), place the more posterior tunnel first, since it is more difficult to access once the anterior tunnel and its instrumentation are in place.
6. Over-ream the guide pin with a cannulated reamer to create the tibial tunnel from the anteromedial tibial cortex to the root footprint.
7. Shuttle the suture limbs through the tunnel using a passing wire or suture relay, confirming smooth excursion without excessive friction or fraying at the tunnel aperture.
8. Reduce the meniscal root to its anatomic footprint and fix the sutures over a button/post construct on the anteromedial tibial cortex (transtibial pull-out); alternatively, for posterolateral root tears where tunnel trajectory is more technically difficult, place a suture anchor directly into the footprint (all-inside fixation) instead of drilling a tunnel.
9. Tension the repair with the knee positioned near full extension — tensioning in excessive flexion under-tensions the repair once the knee is brought back to extension, risking a lax construct; a subcortical button/tensioning system that allows progressive, incremental tightening without losing tension during final suture tying is a useful technical adjunct where available.
10. Confirm reduction of the root to its footprint and adequate tension by direct visualization and probing; assess whether meniscal extrusion has improved.
11. Address concurrent extrusion if present — some surgeons add a centralization stitch (anchoring the meniscal body to the tibial plateau or capsule to reduce residual extrusion), though evidence for extrusion correction with centralization remains mixed and should be viewed as an adjunct, not a substitute for anatomic root fixation.
12. If concurrent varus malalignment exceeds 5°, proceed with the planned staged or concurrent high tibial osteotomy — isolated root repair in a malaligned knee has substantially worse survivorship.
13. Perform a final range-of-motion check to confirm the fixation construct tolerates full flexion-extension without loss of tension or suture cut-through, then close portals and the tibial tunnel exit incision in standard fashion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Measure extrusion explicitly on coronal MRI rather than eyeballing it — it is easy to under-appreciate on a quick read and it materially changes the surgical plan.
- When using a two-tunnel (double-tunnel) technique, drill the more posterior tunnel first; sequencing the anterior tunnel first can make the posterior trajectory awkward or impossible to achieve cleanly.
- If fluoroscopy is not routinely used, adopt a reproducible, low-cost method (e.g., measuring guide pin protrusion length against a known reference) to confirm guide pin depth before over-reaming — pin deflection and uncertain depth are common, under-appreciated sources of tunnel malposition.
- Tunnel accuracy matters quantitatively: placing the tibial tunnel center within roughly 5–6 mm of the true anatomic footprint center is associated with improved healing and outcomes at one year — treat "close" as not good enough when a small adjustment can get you closer to anatomic.
- Tension the final construct with the knee near full extension, not in flexion, to avoid an under-tensioned repair once the knee is brought to extension postoperatively.
- Consider an adjustable/subcortical button-tensioning system when available — it allows incremental re-tensioning during final suture management without the "one-shot" limitation of a fixed knot.
- For posterolateral root tears, have a low threshold to switch from a transtibial tunnel to a direct suture-anchor construct — the posterolateral trajectory is inherently more difficult to achieve anatomically with a straight tibial tunnel.
- Always probe the posterior horn of both menisci (not just the injured root) before finishing — root tears and ramp lesions can coexist, and missing a concurrent ramp lesion undermines the overall repair construct's biomechanical goal.
- Counsel patients explicitly and document the alignment/extrusion/OA-grade findings — this both guides the operative plan and sets realistic expectations for outcome.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Tunnel malposition and inadequate fixation tension are the main technical failure modes.
- Extrusion frequently persists even after confirmed root healing and does not by itself indicate failure.
- Incomplete healing on second-look MRI/arthroscopy occurs in a meaningful minority of cases even with technically sound fixation.
- Missing a concurrent ramp lesion — probe the posterior horn of both menisci, not just the root.
- Proceeding without correcting a concurrent >5° varus malalignment risks poor repair survivorship regardless of technical success.
- Guide pin deflection during tunnel drilling, especially without fluoroscopic confirmation, is a recognized and under-discussed source of tunnel malposition — verify trajectory before committing to the full-diameter reamer pass.
- Under-tensioning the repair by tying down sutures with the knee in flexion produces a functionally lax construct once the knee returns to extension.
- Counsel patients that root repair reliably improves pain and function and slows — but does not fully arrest — progression of joint-space narrowing; it is a joint-preservation procedure, not a guaranteed cure for an already-established degenerative trajectory.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weight-bearing**: more conservative than peripheral repair given the biomechanical demand on the tibial fixation construct — non-weight-bearing or toe-touch weight-bearing for 4–6 weeks in a brace locked in extension or limited to 0–20/30° (axial load through flexion drives failure of pull-out fixation). Progressive weight-bearing weeks 6–8, full weight-bearing by 8–10 weeks.
- **ROM**: gradual progression from 0–90° over the first 6 weeks to full ROM by ~10–12 weeks; avoid deep flexion/pivoting until 4–5 months.
- Return to sport: generally 6–9 months, later than peripheral repair, reflecting slower root-tunnel healing biology and the higher consequence of failure.
