## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Nunley-Vertullo Stage II or III subtle (athletic) Lisfranc injuries — any diastasis ≥1 mm between the 1st and 2nd metatarsal bases on weight-bearing imaging, or loss of arch height.
- Any frank Myerson-pattern diastasis, instability, or fracture-dislocation of the tarsometatarsal complex, regardless of mechanism.
- Purely ligamentous injury confirmed on MRI/weight-bearing CT with a normal-appearing but unstable joint on exam or stress imaging.
- Open Lisfranc fracture-dislocation, or any pattern with skin tenting/vascular compromise requiring urgent reduction.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Nunley-Vertullo Stage I injuries and select truly nondisplaced (<2 mm) injuries confirmed on weight-bearing imaging — treat non-operatively (NWB cast/boot, progressive weight-bearing).
- Active soft-tissue infection or unresolved compartment syndrome — address emergently (fasciotomy) before definitive fixation; delay ORIF until the soft-tissue envelope allows.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease or uncontrolled diabetes — weigh operative risk carefully and consider a lower-profile, less-extensile construct or staged treatment.
- Longstanding, neglected Lisfranc injury with established degenerative change — primary arthrodesis is generally favored over ORIF in this setting since the articular surfaces are no longer salvageable.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: weight-bearing AP/oblique/lateral foot radiographs (non-weight-bearing films are notorious for missing subtle injury) — look for the "fleck sign," loss of alignment of the medial border of the 2nd metatarsal with the medial border of the middle cuneiform, and loss of arch height on the lateral view. CT defines bony detail/comminution and is essential for surgical planning; MRI or weight-bearing CT is used for suspected purely ligamentous injury with normal or equivocal weight-bearing radiographs.
- **Classification**: determine the Myerson pattern (A, B1, B2, C1, C2) to plan which rays require fixation and in which direction, and the Nunley-Vertullo stage for lower-energy/athletic injuries to guide the operative-vs-non-operative decision.
- **Implant selection**: have both a screw set (3.5/4.0 mm cortical/cannulated screws for the medial three TMT joints) and a low-profile dorsal bridge plating system available; K-wires (0.062"–0.078") for the mobile 4th–5th rays. Consider a arthrodesis instrument set (curettes, small osteotomes, joint preparation burrs) on standby for cases where intraoperative findings (unsalvageable articular surface, comminution) push toward primary fusion.
- **Equipment**: mini and large pointed reduction (Weber) clamps for direct joint reduction, a small distractor or external fixator for highly unstable/comminuted patterns, and a headlamp for deep dorsal exposure.
- **Case-specific planning**: image and plan to stress the entire midfoot intraoperatively, since Lisfranc injuries frequently co-occur with calcaneocuboid and cuboid injuries that would otherwise be missed. Counsel the patient preoperatively that intraoperative findings may shift the plan from ORIF to primary arthrodesis (or a hybrid), and obtain consent for both.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal anesthesia with a popliteal sciatic ± saphenous block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to internally rotate the limb for a straight dorsal approach to the midfoot; a small bump under the ankle/heel improves access and fluoroscopic visualization.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet inflated for the duration of the procedure to maintain a bloodless field for the meticulous dorsal dissection required.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm positioned to obtain AP, oblique, and lateral views of the midfoot without repositioning the leg; weight-bearing-equivalent stress views are simulated intraoperatively by applying manual load/stress across the TMT joints under fluoroscopy when diagnosis is equivocal.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire foot and ankle free, including the heel, to allow full visualization for stress testing and to access the 4th–5th ray incision if needed.
- **Landmarks**: mark the planned dorsal incisions before inflating the tourniquet — typically one longitudinal incision in the interval between the 1st and 2nd metatarsals (for the medial column and 1st–2nd TMT) and a second, more lateral longitudinal incision over the 4th metatarsal (for the 3rd–5th TMT joints), preserving an adequate skin bridge (generally ≥4–6 cm) between incisions to protect flap viability.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Position, prep, and inflate the tourniquet** as above.
2. **Make the medial dorsal incision** in the 1st–2nd metatarsal interval, protecting the dorsalis pedis artery and deep peroneal nerve, which cross obliquely in this interval — identify and retract them before deep dissection to the 1st–2nd TMT joints and the Lisfranc ligament origin/insertion.
3. **Débride the TMT joints of hematoma and interposed debris**, and confirm the Myerson displacement pattern directly.
4. **Begin the reduction at the medial column**: restore the relationship between the medial cuneiform and navicular first, since this establishes the platform for every subsequent reduction.
5. **Reduce the 2nd metatarsal base to the middle cuneiform** next — this is the anatomic "keystone" reduction and the technical crux of the case; use a pointed reduction (Weber) clamp placed from the medial cuneiform to the 2nd metatarsal base under direct visualization to close the diastasis, confirming restoration of the normal recessed "Roman arch" relationship on fluoroscopy.
6. **Sequence the remaining rays 1st, then 3rd, then 4th, then 5th**, reducing and provisionally pinning each with K-wires before committing to definitive fixation, since each ray's reduction depends on the ones already secured.
7. **Fix the 1st TMT joint**: place a screw from the 1st metatarsal base into the medial cuneiform (or, for a very unstable medial column, a small plate).
8. **Fix the Lisfranc interval (medial cuneiform to 2nd metatarsal base)**: place the "Lisfranc screw" from the medial aspect of the medial cuneiform across to the base of the 2nd metatarsal, and add a second screw from the 2nd metatarsal base into the middle cuneiform for added stability — or substitute a dorsal bridge plate spanning the 1st–2nd and/or 2nd–3rd TMT joints if the articular surface should be spared a transarticular screw.
9. **Fix the 3rd TMT joint** similarly with a transarticular screw or a bridge plate extension, depending on comminution and articular quality.
10. **Fix the 4th–5th rays (lateral column) with K-wires or a bridge plate only** through the second, more lateral incision — avoid rigid screw fixation of the lateral column, which tolerates and requires more physiologic motion; lateral column injuries are essentially never fused primarily.
11. **Reassess the entire midfoot under fluoroscopy and by manual stress** once provisional fixation is complete, confirming restoration of the medial and lateral column alignment, arch height, and no residual diastasis at any TMT interval before removing provisional wires and finalizing hardware.
12. **Consider primary arthrodesis of the medial three TMT joints instead of ORIF** when intraoperative findings reveal a purely ligamentous injury with no reconstructable articular surface, a comminuted/unreconstructable fracture-dislocation, or a delayed presentation with early degenerative change — prepare the joint surfaces (curette to bleeding subchondral bone, multiple drill holes or a burr for surface preparation), reduce anatomically, and compress with screws or a plate/screw hybrid construct across the fused joints.
13. **Irrigate, deflate the tourniquet, and confirm hemostasis** before closure; close in layers with attention to the skin bridge between incisions, and apply a well-padded splint with the foot in neutral position.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Reduce the medial cuneiform-to-2nd-metatarsal interval before anything else — it is the anatomic keystone, and every other ray's reduction becomes easier once this relationship is restored.
- Sequence fixation medial-to-lateral (1st, then 3rd, then 4th, then 5th, having already secured the 2nd) rather than fixing rays in numerical or displacement order — this stepwise approach uses each secured ray as a stable reference for the next.
- Use a pointed reduction (Weber) clamp placed directly across the medial cuneiform and 2nd metatarsal base for the key reduction — a purely percutaneous or indirect reduction of this interval is unreliable given the depth and obliquity of the joint.
- Choose dorsal bridge plating over transarticular screws when preserving native articular cartilage matters (young, high-demand patients where late fusion remains an option) — the plate avoids drilling through cartilage and generally requires a less aggressive removal timeline discussion.
- Protect the dorsalis pedis artery/deep peroneal nerve bundle early in the medial incision — it crosses obliquely between the 1st and 2nd metatarsal bases and is at risk during both exposure and clamp placement.
- Stress the entire midfoot manually under fluoroscopy after provisional fixation, not just the injured-appearing joints — occult calcaneocuboid or cuboid instability is easy to miss if you stop examining once the obvious Lisfranc interval looks reduced.
- Maintain a generous skin bridge (≥4–6 cm) between the medial and lateral dorsal incisions — this is a well-known determinant of wound-healing success in a region with a thin, tenuous dorsal soft-tissue envelope.
- When comminution or delayed presentation makes anatomic ORIF unlikely, do not force a joint-preserving construct — converting intraoperatively to primary arthrodesis of the medial three rays is a legitimate and often superior choice, not a failure of the ORIF plan.
- Counsel and consent patients preoperatively for the possibility of intraoperative conversion from ORIF to primary arthrodesis, since the decision is frequently made only once the articular surfaces are directly visualized.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Nonanatomic reduction (>2 mm residual displacement) is strongly associated with post-traumatic arthritis and poor functional scores, independent of the construct chosen.
- Rigid screw fixation of the lateral column risks breakage since that column requires physiologic motion — use K-wires or a bridge plate instead.
- The evidence comparing ORIF and primary arthrodesis is mixed: a 2024 RCT-only meta-analysis (5 RCTs, 241 patients) found no significant difference in patient-reported outcomes or complication rates, though primary arthrodesis trended toward fewer secondary procedures/hardware removals; a separate 2024 meta-analysis of 18 studies found better AOFAS and VAS scores with primary arthrodesis (AOFAS 84.4 vs 75.7); a large multicenter RCT found no difference in VAS-FA between arms. Practical synthesis: favor ORIF when joint surfaces are reducible/preservable (especially in younger, high-demand patients), favor primary arthrodesis when injury is purely ligamentous, comminuted, or presentation is delayed.
- Compartment syndrome of the foot is a classic association with high-energy Lisfranc injury — maintain a low threshold to measure compartment pressures, especially with tense midfoot swelling and pain out of proportion to exam findings.
- Missed calcaneocuboid or cuboid injury (from stopping the intraoperative exam once the medial Lisfranc interval is addressed) leaves a source of persistent instability/pain — always stress and image the lateral column as well.
- Inadequate skin bridge between the medial and lateral dorsal incisions risks wound-edge necrosis in this thin dorsal soft-tissue region — plan incision placement before the tourniquet goes up, not after.
- Injury to the dorsalis pedis artery/deep peroneal nerve during medial exposure or clamp placement is avoidable with deliberate early identification and retraction.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–6 weeks**: NWB, splint/cast, elevation.
- **6–10 weeks**: transition to boot, begin protected/progressive weight-bearing guided by fixation construct stability and radiographic maintenance of reduction; bridge-plated/K-wire lateral column fixation generally allows earlier progressive WB than transarticular screws across weight-bearing medial rays.
- **10–12 weeks**: full weight-bearing, gait retraining, arch-support orthotic consideration.
- **3–6 months**: hardware removal (if transarticular screws used) typically performed at ~4–6 months before full return to high-demand activity, since retained screws across a mobile joint risk breakage under load.
- **Return to sport** for athletes: typically 6–9 months, later for high-demand cutting/jumping sports; counsel patients that post-traumatic arthritis is common even with anatomic reduction and good early function. Sedentary work 6–10 weeks, manual labor 4–6 months.
