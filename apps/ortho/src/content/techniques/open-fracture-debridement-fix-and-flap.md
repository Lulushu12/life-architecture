## Indications

Any open fracture requiring formal surgical debridement and definitive skeletal fixation with soft-tissue coverage, from Gustilo I through IIIC. The sequencing and antibiotic strategy below scale with the intra-operative grade; IIIB/IIIC injuries in particular require coordinated orthoplastic (combined orthopedic + plastic surgery) planning.

## Positioning & setup

Antibiotics are started as early as possible, ideally in the ED before any operative positioning. Formal debridement, irrigation, and fixation are performed in theatre — the emergency department is not the place for repeated wound inspection, probing, or irrigation; the wound is photographed once, gross debris removed, and the limb covered with saline-soaked gauze and splinted.

## Steps

1. **Antibiotics**: administer as soon as possible after injury/on ED arrival, target ≤ 1 hour. First-generation cephalosporin (e.g., cefazolin) for Gustilo I/II; add Gram-negative coverage (aminoglycoside, or a broader beta-lactam per local protocol) for type III; add high-dose penicillin (or metronidazole) for farm/soil/aquatic/fecal contamination to cover Clostridium. Stop 24 h after wound closure for I/II; do not exceed 24 h post-injury/post-each-procedure for type III, and never continue beyond 72 h total absent documented infection.
2. **ED handling**: document neurovascular status before and after any manipulation, photograph the wound once, remove only gross debris, cover with saline-soaked dressing, splint. Do not repeatedly irrigate or probe the wound in the ED — that step is done once, definitively, in theatre.
3. **Formal debridement**: systematic, layer-by-layer excision of all non-viable tissue in theatre, within 24 hours of injury (not a rigid "6-hour rule"); heavily contaminated wounds (agricultural, aquatic, sewage) should still go emergently. Irrigate with low-pressure, high-volume normal saline — high-pressure pulsatile lavage showed no benefit and possible harm (FLOW trial).
4. **Skeletal stabilization**: provisional external fixation (spanning or fracture-specific) for damage control in contaminated/complex wounds or the physiologically unstable patient. Definitive fixation (IM nailing, plating) can be performed acutely at the same setting in Gustilo I–IIIA wounds if adequately debrided and the patient is stable; in IIIB/IIIC, coordinate skeletal fixation timing with the soft-tissue reconstruction plan — IM nailing prior to flap coverage does not appear to increase complication rates compared with nailing after flap coverage.
5. **Fix-and-flap vs staged fix-then-flap**: single-stage "fix and flap" (definitive fixation and free/local flap coverage in the same anesthetic, ideally within 72 h) at a combined orthoplastic centre reduces infection, nonunion, and number of procedures compared with staged approaches. When a specialist combined unit isn't immediately available, staged fix-then-flap with early transfer to a Major Trauma Centre within 72 h — and coverage completed by day 7 — is the accepted fallback.
6. **Vascular injury (IIIC)**: place a temporary shunt to restore perfusion first if skeletal stabilization would otherwise prolong ischemia beyond ~6 h; perform definitive vascular repair after or with skeletal stabilization depending on shunt use. Have an early, honest discussion of primary amputation for a mangled extremity with combined severe nerve, vascular, bone, and soft-tissue injury.
7. **Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT)**: acceptable as a temporizing dressing between debridement and definitive coverage, but is not a substitute for timely debridement/coverage and should not be used to justify delaying flap coverage beyond the target window.

## Pitfalls

- Treating skin wound size as a proxy for injury severity rather than formally grading intra-operatively after debridement.
- Repeatedly removing ED dressings to "look at" an open wound — this increases contamination and pain without changing management; photograph once, cover, splint, move to theatre.
- Applying a rigid "6-hour rule" for debridement — current evidence supports a 24-hour window, with faster action only for heavily contaminated wounds; do not assume any wound outside 6 hours is automatically high-risk, nor rush an unprepared team to beat an arbitrary clock.
- Delaying flap coverage beyond 7 days significantly raises deep infection risk regardless of strategy — do not let NPWT or staged planning drift past this window.
- Continuing "just in case" antibiotics beyond the 72-hour ceiling increases resistant-organism selection without reducing infection rate.
- Factors favoring primary amputation over prolonged salvage include warm ischemia time > 6 h, combined tibial nerve transection (anticipated insensate plantar foot), extensive multi-compartment crush/muscle loss, and patient factors making prolonged reconstruction unsafe — functional outcomes between successful salvage and amputation with modern prosthetics were broadly comparable at 2 years in the LEAP cohort, supporting shared decision-making over reflexive salvage-at-all-costs.

## Post-op protocol

**Open fracture (lower limb, post fix-and-flap)**
- Weeks 0–2: flap monitoring (Doppler/clinical), strict limb elevation, non-weight-bearing, ankle/foot pumps and passive ROM of uninvolved joints to prevent stiffness/DVT; dangling protocol for free flaps typically starts around day 5–7 per plastics team.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive mobilization out of bed, touch-down to partial weight-bearing per fixation stability and fracture pattern (nailed diaphyseal fractures often allowed partial weight-bearing earlier than plated metaphyseal/articular fractures), physiotherapy for joint ROM, edema control, scar/graft site desensitization.
- Weeks 6–12: progression to full weight-bearing guided by radiographic callus and clinical exam (no pain/tenderness at fracture site); strengthening, gait re-training, proprioception work.
- Months 3–6+: sedentary work often feasible by 6–8 weeks once pain-controlled and mobile; manual labor/heavy lifting typically deferred until radiographic union confirmed and functional strength ≥ 80–90% of contralateral limb, often 4–6 months for IIIB injuries; return to impact sport individualized, generally not before 6–9 months with full radiographic union.
