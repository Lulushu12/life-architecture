## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Any open fracture requiring formal surgical debridement and definitive skeletal fixation with soft-tissue coverage, from Gustilo I through IIIC. The sequencing and antibiotic strategy scale with the intra-operative grade; IIIB/IIIC injuries in particular require coordinated orthoplastic (combined orthopedic + plastic surgery) planning from the outset.
- Gustilo IIIC injuries with a repairable vascular injury and a viable distal limb — vascular restoration and skeletal stabilization proceed together as a life/limb-salvage sequence rather than as separate, sequential decisions.
- Heavily contaminated wounds (agricultural, aquatic, sewage, fecal) regardless of Gustilo grade — these mandate emergent debridement rather than waiting within the standard 24-hour window.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- A mangled extremity with combined warm ischemia time > 6 hours, complete tibial nerve transection (anticipated insensate plantar foot), extensive multi-compartment muscle loss, and a patient physiologically unable to tolerate prolonged reconstructive surgery — primary amputation should be discussed honestly as a genuine option, not pursued only after a failed salvage attempt.
- Physiologic instability from concurrent life-threatening injuries — in the polytrauma patient, definitive fix-and-flap is deferred in favor of damage-control external fixation and would-management until the patient is resuscitated (see damage-control-orthopaedics-polytrauma-staging).
- A soft-tissue envelope or vascular bed too compromised to support flap survival at the planned site — this changes the reconstructive plan (alternative flap choice, delayed coverage after further demarcation) rather than being an absolute barrier to eventual coverage.
- Active, uncontrolled systemic sepsis unrelated to the limb — source control elsewhere takes priority before committing to a prolonged combined orthoplastic procedure.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: orthogonal plain radiographs including the joint above and below; CT angiography for any asymmetric pulses or ABI < 0.9 without hard signs of vascular injury; whole-body CT in the polytrauma patient to confirm no higher-priority injury changes sequencing.
- **Wound and zone-of-injury assessment**: a single ED photograph (per BOAST guidance) reviewed preoperatively substitutes for repeated dressing removal; plan the debridement extent conceptually before scrubbing in, anticipating that the true zone of injury (periosteal stripping, muscle devitalization) usually extends well beyond the visible skin wound.
- **Implant and fixation planning**: select IM nail versus plate versus external fixator based on fracture pattern, contamination burden, and soft-tissue plan — template canal diameter/nail length for nailing, or plate length/working length for plating; have a spanning external fixator set available as a fallback for any case where debridement reveals more extensive contamination or soft-tissue loss than anticipated.
- **Orthoplastic coordination**: for anticipated IIIB/IIIC injuries, involve the plastic surgery/microsurgical team in preoperative planning, not as an intraoperative consult — confirm flap choice (local fasciocutaneous/muscle flap vs free flap), recipient vessel planning, and whether a combined single-anesthetic fix-and-flap is logistically achievable at this centre or whether staged transfer to a combined unit is the plan.
- **Vascular planning for IIIC**: confirm vascular surgery/microsurgical availability and a shunt set is in the room before starting if there is any arterial injury; plan the sequence (shunt → skeletal stabilization → definitive vascular repair, or definitive repair first) based on anticipated ischemia time and the complexity of the planned skeletal fixation.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: debridement set (pulse or bulb irrigation for low-pressure high-volume lavage, not high-pressure pulsatile lavage), culture-collection supplies, the selected fixation implant system, temporary vascular shunt set (sized appropriately for the injured vessel) if a vascular injury is anticipated, and NPWT dressing supplies for temporizing between debridement and coverage.
- **Antibiotic plan**: confirm timing and choice before the patient reaches theatre — this should already be underway from the ED, not started at induction.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard for the index debridement, particularly when the full extent of injury and the eventual need for vascular repair or flap surgery is not yet certain; regional/neuraxial blocks are added for postoperative analgesia once compartment syndrome surveillance is no longer a concern, but a dense block placed before debridement can mask early compartment syndrome in an already high-risk limb and should be used cautiously in that window.
- **Positioning**: supine for most lower-limb open fractures, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip for tibial injuries to neutralize rotation; position to allow simultaneous or immediately sequential access for a flap harvest site (thigh for anterolateral thigh flap, latissimus dorsi region, or other planned donor site) without a full re-prep and re-drape between fixation and coverage.
- **Tourniquet**: a thigh tourniquet may be used for the debridement/fixation portion but should be released before assessing final muscle viability and before flap inset, since tourniquet-induced pallor can mask genuinely nonviable tissue and true perfusion must be confirmed before closure; in IIIC injuries with an arterial repair or shunt in place, tourniquet use is typically avoided altogether once perfusion has been restored.
- **Table/imaging setup**: image intensifier positioned and confirmed for orthogonal views of the fracture before draping; if a combined vascular/microsurgical procedure is anticipated, coordinate table and equipment layout with those teams in advance rather than improvising mid-case.
- **Draping**: wide, generous prep and drape — circumferential limb prep is preferred so the limb can be freely manipulated for fixation and, if needed, flap harvest and inset in the same field; include the planned flap donor site in the same sterile field for a genuine single-anesthetic fix-and-flap.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: mark the wound margins, the planned debridement extent (a margin around the visible zone of injury), fixation approach landmarks, and — if a flap is planned in the same setting — donor-site landmarks and recipient vessel locations.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm antibiotics have already been given**: first-generation cephalosporin (e.g., cefazolin) for Gustilo I/II; add Gram-negative coverage (aminoglycoside, or a broader beta-lactam per local protocol) for type III; add high-dose penicillin (or metronidazole) for farm/soil/aquatic/fecal contamination to cover Clostridium — administered as soon as possible after injury, target ≤ 1 hour, not delayed to the start of this case.
2. **Re-confirm neurovascular status** under anesthesia before manipulation, documented against the pre-manipulation ED exam.
3. **Extend the wound as needed** along the limb's long axis (not transversely) to obtain full visualization of the zone of injury — an inadequate exposure is a common reason for incomplete debridement, since the visible skin wound rarely represents the true extent of deep injury.
4. **Perform systematic, layer-by-layer debridement**: skin (excise only frankly nonviable margins, preserving all viable skin since it will be needed for later closure or flap inset), subcutaneous fat (typically the least vascular layer and often the most extensively injured — debride generously here), fascia, muscle (assess by the "four C's" — color, consistency, contractility to stimulation, capacity to bleed — and excise anything failing this assessment), and bone (remove only bone fragments completely devascularized and unattached to any soft-tissue sleeve; preserve articular fragments and any diaphyseal fragment with a viable soft-tissue attachment even if comminuted, since these can still contribute to healing and reconstruction).
5. **Irrigate with low-pressure, high-volume normal saline** (the FLOW trial found no benefit and possible harm from high-pressure pulsatile lavage or additives) — a practical volume target scales with Gustilo grade (roughly 3 L for type I, 6 L for type II, 9 L for type III, using bulb or gravity/low-pressure delivery rather than a pulsatile jet lavage device).
6. **Send deep tissue cultures** from the debrided wound bed before or independent of antibiotic re-dosing, particularly in heavily contaminated or delayed-presentation wounds, to guide any subsequent antibiotic adjustment.
7. **Reassess the wound after debridement and formally grade it** (Gustilo-Anderson, and/or OTA-OFC component scoring) — this intraoperative grading, not the ED impression, is what should drive antibiotic duration and reconstructive planning from this point forward.
8. **Stabilize the skeleton**: apply provisional external fixation for damage control if the wound remains contaminated, the soft-tissue plan is not yet finalized, or the patient is physiologically unstable; proceed to definitive fixation (IM nailing or plating) in the same setting for Gustilo I–IIIA wounds that are adequately debrided and in a stable patient. For IIIB/IIIC wounds, sequence skeletal fixation with the soft-tissue reconstruction plan — IM nailing before flap coverage does not appear to increase complication rates compared with nailing after flap coverage in recent retrospective data, which gives real flexibility when a fully simultaneous combined procedure is not logistically achievable.
9. **For a vascular injury (IIIC)**: if skeletal stabilization would otherwise prolong ischemia beyond roughly 6 hours, place a temporary intraluminal shunt first to restore perfusion (typically achievable within about 30 minutes of decision to shunt), then proceed with debridement completion and skeletal stabilization with the limb perfused, followed by definitive vascular repair (primary repair or interposition graft) once the skeleton is stable — shunting before definitive fixation measurably reduces total ischemia time and the incidence of postoperative compartment syndrome compared with repairing the vessel before stabilizing an unstable skeleton.
10. **Reassess compartments before closure** in any limb with a prolonged ischemia period, high-energy crush mechanism, or extensive soft-tissue swelling — have a low threshold to proceed to four-compartment fasciotomy rather than closing over a limb at risk (see four-compartment-fasciotomy-leg).
11. **Achieve soft-tissue coverage**: for wounds closable without undue tension, primary closure or local tissue rearrangement; for IIIB/IIIC wounds with exposed bone/hardware, proceed to local flap (e.g., gastrocnemius, soleus) or free flap (e.g., anterolateral thigh, latissimus dorsi) coverage in the same anesthetic ("fix and flap") whenever a combined orthoplastic team and appropriate recipient vessels are available — this single-stage approach, completed ideally within 72 hours, reduces infection, nonunion, and total procedure count compared with staged approaches.
12. **If a combined single-stage procedure is not achievable**, close what can safely be closed, dress the remaining wound (NPWT is an acceptable temporizing dressing, not a substitute for timely coverage), and arrange transfer to a Major Trauma Centre/combined orthoplastic unit within 72 hours, with the explicit goal of completing definitive coverage by day 7 — deep infection risk rises sharply beyond that window regardless of strategy.
13. **Inset the flap** (if performed) with meticulous pedicle geometry (avoid kinking, twisting, or tension on the vascular pedicle), confirm flap perfusion (color, capillary refill, Doppler signal on the pedicle and within the flap) before final skin closure, and plan the postoperative flap-monitoring protocol with the plastic surgery team before leaving theatre.
14. **Close in layers** where primary closure is appropriate, leaving drains as indicated; document final Gustilo/OTA-OFC grade, debridement extent, culture status, fixation construct, and coverage plan clearly in the operative note to guide the next team member who sees this patient.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Extend the traumatic wound generously along the limb's axis before starting debridement rather than working through a small window — under-exposure is one of the most common reasons for incomplete debridement of deep muscle and bone.
- Debride subcutaneous fat more aggressively than skin — it is a relatively avascular layer that is both frequently more injured than it looks and a common site of missed nonviable tissue left behind at the first debridement.
- Preserve any bone fragment, however small, that retains a soft-tissue (periosteal or muscular) attachment — these fragments often remain viable and can be useful for later reconstruction, whereas a "clean sweep" of all loose-looking fragments can needlessly enlarge the eventual bone defect.
- Use the "four C's" (color, consistency, contractility, capacity to bleed) systematically on every muscle bundle in the field rather than a gestalt impression — this is especially important after tourniquet use, since tourniquet-induced pallor can make viable muscle look falsely ischemic.
- Send deep cultures before heavy re-irrigation or antibiotic bead/cement placement distorts the local bacterial picture — cultures taken after extensive antibiotic-loaded material is already in the wound are less reliable for guiding later treatment.
- When a temporary vascular shunt is used in a IIIC injury, secure it well and document dwell time explicitly — most published shunt series report insertion within about 30 minutes of the decision to shunt and total dwell times measured in a few hours, which is the practical window to plan skeletal work around.
- Plan pedicle geometry for a free flap before, not during, inset — a kinked or twisted pedicle discovered after inset requires re-elevation and is far more easily avoided by deliberate planning of pedicle length and orientation up front.
- Keep a shared, explicit timeline (from injury, not from admission) visible to the whole team — antibiotic timing, debridement timing, and coverage timing targets are all referenced to time from injury, and it is easy to lose track of this once a patient has been through several handoffs.
- When a fully combined single-stage fix-and-flap isn't achievable locally, commit early to the transfer decision (within the first 24–48 hours) rather than attempting a staged local solution that drifts past the 7-day coverage window — early transfer to a combined unit is explicitly preferred over a delayed local attempt.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Treating skin wound size as a proxy for injury severity rather than formally grading intra-operatively after debridement.
- Repeatedly removing ED dressings to "look at" an open wound — this increases contamination and pain without changing management; photograph once, cover, splint, move to theatre.
- Applying a rigid "6-hour rule" for debridement — current evidence supports a 24-hour window, with faster action only for heavily contaminated wounds; do not assume any wound outside 6 hours is automatically high-risk, nor rush an unprepared team to beat an arbitrary clock.
- Under-exposing the wound and thereby under-debriding deep muscle, fascia, or bone that lies beyond the visible skin defect — extend the incision rather than working through a limited window.
- Using high-pressure pulsatile lavage or additive solutions — the FLOW trial found no benefit and possible harm compared with low-pressure, high-volume normal saline irrigation.
- Delaying flap coverage beyond 7 days significantly raises deep infection risk regardless of strategy — do not let NPWT or staged planning drift past this window.
- Continuing "just in case" antibiotics beyond the 72-hour ceiling increases resistant-organism selection without reducing infection rate.
- Closing over a limb at risk of compartment syndrome without reassessing compartments first — a prolonged ischemia period, high-energy crush, or marked swelling should prompt a low threshold for fasciotomy rather than tight primary closure.
- Kinking, twisting, or placing tension on a free-flap pedicle at inset — meticulous pedicle geometry and confirmed perfusion (color, capillary refill, Doppler signal) before final closure prevent a return trip to theatre for flap compromise.
- Delaying temporary vascular shunt placement in a IIIC injury while skeletal work proceeds first — this needlessly prolongs ischemia time; shunt first if skeletal stabilization will otherwise exceed roughly 6 hours of ischemia, then stabilize the skeleton with the limb perfused.
- Factors favoring primary amputation over prolonged salvage include warm ischemia time > 6 h, combined tibial nerve transection (anticipated insensate plantar foot), extensive multi-compartment crush/muscle loss, and patient factors making prolonged reconstruction unsafe — functional outcomes between successful salvage and amputation with modern prosthetics were broadly comparable at 2 years in the LEAP cohort, supporting shared decision-making over reflexive salvage-at-all-costs.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Open fracture (lower limb, post fix-and-flap)**
- Weeks 0–2: flap monitoring (Doppler/clinical), strict limb elevation, non-weight-bearing, ankle/foot pumps and passive ROM of uninvolved joints to prevent stiffness/DVT; dangling protocol for free flaps typically starts around day 5–7 per plastics team.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive mobilization out of bed, touch-down to partial weight-bearing per fixation stability and fracture pattern (nailed diaphyseal fractures often allowed partial weight-bearing earlier than plated metaphyseal/articular fractures), physiotherapy for joint ROM, edema control, scar/graft site desensitization.
- Weeks 6–12: progression to full weight-bearing guided by radiographic callus and clinical exam (no pain/tenderness at fracture site); strengthening, gait re-training, proprioception work.
- Months 3–6+: sedentary work often feasible by 6–8 weeks once pain-controlled and mobile; manual labor/heavy lifting typically deferred until radiographic union confirmed and functional strength ≥ 80–90% of contralateral limb, often 4–6 months for IIIB injuries; return to impact sport individualized, generally not before 6–9 months with full radiographic union.
