## Indications

Surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach) with trochanteric flip osteotomy is used for larger or weight-bearing-surface (Pipkin II) femoral head fragments requiring anatomic ORIF, and is preferred over an isolated posterior (Kocher-Langenbeck) approach for anterior/superior head fragments because it preserves the medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA) and provides 360° visualization of the femoral head and acetabulum. It is also used for displaced/non-congruent Pipkin I fragments requiring excision or ORIF. Combined injuries (Pipkin III with femoral neck fracture, Pipkin IV with acetabular fracture) may instead favor a Kocher-Langenbeck or combined approach when the acetabular component predominates, or primary arthroplasty in older Pipkin III patients.

## Positioning & setup

Emergent closed reduction of the hip dislocation must occur first (within 6 h to minimize AVN risk), followed by post-reduction CT to characterize fragment size/location and detect intra-articular loose bodies. Definitive surgical positioning and approach selection then depend on fragment location and any combined injury pattern.

## Steps

1. **Confirm emergent closed reduction has been performed** (within 6 h of injury) and review post-reduction CT for fragment size, location, and joint congruency.
2. **Select approach based on fragment location and combined injury:**
   - Pipkin I (small, non-weight-bearing, inferior to fovea) with no mechanical block: excise or observe if joint is stable and congruent.
   - Pipkin II (weight-bearing dome fragment) or displaced Pipkin I requiring ORIF: proceed with surgical hip dislocation.
   - Pipkin IV (with acetabular fracture): approach selection follows the acetabular component — often posterior/Kocher-Langenbeck when acetabular injury predominates, or a combined/surgical dislocation approach.
3. **Perform the trochanteric flip osteotomy** as part of the Ganz surgical hip dislocation, preserving the MFCA and providing full visualization of the femoral head and acetabulum.
4. **Reduce the femoral head fragment anatomically** and fix with small fragment or headless compression screws, countersunk below the articular cartilage.
5. **For combined Pipkin III (with femoral neck fracture):** prioritize femoral neck fixation urgency in young patients (or plan arthroplasty if elderly); treat as a combined injury rather than sequentially independent problems. In older patients with a Pipkin III pattern, primary arthroplasty is a reasonable alternative to attempting fixation of both the head and neck components given AVN rates reported up to 50%+ in some series.
6. **Repair the trochanteric osteotomy** at closure; this site requires its own union-specific precautions post-operatively.

## Pitfalls

- Delayed reduction of the dislocation is the dominant driver of AVN risk — do not delay emergent reduction to await imaging or surgical planning.
- An isolated posterior (Kocher-Langenbeck) approach risks further disrupting an already-compromised blood supply in anterior/superior head fragments; the surgical hip dislocation approach avoids this by preserving the MFCA.
- Heterotopic ossification is a recognized complication, particularly after surgical hip dislocation or extensile posterior approaches — perioperative NSAID or single-fraction radiotherapy prophylaxis should be considered in high-risk cases.
- Sciatic nerve palsy must be assessed pre- and post-reduction; most peroneal-division injuries have a guarded but non-zero recovery potential over 12–18 months.
- Recurrent instability can result if the acetabular/capsular injury (Pipkin IV) is not adequately addressed alongside the femoral head component.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is very common by 5–10 years given the inherent articular injury, independent of AVN.

## Post-op protocol

- Protected/touch-down weight-bearing for 6–8 weeks given the articular fixation, then progressive loading to full weight-bearing by 10–12 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing.
- Hip precautions per the surgical approach used; trochanteric flip osteotomy patients also need trochanteric-osteotomy-specific abductor precautions until osteotomy union, typically 6–8 weeks.
- Radiographic milestones at 2, 6, and 12 weeks; union confirmed by trabecular consolidation typically by 12–16 weeks.
- Standard fragility/trauma physiotherapy progression applies where relevant: early bed mobility/transfers, gait training with assistive device, progressive resistive exercise, and abductor/extensor strengthening from week 6–12.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks once pain-controlled; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing. High-demand recreational activity/sport (relevant mainly to younger patients) is generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.
