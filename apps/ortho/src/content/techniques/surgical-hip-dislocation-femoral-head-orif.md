## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach) with trochanteric flip osteotomy is used for larger or weight-bearing-surface (Pipkin II) femoral head fragments requiring anatomic ORIF, and is preferred over an isolated posterior (Kocher-Langenbeck) approach for anterior/superior head fragments because it preserves the medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA) and provides 360° visualization of the femoral head and acetabulum. It is also used for displaced/non-congruent Pipkin I fragments requiring excision or ORIF. Combined injuries (Pipkin III with femoral neck fracture, Pipkin IV with acetabular fracture) may instead favor a Kocher-Langenbeck or combined approach when the acetabular component predominates, or primary arthroplasty in older Pipkin III patients.

**Contraindications/relative contraindications:**

- Active local or systemic infection at the planned surgical field — treat the septic source first.
- Elderly, low-demand patients with a Pipkin III pattern (femoral head fracture plus femoral neck fracture) — primary arthroplasty is a reasonable alternative to attempting fixation of both components given AVN rates reported up to 50%+ in some series.
- Very small, non-weight-bearing Pipkin I fragments with a stable, congruent joint — these can be observed or excised through a less extensile approach rather than requiring full surgical hip dislocation.
- Delayed presentation with established femoral head AVN or advanced chondral injury — reconstruction is unlikely to be worthwhile and arthroplasty should be considered instead.
- Predominant acetabular component (Pipkin IV where the acetabular fracture is the primary structural problem) — a Kocher-Langenbeck or combined approach targeting the acetabular fracture is often more appropriate than surgical hip dislocation alone.
- Medical instability precluding a longer, higher-blood-loss procedure — surgical hip dislocation is a bigger operation than an isolated posterior approach and requires the patient tolerate a correspondingly longer anesthetic.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Emergent closed reduction of the hip dislocation must occur first** (within 6 h of injury to minimize AVN risk) — this occurs in the emergency department or immediately on presentation and precedes definitive surgical planning; document a full neurovascular exam, specifically sciatic nerve function, before and immediately after reduction.
- **Post-reduction CT** (fine cuts through the hip) is mandatory to characterize fragment size, location (relative to the fovea), displacement, and to detect intra-articular loose bodies or marginal impaction not appreciated on plain films.
- **Approach planning:** confirm fragment location on CT (anterior/superior fragments favor surgical hip dislocation; predominantly posterior/inferior fragments with an associated posterior wall acetabular injury may favor Kocher-Langenbeck) and screen specifically for a combined femoral neck fracture (Pipkin III) or acetabular fracture (Pipkin IV) that changes the operative plan and urgency calculus.
- **Implant selection:** small-fragment or headless compression screws sized for countersinking below the articular cartilage; have both available since fragment size and bone quality often dictate the final choice intraoperatively.
- **Equipment:** oscillating saw and osteotomes for the trochanteric flip osteotomy, a full set of retractors for circumferential femoral head/acetabular exposure, small/mini-fragment fixation set, and fixation (typically two or more 3.5 mm cortical/cancellous screws) for trochanteric osteotomy repair at closure.
- **Adjunct planning:** discuss perioperative heterotopic ossification prophylaxis (NSAID course or single-fraction radiotherapy) for high-risk patients given reported rates up to 60% after this approach.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia is standard given the length and extensile nature of this procedure; regional block as an analgesic adjunct per anesthesia/surgeon preference.
- **Positioning:** lateral decubitus position, the pelvis firmly secured with anterior and posterior supports (peg-board or bean-bag) to allow safe intraoperative hip flexion/rotation without pelvic movement; the operative leg draped free from the iliac crest to below the knee to permit the full range of motion needed for dislocation and circumferential head/acetabular inspection.
- **Fluoroscopy:** available for intraoperative confirmation of screw position/length and to confirm no intra-articular hardware penetration; positioned to allow both AP and oblique/lateral imaging of the hip without repositioning the patient.
- **Draping and landmarks:** mark the greater trochanter and the planned incision, typically a slightly curved (Gibson-type) incision centered over the greater trochanter, extending proximally toward the posterior superior iliac spine and distally along the femoral shaft axis; drape widely enough to extend distally if additional femoral exposure is needed.
- **Tourniquet:** not used (pelvis/proximal-femur-adjacent procedure).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm emergent closed reduction has already been performed** (within 6 h of injury) and review the post-reduction CT in the room for fragment size, location, and joint congruency before incision.
2. **Make the skin incision** and develop the interval through the fascia lata/gluteus maximus, identifying and protecting the sciatic nerve throughout the exposure — keep the hip flexed and the knee flexed (relaxing the sciatic nerve) during retraction of the posterior structures.
3. **Identify the interval between gluteus medius and minimus anteriorly and the short external rotators posteriorly**, and expose the anterior femur at the level of the vastus lateralis ridge — palpate and protect the piriformis tendon, which marks the posterior extent of safe anterior dissection and the location of the deep branch of the MFCA as it courses toward the piriformis fossa.
4. **Perform the trochanteric flip osteotomy.** Osteotomize the greater trochanter in the coronal plane with the vastus lateralis and gluteus medius/minimus insertions kept attached to the fragment (a "digastric" flip that preserves both muscle attachments), typically 1–1.5 cm thick, extending from just proximal to the vastus ridge to the piriformis fossa posteriorly — cutting from anterior to posterior with an oscillating saw and completing the posterior-most portion with an osteotome to protect the deep MFCA branch running just deep to the piriformis tendon at the posterior extent of the cut.
5. **Mobilize the osteotomized fragment anteriorly** with the attached abductors and vastus lateralis, exposing the underlying hip capsule without detaching the short external rotators from the femur — this is the key vascular-protective step distinguishing this approach from a standard posterior (Kocher-Langenbeck) exposure, since the MFCA's deep branch runs within the tissue kept attached to (not detached from) the femur.
6. **Perform an anterior Z-shaped (or curved) capsulotomy**, preserving the posterior capsule and short external rotators intact to protect the retinacular vessels — incorporate any capsular tear from the original dislocation event into the planned capsulotomy rather than creating a separate, additional capsular injury.
7. **Dislocate the femoral head anteriorly** (with the leg positioned in flexion, external rotation, and adduction) to gain full circumferential access to the head and acetabulum — confirm at this point that the ligamentum teres, if intact, is either preserved or sharply released as needed for exposure without avulsing MFCA branches at the base of the femoral neck.
8. **Inspect the joint circumferentially**, removing any loose osteochondral fragments and assessing the acetabulum for chondral injury or an associated wall fracture not previously appreciated.
9. **Reduce the femoral head fragment anatomically** under direct vision, using provisional K-wire fixation to hold reduction before definitive fixation.
10. **Fix the fragment with small fragment or headless compression screws**, placed from a direction that allows countersinking the screw heads below the articular cartilage surface (typically inserted from a peripheral, non-weight-bearing entry point and directed to capture and compress the fragment, or antegrade through the fragment with headless screws if fragment size/orientation allows).
11. **Confirm fixation and joint congruency** with direct visualization and fluoroscopy, checking for any intra-articular hardware prominence through a full range of motion before reduction.
12. **Reduce the hip** and confirm stability through a full range of motion.
13. **For combined Pipkin III (with femoral neck fracture):** address the femoral neck fracture with its own urgency and fixation principles (see the femoral neck fixation/arthroplasty technique guides) — prioritize femoral neck fixation urgency in young patients, or plan arthroplasty if elderly, treating this as a combined injury rather than sequentially independent problems; in older patients with a Pipkin III pattern, consider primary arthroplasty as an alternative to attempting fixation of both the head and neck components given AVN rates reported up to 50%+ in some series.
14. **Repair the trochanteric osteotomy**, typically with two or more parallel 3.5 mm cortical or cancellous screws, confirming compression across the osteotomy site and restoring the trochanteric height/offset; this site requires its own union-specific postoperative precautions.
15. **Repair the capsule** as completely as feasible, and reattach any tagged short external rotators/capsular tissue that was mobilized during exposure.
16. **Irrigate and close in layers** over a drain per surgeon preference, given the extensive dissection and expected blood loss of this approach.
17. **Obtain final intraoperative or immediate postoperative imaging** confirming joint congruency, screw position, and trochanteric osteotomy fixation before the patient leaves the table.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Keep the hip and knee flexed during posterior retraction to relax tension on the sciatic nerve throughout the exposure — this single positioning detail materially reduces traction-related nerve risk during a long case.
- Cut the posterior-most portion of the trochanteric osteotomy with an osteotome rather than the saw, working from known bone toward the piriformis fossa — this protects the deep MFCA branch, which runs just deep to the piriformis tendon at exactly this location.
- Keep the digastric (dual-muscle-attached) character of the osteotomized fragment intact — both the vastus lateralis and gluteus medius/minimus should remain attached to the flipped trochanteric fragment, since this is what keeps the MFCA-bearing tissue undisturbed during mobilization.
- Incorporate any pre-existing capsular tear from the original traumatic dislocation into your planned capsulotomy rather than creating an additional separate rent — this both saves tissue for repair and avoids further destabilizing an already-compromised capsule.
- Use the anterior dislocation position (flexion, external rotation, adduction) deliberately — it is what provides the 360° circumferential access that is this approach's main advantage over an isolated posterior exposure.
- Fix the osteotomy with genuine interfragmentary compression (not just position screws) — trochanteric nonunion is a recognized complication and compressive fixation technique meaningfully reduces this risk.
- Consider perioperative heterotopic ossification prophylaxis (single-dose NSAID course, or single-fraction radiotherapy in higher-risk patients) proactively rather than reactively — rates up to 60% have been reported after this approach, and prophylaxis is far easier to deliver before it develops than to treat afterward.
- Reassess the joint through a full range of motion after fixation and before final capsular closure, specifically checking for any residual screw prominence against the acetabular cartilage — this is easiest to correct before the capsule is repaired and the head reduced.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Delayed reduction of the dislocation is the dominant driver of AVN risk — do not delay emergent reduction to await imaging or surgical planning.
- An isolated posterior (Kocher-Langenbeck) approach risks further disrupting an already-compromised blood supply in anterior/superior head fragments; the surgical hip dislocation approach avoids this by preserving the MFCA, provided the digastric trochanteric fragment and posterior soft tissues are handled as described above.
- Heterotopic ossification is a recognized complication, particularly after surgical hip dislocation or extensile posterior approaches, reported in up to 60% of patients in some series — perioperative NSAID or single-fraction radiotherapy prophylaxis should be considered in high-risk cases.
- Sciatic nerve palsy must be assessed pre- and post-reduction; most peroneal-division injuries have a guarded but non-zero recovery potential over 12–18 months.
- Recurrent instability can result if the acetabular/capsular injury (Pipkin IV) is not adequately addressed alongside the femoral head component.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is very common by 5–10 years given the inherent articular injury, independent of AVN.
- Trochanteric osteotomy nonunion and lateral thigh pain are approach-specific complications distinct from the femoral head fracture itself — secure, compressive osteotomy fixation and adherence to trochanteric-specific postoperative precautions reduce this risk.
- Injury to the deep branch of the MFCA at the posterior extent of the trochanteric osteotomy (near the piriformis fossa) can occur if the osteotomy is completed with the saw rather than an osteotome in this zone, or if the digastric muscle attachments are inadvertently released — this defeats the vascular-protective rationale for choosing this approach in the first place.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Protected/touch-down weight-bearing for 6–8 weeks given the articular fixation, then progressive loading to full weight-bearing by 10–12 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing.
- Hip precautions per the surgical approach used; trochanteric flip osteotomy patients also need trochanteric-osteotomy-specific abductor precautions (avoiding active abduction/resisted abductor loading) until osteotomy union, typically 6–8 weeks.
- Radiographic milestones at 2, 6, and 12 weeks; union confirmed by trabecular consolidation typically by 12–16 weeks, with specific attention to trochanteric osteotomy healing on the same films.
- Standard fragility/trauma physiotherapy progression applies where relevant: early bed mobility/transfers, gait training with assistive device, progressive resistive exercise, and abductor/extensor strengthening from week 6–12 (deferred/gentler for the abductors specifically until osteotomy union is confirmed).
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks once pain-controlled; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing. High-demand recreational activity/sport (relevant mainly to younger patients) is generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.
