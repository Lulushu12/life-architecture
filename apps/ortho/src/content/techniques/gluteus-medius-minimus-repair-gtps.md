## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Confirmed partial- or full-thickness gluteus medius/minimus tear on MRI with a positive clinical correlate (weakness, Trendelenburg gait, refractory lateral hip pain) after at least 6 months of failed conservative management (progressive abductor loading program ± peritrochanteric injection).
- Acute, traumatic full-thickness tears (e.g., after a fall or peri-THA abductor avulsion) with significant weakness may be considered for earlier surgical repair rather than a full 6-month conservative trial, since tendon retraction and fatty infiltration progress over time and worsen reparability.
- Failed prior repair with retained repairable tissue.

**Contraindications**
- Active local or systemic infection.
- Severe chronic full-thickness tear with advanced muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration (Goutallier grade ≥3) and significant tendon retraction — repair integrity and functional recovery are poor in this group, and reconstruction, tendon transfer, or non-operative management may be more appropriate than an attempted primary repair.
- Medical comorbidities precluding a period of protected weight-bearing/bracing and a structured rehabilitation program.
- Relative: uncontrolled diabetes or active smoking, both associated with impaired tendon-to-bone healing; optimize before elective repair where possible.
- Relative: an unrealistic patient expectation of a rapid return to high-impact activity — repair biology requires a prolonged protection phase, and outcomes correlate with adherence to the rehabilitation timeline.

## Preoperative Planning

- **MRI with dedicated hip/pelvis protocol**: characterize tear thickness (partial vs full), tear pattern (medius vs minimus vs combined), degree of retraction, and Goutallier-type fatty infiltration/atrophy grading (used analogously to the rotator cuff) — this is the single most important preoperative variable for counseling and for planning endoscopic versus open repair.
- **Correlate imaging with exam**: resisted abduction weakness, Trendelenburg sign, and single-leg stance test findings should match the MRI-described tear; a mismatch (severe imaging findings with preserved strength, or vice versa) warrants re-review before committing to surgery.
- **Plan approach based on tear size/retraction**: small-to-moderate, minimally retracted tears with good tissue quality are generally addressed endoscopically; large, chronic, retracted tears, revision settings, or tears requiring adjunctive tissue augmentation (allograft/synthetic patch or local tissue transfer such as tensor fasciae latae) are more reliably approached open.
- **Anchor selection and count**: plan a transosseous-equivalent (suture bridge) or double-row construct with suture anchors sized for the trochanteric facets; have both standard and small/revision-diameter anchors available in case bone quality at the facet is compromised (common in older patients with chronic tears).
- **Augmentation materials**: if fatty infiltration or retraction is significant, discuss with the patient in advance the possibility of intraoperative conversion to an augmented repair (allograft dermal patch, synthetic mesh, or local tissue transfer) and have these available even when the primary plan is a straightforward repair.
- **Imaging for coexisting pathology**: assess for external coxa saltans (snapping IT band) and IT band thickening on exam/imaging, since a concomitant ITB release/bursectomy may be added at the time of repair.
- **Equipment**: arthroscopic tower and pump (for endoscopic technique), radiofrequency ablation device for bursal/tendon debridement, suture anchor system, and a lateral positioning device/hip distractor as needed; for open repair, a standard retractor set and access to patch/augmentation material.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia (with or without regional block/peripheral nerve catheter for postoperative analgesia) is standard; some centers use spinal anesthesia with sedation.
- **Position**: lateral decubitus position with the operative hip up is most commonly used for both endoscopic and open gluteal repair, providing direct access to the peritrochanteric space and greater trochanter. A hip positioner/peg board or beanbag secures the pelvis; the operative leg is prepped free to allow intraoperative positioning (abduction to relax the abductors during repair, adduction/internal rotation to assess tension and impingement).
- **Bony landmarks marked**: greater trochanter (anterior, lateral, and posterior facets), the course of the IT band, and the anterior superior iliac spine as a reference point, marked before draping.
- **Draping**: circumferential free-leg drape from above the iliac crest to below the knee to allow full intraoperative hip range of motion for tensioning and impingement testing during repair.
- **Fluoroscopy**: not routinely required for isolated gluteal repair, though some surgeons use it to confirm anchor trajectory and depth in osteopenic trochanteric bone.
- **Tourniquet**: not used — the peritrochanteric space is not amenable to tourniquet control, and hemostasis is achieved with radiofrequency ablation and careful technique instead.
- **Traction**: not required (distinguishes this from central-compartment hip arthroscopy) — the peritrochanteric space is accessed without joint distraction.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Establish the peritrochanteric portal(s)** (endoscopic technique): create a standard lateral viewing portal centered over the greater trochanter, with an anterior and/or posterior accessory working portal established under direct visualization, working within the peritrochanteric bursal space superficial to the abductor tendons and deep to the IT band.
2. **Perform a bursectomy** to clear the trochanteric bursa and obtain a working space, using radiofrequency ablation and a shaver, taking care to preserve enough of the IT band and superficial soft tissue for later closure/tensioning.
3. **Identify the tear pattern**: visualize the gluteus medius insertion onto the posterosuperior and lateral facets and the gluteus minimus insertion onto the anterior facet, tracing the tear from its origin to define full extent, thickness, and retraction.
4. **Debride degenerative tendon edges** back to a bleeding, healthy tendon margin, and lightly decorticate/abrade the footprint on the trochanteric facet(s) to promote a bleeding bony bed for healing — avoid over-aggressive decortication that weakens the facet and compromises anchor purchase.
5. **Place suture anchors** in the appropriate facet(s): posterosuperior/lateral facet for gluteus medius, anterior facet for gluteus minimus, spacing anchors to recreate the native tendon footprint rather than clustering them centrally.
6. **Pass sutures through the tendon** in a mattress or similar configuration, respecting native fiber orientation, and perform a transosseous-equivalent (suture bridge) repair: medial-row anchors fix the tendon initially, and lateral-row (knotless) anchors tension the repair construct laterally over the footprint, maximizing footprint contact area and compression.
7. **Assess repair tension through a range of motion** — take the hip through abduction/adduction and internal/external rotation to confirm the repair is secure without excessive tension at end-range motion that could predispose to early failure.
8. **For large, retracted, or poor-quality tears**, convert to (or proceed primarily with) an open approach: a direct lateral incision centered over the trochanter, splitting the IT band longitudinally, mobilizing the retracted tendon edges (releasing adhesions and, if needed, performing an interval slide) to achieve a tension-free repair to the facets, and applying local tissue augmentation (gluteus maximus/TFL rotation flap) or a synthetic/biologic patch when native tissue alone cannot bridge the defect.
9. **Address coexisting external coxa saltans/thickened ITB**, if present and symptomatic, with a longitudinal release or a cruciate/Z-lengthening of the posterior IT band at the time of repair — this is rarely performed as an isolated procedure now that GTPS is understood as primarily a tendinopathy, but is a reasonable adjunct when snapping is a documented independent symptom.
10. **Close the IT band** (open technique) or portal sites (endoscopic technique) in layers, restoring the IT band's tension over the greater trochanter to avoid iatrogenic instability of the lateral soft-tissue envelope.
11. **Apply a hip abduction brace** (per surgeon preference) to protect the repair in a position of reduced abductor tension during the initial healing phase.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Debride only to a healthy, bleeding tendon margin — over-resection of tendon length makes an already-retracted tear harder to mobilize for a tension-free repair.
- Recreate the anatomic footprint with anchor placement (posterosuperior/lateral facet for medius, anterior facet for minimus) rather than compressing the repair into a single central point; this better restores the native lever arm and compressive contact area.
- Use a knotless lateral-row (suture bridge) construct where possible — it distributes tension more evenly across the footprint than a simple single-row repair and reduces the risk of suture cutting through degenerative tendon.
- Take the repair through a full range of motion intraoperatively before closing — this is the best way to detect excessive tension or impingement that predicts early postoperative failure, and it is far easier to adjust anchor position or add a relaxing maneuver at this point than to revise later.
- For chronic retracted tears, do not hesitate to plan for augmentation preoperatively rather than deciding intraoperatively — having allograft/patch material opened and available avoids a difficult intraoperative choice between an under-tensioned primary repair and an unplanned conversion.
- In revision or severely atrophic cases, counsel the patient explicitly that the goal may shift from restoring full strength to reducing pain and improving gait mechanics — this reframes success criteria appropriately for a lower-quality tissue substrate.
- Avoid over-aggressive decortication of the trochanteric facet — enough bleeding bone to promote healing is needed, but excessive removal weakens anchor purchase, particularly in osteopenic bone common in the older population who develop these tears.
- When adding an ITB procedure, perform it after the tendon repair is secured and tensioned, not before, so the abductor repair — not IT band tension — dictates final limb positioning.
- Use a peripheral nerve block or catheter for the first 24–48 hours postoperatively; pain control in this window materially affects the patient's ability to begin protected, brace-controlled movement on schedule.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Outcomes deteriorate with chronic full-thickness tears and significant muscle atrophy (Goutallier grade ≥3), where repair integrity and functional recovery are compromised — set expectations accordingly during preoperative counseling.
- Fatty infiltration (poor muscle quality) is the strongest negative predictor of persistent abductor weakness/Trendelenburg gait if the repair fails.
- Retear risk is highest in large, chronic tears with significant retraction; a repair placed under excessive tension to achieve footprint coverage is more likely to fail early — favor mobilization/augmentation over forcing a tight primary repair.
- Under-decortication of the facet leaves a nonbleeding bed and impairs healing; over-decortication weakens anchor fixation — judge the balance rather than defaulting to maximal preparation.
- Anchor pull-out in osteopenic trochanteric bone is a recognized technical failure mode — confirm anchor purchase intraoperatively and have revision-diameter anchors available.
- Persistent postoperative Trendelenburg gait can result from repair failure, inadequate preoperative muscle quality, or simply insufficient postoperative strengthening — differentiate with exam and repeat imaging (MRI or ultrasound) before assuming rehabilitation alone will resolve it.
- Repeated preoperative corticosteroid injection carries a theoretical concern for tendon attritional weakening/progression to full-thickness tear — a rationale for limiting injections to 1–2 per year rather than repeated dosing before considering surgery, and worth discussing with referring providers who may have already given multiple injections before referral.
- Iatrogenic injury to the IT band tensioning/closure can produce persistent lateral snapping or a sense of instability if the band is closed too loosely or too tightly — restore native tension during closure rather than closing under arbitrary tension.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Protected weight-bearing (crutches, brace in abduction/neutral rotation per surgeon preference) to protect repair | Passive ROM only, avoid active abduction, cryotherapy |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to full weight-bearing, wean brace/crutches | Begin isometric then gentle active-assisted abductor activation, gait retraining (avoid Trendelenburg compensation pattern) |
| III | 3–6 months | Full | Progressive isotonic strengthening, closed-chain single-leg work, return to low-impact activity |
| IV | 6–9 months | — | Return to running/impact activity once strength ≥85–90% of contralateral side and pain-free single-leg stance/step-down testing achieved |
