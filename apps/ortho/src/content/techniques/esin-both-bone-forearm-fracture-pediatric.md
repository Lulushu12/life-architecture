## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Unstable or unacceptable-alignment both-bone diaphyseal forearm fractures in school-age children, where closed reduction and casting cannot achieve or maintain acceptable alignment per age-based remodeling thresholds.
- Angulation exceeding age-based remodeling potential, rotational instability, or age >10 years at presentation.
- Failed closed reduction, or loss of an initially acceptable reduction on serial films (redisplacement occurs in a meaningful minority of unstable patterns, roughly 20–30%).
- Open fractures (after formal irrigation/debridement), floating elbow (ipsilateral supracondylar humerus fracture), and refracture through a previously healed segment.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Young children (typically <8–9 years) with acceptable alignment achievable and maintainable by closed reduction and casting — remodeling potential is high enough that surgery is unnecessary.
- Fractures within roughly 2–3 cm of the physis at either end where an antegrade/retrograde nail entry point would risk physeal injury — consider alternate entry sites or plate fixation.
- Severely comminuted or segmental fracture patterns where ESIN cannot achieve adequate length/rotational control — favor plate fixation instead.
- Adolescents near skeletal maturity with exhausted remodeling potential requiring anatomically exact, rotationally precise reduction — plating is generally preferred in this group.
- Grossly contaminated open fractures with extensive soft-tissue loss may be better served by temporary external fixation followed by staged definitive fixation.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: orthogonal (AP and lateral) forearm radiographs including the elbow and wrist joints to rule out associated Monteggia or Galeazzi patterns; measure canal diameter at the narrowest (isthmic) point of both the radius and ulna to template nail size.
- **Implant sizing**: select nail diameter so that each nail occupies approximately 40% of the canal's narrowest diameter (two nails together approximating ~70–80% fill) — undersized nails allow malalignment and rotational instability, oversized nails are difficult to pass and risk iatrogenic comminution or entry-site fracture. Titanium elastic nails (TEN) or stainless steel equivalents (e.g., "Nancy nails") in 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, or 3.5 mm diameters cover most pediatric forearms; have adjacent sizes opened/available in case intraoperative canal assessment differs from templated measurements.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: image intensifier, a dedicated flexible nail instrument set (awl/entry starter, nail benders, insertion handle/T-handle, nail cutter), a radiolucent hand table, and a tourniquet. Pre-bend nails on the back table to the desired gentle C-shaped curve (apex typically at the fracture site) rather than trying to bend them after partial insertion.
- **Case-specific planning**: identify fracture level and personality (transverse vs. oblique/spiral, comminution) on imaging — this determines whether closed nailing alone will achieve reduction or whether a small open/mini-open reduction assist at the fracture site will be needed to pass the nail across a displaced, overlapping fracture.
- **Associated injury check**: confirm radiocapitellar and radioulnar joint alignment on the pre-op films to exclude a missed Monteggia (ulna fracture + radial head dislocation) or Galeazzi (radius fracture + DRUJ disruption) equivalent, which changes the fixation priorities.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard; a supraclavicular or axillary regional block is a reasonable postoperative analgesia adjunct in this population, with less of the compartment-syndrome-masking concern that applies to supracondylar humerus fixation, though vigilance for compartment syndrome should still be maintained clinically.
- **Positioning**: supine, with the arm extended on a radiolucent hand table.
- **Table setup**: the hand table should allow full fluoroscopic access to both the proximal and distal forearm without repositioning the limb between the two nail insertion sites (proximal ulna and distal radius, or wherever entry points are planned).
- **Tourniquet**: apply a pneumatic tourniquet high on the arm; inflate as needed for the exposure portions (entry site dissection) but it can typically be deflated once nails are seated, and is not required for the entire case.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire forearm circumferentially from above the tourniquet to the fingertips to allow intraoperative assessment of forearm rotation and access to both bones.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the distal radius entry site (dorsal, proximal to the physis, in line with Lister's tubercle, or the radial styloid approach depending on surgeon preference) and the ulnar entry site (proximal ulna, olecranon apophysis region for antegrade, or distal ulna for retrograde) before incision.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm to allow orthogonal (AP/lateral) views of the entire forearm, ideally from a single position or with minimal repositioning, since the nails will be advanced under intermittent fluoroscopic control from entry site to fracture site to final position.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Radius nail — entry site exposure**: make a small (1–2 cm) longitudinal incision over the distal radial metaphysis, dorsal or dorsoradial, proximal enough to the physis to avoid physeal injury (typically 1.5–2 cm proximal). Bluntly dissect to bone, protecting the superficial radial nerve branches and extensor tendons.
2. **Radius nail — entry portal**: use an awl or drill to create a cortical entry portal angled roughly 45° to the shaft, oriented to allow the nail to pass proximally up the medullary canal without immediately impinging the far cortex.
3. **Radius nail — pre-bent nail insertion**: insert the pre-bent (gentle C-curve, apex directed to reproduce/counteract the fracture deformity) nail retrograde, advancing it under fluoroscopic guidance to the fracture site using a T-handle or insertion device, with gentle oscillating rotational movements to ease proximal progression rather than pure axial force.
4. **Radius nail — cross the fracture**: with the fracture reduced (by closed manipulation, or with a percutaneous joystick/reduction clamp if closed reduction is not holding), advance the nail across the fracture site into the proximal radial metaphysis, stopping short of the physis, and confirm position on AP and lateral fluoroscopy.
5. **Ulna nail — entry site exposure**: make a small incision over the chosen ulnar entry point (proximal olecranon apophysis for antegrade nailing, or distal ulnar metaphysis for retrograde nailing, per surgeon preference and fracture level), protecting the ulnar nerve if the entry is proximal-medial.
6. **Ulna nail — entry portal and insertion**: create the entry portal, insert the pre-bent ulnar nail, and advance it under fluoroscopic guidance across the reduced ulna fracture into the distal (if antegrade) or proximal (if retrograde) metaphysis.
7. **Confirm alignment and rotation on both bones**: with both nails across their respective fractures, take the forearm through passive pronation/supination under fluoroscopy to confirm no impingement, crossing, or block to rotation, and confirm angular alignment on both AP and lateral views.
8. **Trim and bury the nails**: cut each nail leaving a short segment (typically 5–10 mm) outside the cortex, bent away from the skin and tendons, to allow later percutaneous or mini-open removal while minimizing soft-tissue irritation — nails cut too long are a leading cause of symptomatic prominence requiring earlier removal.
9. **Single-bone nail option**: in select length-stable both-bone patterns, nailing the radius alone (with closed reduction and cast control of the ulna) is an option, but most surgeons nail both bones for reliable rotational control, particularly in more proximal or more unstable fracture patterns.
10. **Consider plating instead**: if intraoperative assessment reveals a comminuted or segmental pattern that will not achieve stable length/rotational control with flexible nails, convert to open reduction and plate fixation rather than accepting a suboptimal nail construct.
11. **Closure**: irrigate and close entry-site incisions in layers; apply a well-padded splint or cast for the initial postoperative period per surgeon preference (see Postoperative Protocol).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Pre-bend nails on the back table before insertion rather than trying to shape them once partially inserted — the apex of the bend should generally sit at or near the fracture site to help maintain the reduction as the nail is advanced.
- Use gentle oscillating (back-and-forth rotational) movements of the T-handle to advance the nail proximally through tight canal segments rather than forcing straight axial pressure, which risks the nail catching the cortex and creating a false passage.
- If closed reduction will not hold long enough to pass the nail across the fracture, a small percutaneous joystick (a separate K-wire placed into the fragment) or a mini-open reduction at the fracture site is far more efficient than repeated failed closed attempts.
- Choose nail entry points and curve direction so the two nails' apices oppose each other across the fracture (roughly a "C-C" configuration mirrored between the two bones) — this maximizes three-point fixation and rotational control.
- Confirm forearm rotation intraoperatively before closing — a construct that looks perfect on static AP/lateral films can still block pronation or supination if a nail tip abuts the opposite bone or the interosseous membrane.
- Leave the nail tip slightly proud of the cortex (5–10 mm) rather than deeply buried — deeply buried nails are considerably harder to retrieve at the planned removal surgery.
- When treating a single-bone (radius-only) nail strategy, confirm the ulna reduction is truly stable through a trial range of forearm rotation intraoperatively before committing — an unstable ulna will erode the benefit of a well-nailed radius.
- Reassess the whole construct (both bones, both fluoroscopic planes, full rotational arc) as a final step before leaving the OR — small malalignments are far easier to correct on the table than after the wound is closed.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Nails cut too long are a common, avoidable cause of soft-tissue irritation and the leading indication for elective early removal.
- ESIN is load-sharing and preserves periosteal biology, allowing earlier functional, cast-free (or lightly protected) mobilization than plating — but it depends on adequate rotational and length control being achievable, which is not guaranteed in unstable or comminuted patterns; do not force a flexible-nail solution onto a pattern that needs a plate.
- Malunion (usually rotational) causes loss of pronosupination; malunion is more common with under-constrained flexible nailing in unstable fracture patterns — verify rotational alignment intraoperatively, not just angular alignment.
- Refracture risk is elevated in the first 6 months after cast removal (or nail removal), particularly with incomplete remodeling of angulation or premature return to high-risk activity.
- Radioulnar synostosis is rare but serious, and is most associated with both-bone fractures at the same level with substantial initial displacement/comminution and single-incision, same-level surgical approaches — use separate incisions at different levels for the radius and ulna entry sites where possible, and avoid excessive periosteal stripping at the fracture site during any open reduction assist.
- Compartment syndrome signs (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment, paresthesia) must not wait for pulselessness — this is the most feared complication of both-bone forearm fractures, particularly in floating-elbow injuries, and can occur even after seemingly straightforward closed nailing from soft-tissue swelling.
- Nail entry too close to the physis (particularly at the distal radius) risks iatrogenic physeal injury — confirm entry point distance from the physis fluoroscopically before committing to the cortical portal.
- An oversized nail that cannot be advanced despite repeated attempts risks iatrogenic cortical perforation or comminution at the entry site — downsize rather than persist with excessive force.

## Postoperative Protocol

- After ESIN, a short period of cast/splint protection (2–4 weeks) is common, though some centers mobilize early with the nails alone providing sufficient stability.
- **Forearm rotation** (pronation/supination) is the functional milestone to track and typically lags the longest.
- Unrestricted sport/monkey-bar activity is generally permitted at **3 months** post-ESIN once union is confirmed.
- Nail removal is elective at **4–9 months** — not mandatory in all cases but standard in most pediatric practice given the small nail diameter and growing bone.
- For comparison, nonoperative cast treatment runs 6 weeks total, transitioning from long-arm to short-arm cast at 3–4 weeks in many protocols.
