## Indications

Unstable or unacceptable-alignment both-bone diaphyseal forearm fractures in school-age children, where closed reduction and casting cannot achieve or maintain acceptable alignment, per age-based remodeling thresholds — including angulation exceeding the age-based remodeling potential, instability, or age >10 years.

## Positioning & setup

ESIN is inserted via **retrograde radius** and **antegrade or retrograde ulna** nail entry points.

## Steps

1. Retrograde insertion of the radial nail.
2. Antegrade or retrograde insertion of the ulnar nail.
3. A single-bone (radius-only) nail with the ulna reduced and cast-controlled is an option in some length-stable both-bone patterns, but most surgeons nail both bones for reliable rotational control.
4. Consider **plate fixation** instead of ESIN for adolescents nearing skeletal maturity (where remodeling potential is exhausted and anatomic, rotationally exact reduction is required), refracture, comminuted/segmental patterns, and cases where ESIN cannot achieve or maintain adequate rotational and length control.

## Pitfalls

- ESIN is load-sharing and preserves periosteal biology, allowing earlier functional, cast-free (or lightly protected) mobilization than plating — but it depends on adequate rotational and length control being achievable, which is not guaranteed in unstable or comminuted patterns.
- Malunion (usually rotational) causes loss of pronosupination; malunion is more common with under-constrained flexible nailing in unstable fracture patterns.
- Refracture risk is elevated in the first 6 months after cast removal, particularly with incomplete remodeling of angulation or premature return to high-risk activity.
- Radioulnar synostosis is rare but serious, and is most associated with both-bone fractures at the same level with substantial initial displacement/comminution.
- Compartment syndrome signs (pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch, tense compartment, paresthesia) must not wait for pulselessness — this is the most feared complication of both-bone forearm fractures, particularly in floating-elbow injuries.

## Post-op protocol

- After ESIN, a short period of cast/splint protection (2–4 weeks) is common, though some centers mobilize early with the nails alone providing sufficient stability.
- **Forearm rotation** (pronation/supination) is the functional milestone to track and typically lags the longest.
- Unrestricted sport/monkey-bar activity is generally permitted at **3 months** post-ESIN once union is confirmed.
- Nail removal is elective at **4–9 months** — not mandatory in all cases but standard in most pediatric practice given the small nail diameter and growing bone.
- For comparison, nonoperative cast treatment runs 6 weeks total, transitioning from long-arm to short-arm cast at 3–4 weeks in many protocols.
