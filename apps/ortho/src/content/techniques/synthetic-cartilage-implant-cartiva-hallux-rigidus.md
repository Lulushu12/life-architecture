## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- A motion-preserving alternative to arthrodesis for Coughlin-Shurnas grade 3 (and selected grade 4) hallux rigidus, for patients who prioritize retained 1st MTP motion and accept a somewhat higher reoperation likelihood than fusion.
- Patients seeking to avoid the shoe-wear/push-off limitations of permanent fusion, particularly those who value continued high-heeled or dress-shoe wear.
- Adequate remaining metatarsal head bone stock to accept a press-fit implant without excessive residual bone loss.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Arthrodesis remains preferable for grade 4 disease, high-demand laborers, or patients who prioritize the single most durable outcome.
- **Inflammatory arthritis** of the 1st MTP is a specific contraindication per the device instructions for use.
- **Severe sesamoid osteoarthritis** is a specific contraindication — the implant addresses the metatarsal head articular surface, not sesamoid-side disease, and will not relieve sesamoid-driven pain.
- Active infection, inadequate bone stock for a stable press-fit, or a metatarsal head too small/deformed to accept a properly seated 10 mm implant.
- When placing (or revising toward) two implants in the same articular area, an insufficient bone bridge (**<5 mm at surface and base** between implants) should be avoided per the device instructions for use.
- Patients unwilling to accept a meaningfully higher reoperation/revision rate than fusion in exchange for motion preservation — this trade-off must be explicit in consent.

## Preoperative Planning

- Weight-bearing AP and lateral radiographs to confirm Coughlin-Shurnas grade, joint-space narrowing pattern, and metatarsal head bone stock/size adequate for a standard 10 mm × 10 mm cylindrical implant.
- Assess the sesamoids specifically for severe osteoarthritis on imaging — this is a contraindication distinct from the general 1st MTP grading and must be checked separately.
- Rule out inflammatory arthropathy as the driver of joint destruction (history, exam, inflammatory markers if suspicion exists) — this changes both the diagnosis and the implant's suitability.
- Have the manufacturer's dedicated instrument set available: guide pin, cannulated drill/reamer sized to the implant diameter, depth gauge, and trial sizers, along with the sterile-packaged implant itself in the planned size — confirm implant availability and size options before the case.
- Have a backup plan and consent in place for intraoperative conversion to arthrodesis if the metatarsal head bone quality or size is found intraoperatively to be inadequate for a stable press-fit.
- Counsel the patient explicitly on the revision-rate trade-off versus fusion (see Pitfalls) as part of shared decision-making before the day of surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Regional (ankle/popliteal block) with sedation, spinal, or general anesthesia, as for cheilectomy/arthrodesis.
- Supine, foot free at the end of the table, ipsilateral hip bump for neutral rotation.
- Thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination.
- Mini C-arm positioned for AP and lateral fluoroscopy of the 1st MTP — used intraoperatively to confirm guide-pin position, drilling depth/trajectory, and final implant seating.
- Mark the dorsal or dorsomedial incision as for cheilectomy.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a dorsal or dorsomedial longitudinal incision over the 1st MTP joint, protecting the EHL tendon and dorsomedial cutaneous nerve.
2. Incise the capsule, expose the joint, and perform a limited synovectomy/debridement of impinging dorsal osteophyte as needed to gain adequate visualization and motion for instrumentation — this step overlaps substantially with a standard cheilectomy.
3. Inspect the metatarsal head and phalangeal articular surfaces directly, and reassess the sesamoids for degenerative change not fully appreciated on preoperative imaging; if severe sesamoid arthritis or inadequate bone stock is found, be prepared to convert to arthrodesis rather than proceed.
4. Place the guide pin centrally in the metatarsal head articular surface under fluoroscopic guidance, aimed to seat the implant perpendicular to the load-bearing axis of the joint — malalignment of the guide pin is the root cause of most subsequent implant malposition.
5. Confirm guide-pin position and trajectory on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy before drilling.
6. Use the cannulated drill/reamer over the guide pin to create the implant pilot hole to the manufacturer-specified diameter and depth, removing minimal additional bone beyond what is needed for the implant — this is deliberately a much more conservative resection than arthrodesis preparation.
7. Use the depth gauge to confirm the pilot hole depth matches the implant length, leaving the distal tip of the implant intended to sit slightly proud as a spacer within the joint per the device design intent — under- or over-reaming the depth is a recognized source of a poorly seated implant.
8. Trial-size if the instrument set provides trial implants, confirming fit and motion before committing to the definitive implant.
9. Press-fit the polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogel implant into the prepared pilot hole, seating it fully and evenly without cocking it to one side — an implant seated off-axis will not function as a smooth, congruent spacer and is a recognized mechanism of early symptomatic failure.
10. Confirm implant seating and position with fluoroscopy in both planes.
11. Take the joint through a full passive range of motion intraoperatively to confirm smooth, painless gliding of the implant against the phalangeal articular surface without impingement or subluxation.
12. Address any residual dorsal impingement (osteophyte) not already resected, since a residual bony block will limit the motion the implant is intended to preserve.
13. Irrigate, repair the capsule, close in layers, and apply a light compressive, non-restrictive dressing to allow early motion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Center and confirm the guide pin trajectory on **both** AP and lateral fluoroscopy before drilling — a pin that looks central on one view can be eccentric on the other, and pilot-hole malposition is difficult to correct after drilling has begun.
- Aim for a conservative, minimal bone resection consistent with the implant's design intent; this is not a joint resurfacing or a fusion preparation, and over-resection sacrifices bone stock that would be valuable if later revision to arthrodesis is needed.
- Confirm pilot-hole depth carefully with the depth gauge rather than estimating — both under-reaming (implant sits proud and impinges) and over-reaming (implant sits recessed and loses its spacer function) degrade outcomes.
- Use trial sizers if available before committing to the final implant — this catches sizing or positioning problems while they are still easily correctable.
- Take the joint through full passive motion after implantation, on the table, to directly confirm smooth tracking — this is the best intraoperative signal that the implant is well seated and appropriately oriented.
- Treat any residual dorsal impingement at the time of implantation, not as an afterthought — since the whole rationale for this implant is motion preservation, leaving a mechanical block defeats the purpose.
- Keep a low threshold intraoperatively to convert to arthrodesis if bone quality, sesamoid disease, or implant seating is unsatisfactory — this decision is far easier to make on the table than to manage as an early revision.
- Set patient expectations explicitly for a nontrivial chance of postoperative stiffness requiring manipulation under anesthesia (MUA) in some cohorts, and build early, assertive ROM therapy into the postoperative plan from the outset to reduce this risk.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The pivotal **MOTION randomized trial** (Baumhauer et al., *JBJS* 2016) found the implant non-inferior to arthrodesis for pain and function at 2 years, but mid-to-long-term series report a **secondary/revision surgery rate meaningfully higher than fusion** — commonly cited in the roughly 8–14% range at 4–7 years across published cohorts, though the true rate is series-dependent (one 5-year series reported only 5% revision with no loosening; a separate shorter-follow-up cohort reported an 18.2% total reoperation rate including MUA for stiffness).
- Recognized failure modes are **implant subsidence** and **progressive metatarsal-side degeneration**.
- Counsel patients that the implant trades a small-but-real revision-risk premium for motion preservation, and that **revision to fusion after a failed implant remains a reliable salvage**.
- Roughly a third of patients in some cohorts develop postoperative stiffness requiring manipulation under anesthesia (MUA) — set expectations preoperatively.
- Not an independently superior procedure to arthrodesis — it is a different risk/benefit trade (motion vs. durability), and patient selection (grade, activity demand, motion priority) should drive the choice.
- Placing the implant with an inadequate bone bridge relative to a second implant, or in the setting of severe sesamoid arthritis or inflammatory disease, are specific, avoidable technical/selection errors called out in the device's instructions for use.
- Malpositioned or eccentric guide-pin placement is the most common technical root cause of implant malposition and early mechanical symptoms.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Similar early protected weight-bearing to cheilectomy: postoperative shoe, weight-bear as tolerated.
- **Earlier initiation of ROM** than after arthrodesis, given the motion-preserving intent.
- Return to activity generally parallels cheilectomy timelines — **6–8 weeks** for most activity.
- General return-to-sport gating: near-full pain-free active ROM, pain-free single-leg heel-rise/hop, swelling resolved, graduated activity progression.
