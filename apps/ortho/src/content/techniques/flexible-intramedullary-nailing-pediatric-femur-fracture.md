## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- First-line for most length-stable diaphyseal femoral shaft fractures in children **5–11 years** (transverse, short-oblique, or spiral patterns without significant comminution).
- Polytrauma or length-unstable fractures in the younger (6 months–5 years) bracket where a spica cast would not maintain length.
- Bilateral femur fractures, floating knee, or fractures with associated head injury where early mobilization and easier nursing care are priorities.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Children **>11 years or >45–50 kg** — flexible nailing carries an unacceptably high complication rate (malunion, painful nail prominence) in heavier/older patients because the implant cannot adequately control the greater bending and torsional loads; a rigid, trochanteric-entry locked IM nail (or submuscular plating for very proximal/distal metaphyseal fractures) is preferred.
- Length-unstable patterns (comminuted, segmental, or long-oblique/spiral fractures with a short intact segment) at any age within the flexible-nail bracket — these tend to shorten around flexible nails, which are load-sharing rather than load-bearing constructs; consider rigid nailing, plating, or adjunctive length-control strategies.
- Very proximal (subtrochanteric) or very distal (supracondylar) metaphyseal fractures — flexible nails have limited fixation purchase in the metaphysis and poor control of these fracture levels; submuscular bridge plating is generally preferred.
- Children under roughly 6 months, or with weight/size too small for standard nail diameters to be meaningfully sized — treated with Pavlik harness instead.
- Severe open fractures/soft-tissue compromise or hemodynamically unstable polytrauma — external fixation for damage control, with staged conversion once the soft-tissue envelope and physiology allow.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: full-length AP and lateral femur radiographs (including hip and knee) to assess fracture pattern, comminution, and confirm length-stability; measure the femoral canal diameter at its narrowest point on both AP and lateral views for nail sizing templating.
- **Implant sizing**: select nail diameter so each nail occupies approximately 40% of the narrowest canal diameter, with the two nails together approximating ~80% canal fill — undersized nails permit shortening/angulation, oversized nails are difficult to pass and risk cortical perforation, especially around the femoral bow. Titanium elastic nails are most common; stainless steel nails offer greater stiffness and may be preferred in heavier children near the upper weight limit for this technique, at the cost of being harder to contour.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: flexible nail set with matched sizes (typically 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0 mm), nail bender, insertion handle/T-handle or power inserter, nail cutter, image intensifier, and a fracture table or radiolucent table capable of traction if closed reduction requires it.
- **Case-specific planning**: for fractures near the length-stability borderline, plan the fixation strategy (flexible nail vs. rigid nail vs. plate) based on fracture pattern and weight, not age alone — a comminuted fracture in a 7-year-old at the lower weight range may still be a poor flexible-nail candidate.
- **Consent/counseling**: discuss the expected femoral overgrowth (0.5–1.5 cm) with families, and the likely need for a second, planned procedure for nail removal.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard; a femoral or fascia iliaca block is an effective postoperative analgesia adjunct and does not carry the same compartment-syndrome-masking concern seen with supracondylar humerus surgery, though thigh compartment monitoring should still be clinically maintained in high-energy injuries.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table (a fracture table with traction is optional and used selectively for reduction assistance in comminuted or shortened patterns; many surgeons perform this on a flat radiolucent table with manual traction).
- **Table setup**: position the uninjured leg out of the fluoroscopic field (a well leg holder or simple abduction/scissoring position works well) to allow unobstructed AP and lateral imaging of the entire femur.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the entire leg circumferentially from the hip to well below the knee to allow access to both distal medial and lateral entry sites and to permit intraoperative assessment of rotational alignment by comparing thigh-foot angle or profile to the contralateral limb.
- **Tourniquet**: not typically used for femoral shaft procedures given the level of the incisions relative to a thigh tourniquet; hemostasis is achieved with local measures.
- **Surface landmarks**: mark the distal medial and lateral femoral metaphyseal entry sites (proximal to the distal femoral physis, typically 2–3 cm) before incision; for rigid trochanteric-entry nailing, mark the tip of the greater trochanter and the piriformis fossa region to plan an entry point lateral to the piriformis fossa/trochanteric tip.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm to allow full-length imaging of the femur in both planes with minimal repositioning; confirm you can visualize both the entry sites and the fracture site without draping interference.

## Step-by-Step Technique

### Flexible (elastic stable) intramedullary nailing

1. **Pre-bend both nails** on the back table to a gentle C-shaped curve with the apex of the bend positioned to sit at the fracture site once inserted — the bow should be roughly three times the canal diameter for adequate three-point fixation.
2. **Medial entry site**: make a small (1–2 cm) longitudinal incision over the distal medial femoral metaphysis, proximal to the distal femoral physis (confirm physeal level fluoroscopically before drilling), and bluntly dissect to bone protecting the saphenous nerve/vein.
3. **Lateral entry site**: make a matching incision over the distal lateral femoral metaphysis at a similar level, protecting the iliotibial band and lateral structures.
4. **Create entry portals**: use an awl or drill angled roughly 45° to the shaft at each entry site, oriented to allow the nail to pass proximally without early cortical impingement.
5. **Insert the nails retrograde**: advance each pre-bent nail proximally under fluoroscopic guidance using an insertion handle or T-handle, with gentle rotational/oscillating movements to negotiate the canal, alternating advancement of the medial and lateral nails to help maintain length and alignment as they approach the fracture site.
6. **Reduce the fracture**: achieve reduction by longitudinal traction and manual manipulation (with fluoroscopic confirmation of length, alignment, and rotation), then advance both nails across the fracture site so that they cross in a symmetric **"C-C" or "S-S" configuration** — each nail's curve opposing the other — which is the key to maximal three-point fixation and stability.
7. **Advance nails to final position**: seat the nail tips in the proximal femoral metaphysis (medial nail directed toward the medial/calcar region, lateral nail toward the greater trochanteric region), stopping short of the proximal physis, and confirm final position and fracture alignment on AP and lateral fluoroscopy.
8. **Assess rotational and length control**: compare thigh-foot angle or the profile of the patella/foot to the contralateral limb intraoperatively to confirm no rotational malalignment, and confirm restored length by comparing to preoperative measurements or the contralateral limb.
9. **Trim the nails**: cut each nail leaving a short segment outside the cortex (typically 1–1.5 cm at the knee to allow later retrieval, bent away from the skin) — nails cut too long are a leading cause of symptomatic soft-tissue irritation requiring earlier removal, while nails cut too short can migrate proximally and become difficult to retrieve.
10. **Closure**: close entry-site incisions in layers.

### Rigid trochanteric-entry locked nailing (older/heavier children, length-unstable patterns)

11. **Entry point**: with the patient positioned appropriately (lateral or supine per system/surgeon preference), establish the entry point lateral to the piriformis fossa, at or near the tip of the greater trochanter — deliberately avoiding a piriformis-fossa entry point, which risks injury to the lateral ascending cervical vessels supplying the femoral head and iatrogenic osteonecrosis in a patient whose trochanteric apophysis is not yet mature.
12. **Ream and pass the nail** per the specific implant system's technique, achieving reduction (open or closed, per fracture pattern) before or during nail passage, then lock the nail proximally and distally per standard locked-nail technique.

### Submuscular bridge plating (very proximal/distal metaphyseal fractures)

13. **Approach and plate passage**: through limited proximal and distal incisions, pass a pre-contoured locking plate submuscularly across the fracture in a bridging fashion, achieving indirect reduction, then place locking screws proximal and distal to the fracture while preserving the fracture-site biology (no direct fracture-site exposure).

### External fixation (damage control)

14. **Damage-control stabilization**: for open fractures, severe soft-tissue injury, or polytrauma, apply an external fixator across the fracture for provisional stabilization, with conversion to definitive fixation (flexible nail, rigid nail, or plate as appropriate) once the soft-tissue envelope and overall physiology allow.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Pre-bend nails to a curve roughly three times the canal diameter — an under-bent nail will not achieve adequate three-point fixation against the endosteal cortex, while an over-bent nail is difficult to advance and can bind in the canal.
- Advance the medial and lateral nails in alternating small increments rather than fully seating one before starting the other — this helps maintain length and prevents one nail from "walking" the fracture into a malaligned position while the other is being placed.
- Aim for true symmetric crossing of the two nails at the fracture site (the "C-C"/"S-S" configuration) — asymmetric or minimally divergent nails provide inferior rotational and bending stability even if both nails are technically "across" the fracture.
- Check rotation by direct visual/tactile comparison of the thigh-foot angle or hip internal/external rotation arc to the contralateral limb before final nail seating — this is more reliable intraoperatively than trying to judge rotation from a single fluoroscopic image.
- In a comminuted or shortened fracture, use gentle longitudinal traction (manual or via a footholder/fracture table) while advancing the second nail to help restore and hold length as the nails cross.
- Leave enough nail proud of the cortex at the knee entry sites (roughly 1–1.5 cm, bent away from soft tissue) to allow straightforward removal at the planned second surgery — a nail cut flush or buried is considerably harder to retrieve later.
- For trochanteric-entry rigid nailing, confirm the entry point fluoroscopically before reaming — an entry point too medial (toward the piriformis fossa) is the single most important technical error to avoid in this age group, given the osteonecrosis risk.
- Counsel families up front that mild bayonet apposition/acceptable shortening is an intentional part of the plan in the 2–10-year bracket, not a technical shortfall — this avoids confusion at the first postoperative visit when the limb looks slightly short or overlapped on film.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Nails cut too long are a leading complication requiring elective removal due to soft-tissue irritation.
- Inadequate divergence of the two flexible nails at the fracture site compromises construct stability.
- Piriformis or greater-trochanteric-tip entry before the trochanteric apophysis is mature risks iatrogenic osteonecrosis of the proximal femoral epiphysis — use lateral (trochanteric)-entry nails in children, not piriformis-entry.
- Overgrowth of the injured femur (typically 0.5–1.5 cm, from post-traumatic hyperemia) is expected in children under ~10 and is factored into acceptable shortening targets; mild bayonet apposition is intentionally accepted in the 2–10-year bracket.
- Malunion (rotational or angular) is more common with under-constrained flexible nailing in unstable fracture patterns — assess this risk preoperatively and choose rigid fixation or plating for length-unstable patterns rather than forcing a flexible-nail solution.
- Nail migration (proximal or distal) can occur if nails are cut too short at the entry site or if the tips are not adequately seated in dense metaphyseal bone — leave an adequate stump and confirm secure tip position fluoroscopically.
- Symptomatic hardware prominence at the knee (medial or lateral) is common enough that most centers plan elective removal rather than treating it as an unexpected complication.

## Postoperative Protocol

- After flexible nailing: protected weight-bearing (crutches/walker) for **2–4 weeks**, then progressive weight-bearing as tolerated; return to running/sport typically at **3–4 months** once solid radiographic union (bridging callus on 3 of 4 cortices) is present.
- After rigid nailing in adolescents: weight-bearing progression follows adult-pattern protocols more closely — partial weight-bearing early, progressing over **6–12 weeks** based on union.
- Spica casting (younger children): maintained 4–6 weeks (shorter end for younger/faster-healing children), then transitioned to a removable brace or observed for progressive weight-bearing as pain allows — children are remarkably good at self-limiting weight-bearing, and formal restriction protocols are less rigid than in adults.
- Formal physiotherapy is used more often after femur fracture than elbow fracture, particularly for adolescents with rigid fixation, to address quadriceps/hip-abductor strength deficits.
