## Indications

Age-based ladder: flexible (elastic stable) IM nailing is first-line for most length-stable diaphyseal femoral shaft fractures in children **5–11 years**. For **>11 years or >45–50 kg**, or a length-unstable pattern at any age in this bracket, flexible nailing has an unacceptably high complication rate (malunion, painful nail prominence) and a rigid, trochanteric-entry locked IM nail (or submuscular plating for very proximal/distal metaphyseal fractures) is preferred. Younger children are managed lower on the ladder: Pavlik harness <6 months; early hip spica (±traction if shortening >2–3 cm) for 6 months–5 years, with flexible nailing reserved for polytrauma or length-unstable patterns in that bracket.

## Positioning & setup

Flexible nails are inserted **retrograde through medial and lateral distal metaphyseal entry points**. Rigid trochanteric-entry nails enter **lateral to the piriformis fossa/greater trochanteric tip**, deliberately avoiding piriformis-entry to protect the lateral ascending cervical vessels supplying the femoral head — the proximal femoral epiphysis is vulnerable to iatrogenic osteonecrosis from piriformis/greater-trochanteric-tip entry nailing before the trochanteric apophysis is mature.

## Steps

1. **Flexible IM nailing**: retrograde insertion through medial and lateral distal metaphyseal entry points.
2. Cross the two nails in a symmetric **"C-C" or "S-S" configuration** at the fracture site for maximal stability.
3. Select nail diameter per bone — each nail approximating **~40% of the canal diameter**, with the two nails together approximating ~80%.
4. **Rigid trochanteric-entry locked nailing** for older/heavier children or length-unstable patterns, entering lateral to the piriformis fossa/greater trochanteric tip.
5. **Submuscular bridge plating** as an alternative for very proximal or distal metaphyseal fractures poorly suited to flexible nails.
6. **External fixation** reserved for open fractures, severe soft-tissue injury, or polytrauma requiring damage-control stabilization, with conversion to definitive fixation once the soft-tissue envelope allows.

## Pitfalls

- Nails cut too long are a leading complication requiring elective removal due to soft-tissue irritation.
- Inadequate divergence of the two flexible nails at the fracture site compromises construct stability.
- Piriformis or greater-trochanteric-tip entry before the trochanteric apophysis is mature risks iatrogenic osteonecrosis of the proximal femoral epiphysis — use lateral (trochanteric)-entry nails in children, not piriformis-entry.
- Overgrowth of the injured femur (typically 0.5–1.5 cm, from post-traumatic hyperemia) is expected in children under ~10 and is factored into acceptable shortening targets; mild bayonet apposition is intentionally accepted in the 2–10-year bracket.
- Malunion (rotational or angular) is more common with under-constrained flexible nailing in unstable fracture patterns.

## Post-op protocol

- After flexible nailing: protected weight-bearing (crutches/walker) for **2–4 weeks**, then progressive weight-bearing as tolerated; return to running/sport typically at **3–4 months** once solid radiographic union (bridging callus on 3 of 4 cortices) is present.
- After rigid nailing in adolescents: weight-bearing progression follows adult-pattern protocols more closely — partial weight-bearing early, progressing over **6–12 weeks** based on union.
- Spica casting (younger children): maintained 4–6 weeks (shorter end for younger/faster-healing children), then transitioned to a removable brace or observed for progressive weight-bearing as pain allows — children are remarkably good at self-limiting weight-bearing, and formal restriction protocols are less rigid than in adults.
- Formal physiotherapy is used more often after femur fracture than elbow fracture, particularly for adolescents with rigid fixation, to address quadriceps/hip-abductor strength deficits.
