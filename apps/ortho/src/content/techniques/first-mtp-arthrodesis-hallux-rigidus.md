## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- The reference-standard, most predictable procedure for Coughlin-Shurnas grade 3–4 hallux rigidus (particularly grade 4, where cheilectomy is unreliable).
- Revision surgery after failed cheilectomy, failed synthetic cartilage implant, or failed Keller resection arthroplasty.
- High-demand laborers and patients who prioritize the single most durable, predictable outcome over motion preservation.
- Inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic arthritis) affecting the 1st MTP, and severe hallux valgus with concomitant end-stage 1st MTP arthritis, where fusion addresses both deformity and pain simultaneously.
- Failed prior 1st MTP surgery of any kind where salvage to a motion-preserving option is not realistic.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active local or systemic infection.
- Inadequate bone stock for stable fixation (severe osteoporosis, prior extensive bone loss) without a plan for structural grafting.
- Ipsilateral hindfoot/midfoot arthritis or significant IP joint arthritis that would be functionally worsened by eliminating 1st MTP motion — evaluate the whole ray before committing.
- Patient unwilling to accept permanent loss of 1st MTP motion (limits high-heeled shoe-wear, alters push-off mechanics) — counsel thoroughly, since this is irreversible.
- **Resection (Keller) arthroplasty** is a lower-demand alternative reserved for typically elderly, low-demand patients — it relieves pain reliably but sacrifices intrinsic muscle balance and push-off strength, with a recognized risk of transfer metatarsalgia and cock-up deformity, and is not first-line in an active patient.

## Preoperative Planning

- Weight-bearing AP and lateral radiographs of the foot to confirm the arthritis grade, bone stock, and any deformity (hallux valgus, hallux varus) that must be corrected simultaneously with the fusion.
- Assess contralateral 1st MTP dorsiflexion and the ipsilateral IP joint and hindfoot for compensatory arthritis that could be aggravated by fusion.
- Plan fixation construct: crossed lag screws alone are simple and low-profile but rely entirely on bone quality for compression; a dorsal plate combined with an interfragmentary compression screw provides the most rigid construct and the highest published union rates, and is generally preferred when bone quality is compromised or in revision cases.
- Have joint-preparation instrumentation available — either flat-cut resection guides (simpler, less bone stock preserved) or reamers/conical "cup and cone" reamer sets (preserve length, self-center, and allow fine position adjustment before fixation).
- Template the desired fusion angle (dorsiflexion, valgus) preoperatively against the contralateral foot and standard targets so the intraoperative goniometer/visual check has a clear reference.
- If there is bone loss or a shortened 1st ray from prior surgery, plan for structural or morselized autograft/allograft to maintain length and avoid transfer metatarsalgia from an overly short ray.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Regional (ankle or popliteal block) with sedation, spinal, or general anesthesia; regional block provides useful postoperative analgesia given the moderate pain of joint preparation.
- Supine position, foot free at the end of the table, with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to neutralize rotation and present the medial/dorsal 1st MTP squarely to the surgeon.
- Thigh or calf tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination.
- Critically, **position and drape the foot so the sole can be visualized or simulated against a flat surface intraoperatively** (a flat board or the base of the C-arm works well) — this is how dorsiflexion angle relative to the floor is judged, and is more reliable than eyeballing the joint alone.
- Mini C-arm set up for AP and lateral views of the 1st MTP without breaking sterility.
- Mark the dorsal or dorsomedial incision over the 1st MTP joint.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a dorsal or dorsomedial longitudinal incision over the 1st MTP joint, protecting the EHL tendon and dorsomedial cutaneous nerve as in a cheilectomy approach.
2. Incise the capsule longitudinally, expose the joint, and perform a thorough synovectomy and release of capsular/collateral ligament adhesions circumferentially to allow full mobilization of the joint for preparation and positioning.
3. Prepare the metatarsal head and proximal phalanx base articular surfaces for fusion — either with flat parallel resection cuts (predictable planar apposition, but sacrifices length and requires careful matching of the cut angles to the planned fusion position) or with reciprocal reamers/"cup and cone" preparation (preserves bone length, self-centers, and allows the position to be fine-tuned by rotating the phalanx on the metatarsal head after reaming, since the round-on-round surfaces remain congruent through a range of positions).
4. Remove all remaining cartilage down to bleeding subchondral bone; perforate the subchondral plate with a small drill bit ("fish-scaling" or multiple drill holes) to promote a vascular healing response, particularly if using a flat-cut technique.
5. Provisionally reduce the joint and pin with a single K-wire or use a dedicated positioning clamp.
6. Position the fusion at **~15–20° dorsiflexion relative to the floor** (approximately 10–15° relative to the 1st metatarsal shaft), **5–15° valgus**, and **neutral rotation** — check dorsiflexion against a flat surface simulating the floor (not against the metatarsal shaft alone, which can be misleading if the shaft has its own deformity), and check valgus/rotation by inspecting the nail plate orientation relative to the lesser toes and the pulp of the hallux relative to the 2nd toe.
7. Confirm position with fluoroscopy (lateral view for dorsiflexion, AP for valgus) before committing to definitive fixation.
8. Achieve definitive fixation with contemporary constructs — commonly crossed lag screws from the phalanx into the metatarsal head, or a dorsal compression plate combined with an interfragmentary compression screw placed independently of the plate for added compression across the fusion site.
9. Take the construct through gentle passive stress to confirm rigid, gap-free fixation, and re-check final position with fluoroscopy in both planes.
10. Address any residual first-ray deformity (e.g., a bunionette-type prominence, or persistent IP-level hallux valgus interphalangeus requiring an Akin) before closure if planned.
11. Irrigate, repair the capsule, close in layers, and apply a supportive but non-restrictive dressing/splint that preserves access for early wound checks while protecting the fusion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a flat, rigid reference surface (a board, tray, or the C-arm base) intraoperatively to judge dorsiflexion relative to the floor — judging it against the metatarsal shaft alone is a common source of malposition when the shaft itself is deformed.
- Judge rotation by inspecting the hallux nail plate — it should be roughly coplanar with, or very slightly externally rotated relative to, the lesser toe nail plates; excess internal or external rotation is easy to miss on imaging alone.
- Cup-and-cone reaming allows position to be adjusted after preparation without recutting bone — favor this technique when precise position or preservation of ray length is a priority.
- Confirm final position with the patient's foot simulated in a weight-bearing attitude (or against a flat surface) before final fixation, not just on the operating table with the leg elevated — apparent dorsiflexion can look different once gravity and floor contact are simulated.
- Perforate the subchondral plate on both sides even with a reamer/cup-and-cone technique if bleeding bone is not clearly visualized — this measurably improves fusion biology.
- Placing an independent compression screw across the fusion site in addition to a dorsal plate (rather than relying on the plate alone) improves compression and has been associated with the highest reported union rates in comparative constructs.
- Address hallux valgus interphalangeus with a concurrent Akin osteotomy at the same setting if present — it is far easier to correct simultaneously than to revise afterward.
- Counsel and plan for the possibility of a prominent hardware requiring removal later — dorsal plates in particular can be symptomatic under shoe pressure in a thin dorsal soft-tissue envelope.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Excess dorsiflexion** at fusion causes IP joint/plate-level overload and shoe-wear difficulty.
- **Insufficient dorsiflexion or plantarflexion** causes painful push-off and transfer metatarsalgia to the lesser toes — fusion angle precision matters as much as achieving union.
- Nonunion occurs in roughly **3–10%** depending on series and fixation.
- Permanent loss of 1st MTP motion alters push-off mechanics and limits high-heel shoe-wear preference — counsel patients preoperatively; this trade-off is generally well tolerated functionally but should not be a surprise postoperatively.
- Reserve Keller resection arthroplasty for genuinely low-demand patients — it is not a substitute for arthrodesis in an active patient given its transfer metatarsalgia and cock-up deformity risk.
- Malrotation is easy to miss intraoperatively (imaging planes do not capture it well) and is a common source of postoperative dissatisfaction even with solid union and correct dorsiflexion/valgus — deliberately assess nail-plate rotation before final fixation.
- Prominent hardware (particularly dorsal plates) can require later removal.
- EHL tendon adhesion/irritation and dorsomedial cutaneous nerve injury are recognized risks of the approach.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: non-weight-bearing or heel-weight-bearing in a boot.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive weight-bearing in a boot.
- Transition to a supportive shoe once union is confirmed, typically **8–10 weeks radiographically** (clinical union often trails slightly).
- Return to low-impact activity **~10–12 weeks**; high-impact/running only after solid union, often **3–4 months**.
- Union rates of **90–97%** are achieved with contemporary fixation.
- General return-to-sport gating: radiographic union confirmed, near-full pain-free active ROM versus the contralateral side, pain-free single-leg heel-rise/hop, swelling resolved, graduated walk → run → sport-specific drills; contact/pivoting sport typically not cleared before **12–16 weeks** for fusion procedures.
