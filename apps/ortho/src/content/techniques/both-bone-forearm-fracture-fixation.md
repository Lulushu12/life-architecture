## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Displaced diaphyseal both-bone forearm fractures (radius and ulna shaft) in adults — essentially all displaced adult diaphyseal forearm fractures merit operative fixation given radiographic and functional data.
- Fractures with angulation >10° or rotational malalignment >10° on either bone, or displacement that would produce these on healing — malunion of this magnitude measurably reduces forearm pronosupination.
- Open both-bone forearm fractures (after debridement) — internal fixation (or, for grossly contaminated wounds, IM nailing/external fixation as a staged approach) restores length/rotation and stabilizes soft tissue.
- Segmental or comminuted diaphyseal fractures, especially with associated floating elbow or polytrauma, where soft-tissue coverage or multiple-injury logistics favor a less invasive strategy (IM nailing).
- Both-bone forearm fracture as a component of a Monteggia, Galeazzi, terrible-triad, or Essex-Lopresti pattern — fix per the priorities of the combined injury (see the relevant technique guide), but the same dual-plating/rod principles apply to the diaphyseal component.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Active local infection or a grossly contaminated open wound not yet debrided — provisional external fixation until soft tissues allow definitive plating.
- Severe segmental bone loss precluding primary plate fixation — consider a staged approach (external fixation → bone grafting/definitive fixation, or vascularized bone transfer for large defects).
- Medically unstable polytrauma patient (damage-control orthopedics) — temporize with external fixation or splinting until physiology allows definitive surgery.
- Truly non-displaced, minimally angulated fractures in a genuinely low-demand patient — rare in adults; non-operative management is not appropriate as definitive treatment for displaced both-bone forearm fractures generally.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP/lateral/oblique forearm radiographs including the elbow and wrist joints above and below the fracture (mandatory — image the joint above and below to avoid missing an associated Monteggia/Galeazzi component). CT is rarely needed for simple diaphyseal fractures but useful for segmental/comminuted or intra-articular extension.
- **Templating:** measure fracture length/obliquity and plan plate length (typically a 7- to 8-hole 3.5mm plate for the radius, similarly sized for the ulna, longer for comminuted/segmental patterns) to achieve adequate working length and screw purchase — generally at least 6-8 cortices of fixation on each side of the fracture.
- **Implant selection:** 3.5mm dynamic compression plate (DCP) or locking compression plate (LCP) for both bones; reserve locking constructs for osteoporotic bone, comminution, or metaphyseal extension.
- **IM nail alternative:** if planning nailing, confirm canal diameter/nail sizing on radiographs and have both a radius and ulna nail system available with appropriate end caps; confirm the nail system allows rotational control (interlocking, or a nail with an anatomic bend matched to the radial bow).
- **Case-specific planning:** review the contralateral forearm radiograph if the radial bow or rotational landmarks are difficult to judge on the injured side; plan tourniquet use and duration; discuss regional vs. general anesthesia with the anesthesia team.
- **Equipment checklist:** small fragment set, pointed/serrated reduction clamps, radiolucent hand table, mini-fluoroscopy (C-arm), pneumatic tourniquet, bone hook, dental pick for comminuted fragments, bone graft/substitute on standby for defects encountered intraoperatively.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or regional (supraclavicular/axillary brachial plexus block) — regional block is well suited to this extremity-only procedure and provides useful postoperative analgesia; general anesthesia is often preferred if a tourniquet will be inflated for a prolonged period or the patient cannot tolerate an awake block.
- **Position:** supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, shoulder abducted ~80-90°, elbow flexed, forearm pronated for ulna exposure and pronated/supinated as needed intraoperatively for radius exposure via the Henry (volar) or Thompson (dorsal) approach.
- **Tourniquet:** pneumatic tourniquet on the upper arm, exsanguinate with an Esmarch bandage or elevation, inflate to standard limb pressure (250-300 mmHg, or ~100 mmHg above systolic); document tourniquet time, aim to keep it under ~2 hours, deflate and achieve hemostasis before closure if the case runs long.
- **Fluoroscopy:** mini C-arm positioned across the table from the surgeon, draped into the field, used to confirm reduction, plate position, screw length, and rotational alignment (compare the bicipital tuberosity-radial styloid relationship) before and after fixation.
- **Draping:** standard extremity prep (chlorhexidine or iodine-based) from above the tourniquet to the fingertips, with a circumferential drape allowing free forearm rotation intraoperatively.
- **Landmarks marked:** palpate and mark the subcutaneous border of the ulna (for the ulnar incision), the mobile wad and Lister's tubercle (dorsal radius), and the FCR interval (volar radius/Henry approach) before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**A. Ulna fixation** (typically performed first if simple, as it re-establishes a length/rotation reference for the radius; sequence can be reversed if the radius fracture is simpler or more proximal)

1. Make a longitudinal incision directly over the subcutaneous border of the ulna, centered on the fracture.
2. Elevate the flexor carpi ulnaris and extensor carpi ulnaris subperiosteally off the ulnar border only as much as needed to expose the fracture — minimize periosteal stripping to protect blood supply and reduce nonunion/infection risk.
3. Identify and clean the fracture ends; irrigate out hematoma and debris.
4. Reduce the fracture anatomically using pointed reduction clamps; for simple transverse/short oblique patterns, use interfragmentary lag screw technique first if the pattern allows, then neutralize with a plate.
5. Apply a 3.5mm DCP or LCP along the subcutaneous (dorsal or ulnar) border of the ulna, contoured to the bone's natural gentle curve; achieve a minimum of 6-8 cortices of fixation on each side of the fracture (typically 3 bicortical screws per fragment for simple fractures, more for comminuted patterns using bridging/locking technique).
6. Confirm reduction and hardware position on fluoroscopy (AP and lateral).

**B. Radius fixation**

7. Choose the approach: **Henry (volar, anterior)** along the interval between brachioradialis/FCR and pronator teres/flexor digitorum superficialis is preferred for most diaphyseal fractures (extensile, avoids the PIN risk of the dorsal approach in the proximal third); **Thompson (dorsal)** between ECRB and EDC is an alternative, particularly for very proximal-third fractures, but carries higher risk to the posterior interosseous nerve (PIN), which must be identified and protected as it passes through the supinator.
8. If using the Henry approach: incise along a line from the biceps tendon to the radial styloid; develop the interval between brachioradialis (radial) and FCR/pronator teres (ulnar); ligate/coagulate the radial recurrent vessels ("leash of Henry") crossing the field near the proximal third — this is the most common source of troublesome bleeding in this approach.
9. For proximal-third fractures, **supinate the forearm** to move the PIN away from the field before subperiosteally elevating the supinator off the radius — this is the single most important maneuver protecting the PIN during volar exposure of the proximal radius.
10. Expose the fracture, again minimizing periosteal stripping of comminuted fragments (preserve soft-tissue attachments on butterfly fragments rather than stripping them free — devascularized fragments risk nonunion).
11. Reduce anatomically with pointed clamps; use provisional K-wire fixation if needed to hold rotation while applying the plate.
12. Apply a 3.5mm DCP/LCP along the flat volar (or dorsal, if Thompson approach) surface of the radius, again achieving a minimum of 6-8 cortices of fixation per side.
13. **Restore the radial bow:** confirm the plate and reduction reproduce the normal lateral bow of the radius (apex-radial) — the bicipital tuberosity-radial styloid relationship (normally the tuberosity points roughly opposite the styloid processes when the forearm is in neutral/lateral view) is a useful intraoperative rotational check; loss of the radial bow is the single most important technical determinant of postoperative pronosupination loss.
14. **IM nailing alternative** (radius and/or ulna): ream or use unreamed technique per system, insert the nail retrograde (radius, through a dorsal or volar entry point) or antegrade (ulna, through the olecranon tip), restore length and rotation under fluoroscopy using indirect reduction techniques (traction, percutaneous joysticks/K-wires), and lock proximally and distally to control rotation — reserve for segmental/comminuted fractures, contaminated open wounds, or damage-control scenarios; be meticulous about rotational control, since IM nailing is less able to precisely restore the radial bow than plating.

**C. Closure and final checks**

15. Take the forearm through a full passive pronation-supination arc under direct vision to confirm no hardware impingement at the PRUJ or DRUJ and no tethering of the interosseous membrane.
16. Obtain final AP/lateral fluoroscopy of both bones confirming anatomic alignment, plate position, and appropriate screw lengths (no dorsal cortex penetration into the extensor compartment).
17. Irrigate copiously, achieve hemostasis (deflate tourniquet if not already done), close fascia/subcutaneous layers separately for each incision, close skin, and apply a well-padded splint with the elbow at 90° and forearm in neutral rotation.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Fix the ulna first when it is the simpler fracture — a well-reduced, well-fixed ulna gives a length and rotation reference for reducing the radius.
- Use the bicipital tuberosity-radial styloid relationship intraoperatively as a quick rotational check before final tightening of the radius plate — compare with a preoperative contralateral forearm film if rotation is difficult to judge from the injured side alone.
- Ligate the radial recurrent vessels ("leash of Henry") proactively during the volar approach rather than reacting to bleeding — they cross the field predictably near the radial neck/proximal radius.
- Always supinate the forearm before dissecting around the proximal radius through a volar approach to protect the PIN as it courses through the supinator.
- Preserve periosteal/soft-tissue attachments on butterfly fragments — do not free them completely for "cleaner" visualization; devascularized fragments are the most common technical driver of nonunion in comminuted patterns.
- Use separate incisions for radius and ulna even when both fractures are at a similar level and a single incision seems tempting — bridging soft tissue between the two bones is a recognized risk factor for radioulnar (cross-union) synostosis.
- When comminution precludes anatomic reduction of a fragment, prioritize restoring overall length, rotation, and the radial bow over anatomically reducing every fragment — a bridging plate technique with indirect reduction preserves fragment vascularity better than forcing every piece into place.
- Take the forearm through a full rotational arc before closing to confirm no hardware-related PRUJ/DRUJ impingement — this fast check prevents a return to the OR for a late rotational block.
- If IM nailing is chosen, use fluoroscopic rotational landmarks (radial styloid profile, bicipital tuberosity) even more carefully than with plating, since the nail does not visually reproduce the radial bow the way a contoured plate does.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Even modest loss of the radial bow or rotational malalignment measurably reduces the pronosupination arc — restoring it is the primary technical goal, not simply achieving union.
- A single-incision approach to both bones is a risk factor for radioulnar synostosis; use separate incisions.
- 2024 meta-analyses comparing IM nailing vs. ORIF show broadly comparable union rates, with IMN offering shorter operative time/smaller incisions, but IMN has been associated with a higher malrotation/malunion risk in some series if not meticulously performed.
- Compartment syndrome is a highest-risk complication in high-energy both-bone forearm fractures and floating-elbow patterns — disproportionate pain and pain on passive stretch trump pulse status; missed compartment syndrome evolves to Volkmann's ischemic contracture.
- **PIN injury** — most common with the Thompson (dorsal) approach to the proximal radius or with inadequate supination during volar proximal dissection; identify the nerve directly if the exposure extends near the supinator rather than relying on landmarks alone in ambiguous cases.
- **Radioulnar cross-union (synostosis)** — beyond single-incision technique, risk is increased by shared traumatic hematoma or bone graft placed across the interosseous space, a high-energy/comminuted injury, and delayed fixation; avoid placing bone graft material where it could bridge the interosseous space.
- **Infection** — higher risk with open fractures and prolonged surgical time; adhere to standard open-fracture principles (early debridement, timely antibiotics) when applicable.
- **Nonunion** is uncommon with modern rigid plating (<5% in most series) but is more likely with inadequate fixation rigidity, excessive periosteal stripping, or a comminuted pattern bridged without adequate stability.
- Corrective osteotomy is the salvage option for symptomatic malunion once healed; adults rarely remodel meaningfully, so earlier correction is often reasonable once union is solid.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0-2 weeks:** splint/sling for comfort; begin gentle elbow and wrist AROM early (adjacent joints), guarded forearm rotation.
- **2-6 weeks:** progressive AROM of forearm rotation and adjacent joints; avoid axial loading (no weight-bearing through the arm) until union is progressing.
- **6-12 weeks:** progressive strengthening once radiographic union (typically 8-12 weeks for diaphyseal forearm fractures with rigid plate fixation); light functional use. Nonunion of the forearm diaphysis is uncommon with modern plating (<5% in most series), though higher with nailing in some series if rotational stability is inadequate.
- **Return to work/sport:** light duty ~6 weeks; full manual labor/contact sport 3-4 months, contingent on union and rotational strength.
