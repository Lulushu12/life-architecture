## Indications

Progressive neurogenic claudication limiting function despite 3–6 months of conservative care (flexion-biased exercise, weight loss, walking-pole gait training, NSAIDs) in the absence of a progressive neurological deficit warranting earlier intervention. Decompression is performed with or without fusion depending on stability criteria.

## Positioning & setup

Posterior approach; technique choice (open vs minimally invasive tubular) depends on the extent of decompression required and whether facet/midline structures need to be preserved.

## Steps

1. **Select the decompression technique.** Laminectomy is the classic, wide-exposure option but carries greater risk of iatrogenic instability from facet/interspinous ligament disruption. Laminotomy/hemilaminectomy is more limited. Bilateral decompression via unilateral laminotomy (the "over-the-top" technique) preserves the midline posterior tension band and spinous process while still achieving bilateral canal/lateral recess decompression, reducing iatrogenic instability. Minimally invasive tubular decompression is a further muscle-sparing option.
2. **Preserve as much of the pars interarticularis, facet joint capsule, and midline structures as possible**, particularly when fusion is not planned, to minimize iatrogenic instability.
3. **Decide whether to add fusion.** Add fusion when: pre-existing spondylolisthesis has dynamic instability, extensive facetectomy (>50%) is anticipated to iatrogenically destabilize the segment, the procedure is a same-level revision decompression, adjacent scoliosis/deformity requires correction, or predominant mechanical back pain is present.

## Pitfalls

- Iatrogenic instability risk rises with facetectomy >50%, complete bilateral pars resection, or destabilization of an already borderline-unstable segment — favor facet/pars-preserving techniques (over-the-top, tubular) whenever feasible.
- Dural tear/CSF leak occurs in roughly 2–3% of open decompression; manage with primary repair or sealant, flat bed rest 24–48h if repaired, and watch for pseudomeningocele/headache.
- Wrong-level surgery is mitigated by intraoperative localization imaging and a formal surgical time-out with level confirmation, particularly in obese patients and transitional anatomy.
- Age alone is not a contraindication — decompression outcomes in octogenarians can be favorable — but comorbidity burden, bone quality, and frailty status should drive construct choice (favor decompression alone or a facet-sparing technique over fusion where reasonable) and realistic goal-setting around walking tolerance rather than pain elimination.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2:** mobilize day 0–1; no brace required routinely; avoid heavy lifting/twisting.
- **Weeks 2–6:** progressive walking program (particularly valuable given the claudication indication); core stabilization initiated.
- **Weeks 6–12:** strengthening progression; return to full activity/work by 6–12 weeks depending on occupation; claudication-related walking tolerance is typically reassessed as the primary functional outcome.
- Routine imaging: standing radiographs at 6–12 weeks if instability was a pre-operative concern; obtain earlier/additional imaging for progressive deformity or new mechanical back pain.
