## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Progressive neurogenic claudication limiting function despite 3–6 months of conservative care (flexion-biased exercise, weight loss, walking-pole gait training, NSAIDs) in the absence of a progressive neurological deficit warranting earlier intervention. Also indicated for progressive motor deficit or cauda equina syndrome from stenosis (managed emergently — see the dedicated CES technique guide). Decompression is performed with or without fusion depending on stability criteria.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Active systemic or local infection and uncontrolled medical comorbidity precluding safe general (or, where used, regional) anaesthesia are contraindications to elective decompression.
- Predominant vascular claudication without a concordant structural stenosis on imaging is a contraindication — investigate and treat the vascular cause instead; a positive ankle-brachial index or diminished pulses should prompt vascular referral before proceeding.
- Imaging findings of stenosis without a concordant clinical picture (asymptomatic incidental stenosis) are not an indication for surgery — degenerative MRI findings are near-ubiquitous with age and must be correlated with the clinical exam.
- Gross, uncorrected dynamic instability on flexion-extension films is a relative contraindication to decompression *alone* — this population should be planned for concurrent fusion rather than decompression in isolation (see the companion degenerative spondylolisthesis fusion guide).
- Severe multilevel stenosis in a very high-surgical-risk patient may be relatively better served by a staged or minimally invasive/tubular strategy, or by an interspinous spacer in carefully selected cases, rather than a single extensive open decompression — discuss the risk-benefit trade-off explicitly with frail or heavily comorbid patients.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI to define the levels and pattern of stenosis (central, lateral recess, foraminal) and to grade severity (Schizas grade C/D correlates best with symptomatic, surgery-warranting stenosis when clinically concordant); standing lateral and flexion-extension radiographs at every planned level to screen for occult instability that would change the plan toward fusion, since static MRI alone will miss this. CT is useful for characterising bony foraminal stenosis and facet morphology, and for surgical planning in patients with prior instrumentation or unusual anatomy.
- **Levels to be decompressed:** map each stenotic level against the patient's symptom pattern (claudication distribution, dermatomal features if present) rather than decompressing every radiographically abnormal level indiscriminately — decompressing an asymptomatic, mildly stenotic level adds morbidity (facet violation, dural tear risk) without benefit.
- **Instability screening:** explicitly document flexion-extension findings (translation <4 mm and <10–15° angulation is reassuring for stability) at each level to be decompressed — this determines whether an over-the-top/facet-sparing approach alone is appropriate or whether concurrent instrumentation should be planned.
- **Comorbidity and frailty assessment:** age alone is not a contraindication (decompression outcomes in octogenarians can be favourable), but document comorbidity burden, bone quality, and a frailty assessment (e.g., modified frailty index) to guide technique choice (favouring a facet-sparing or minimally invasive approach in frailer patients) and to set realistic goals around walking tolerance rather than complete pain elimination.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** standard posterior lumbar decompression set (Kerrison rongeurs in multiple sizes, curettes, nerve root retractors, high-speed burr with a matchstick or acorn tip for undercutting), bipolar cautery, and — for minimally invasive tubular technique — a graduated tubular dilator/retractor system with a compatible headlight source or microscope/endoscope. Fluoroscopy or a C-arm should be available for level localisation regardless of technique.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anaesthesia:** general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation is standard; some centres use spinal/regional anaesthesia for shorter single-level decompressions in appropriately selected patients, though this is institution- and surgeon-dependent and less common for multilevel cases.
- **Positioning:** prone on a radiolucent spinal frame (Jackson table, Wilson frame, or Andrews frame) with the abdomen free to reduce epidural venous pressure and bleeding; hips and knees flexed to reduce lumbar lordosis, which opens the interlaminar spaces and facilitates decompression — this flexed position is particularly helpful in stenosis surgery since it is the same position that relieves the patient's claudication clinically.
- **Table setup:** pad all pressure points (chest, iliac crests, knees, shins, feet); protect the eyes and free the genitalia and abdomen; confirm the frame will not obstruct fluoroscopy at any planned level.
- **Draping and imaging setup:** obtain a lateral spot radiograph or fluoroscopic image after positioning to confirm the levels before prepping, and mark the incision accordingly; for multilevel decompressions, mark each level's approximate skin projection before draping to plan incision length.
- **Surface landmarks:** palpate and mark the iliac crests (approximate L4-5 disc level), posterior superior iliac spines, and the midline spinous process line before prep, then confirm the specific levels radiographically once positioned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Time-out and level confirmation.** Confirm patient, procedure, and level(s) with fluoroscopic/spot radiograph localisation before incision — this is particularly important in obese patients and those with transitional lumbosacral anatomy, where level-counting by palpation alone is unreliable.
2. **Exposure.** Midline posterior incision centred over the planned level(s); subperiosteal dissection of the paraspinal musculature off the spinous processes and lamina with a Cobb elevator, extending laterally only as far as needed for the planned technique (further for a formal laminectomy, more limited for a tubular or over-the-top approach) — over-stripping the musculature beyond what is needed adds morbidity without benefit.
3. **Select the decompression technique** based on stenosis pattern, instability status, and patient factors:
   - **Laminectomy** (classic, wide exposure): remove the spinous process and bilateral lamina at the affected level(s) along with the ligamentum flavum, giving direct visualisation of the thecal sac and both lateral recesses. Carries the greatest risk of iatrogenic instability from facet/interspinous ligament disruption if not performed carefully.
   - **Laminotomy/hemilaminectomy:** more limited removal of lamina on one side, preserving more of the midline and contralateral structures — appropriate for unilateral or asymmetric stenosis.
   - **Bilateral decompression via unilateral laminotomy ("over-the-top" technique):** approach from the more symptomatic or more stenotic side. Perform a laminotomy and medial facetectomy on that side, then use a burr to undercut the base of the spinous process and the contralateral lamina from the ipsilateral side, working under the spinous process to decompress the contralateral lateral recess and central canal while preserving the midline posterior tension band (spinous process, interspinous/supraspinous ligaments) and the contralateral facet capsule intact. This is the workhorse technique for reducing iatrogenic instability risk while still achieving bilateral decompression in a patient not planned for fusion.
   - **Minimally invasive tubular decompression:** dock a graduated tubular retractor over the lamina at the target level under fluoroscopic guidance through a paramedian muscle-splitting approach, then perform the equivalent laminotomy/undercutting decompression through the tube using a microscope or endoscope for visualisation — further reduces approach-related muscle disruption compared with an open over-the-top technique, at the cost of a steeper learning curve and a more limited field of view for multilevel disease.
4. **Perform the central and lateral recess decompression.** Remove the ligamentum flavum with Kerrison rongeurs, working from a normal, non-stenotic area toward the stenotic segment to stay oriented in a canal that may be markedly narrowed. Undercut the medial facet with a burr or Kerrison as needed to decompress the lateral recess, taking care to preserve at least the lateral half to two-thirds of each facet joint wherever possible.
5. **Decompress the foramen if indicated.** For concordant foraminal stenosis, extend the decompression laterally with a foraminotomy — undercutting the pars and pedicle margin as needed to free the exiting root, while being mindful that aggressive foraminal work further erodes the pars and adds to cumulative instability risk.
6. **Preserve stabilising structures wherever possible.** Throughout the decompression, particularly when fusion is not planned, actively preserve as much of the pars interarticularis, facet joint capsule, and midline ligamentous structures as the adequacy of decompression allows — this is a running priority throughout the case, not a single step.
7. **Confirm adequacy of decompression.** After bony and ligamentous decompression, visually and manually palpate the thecal sac and traversing/exiting roots at each level to confirm they are free and pulsatile, and confirm the lateral recess and foramen are adequately opened with a nerve hook or blunt probe.
8. **Decide whether to add fusion**, reassessing intraoperatively as well as from pre-operative planning. Add fusion when: pre-existing spondylolisthesis has dynamic instability, extensive facetectomy (>50% at any level) was required and is anticipated to iatrogenically destabilise the segment, the procedure is a same-level revision decompression, adjacent scoliosis/deformity requires correction, or predominant mechanical back pain is present. If any of these emerge only once the decompression is underway, be prepared to convert to an instrumented plan rather than accepting an unstable segment.
9. **Haemostasis.** Achieve meticulous haemostasis with bipolar cautery and haemostatic agents before closure; epidural bleeding is a common cause of a post-operative haematoma if not adequately controlled.
10. **Closure.** Close the fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers; consider a drain for larger multilevel exposures with more extensive epidural bleeding surface area.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Start the ligamentum flavum resection from a clearly normal, non-stenotic area (proximal or distal to the tightest segment) and work toward the stenosis — this keeps you oriented to the epidural plane in a canal too narrow to safely identify landmarks by working outward from the middle.
- When undercutting for the over-the-top technique, take the burr down methodically in thin passes rather than trying to remove the full thickness in one pass — this reduces the risk of plunging into the canal in a stenotic segment where the working space beneath the lamina is minimal.
- Use a matchstick or acorn burr tip under the lamina/facet with the flavum still partially in place as a protective buffer over the dura during the initial undercutting, removing the flavum definitively only once the bony work in that area is complete.
- In severe stenosis, the epidural fat and venous plexus are often effaced or absent — expect more adherent dura and a higher baseline dural tear risk than in a straightforward discectomy, and proceed with correspondingly more caution during flavum removal.
- Decompress the concordant levels dictated by the clinical exam and imaging correlation, not every radiographically stenotic level — over-decompression adds morbidity (more facet violation, more dural tear risk, more theoretical instability) without added benefit at asymptomatic levels.
- When a foraminotomy is needed, decompress the exiting root under direct vision by following it laterally from the pedicle rather than working blindly lateral to the disc space — this reduces the risk of missing residual compression or injuring the root.
- Reassess for instability throughout the case, not only pre-operatively — a facet that turns out to require more than 50% resection to achieve adequate decompression should prompt intraoperative reconsideration of adding instrumentation, so keep this conversation (and, ideally, instrumentation availability) open pre-operatively with the patient and team even for cases planned as decompression-alone.
- In frail or osteoporotic patients, favour the least destabilising technique that still achieves adequate decompression (tubular or over-the-top over formal laminectomy) — the goal in this population is durable symptom relief with the lowest added morbidity, not a "definitive" wide exposure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Iatrogenic instability risk rises with facetectomy >50%, complete bilateral pars resection, or destabilization of an already borderline-unstable segment — favor facet/pars-preserving techniques (over-the-top, tubular) whenever feasible.
- Dural tear/CSF leak occurs in roughly 2–3% of open decompression; manage with primary repair or sealant, flat bed rest 24–48h if repaired, and watch for pseudomeningocele/headache. Dura in severe stenosis is often thinned and adherent to the flavum, raising tear risk further — proceed deliberately during flavum removal in these cases.
- Wrong-level surgery is mitigated by intraoperative localization imaging and a formal surgical time-out with level confirmation, particularly in obese patients and transitional anatomy.
- Age alone is not a contraindication — decompression outcomes in octogenarians can be favorable — but comorbidity burden, bone quality, and frailty status should drive construct choice (favor decompression alone or a facet-sparing technique over fusion where reasonable) and realistic goal-setting around walking tolerance rather than pain elimination.
- Epidural haematoma is an uncommon but important cause of early post-operative neurological deterioration; maintain a low threshold for emergent MRI and re-exploration for a new or progressive post-operative deficit.
- Missed or under-decompressed lateral recess/foraminal stenosis is a recognised cause of persistent post-operative claudication or radiculopathy despite an apparently adequate central decompression — confirm lateral recess and foraminal patency explicitly with a probe before closing, not just the central canal.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2:** mobilize day 0–1; no brace required routinely; avoid heavy lifting/twisting.
- **Weeks 2–6:** progressive walking program (particularly valuable given the claudication indication); core stabilization initiated.
- **Weeks 6–12:** strengthening progression; return to full activity/work by 6–12 weeks depending on occupation; claudication-related walking tolerance is typically reassessed as the primary functional outcome.
- Routine imaging: standing radiographs at 6–12 weeks if instability was a pre-operative concern; obtain earlier/additional imaging for progressive deformity or new mechanical back pain.
