## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Arthroplasty is standard of care for displaced (Garden III–IV) femoral neck fractures in patients over roughly 60–65 with low-to-moderate functional demand: fixation in this group carries unacceptable reoperation rates (30–40% for AVN/nonunion/screw cutout) versus ~5–10% arthroplasty revision at comparable follow-up. Arthroplasty is also used when reduction fails or the fracture is comminuted in younger patients initially planned for fixation.

**Contraindications/relative contraindications:**

- Active local or systemic infection — treat the septic source first; a temporizing girdlestone or delayed arthroplasty may be needed in select cases.
- Non-ambulatory, severely cognitively impaired, or comfort-care patients where the goal is comfort rather than restoration of ambulation — non-operative pain management is a reasonable alternative to discuss with family.
- Severe acetabular arthritis or dysplasia favors THA over hemiarthroplasty regardless of age/demand, since a hemiarthroplasty head will articulate poorly against an already-arthritic or dysplastic socket.
- High baseline dislocation risk (neuromuscular disease, cognitive impairment with poor precaution compliance, prior contralateral hip instability) favors hemiarthroplasty or a dual-mobility/constrained construct over standard THA.
- Severe cardiopulmonary instability is a relative contraindication to cementation (bone cement implantation syndrome risk) — favor an uncemented stem or ensure anesthesia is prepared to manage hemodynamic changes during cementation.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Templating:** template the contralateral hip on a calibrated AP pelvis radiograph for femoral offset, leg length, and stem/head sizing; confirm acetabular morphology to rule out occult arthritis that would favor THA over hemiarthroplasty.
- **Implant selection:** decide hemiarthroplasty vs. THA and cemented vs. uncemented stem before the day of surgery (see decision framework below) — do not leave this as an intraoperative decision when avoidable, since it affects positioning, draping, and consent.
- **Head/liner selection for THA:** consider a dual-mobility construct in patients with elevated dislocation risk (cognitive impairment, neuromuscular disease, anticipated non-compliance with precautions).
- **Equipment:** full hip arthroplasty instrument set (broaches/reamers, trial components, cement gun/pressurizer if cementing), fluoroscopy or a radiolucent table if intraoperative imaging is planned, cell salvage/blood conservation setup if significant blood loss is anticipated.
- **Medical optimization:** confirm anesthesia is aware of and prepared for bone cement implantation syndrome risk if a cemented stem is planned (hypotension, hypoxia, arrhythmia at the time of cementation) — have vasopressors immediately available and consider a brief pause in cementing if hemodynamics are unstable at the time of component insertion.
- **Blood management:** type and crossmatch; confirm tranexamic acid dosing (topical or IV, typically 1 g at incision ± a second dose) with anesthesia, applying individualized caution in patients with recent VTE or high thrombotic risk.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** spinal or general anesthesia per patient factors; regional block (fascia iliaca/femoral nerve) as an analgesic adjunct is standard in the fragility-fracture pathway.
- **Positioning:** lateral decubitus for a posterior or posterolateral approach (most widely used, with capsular repair), or supine for an anterolateral (Hardinge/modified) or direct anterior approach — approach is surgeon-preference-driven, with no clear outcome superiority once posterior capsular repair is performed.
  - For the **posterior approach**: lateral decubitus, pelvis stabilized with anterior and posterior peg supports or a bean bag, ensuring the pelvis remains perpendicular to the floor (a rotated pelvis is a leading cause of component malposition). Operative leg draped free to allow full range of motion for dislocation, trialing, and stability testing.
  - For the **direct anterior approach**: supine, often on a specialized traction table allowing hip extension/external rotation for femoral exposure, with fluoroscopy positioned for intraoperative component-position and leg-length confirmation.
- **Draping:** wide prep from the iliac crest to below the knee, allowing full limb manipulation for trialing and dislocation/reduction maneuvers.
- **Landmarks:** mark the greater trochanter, anterior superior iliac spine, and the planned incision (posterior: centered over the greater trochanter, curving posteriorly proximally; anterior: 2 cm lateral and 2 cm distal to the ASIS along the interval).
- **Tourniquet:** not used (proximal femoral/pelvic-adjacent procedure).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the hemiarthroplasty vs. THA decision** using the HEALTH trial framework (NEJM 2019, 1495 patients ≥50 years with displaced femoral neck fractures): the primary outcome (secondary hip procedure within 24 months) occurred in ~8% of both groups with no significant difference; serious adverse events trended higher with THA (41.8% vs. 36.7%); functional/pain scores modestly favored THA but fell below the threshold of clinical importance.
   - **Hemiarthroplasty** — appropriate default for most low-demand, older (>75–80), or cognitively impaired patients.
   - **THA** — favored for patients who were ambulatory without aids, cognitively intact, with reasonable life expectancy and higher functional demand, accepting a modestly higher dislocation/reoperation-for-instability risk in exchange for better long-term function.
2. **Position and prep/drape** per the chosen approach as above.
3. **Approach and expose the hip capsule.** For a posterior approach: incise the fascia lata/gluteus maximus fibers in line with the skin incision, identify and protect the sciatic nerve, tag and detach the short external rotators (piriformis, obturator internus/externus, gemelli) with a cuff of tissue for later repair, and perform a capsulotomy — preserve as much capsule as possible for repair, using a T-shaped or inverted-T capsulotomy that leaves repairable tissue margins rather than an extensive capsulectomy.
4. **Dislocate the hip** (already partially destabilized by the fracture) and remove the femoral head fragment; measure it directly to cross-check templated component sizing.
5. **Prepare the acetabulum (THA only):** ream sequentially to the templated size, preserving the subchondral bone and achieving even reaming across the entire socket; trial and place the acetabular component at the templated abduction (~40°) and anteversion (~15–20°, adjusted for approach and pelvic position) — pelvic rotation on the table is the most common source of malpositioned abduction/anteversion, so confirm pelvic position visually and/or with a positioning guide before final component impaction.
6. **Prepare the femur.** Open the canal at the piriformis fossa/trochanteric entry point, broach sequentially to the templated size while maintaining the planned version (typically matching native anteversion, roughly 10–15°, adjusted per approach), and trial the stem with trial head/neck components.
7. **Assess trial stability and leg length.** Reduce the trial construct and assess range of motion (flexion, internal/external rotation in flexion and extension, abduction), soft-tissue tension, and leg length against the contralateral limb before committing to final component insertion — this is the last opportunity to adjust offset/length/version cheaply.
8. **Cement or press-fit the final femoral stem** per the pre-operative plan. For cemented stems: prepare the canal (pulsatile lavage, dry the canal, place a distal cement restrictor), pressurize cement with a gun, and insert the stem centralized in the canal with a centralizer as needed, holding position until cement cures; alert anesthesia immediately before cementation and monitor closely for bone cement implantation syndrome (hypotension, hypoxia, arrhythmia) during and immediately after cement pressurization and stem insertion.
9. **Place the final femoral head** (bipolar/unipolar for hemiarthroplasty, or the definitive head for THA) matched to templated offset and leg length.
10. **Reduce the hip** and reassess stability through a full range of motion, checking specifically for the posterior instability arc (flexion + adduction + internal rotation) if a posterior approach was used, and for anterior instability (extension + external rotation) if an anterior/anterolateral approach was used.
11. **Repair the posterior capsule and short external rotators** (if a posterior approach was used) back to the greater trochanter through transosseous tunnels or suture anchors, using a secure locking-loop stitch technique — robust capsular repair is one of the most effective, technique-dependent steps for reducing postoperative dislocation risk with this approach.
12. **Irrigate, achieve hemostasis, and close in layers** (fascia lata, subcutaneous tissue, skin), with a drain per surgeon preference for expected blood loss.
13. **Obtain intraoperative or immediate postoperative fluoroscopic/radiographic images** confirming component position, stem alignment, and leg length before the patient leaves the table/recovers from anesthesia.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Preserve a repairable cuff of capsule and short external rotators at the initial exposure rather than performing an extensive capsulectomy — a robust locking-loop posterior capsular repair is one of the most effective, technically controllable levers for reducing dislocation risk with a posterior approach.
- Confirm pelvic position (neutral, not rotated) before final acetabular component impaction in THA — pelvic tilt/rotation on the table is the most common cause of cup malposition, more so than jig or freehand technique error.
- Measure the excised native femoral head directly against your templated component size before finalizing broach/reamer size — it is a fast, reliable intraoperative cross-check against pre-operative templating error.
- Trial extensively before final cementation/impaction — assess ROM, stability through the relevant instability arc for your approach, and leg length with trial components, since this is the cheapest point in the case to make offset/length/version adjustments.
- Alert anesthesia by name immediately before cement pressurization and stem insertion — do not cement silently; bone cement implantation syndrome is time-critical and anesthesia needs advance warning to watch for the hemodynamic signature.
- Consider a dual-mobility articulation for THA in patients with elevated baseline dislocation risk (cognitive impairment, neuromuscular disease, anticipated precaution non-compliance) — this meaningfully lowers dislocation risk in exactly the population where standard THA carries the most risk.
- When comparing capsulotomy strategies for hemiarthroplasty, an inferior or joint-capsule-preserving capsulotomy pattern (rather than a superior capsulotomy) has been associated with improved postoperative stability in recent comparative series — favor preserving as much of the capsule as the exposure allows.
- Direct the femoral head to be marginally under-corrected rather than over-lengthened when leg-length judgment is uncertain intraoperatively — patients tolerate mild shortening better than overlengthening, and revision for overlengthening is more common than for undercorrection.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Dislocation is higher with THA than hemiarthroplasty; further reduced with dual-mobility constructs or robust posterior capsular repair. An inadequately repaired or excised posterior capsule/short external rotator cuff is the most common technical driver of early posterior dislocation.
- Periprosthetic fracture risk is lower with cemented stems; uncemented stems in osteoporotic fragility-fracture bone carry a materially higher periprosthetic fracture risk both intraoperatively (during broaching/impaction) and postoperatively.
- Acetabular erosion is a hemiarthroplasty-specific long-term complication in more active patients — part of the rationale for choosing THA in higher-demand patients up front.
- Aseptic loosening is a longer-term concern, more relevant to THA given longer expected implant life.
- Serious adverse events trend higher with THA overall despite its functional advantages — individualize selection rather than defaulting to either implant.
- Structures at risk: the sciatic nerve during posterior exposure/retraction (identify and protect early, avoid aggressive posterior retractor placement); the femoral nerve with anterior/anterolateral approaches; the superior gluteal nerve with an overly proximal anterolateral (Hardinge) split (stay within roughly 3–5 cm of the trochanteric tip when splitting gluteus medius).
- Bone cement implantation syndrome (hypotension, hypoxia, arrhythmia, occasionally cardiac arrest) can occur at cementation/stem insertion — mitigate with a dried, vented canal, controlled cement pressurization, and clear anesthesia communication before each cementation step.
- Leg-length discrepancy and component malposition are largely preventable with careful trialing and pelvic-position confirmation before final component impaction — these are the most common sources of patient dissatisfaction after otherwise technically sound surgery.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) postoperative day 0–1.
- Hip precautions (posterior approach: avoid combined flexion >90° + adduction + internal rotation) for 6 weeks if a posterior approach is used without robust capsular repair; often relaxed sooner with modern capsular repair techniques and anterior approaches.
- Assisted ambulation with walker/crutches transitioning to cane by 2–4 weeks as balance allows; formal PT for gait, transfers, and strengthening starting POD1.
- Radiographic milestones: AP/lateral films at 2, 6, and 12 weeks.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks post-arthroplasty once pain-controlled and independent with transfers/short-distance ambulation.
- Driving typically resumes at 4–6 weeks post-arthroplasty (later if right-sided posterior-approach THA, given braking-reflex concerns), per surgeon assessment and local licensing regulations.
- Universal secondary-prevention bundle: falls-risk assessment, bone mineral density testing (or presumptive treatment given the fracture itself qualifies as a major osteoporotic fracture), and anti-osteoporotic pharmacotherapy initiated ideally before discharge.
