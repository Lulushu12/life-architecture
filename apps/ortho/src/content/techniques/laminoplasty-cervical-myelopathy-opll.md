## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Multilevel (≥3 level) degenerative cervical myelopathy/OPLL with preserved or lordotic alignment and predominantly posterior or multisegmental compression.
- OPLL with K-line positive status (and, ideally, favorable dynamic/flexion K-line and a lower canal-occupying ratio) — the cord can adequately migrate away from the ossified mass after canal expansion.
- Patients for whom avoiding fusion-related morbidity and preserving segmental motion is a priority, given adequate alignment.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Kyphotic alignment — cord drift-back does not work without adequate lordosis, so laminoplasty loses its mechanism of effect; this is an absolute contraindication in meaningful kyphosis, not a relative preference.
- Significant segmental instability.
- K-line negative OPLL with high canal-occupying ratio — posterior indirect decompression is predicted to be inadequate.
- Predominant axial neck pain as the primary complaint (laminoplasty can worsen posterior neck pain in a subset of patients and does not reliably improve axial pain that is not cord-compression related).
- Prior extensive posterior cervical surgery/scarring that compromises a clean hinge-and-door exposure.

## Preoperative Planning

- MRI for cord signal/compression pattern; CT to assess laminar and lateral mass bony anatomy at each planned level (thickness available for hinge creation), and for OPLL, canal-occupying ratio and morphologic type (segmental, continuous, mixed, localized).
- Flexion-extension radiographs to confirm adequate baseline lordosis (or at minimum a neutral, non-kyphotic segment) and to rule out instability — this is the single most important preoperative check, since it determines candidacy outright.
- **K-line assessment**: draw on lateral radiograph/midsagittal CT or MRI connecting canal midpoints at C2 and C7; also assess dynamic (flexion) K-line status, since a K-line that becomes negative in flexion identifies patients who look favorable in neutral imaging but are not — use K-line alongside canal-occupying ratio and alignment, not as a stand-alone algorithm.
- **Hinge-side and open-side planning**: decide which side will be the hinge (typically the side with less neurologic symptomatology, or by surgeon preference/handedness) and which will be the open (expansion) side, per level, based on lateral mass anatomy and any asymmetric compression.
- **Fixation method selection**: decide between mini-plate fixation and suture-anchor/suture-suspension technique for holding the open door — comparative literature shows titanium miniplate fixation holds the lamina more securely, better resists hinge-fracture displacement, and better promotes spinal cord expansion than suture-anchor techniques, though both are used successfully; have the chosen implant set (miniplates and screws, or suture anchors and heavy suture) available for all planned levels plus contingency.
- Bone graft/spacer material (allograft strut, hydroxyapatite spacer, or autograft from the laminectomy itself) for propping the open door if using a spacer-based technique rather than plate/suture alone.
- Neuromonitoring (SSEP/MEP) booking for severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery, or any case with a deformity-correction component.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General endotracheal anesthesia; avoid excessive neck extension during intubation and positioning in severe stenosis or myelopathy — awake fiberoptic intubation is reasonable in severe myelopathy with unstable or high-grade stenosis.
- Prone position on a radiolucent frame or horseshoe headrest, neck in slight flexion (enough to open the interlaminar spaces for exposure without excessive flexion that could paradoxically stress a stenotic segment); obtain baseline neuromonitoring signals before and after final positioning.
- Arms tucked, pressure points padded, eyes protected, abdomen free-hanging.
- Prep and drape from occiput/upper cervical to the lower extent of the planned laminoplasty levels; confirm levels with lateral fluoroscopy before incision.
- Have the high-speed burr (including a fine diamond burr for hinge-side thinning) and chosen fixation implants (miniplates or suture anchors) opened and available before starting bony work.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm alignment and OPLL parameters support laminoplasty**: preserved or lordotic C2–C7 alignment, lower canal-occupying ratio, and K-line positive (with dynamic/flexion K-line also favorable) — reconfirm this is not a kyphotic segment before committing to the approach.
2. **Midline posterior incision and subperiosteal exposure** of the spinous processes and laminae bilaterally at all involved levels, preserving as much of the paraspinal musculature and posterior ligamentous complex above and below the construct as feasible (muscle-sparing technique) to reduce postoperative axial neck pain.
3. **Create the open-side trough.** With a high-speed burr, create a complete bicortical trough at the laminofacet junction on the open (expansile) side, through both the outer and inner cortex, at each level to be expanded.
4. **Create the hinge-side gutter.** On the hinge side, use a diamond burr to remove the dorsal cortex and cancellous bone at the laminofacet junction, leaving the ventral cortex intact to allow controlled greenstick (incomplete) fracture — this hinge should be thinned enough to allow controlled opening but not so thin that it fractures completely and uncontrollably.
5. **Open the door.** Using towel clips, a small elevator, or dedicated laminoplasty instruments, gently and gradually elevate the spinous process/lamina complex on the open side, hinging at the prepared gutter, watching for controlled greenstick deformation rather than complete fracture; work level by level, opening incrementally rather than forcing full opening at any single level in one motion.
6. **Assess canal expansion.** Confirm visually and by palpation that adequate canal widening has been achieved at each level, with the dura now visibly more lax/pulsatile — this is the direct evidence that decompression is adequate.
7. **Secure the open door.** Place the chosen fixation — titanium miniplates spanning the open-door gutter to the lateral mass (generally providing more secure fixation and better resistance to hinge-fracture displacement per comparative literature), or suture anchors/heavy suture suspending the spinous process or lamina to the facet capsule/lateral mass, or a structural spacer (allograft/hydroxyapatite) wedged into the trough and secured with suture — at every level, checking that the door remains open under gentle testing before moving to the next level.
8. **Inspect the hinge side** at each level for fracture type — a controlled, non-displaced greenstick hinge is ideal; if a complete hinge fracture with displacement (>1 mm anterior or posterior displacement, or full discontinuity of both cortices) is encountered, consider supplemental fixation (plate or suture) on the hinge side as well to prevent late door closure or hinge-fracture-related instability.
9. **For OPLL with K-line-positive, continuous or mixed morphology**, confirm this posterior indirect decompression strategy is being used specifically to avoid the anterior dural ossification/CSF leak hazard associated with attempted direct resection of a densely adherent, dural-adherent OPLL mass — do not additionally attempt anterior floating/resection in the same setting unless a combined strategy was specifically planned preoperatively.
10. **Repeat trough/hinge/opening/fixation sequence at each level** of the planned construct, working systematically from one end of the construct to the other.
11. **Final inspection and hemostasis.** Confirm all levels are adequately opened and secured, achieve meticulous hemostasis from the epidural venous plexus and muscle bed, and consider a subfascial drain.
12. **Closure.** Reapproximate the paraspinal musculature carefully over the construct (this soft-tissue closure quality is part of what drives postoperative axial pain), then fascia, subcutaneous layer, and skin in layers.
13. **Postoperative imaging.** Obtain radiographs (and CT if hinge integrity or canal expansion adequacy is uncertain) to document door position and canal expansion before mobilization.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Thin the hinge to the point where it deforms under gentle, controlled pressure rather than abruptly cracking — a slow, deliberate opening maneuver (rather than a quick lever motion) gives better tactile feedback for when the hinge has yielded enough versus when it is about to completely fracture.
- Consider mini-plate fixation over suture-anchor/suspension technique when available and affordable for the case — comparative studies report miniplates hold the lamina more securely, better resist hinge-fracture displacement, and promote greater spinal cord expansion than suture-anchor fixation, though suture techniques remain a reasonable and widely used lower-cost alternative.
- If a complete hinge fracture with displacement occurs intraoperatively (discontinuity of both cortices, or >1 mm displacement), do not treat it as a non-event — add supplemental fixation on that hinge to prevent late door re-closure and to promote bony healing/fusion of the hinge, which is part of what maintains long-term canal expansion.
- Use a muscle-sparing exposure and meticulous reapproximation of the paraspinal musculature at closure — posterior muscle/ligament disruption is a specific, named driver of the axial neck pain that is more prominent after laminoplasty than after anterior surgery in some series.
- Open the door incrementally level by level rather than fully opening one level before starting the next — this allows the surgeon to gauge how much force a "normal" hinge in this patient's bone requires, calibrating expectations for subsequent levels.
- When assessing K-line status for OPLL, check the dynamic (flexion) K-line in addition to neutral — a K-line that converts to negative in flexion identifies a patient who looks like a laminoplasty candidate on a single neutral image but is not, and this distinction should change the operative plan before, not during, surgery.
- Palpate/visualize dural pulsatility after opening each level as real-time confirmation of adequate decompression — a segment that remains taut after opening may need a wider trough or reassessment of whether posterior-only decompression is sufficient at that level.
- Counsel and plan for occupational therapy involvement early for hand-intensive rehabilitation — fine motor recovery lags gait recovery and benefits from dedicated OT attention rather than generic PT alone.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **C5 palsy**: ~5–10% (range 4–12% across series), typically deltoid/biceps weakness; usually improves over weeks to months but can be permanent in a minority. Mechanism debated — nerve root tethering during cord drift-back/decompression at C4-5, or direct foraminal nerve root injury during decompression. Can present days to ~2 weeks post-op — monitor accordingly.
- **Hinge fracture**: intraoperative or postoperative displacement of the hinge lamina (>1 mm anterior/posterior displacement, or complete discontinuity of both cortices) can compromise canal expansion or late fixation stability — recognized as a specific technical failure mode distinct from a normal controlled greenstick hinge; mini-plate fixation is associated with better resistance to hinge-fracture displacement than suture-anchor fixation in comparative series.
- **Axial neck pain**: more prominent after laminoplasty than after anterior surgery in some series, related to posterior muscle/ligament disruption; muscle-sparing techniques aim to reduce this.
- **Contraindicated in kyphotic alignment** — cord drift-back does not work without adequate lordosis, so laminoplasty loses its mechanism of effect.
- **Continued OPLL progression**: ossification tends to continue growing under a mobile laminoplasty construct more than under a fused segment — a specific consideration favoring laminectomy-fusion over laminoplasty for OPLL when progression control matters more than motion preservation.
- **Comparative evidence** (2024–2025 meta-analyses, 22 studies/2,128 patients): laminoplasty shows shorter operative time, less blood loss, and lower overall complication/C5 palsy rates than laminectomy-fusion, but laminectomy-fusion better maintains/corrects lordosis and, in OPLL specifically, better controls OPLL progression.
- **Wound infection, epidural hematoma**: standard posterior spine surgery risks; hematoma is particularly concerning given baseline cord compromise — low threshold for urgent imaging with any post-op neurological decline.
- **Inadequate decompression from an under-opened door**: results if the trough/hinge is too conservatively made or if fixation is placed before confirming adequate dural pulsatility/canal widening — verify decompression adequacy at each level before securing fixation, not after.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: collar for comfort/support (soft, not rigid, in most laminoplasty); inpatient mobilization with PT/OT from day 1 given baseline myelopathic gait/balance impairment — fall risk is elevated and early assisted mobility is a priority, not an afterthought.
- **Weeks 2–6**: gait training progression (myelopathic gait recovery lags neurological recovery), gentle ROM, monitor for delayed C5 palsy.
- **Weeks 6–12**: formal outpatient PT — proprioceptive/balance training is disproportionately important (spastic/ataxic gait improvement is often the slowest-recovering domain), strengthening, hand dexterity/fine motor rehabilitation with occupational therapy referral for hand-intensive deficits.
- **Months 3–12+**: neurological recovery is gradual and can continue for 12–24 months post-decompression; mJOA recovery ratio is typically assessed at 1 year but continued gains are documented beyond that in a subset.
- **Return to work**: sedentary 2–4 weeks; manual labor individualized, 8–12+ weeks, often delayed until neurological/functional recovery plateau. Return to contact sport is generally discouraged given underlying cord vulnerability, regardless of construct healing.
