## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- **Arthroscopic Bankart repair**: recurrent anterior shoulder instability with ISIS ≤3–4, on-track Hill-Sachs lesion, and glenoid bone loss <13.5% (acceptable up to <20% in lower-demand, non-contact patients).
- Also indicated as **primary treatment within weeks of injury** for first-time high-risk dislocators (<25 years, male, contact/collision or overhead athlete, ISIS ≥4, off-track lesion) rather than waiting for a second event.
- **Remplissage** (added to Bankart repair): off-track Hill-Sachs lesion with subcritical or otherwise acceptable glenoid bone loss. It is always an *adjunct* to a Bankart repair, never a stand-alone procedure.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Glenoid bone loss approaching or exceeding ~20–25% — isolated Bankart ± remplissage does not correct the glenoid-side deficiency; a bone-block procedure (Latarjet) is indicated instead.
- Comminuted or non-viable bony Bankart fragment unsuitable for incorporation — treat as a bone-loss problem, not a labral repair.
- Active voluntary/psychogenic instability or an unaddressed muscle-patterning (Stanmore Type III) component — surgical repair has poor outcomes until this is excluded or treated.
- Uncontrolled seizure disorder without adequate medical management — recurrence risk from further seizure-related events undermines any soft-tissue repair.
- Relative: significant glenohumeral arthropathy (Samilson-Prieto moderate-severe) — stabilization surgery alone will not address the arthritic component and may accelerate symptoms.
- Remplissage specifically should not be relied upon as sole bony augmentation once glenoid loss nears the critical threshold — it fills the humeral-side defect but does nothing for glenoid arc deficiency.

## Preoperative Planning

- **CT with 3D reconstruction and digital humeral subtraction** is essentially mandatory: quantify glenoid bone loss with the best-fit-circle/PICO method and measure Hill-Sachs size/location to calculate glenoid track status (on-track vs off-track). This single calculation determines whether remplissage is added.
- **MR arthrogram** to characterize labral pattern (Bankart vs ALPSA vs combined anterior-posterior tear), assess for a concomitant SLAP lesion or posterior labral extension, and screen the axillary pouch for an occult HAGL lesion.
- Plain radiographs (AP, true AP/Grashey, axillary lateral, scapular Y; West Point for anteroinferior rim, Stryker notch for Hill-Sachs) as an adjunct/screening set, particularly useful for ISIS scoring.
- Calculate **ISIS score** and glenoid track status preoperatively and document the plan (Bankart alone vs Bankart + remplissage vs bone block) before the patient is consented — do not decide intraoperatively without having planned for the possibility of remplissage or conversion to open Latarjet.
- **Implant planning**: 3–4 suture anchors (biocomposite or all-suture, 2.4–3.0 mm) for the glenoid-side repair; 1–2 separate anchors (often larger-diameter, e.g., 4.5–5.5 mm knotless, or a double-pulley/double-barrel all-suture construct) reserved specifically for the Hill-Sachs defect if remplissage is planned. Confirm anchor availability and any specialized remplissage guide/cannula system (curved suture-passing cannula, percutaneous posterolateral access set) is on hand before draping.
- Consent should explicitly cover the possibility of intraoperative conversion to remplissage (if the Hill-Sachs is found to engage dynamically at exam under anesthesia or arthroscopically) or, less commonly, to an open bone-block procedure if unexpected glenoid comminution/bone loss is found.
- Review exam-under-anesthesia findings against preoperative imaging; a discrepancy (e.g., gross dynamic instability out of proportion to imaged bone loss) should prompt reconsideration of the plan on the table.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically combined with an interscalene regional block for postoperative analgesia (discuss block timing with anesthesia if a postoperative neuro exam of the axillary nerve is planned).
- **Positioning**: either **beach chair** or **lateral decubitus with balanced traction (10–15 lb, arm in ~20–30° abduction and ~20° forward flexion)** — surgeon preference; lateral decubitus provides better joint distraction for posterior working-space access, which is an advantage when remplissage is planned. Beach chair allows easier conversion to open procedures.
- Secure the head in a headrest/positioner (beach chair) or bean bag/axillary roll with well-padded peroneal and axillary pressure points (lateral). Pad all bony prominences.
- Prep and drape widely enough to allow a full circumferential view of the shoulder and to permit conversion to an open (deltopectoral) approach if needed.
- Mark surface landmarks before incision: acromion borders (anterior, lateral, posterior corners), coracoid, clavicle, and the planned portal sites — standard posterior (soft spot, ~2 cm inferior and ~1 cm medial to the posterolateral acromion), anterosuperolateral, anteroinferior (mid-glenoid, just above subscapularis), and, if remplissage is anticipated, an accessory posterolateral (7 o'clock–type) portal for Hill-Sachs anchor access.
- Fluoroscopy is not routinely required for this procedure; have it available only if a bony Bankart fragment or unexpected glenoid pathology is encountered.
- Perform an examination under anesthesia (load-and-shift, sulcus, apprehension) before prepping, with the contralateral shoulder examined first for a laxity baseline.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy.** Establish the posterior viewing portal first, then anterosuperolateral and anteroinferior working portals under direct/outside-in technique. Perform a systematic circumferential survey of the joint — labrum from 12 to 6 o'clock and around to posterior, biceps/superior labral complex, subscapularis, rotator cuff undersurface, and specifically the **axillary pouch** (roll the arm into abduction/external rotation and inspect the inferior capsular recess) to exclude a HAGL lesion. Document any SLAP tear or posterior labral extension that will need concurrent repair.
2. **Mobilize the anteroinferior labrum.** Use an elevator or shaver to release the labrum and capsule off the glenoid neck, working from the articular margin down onto the scapular neck until the subscapularis muscle fibers are visualized — inadequate mobilization is the most common technical reason for a bumper that ends up too medial and too flat. Recognize an **ALPSA lesion** (medialized, healed-down, "scarred flat" labrum) — this requires sharp release and full mobilization back to the glenoid rim before anchors are placed, not simple re-tensioning of already-scarred tissue.
3. **Prepare the glenoid neck/rim.** Lightly decorticate/abrade the anterior glenoid neck with a shaver or rasp to bleeding bone to promote a healing surface for the capsulolabral tissue — avoid aggressive rim resection which reduces the effective glenoid arc.
4. **Place suture anchors.** Place 3–4 anchors low on the glenoid face beginning at the 5–6 o'clock position (right shoulder) and working superiorly to roughly 2–3 o'clock, staying **on the articular face of the glenoid, just off the rim (~2–3 mm)** rather than on the neck — anchors placed too far medial on the neck under-restore the labral bumper and leave a flattened, incompetent repair.
5. **Pass sutures and secure the capsulolabral tissue.** Use a suture-passing device (curved or angled shuttle) to capture a generous bite of labrum and capsule for each anchor, advancing the tissue **superiorly and laterally** onto the glenoid face to restore both the labral bumper and capsular tension (the shift component). Tie sequentially from inferior to superior, checking with a probe after each knot that the labral height and capsular tension are restored without overtightening.
6. **Address concomitant pathology in the same setting.** Repair a SLAP lesion or extend the repair posteriorly if a posterior labral tear is present — failing to treat these is a recognized cause of persistent instability after an isolated anterior repair.
7. **Reassess Hill-Sachs engagement.** With the anterior repair complete, dynamically assess the Hill-Sachs defect arthroscopically (rotate the arm through abduction-external rotation under direct visualization) and correlate with the preoperative track calculation. If off-track (or borderline in a contact/overhead athlete), proceed to remplissage.
8. **Remplissage — portal and access.** Establish an accessory posterolateral (7 o'clock–type) portal, typically via an outside-in percutaneous technique through the deltoid, positioned to give a direct trajectory into the mid-to-lateral extent of the Hill-Sachs defect and the overlying infraspinatus tendon/posterior capsule.
9. **Remplissage — decorticate the defect.** Lightly abrade the base of the Hill-Sachs lesion with a shaver or burr to bleeding bone.
10. **Remplissage — anchor placement.** Place 1–2 anchors within the floor of the Hill-Sachs defect (medial and lateral, or a single central anchor for a smaller defect), using a percutaneous transtendon technique through the infraspinatus so the guidewire/anchor sleeve penetrates tendon and posterior capsule together without creating an iatrogenic tendon split.
11. **Remplissage — suture passage and tensioning.** Pass sutures through the infraspinatus tendon and posterior capsule (a double-pulley/double-barrel technique with knotless anchors is commonly used for a low-profile, tensionable construct), then tie or tension so the tendon-capsule unit fills the defect and renders it extra-articular. Confirm arthroscopically that the defect is adequately filled and no residual engagement occurs through the arc of motion, and visually confirm no deltoid fibers were caught in the knot (a recognized cause of postoperative pain with motion).
12. **Final inspection and closure.** Re-probe all anchor sites for pull-out, confirm full passive ROM is achievable without gross tissue tension, evacuate the joint, remove instruments, and close portals with simple sutures or adhesive strips. Place the arm in a sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Perform the exam under anesthesia **before** prepping and use it to confirm or revise your working diagnosis (direction, degree) against the preoperative imaging — a mismatch should prompt intraoperative reassessment of the plan.
- When mobilizing an ALPSA, take the release all the way to the muscular subscapularis fibers; stopping short is the most common reason the labrum cannot be advanced laterally enough to restore a true bumper.
- Keep anchors on the glenoid face just off the articular margin, not on the neck — a few millimeters of anchor position makes the difference between a competent bumper and a flattened repair.
- Start inferior-most and work superiorly when tying knots; tensioning the most inferior stitch first sets the capsular tension for everything above it and avoids a "puckered" superior repair.
- For remplissage, use a percutaneous (rather than cannulated) approach through the infraspinatus when possible — it gives a more direct, adjustable trajectory into the defect and avoids creating a large iatrogenic split in the tendon.
- Confirm knotless/double-pulley remplissage sutures are not incorporating deltoid fibers before final tensioning — this is a specific, avoidable cause of post-tying pain with shoulder motion.
- Dynamically test Hill-Sachs engagement arthroscopically at the end of the anterior repair rather than relying solely on the preoperative track calculation — measurement variability exists in 2D on/off-track methods, and direct visualization is the final check.
- If a posterior labral extension is found, address it through the same or an additional posterior portal in the same setting rather than as a staged procedure — untreated posterior extension is a recognized cause of "failed" anterior repairs.
- Have a lateral decubitus/traction setup available (or plan for it) if remplissage is anticipated preoperatively — it materially improves posterior working-space access compared to beach chair.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Recurrent instability** is the dominant complication. Rates: isolated arthroscopic Bankart 10–20% overall (up to 67% in high-risk young contact athletes); Bankart + remplissage ~10% vs 30% for Bankart alone at medium-term follow-up (Woodmass et al., *AJSM* 2024 RCT). Recurrence is driven primarily by inadequate preoperative bone-loss/track assessment rather than technical repair failure alone.
- Anchor placement too far medial on the glenoid neck under-restores the labral bumper effect — the single most common technical error in isolated Bankart repair.
- Missing or under-treating an ALPSA leads to inadequate mobilization and residual instability even with technically sound anchor placement.
- Failing to recognize off-track status preoperatively is the single largest driver of "unexplained" recurrence after isolated Bankart repair — this is a planning failure, not usually a technical one.
- Remplissage does not correct glenoid-side bone loss — do not rely on it as sole augmentation when glenoid loss approaches or exceeds ~20–25%; a bone-block procedure is needed instead.
- A small, generally non-significant loss of external rotation (a few degrees) is reported after remplissage on meta-analysis but does not appear to compromise overall shoulder function or 5-year return-to-sport rates.
- Catching deltoid fibers in the remplissage knot causes pain with mobilization postoperatively — visually confirm a clean tendon/capsule bite before final tensioning.
- Missed concomitant lesions (posterior labral extension, SLAP, HAGL) are a preventable cause of persistent or "failed" anterior stabilization — a systematic circumferential survey including the axillary pouch at index arthroscopy reduces this risk.
- Overtensioning the capsulolabral repair can cause iatrogenic loss of external rotation and, rarely, contribute to later arthropathy; tension to restore normal apprehension-free ROM, not maximal tightness.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–4**: sling immobilization (neutral to slight internal rotation, per surgeon preference); passive/active-assisted ROM starting within the first 1–2 weeks (pendulum, passive elevation to tolerance, external rotation limited to 0–20° to protect the repair); no active internal rotation loading, no lifting.
- **Weeks 4–6**: discontinue sling; progress passive/active ROM toward full elevation and external rotation as tolerated; begin isometric rotator cuff/scapular stabilizer activation; avoid combined abduction-external rotation ("apprehension position") loading.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive resistance strengthening (rotator cuff, scapular stabilizers, deltoid), restore full ROM, closed-chain stability drills, begin sport-specific proprioceptive work around week 8–10.
- **Weeks 12–16**: sport-specific/return-to-throw or contact-preparation progressions; plyometric and eccentric loading for overhead athletes.
- **Return to non-contact sport**: ~4–5 months. **Return to contact/collision sport**: typically 5–6 months, contingent on full painless ROM, ≥90% strength symmetry on isokinetic testing, negative apprehension, and successful sport-specific functional testing — not solely time-based.
- Remplissage does not meaningfully alter this timeline in most protocols, though some surgeons extend early external rotation restriction slightly given the infraspinatus tenodesis component.
- Reported recurrence: isolated Bankart 10–20% (up to 67% in high-risk young contact athletes); Bankart + remplissage ~10% vs. 30% for Bankart alone at medium-term follow-up (Woodmass et al., *AJSM* 2024 RCT).
