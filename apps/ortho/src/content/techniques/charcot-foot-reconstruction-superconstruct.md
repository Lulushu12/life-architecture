## Indications

Surgery in Charcot neuroarthropathy is reserved for: recurrent or non-healing ulceration over a bony prominence, gross instability precluding safe bracing, deformity precluding shoe wear, and infection. Surgery is otherwise avoided during active disease (Eichenholtz stage 0–II) except when limb-threatening; elective reconstruction is ideally deferred to **Eichenholtz stage III (consolidation)**. Technique selection follows the anatomic pattern (Brodsky classification).

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed at the approach/room-setup level in the source material; technique selection by pattern of instability/deformity is the operative decision point (see Steps).

## Steps

1. **Exostectomy alone** for a stable foot with an isolated plantar bony prominence.
2. **Realignment arthrodesis using the "superconstruct" principle"** (Sammarco) for unstable or deformed feet: fusion extended beyond the zone of injury to healthy bone, maximally rigid fixation (axial/beaming intramedullary screws, locking plates, combined internal + external fixation), generous bone grafting, and prolonged immobilization/protected weightbearing — reflecting the impaired bone-healing biology of the neuropathic host.
3. **Circular external fixation** for unstable, infected, or severely compromised soft-tissue cases, often as a staged adjunct to internal fixation.
4. **Tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) arthrodesis with a retrograde intramedullary nail** for combined ankle + hindfoot Charcot (Brodsky type 3A), given the instability and limb-threat associated with this pattern.

## Pitfalls

- Charcot surgery has the highest complication burden in foot & ankle surgery: wound complications 20–50%, hardware failure/breakage given prolonged abnormal loading, deep infection, and nonunion requiring revision with even more aggressive fixation.
- Roughly 20–40% of patients develop recurrent or new contralateral Charcot disease over their lifetime — reinforcing the need for lifelong surveillance and bracing regardless of surgical success.
- Operating during active disease (Eichenholtz 0–II) is avoided except when limb-threatening; the "superconstruct" approach specifically exists to compensate for the neuropathic host's impaired bone-healing biology, so standard (non-extended, non-augmented) fixation constructs are more likely to fail in this population.

## Post-op protocol

Total contact casting (TCC), changed weekly initially, transitioning as edema/swelling stabilizes; strict non-weightbearing or minimal weightbearing throughout Eichenholtz 0–II. Once stage III consolidation is confirmed, transition to a **CROW (Charcot restraint orthotic walker) or rigid AFO**, with gradual progression to therapeutic footwear over subsequent months. Many patients require **lifelong bracing** to prevent recurrence/re-ulceration, particularly with midfoot (Brodsky type 1) rocker-bottom residual deformity.

For arthrodesis specifically (non-Charcot comparison bullet applies inversely here): NWB is extended to 8–12+ weeks in Charcot/neuropathic patients (vs. 6 weeks in non-Charcot arthrodesis) given impaired bone healing biology, with serial radiographs (± CT if union uncertain) before progressing weightbearing.
