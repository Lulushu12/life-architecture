## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Recurrent or non-healing ulceration over a bony prominence (typically midfoot rocker-bottom or plantar-medial cuneiform/cuboid prominence) despite adequate offloading.
- Gross instability precluding safe bracing (positive "shopping bag" or manual varus/valgus stress test of the midfoot/hindfoot).
- Deformity precluding shoe or brace wear.
- Infection involving bone (osteomyelitis) requiring resection and stabilization.
- Elective reconstruction is ideally deferred to **Eichenholtz stage III (consolidation)** — surgery during active disease (stage 0–II) is reserved for limb-threatening indications only, given markedly worse bone-healing biology and fixation failure risk in the acute/fragmentation phase.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Active Eichenholtz 0–II disease without a limb-threatening indication — operate on the deformity once it has consolidated, not while it is still evolving.
- Uncorrected critical limb ischemia — obtain vascular assessment (ABI, toe pressures) and revascularize before elective reconstruction; poor perfusion is a leading driver of the wound complication rate in this population.
- Active, uncontrolled infection without prior source control — stage debridement/antibiotic therapy before definitive fixation in infected cases.
- Medically unoptimized diabetes (markedly elevated HbA1c), active smoking, or malnutrition — these are correctable risk factors for nonunion and wound failure and should be addressed preoperatively where feasible, though surgery should not be indefinitely delayed for a limb-threatening indication.
- A patient who can be safely and durably managed in a CROW walker/brace without progressive deformity, instability, or ulceration does not need surgery — bracing remains the default management for stable, consolidated Charcot deformity.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Staging confirmation**: confirm Eichenholtz stage clinically (resolution of warmth, temperature differential <1–2 °C vs. contralateral limb) and radiographically (consolidation, remodeling) before scheduling elective reconstruction; serial exams/radiographs over weeks to months are often needed since a single visit can be misleading.
- **Imaging**: weightbearing radiographs (AP, lateral, oblique) to characterize the Brodsky pattern and degree of deformity; CT for detailed bone stock and deformity assessment in complex/revision cases; MRI selectively when osteomyelitis must be distinguished from Charcot changes (both can produce marrow edema — absence of a sinus tract/ulcer and clinical trend favor Charcot); vascular studies (ABI, toe-brachial index, or formal arterial duplex) in any patient with diminished pulses.
- **Implant selection**: axial/beaming intramedullary screws (typically large-diameter, 6.5–8.0 mm, spanning from the metatarsal heads into the midfoot/hindfoot) and/or locking plates for midfoot superconstructs; a retrograde intramedullary nail for combined ankle+hindfoot (TTC) patterns; a circular external fixator (Ilizarov/Taylor Spatial Frame-type) staged or combined with internal fixation for unstable, infected, or soft-tissue-compromised cases. Have a generous bone graft plan (autograft, allograft, or bone graft substitute) ready, since superconstructs typically require filling of resection voids after correcting significant deformity.
- **Equipment**: full beaming/superconstruct instrumentation set, fluoroscopy capable of full foot/ankle imaging without repositioning, external fixation set available even when the primary plan is internal fixation (soft-tissue or stability findings intraoperatively may require conversion or augmentation).
- **Multidisciplinary planning**: coordinate with vascular surgery (perfusion optimization), infectious disease (culture-directed antibiotics if infection present), and endocrinology/diabetology (glycemic optimization) before the operative date — this is not an isolated orthopedic procedure and outcomes are materially affected by host optimization.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is typical given case length and the frequent need for tourniquet time exceeding what is comfortable under regional block alone; a popliteal block is a useful adjunct for postoperative analgesia in a neuropathic limb where opioid-sparing is especially valuable (many patients already have polypharmacy).
- **Positioning**: supine for isolated midfoot/forefoot beaming and most exostectomies; supine with a bump for combined medial/lateral column work; lateral or supine-with-bolster positioning may be used for TTC nailing depending on surgeon preference and nail entry-point access needs. The leg is prepped free to allow full manipulation for deformity correction and fluoroscopic imaging in multiple planes.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet is used when perfusion status safely allows; in patients with borderline perfusion, discuss with vascular surgery preoperatively whether tourniquet use is appropriate, and consider proceeding without one or with a lower pressure/shorter inflation time if there is any concern.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned to obtain AP, lateral, and oblique views of the entire foot and ankle without repositioning — critical for confirming beaming screw trajectory the full length of the intramedullary canal and for assessing correction in multiple planes throughout the case.
- **Landmarks**: mark planned incisions over the bony prominence for exostectomy, plantar and dorsal midfoot landmarks for beaming portal placement, and the retrograde nail entry point (plantar heel, in line with the long axis of the tibia) for TTC cases.
- **Draping/prep**: circumferential free draping of the leg and foot; given frequent coexisting ulceration, plan the prep and drape sequence to isolate any open wound from clean instrumentation, and consider a separate sterile field/instrument set if debriding an infected ulcer before proceeding to definitive fixation.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**1. Exostectomy alone (stable foot, isolated plantar bony prominence)**
1. Approach directly over the prominence (commonly plantar-medial midfoot) through a longitudinal or curvilinear incision, avoiding placing the incision directly over a chronic ulcer bed when possible.
2. Resect the prominent bone flush with the adjacent plantar contour using a saw or osteotome, confirming adequate resection under fluoroscopy and by direct palpation of the plantar surface.
3. Débride any chronic ulcer/callus tissue overlying the prominence and close in layers, or manage as an open wound with delayed closure if heavily contaminated.

**2. Realignment arthrodesis using the superconstruct principle (Sammarco)**
1. Approach the deformity through medial and/or lateral incisions as needed to access the involved midfoot/hindfoot joints, planning skin bridges carefully given the frequently compromised soft-tissue envelope.
2. Resect deformed/necrotic bone and denude joint surfaces back to bleeding, healthy bone, correcting the deformity to restore a plantigrade foot — this typically requires more aggressive bone resection than in non-Charcot arthrodesis to achieve adequate correction and expose viable bone.
3. Extend the planned fusion **beyond the zone of injury into healthy, non-Charcot bone** — the defining feature of the superconstruct principle; for example, a midfoot (Brodsky 1) pattern is often fused from the metatarsals through to the hindfoot rather than being limited to the tarsometatarsal joints alone.
4. Place axial intramedullary beaming screws from the metatarsal heads (or metatarsal shafts) proximally across the midfoot into the talus/calcaneus, supplemented by locking plates for additional stability where beaming alone is insufficient — the fixation construct should be maximally rigid, generally exceeding what would be used in a non-neuropathic host for an equivalent deformity.
5. Pack all resection voids and areas of bone loss with generous bone graft (autograft, allograft, or graft substitute) to support union across the extended fusion mass.
6. Combine with external fixation (circular frame) when the soft-tissue envelope is compromised, infection is present, or additional stability beyond what internal fixation alone can provide is needed — this may be applied concurrently or in a staged fashion after initial debridement/temporizing fixation.
7. Close in layers over the internal fixation, or leave appropriately draining if infection was present, with plans for delayed closure/staged wound management as needed.

**3. Tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) arthrodesis with retrograde intramedullary nail (combined ankle + hindfoot, Brodsky 3A)**
1. Approach the ankle and subtalar joints (commonly through a lateral transfibular approach with fibular osteotomy used as autograft, or an anterior approach) to prepare the tibiotalar and subtalar joint surfaces.
2. Denude cartilage and sclerotic subchondral bone from both joints down to bleeding cancellous bone, correcting hindfoot and ankle alignment to neutral.
3. Establish the nail entry point at the plantar heel in line with the long axis of the tibia; ream the intramedullary canal across the calcaneus, talus, and tibia.
4. Provisionally reduce and pin the construct, confirm alignment on fluoroscopy in multiple planes, then pass the retrograde nail and lock it proximally and distally (through the calcaneus) with locking screws.
5. Pack the fusion sites with bone graft (fibular autograft from the osteotomy is a convenient local source) and consider supplemental fixation (compression screws) if the nail construct alone does not achieve adequate compression.
6. Close in layers; given the high instability and limb-threat of this pattern, plan for extended non-weightbearing postoperatively from the outset.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm Eichenholtz stage III with both clinical (temperature differential) and radiographic criteria before scheduling elective reconstruction — operating one stage too early is a common, avoidable driver of fixation failure.
- Extend fixation well beyond the visibly deformed segment into radiographically normal bone — the entire rationale of the superconstruct is compensating for the neuropathic host's globally impaired bone-healing biology, not just the diseased segment.
- Use more screws/points of fixation than the equivalent non-Charcot procedure would require — literature on midfoot beaming shows an increased number of screws correlates with a lower complication rate, and including the subtalar joint as part of the construct for more proximal (Sanders-Frykberg II/III) patterns substantially reduces complication rates compared with beaming alone.
- Plan incisions and skin bridges conservatively given the frequently thin, previously ulcerated, or edematous soft-tissue envelope — a wound complication in this population is far harder to manage than in a routine host.
- Use fibular osteotomy autograft opportunistically in TTC nailing — it provides a convenient, well-vascularized local graft source without an additional donor site.
- Do not rely on tourniquet time assumptions from routine cases — these reconstructions are frequently long; plan for possible tourniquet deflation/re-inflation and communicate this with anesthesia in advance.
- Have a low threshold to add or convert to external fixation intraoperatively if soft tissue, stability, or infection findings are worse than anticipated on preoperative imaging — flexibility in the fixation plan is part of managing this patient population safely.
- Counsel the patient and family before surgery that this is a high-complication-rate procedure performed to preserve a functional, shoeable/braceable limb — not to restore normal anatomy or eliminate the need for lifelong bracing.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Charcot surgery has the highest complication burden in foot & ankle surgery: wound complications **20–50%**, hardware failure/breakage given prolonged abnormal loading, deep infection, and nonunion requiring revision with even more aggressive fixation.
- Total complication rates for intramedullary beaming specifically have been reported at **46–51%** (minor 6–23%, major 25–40%), with **screw breakage at the tarsometatarsal junction** the most commonly encountered failure — reinforcing why extended, multi-screw superconstructs outperform limited fixation.
- Including the subtalar joint in the construct for more proximal (Sanders-Frykberg II/III) deformity patterns has been associated with a substantially lower complication rate than beaming limited to the midfoot alone — factor this into the fixation plan for proximal patterns rather than defaulting to a midfoot-only construct.
- Roughly **20–40%** of patients develop recurrent or new contralateral Charcot disease over their lifetime — reinforcing the need for lifelong surveillance and bracing regardless of surgical success.
- Operating during active disease (Eichenholtz 0–II) is avoided except when limb-threatening; standard (non-extended, non-augmented) fixation constructs are more likely to fail in this population given impaired bone-healing biology, which is precisely why the superconstruct approach exists.
- Infection can be difficult to distinguish from active Charcot on imaging alone (both produce marrow edema) — absence of a sinus tract/ulcer and a favorable clinical trend favor Charcot, but a low threshold for bone biopsy/culture is warranted when in doubt before committing to elective fixation.
- Perfusion deficits are a silent driver of wound and fixation failure — do not proceed with elective reconstruction without confirming adequate perfusion (ABI, toe pressures, or vascular surgery clearance) beforehand.

## Postoperative Protocol

Total contact casting (TCC), changed weekly initially, transitioning as edema/swelling stabilizes; strict non-weightbearing or minimal weightbearing throughout Eichenholtz 0–II. Once stage III consolidation is confirmed, transition to a **CROW (Charcot restraint orthotic walker) or rigid AFO**, with gradual progression to therapeutic footwear over subsequent months. Many patients require **lifelong bracing** to prevent recurrence/re-ulceration, particularly with midfoot (Brodsky type 1) rocker-bottom residual deformity.

For arthrodesis specifically: NWB is extended to **8–12+ weeks** in Charcot/neuropathic patients (vs. 6 weeks in non-Charcot arthrodesis) given impaired bone healing biology, with serial radiographs (± CT if union uncertain) before progressing weightbearing. Weightbearing progression after that point is gradual and closely monitored, typically transitioning through a CAM boot before therapeutic footwear/bracing, with lifelong bracing anticipated in most cases regardless of radiographic union quality.
