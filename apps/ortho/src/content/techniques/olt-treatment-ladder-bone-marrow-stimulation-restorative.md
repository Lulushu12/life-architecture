## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT) that has failed a trial of protected weightbearing, NSAIDs, and activity modification (6–12 weeks).
- Imaging features predicting failure of non-operative management: larger lesion size, subchondral cyst formation, or a detached fragment (Hepple stage 3–5).
- Bone marrow stimulation is favored for **primary lesions <100–150 mm² (~<10–15 mm diameter)**, Hepple 1–2b, without a large cystic component.
- Restorative procedures (OATS, particulated allograft, ACI) are favored for lesions ≥100–150 mm², cystic (Hepple 5) lesions, or failure of a prior bone marrow stimulation procedure.
- Fresh osteochondral allograft is reserved for very large lesions or revision cases where autograft harvest would create unacceptable donor-site morbidity.
- Primary fixation (rather than excision/BMS) is indicated for Hepple 3, acute, large, reducible fragments.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Uncorrected hindfoot or ankle malalignment (varus/valgus) or chronic lateral ankle instability — treating the OLT without correcting the underlying malalignment/instability is a major driver of recurrence and should be addressed concurrently or staged before cartilage restoration.
- Diffuse, kissing, or bipolar (tibial + talar) lesions generally respond poorly to any of these techniques and warrant a frank discussion about limited expected benefit, with arthrodesis or arthroplasty reserved for end-stage failure.
- Active infection or uncontrolled inflammatory arthropathy.
- Advanced tibiotalar arthrosis beyond the focal OLT — cartilage restoration is not indicated in the setting of diffuse joint degeneration.
- Poor bone stock/avascular subchondral bone without a viable base for graft incorporation may favor a bone-grafting "sandwich" approach or allograft over autograft/ACI alone.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI is the primary modality for cartilage/subchondral bone edema and Hepple staging; **weightbearing CT (WBCT)** is increasingly used for accurate lesion size/depth/containment mapping under true weightbearing geometry and is superior to conventional non-weightbearing CT for surgical planning — obtain this whenever available for lesions being considered for a restorative procedure.
- **Sizing/selection**: measure lesion area (mm²) and depth on MRI/WBCT to place the lesion on the size-based ladder (bone marrow stimulation vs. restorative); assess containment (contained vs. shoulder/rim-involving) since uncontained lesions behave less predictably with marrow stimulation alone.
- **Graft planning**: for OATS, plan donor site on the ipsilateral knee (typically lateral trochlea or lateral femoral condyle periphery) matched to recipient lesion diameter/depth; for particulated juvenile allograft cartilage or fresh osteochondral allograft, coordinate graft procurement/sizing with the tissue bank in advance of the surgical date, as availability and size-matching can affect scheduling; for ACI/MACI, this is typically a two-stage procedure requiring an initial cartilage biopsy (often arthroscopic) followed by an interval for chondrocyte culture/expansion before the definitive implantation stage — plan this timeline explicitly with the patient.
- **Access planning**: determine preoperatively whether the lesion is accessible arthroscopically (most anterolateral and many anteromedial lesions) or will require a medial or lateral malleolar osteotomy for posterior/central lesion access — this determines equipment needs (osteotomy fixation set) and changes the postoperative weightbearing timeline (osteotomy healing must also be tracked).
- **Equipment**: standard ankle arthroscopy set with microfracture awls or a 0.045–0.062 in K-wire/drill for bone marrow stimulation; osteotomy fixation set (screws) when a malleolar osteotomy is planned; OATS harvesting/delivery system when autograft is planned; have a bone graft option (autograft chips or substitute) available for the "sandwich" technique in lesions with subchondral bone loss.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic nerve block (± saphenous block) combined with general or spinal anesthesia/sedation is standard; ankle arthroscopy portals and distraction are more comfortable for the patient with a well-placed regional block for early postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning**: supine with a thigh tourniquet and a bump under the ipsilateral hip for standard anterior ankle arthroscopy and bone marrow stimulation of anterior/anteromedial or anterolateral lesions; a distal leg holder or noninvasive ankle distraction strap facilitates joint distraction for visualization of posterior lesions. When a malleolar osteotomy is required for posterior/central lesion access (restorative procedures), the leg is positioned to allow direct open access to the medial or lateral malleolus in addition to arthroscopic portals.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet standard, exsanguinated before inflation; if harvesting an OATS autograft from the ipsilateral knee, plan a separate, more proximal tourniquet or sequential use, and prep/drape the ipsilateral knee into the same sterile field from the outset.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm available for confirming osteotomy position/fixation when a malleolar osteotomy is performed, and useful for confirming needle/awl trajectory during bone marrow stimulation of difficult-to-visualize lesions.
- **Landmarks**: mark standard anteromedial and anterolateral ankle arthroscopy portals (medial to tibialis anterior, lateral to peroneus tertius, both just above the joint line) with the ankle in slight plantarflexion, avoiding the saphenous vein/nerve medially and the superficial peroneal nerve branches laterally; mark the malleolar osteotomy site when planned.
- **Draping**: free-drape the leg to allow ankle manipulation (plantarflexion/dorsiflexion, varus/valgus stress) for both portal access and lesion visualization; include the ipsilateral knee in the prepped/draped field whenever autograft harvest is anticipated, even if the final decision to harvest is made intraoperatively.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**1. Arthroscopic bone marrow stimulation (primary lesion <100–150 mm², Hepple 1–2b)**
1. Establish standard anteromedial and anterolateral arthroscopy portals; perform a diagnostic arthroscopy to confirm lesion location, size, and stability, and to assess the remainder of the joint for concurrent pathology.
2. Debride the overlying unstable/fibrillated cartilage cap back to a stable, vertical cartilage rim, exposing the subchondral bone of the lesion.
3. Curette the calcified cartilage layer and any fibrous/sclerotic subchondral bone from the lesion base to expose healthy, bleeding cancellous bone — inadequate removal of the calcified layer is a recognized cause of poor fibrocartilage fill.
4. Perform microfracture (awl) or drilling (K-wire/drill) at 3–4 mm intervals across the lesion base, penetrating into vascular cancellous bone until fat droplets/bleeding are seen, taking care not to converge adjacent perforations (which can fracture the intervening bone bridge).
5. Release the tourniquet/reduce distraction and confirm marrow element ("fat droplet") extrusion from the perforations before completing the procedure.
6. Address any concurrent hindfoot malalignment or chronic lateral instability in the same setting (or as a planned staged procedure) since uncorrected malalignment is a major driver of recurrence.

**2. Restorative procedures (OATS, particulated allograft, ACI) for larger/cystic/failed-BMS lesions**
1. If the lesion is posterior or central and not adequately accessible arthroscopically, perform a medial (for medial talar dome lesions, typically with a chevron-type tibial osteotomy) or lateral (for lateral talar dome lesions, often with a fibular osteotomy) malleolar osteotomy, predrilling and marking the osteotomy before completing the cut to allow accurate anatomic reduction at closure.
2. Expose and prepare the lesion bed: debride to a stable cartilage rim and healthy subchondral bone as in bone marrow stimulation, creating a well-contained, appropriately sized defect for graft placement.
3. **OATS**: harvest a size-matched osteochondral plug (or plugs, mosaicplasty) from a non-weightbearing area of the ipsilateral knee (typically the lateral trochlear ridge or superolateral femoral condyle periphery), then press-fit the plug(s) into the prepared talar defect, matching articular contour and confirming flush seating.
4. **Particulated juvenile allograft cartilage**: prepare the defect bed, then place and secure the particulated cartilage graft (often with fibrin glue) to fill the defect, contouring to match the native articular surface.
5. **ACI/MACI**: in the second stage of this two-stage procedure, prepare the defect bed and implant the cultured chondrocyte membrane/scaffold, securing edges with fibrin glue or sutures as per the specific product technique, ensuring a watertight seal to contain the cell suspension/matrix.
6. If subchondral bone loss is present, perform the "sandwich" technique: fill the bony defect first with autograft/allograft bone chips, compact to restore the subchondral contour, then layer the chosen cartilage restoration technique over the grafted bone.
7. Reduce and fix the malleolar osteotomy anatomically with screws, confirming reduction and hardware position on fluoroscopy before closure.

**3. Hepple 3 fragment fixation (acute, reducible fragment)**
1. Access the lesion arthroscopically or through a limited open/osteotomy approach as needed for adequate visualization and reduction control.
2. Elevate the fragment, debride the fibrous interface and sclerotic base beneath it back to bleeding bone, then anatomically reduce the fragment into its bed.
3. Fix with headless compression screws or bioabsorbable pins placed to avoid the articular surface, achieving stable interfragmentary compression; confirm smooth articular congruity by direct visualization and ankle range of motion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Obtain WBCT rather than relying on conventional non-weightbearing CT when planning a restorative procedure — true weightbearing geometry measures lesion size and containment more accurately and can shift the size-based decision.
- Do not skip removal of the calcified cartilage layer during bone marrow stimulation — leaving it in place is a well-recognized cause of poor fibrocartilage fill and early failure.
- Space microfracture/drilling perforations to avoid convergence — perforations placed too close together can fracture the bony bridge between them and compromise the subchondral plate.
- Always assess and correct concurrent hindfoot malalignment (varus/valgus) or chronic ankle instability in the same treatment plan — this is one of the most consistently cited, correctable drivers of OLT recurrence/progression and is easy to overlook when focused on the cartilage lesion itself.
- When harvesting an OATS autograft, take the plug from a genuinely non-weightbearing area of the knee and match plug diameter/depth carefully — a mismatched or proud graft alters contact mechanics and predisposes to early degeneration.
- Predrill and mark the malleolar osteotomy before completing the cut — this preserves anatomic reduction accuracy at closure and avoids a malreduced osteotomy compounding the primary cartilage procedure.
- Use the "sandwich" technique proactively whenever subchondral bone loss is identified, rather than attempting a cartilage-only restoration over an inadequate bony base — restoring the subchondral platform first improves graft/implant support.
- For ACI/MACI, confirm a watertight seal at implantation — leakage of the cell suspension/matrix compromises graft incorporation and is a technique-dependent failure mode distinct from lesion selection.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- WBCT-based lesion mapping increasingly guides the size/containment decision more accurately than conventional (non-weightbearing) CT — relying on older imaging can misclassify a lesion's true size and change the appropriate rung of the treatment ladder.
- Always assess and correct concurrent hindfoot malalignment (varus/valgus) or chronic ankle instability — uncorrected malalignment is a major driver of OLT recurrence/progression.
- Persistent pain/incomplete relief after bone marrow stimulation occurs in a meaningful minority even at long-term follow-up; **10-year survivorship data confirm durable but not universal benefit**, with a meaningful subset requiring secondary procedures over time — this reinforces size/lesion-quality selection rather than universal first-line use of bone marrow stimulation.
- Donor-site morbidity (ipsilateral knee pain) is a recognized trade-off with OATS.
- Graft delamination/failure is a recognized risk with ACI.
- Subchondral cyst recurrence can occur regardless of technique.
- Malleolar osteotomy nonunion or malreduction is an added, procedure-specific risk whenever a restorative technique requires open posterior/central access — track osteotomy healing on its own timeline separate from graft incorporation.
- Incomplete removal of the calcified cartilage layer or poorly spaced microfracture perforations are technical errors specific to bone marrow stimulation that reduce the quality of fibrocartilage fill.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Bone marrow stimulation**: non-weightbearing or touch-down weightbearing 2–6 weeks depending on lesion size/location; early passive ROM/CPM encouraged to promote fibrocartilage organization; progressive weightbearing from 6 weeks; full activity 3–4 months.

**Restorative procedures (OATS/ACI)**: more conservative — non-weightbearing 6 weeks, protected weightbearing to 10–12 weeks, full activity typically not before 6–9 months given the biology of graft incorporation/maturation. When a malleolar osteotomy was performed for access, weightbearing progression is additionally paced by osteotomy union on serial radiographs, not graft biology alone.
