## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Posterior SC dislocation is a surgical/reduction emergency because of proximity to the trachea, esophagus, great vessels, and vagus/phrenic nerves in the retrosternal space — reduction (closed, or open if closed fails) is indicated in essentially all confirmed true posterior dislocations, especially with any symptoms of mediastinal compression (dyspnea, dysphagia, venous congestion).
- Open reduction with ligament repair/reconstruction is indicated when closed reduction fails, when the joint is unstable after an initially successful closed reduction, or in a chronic/locked posterior dislocation.
- Chronic symptomatic instability (anterior or posterior) after failed nonoperative management, or with persistent secondary scapular dyskinesia.

**Contraindications**:
- Anterior SC dislocation, in general — closed reduction is often unsuccessful/unstable to hold, and function is generally preserved despite residual subluxation, so symptomatic management (sling, activity modification) is preferred over a reduction attempt for most anterior injuries.
- Patients under ~25 years with an apparent SC "dislocation" that has not been confirmed by CT to be a true joint dislocation rather than a medial physeal (Salter-Harris I/II) fracture — physeal fractures remodel well and are usually managed nonoperatively even when displaced; do not consent a young patient for open SC surgery without first excluding this differential.
- Internal fixation across the SC joint with smooth pins or wires — absolutely contraindicated regardless of presentation, given the historical, catastrophic risk of pin migration into the mediastinum/great vessels.
- Attempting closed or open reduction without thoracic/cardiothoracic surgery availability for a posterior dislocation — the risk of great vessel injury during manipulation mandates this backup regardless of how straightforward the case appears.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: bilateral AP views for gross comparison, but plain films (including the serendipity view) are unreliable for distinguishing anterior from posterior direction — **CT is the study of choice** for the SC joint, both for diagnosis/direction and for preoperative planning of open reduction; obtain CT angiography whenever posterior dislocation is suspected, to assess the innominate vessels, and to plan for vascular/thoracic surgical involvement.
- **Age-based differential**: in any patient under 25, review CT specifically for a medial physeal fracture pattern rather than true joint dislocation before finalizing an operative plan — the medial clavicular physis is the last in the body to close (~22–25 years) and does not appear on plain radiographs until the late teens.
- **Multidisciplinary planning**: for posterior dislocation, arrange thoracic/cardiothoracic surgery availability (in the room or immediately on standby) before starting either a closed or open reduction attempt; vascular surgery involvement if CT angiography shows any vessel abnormality.
- **Graft planning for reconstruction**: if instability is expected to require formal ligament reconstruction (chronic dislocation, failed closed reduction, or recurrent instability), plan for autograft (semitendinosus or gracilis, or palmaris longus for smaller figure-of-8 constructs) or allograft tendon, plus drill/tunnel instrumentation appropriately sized for the medial clavicle and manubrium.
- **Equipment**: a padded bolster for interscapular positioning, large sterile towel clips (for the towel-clip reduction maneuver), and — for open cases — small fragment fixation/suture anchor sets and heavy non-absorbable suture or suture tape for ligament/disc repair or figure-of-8 reconstruction.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard for both closed and open reduction of posterior dislocations, given the need for full muscle relaxation and the possibility of urgent conversion to an open/thoracic procedure; ensure the anesthesia and surgical teams have discussed the contingency plan for vascular injury before starting.
- **Position**: supine, with a firm bolster or folded towel placed longitudinally between the scapulae to allow the shoulders to fall posteriorly (extend), improving access to the SC joint and facilitating the reduction maneuvers described below.
- **Table setup**: the operating table should allow rapid conversion to a more head-up or supine-flat position if urgent thoracic access is needed; have a sternotomy tray and vascular instruments immediately available in the room (not just "on standby elsewhere") for confirmed or strongly suspected posterior dislocation.
- **Draping**: prep and drape widely from the base of the neck across the sternum to the contralateral SC joint and distally over the clavicle, in case the incision needs extension or contralateral comparison is needed intraoperatively; a wide prep also accommodates emergent sternotomy if catastrophic vascular injury occurs during manipulation.
- **Landmarks**: mark the sternal notch, both SC joints for comparison, and the planned transverse incision directly over the affected joint before beginning.
- **Fluoroscopy**: available in the room to confirm reduction, though CT remains the definitive pre- and post-reduction imaging study given the SC joint's complex 3D anatomy.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the diagnosis and direction.** Review CT (and CT angiography if obtained) immediately before proceeding to confirm true posterior dislocation (as opposed to a medial physeal fracture in a young patient) and to understand the relationship of the displaced medial clavicle to the great vessels.
2. **Position the patient.** Supine, with a bolster between the scapulae to extend the shoulders and improve joint access; prep and drape widely as above.
3. **Attempt closed reduction under general anesthesia — abduction-traction technique.** Abduct the ipsilateral arm to roughly 90° and apply longitudinal traction, sometimes combined with gentle extension/retraction of the shoulder; this alone reduces many posterior dislocations by indirectly translating the medial clavicle anteriorly as the shoulder girdle is retracted over the interscapular bolster.
4. **Add direct manipulation if traction alone is insufficient.** With traction maintained, apply direct posteriorly-to-anteriorly directed pressure over the medial clavicle, or use a sterile towel clip to firmly grasp the medial clavicle percutaneously (clamping around, not through, the bone) and pull it anteriorly until reduction is achieved — this towel-clip technique is a well-described and effective adjunct when traction/abduction alone fails, and should be performed with the vascular/thoracic team aware and ready given the direct manipulation adjacent to the great vessels.
5. **Confirm reduction.** Assess visually/palpably for restored symmetry with the contralateral joint and confirm with fluoroscopy or immediate postoperative CT; note whether the joint feels stable through a gentle range of motion or immediately re-displaces.
6. **If closed reduction fails, or the joint is unstable after reduction, proceed to open reduction.** Make a transverse incision directly over the SC joint. Carefully dissect down to the joint capsule, staying superficial and controlled given the posterior proximity of the innominate vein/artery, trachea, and esophagus — do not dissect blindly posterior to the medial clavicle.
7. **Expose and assess the joint.** Identify the intra-articular disc and the extent of ligamentous (anterior/posterior capsular, costoclavicular, interclavicular) disruption; débride nonviable disc/ligament tissue while preserving anything repairable.
8. **Reduce the medial clavicle directly under vision.** With the joint exposed, reduce the medial clavicle anteriorly under direct visualization — this is far safer than blind closed manipulation once the capsule is already open, since the great vessels can be kept under indirect observation/protection during the maneuver.
9. **Repair or reconstruct the joint.** For an acute injury with repairable native ligament, perform primary capsular/ligament repair, often reinforced with a suture or suture-tape tension-band construct across the joint. For chronic instability or a joint too attenuated for primary repair, perform a formal ligament reconstruction — most commonly a figure-of-8 free tendon graft (semitendinosus, gracilis, or palmaris longus) passed through drill tunnels in the medial clavicle and manubrium, tensioned with the joint held reduced, and secured with interference screws, button fixation, or suture anchors; some techniques use a double figure-of-8 configuration with internal brace augmentation for added early stability.
10. **Do not use smooth pins or wires across the joint.** Regardless of the fixation/reconstruction technique chosen, avoid any form of internal fixation with smooth Kirschner wires or pins spanning the SC joint — this is obsolete and contraindicated because of historical, well-documented cases of pin breakage and migration into the mediastinum and great vessels, sometimes with fatal outcomes.
11. **Confirm final stability and hemostasis.** Range the joint gently to confirm stability of the repair/reconstruction, achieve meticulous hemostasis (this region is vascular and adjacent to major vessels), and consider a drain if there is any concern for a hematoma tracking toward the mediastinum.
12. **Layered closure.** Close the capsule (if a formal capsular repair is not already the primary construct), subcutaneous tissue, and skin.
13. **Postoperative monitoring.** Obtain postoperative CT to confirm reduction and hardware/graft position, and monitor early for any signs of delayed mediastinal compromise.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always obtain CT (with CT angiography if posterior dislocation is suspected) before committing to a reduction plan — plain films, including the serendipity view, are unreliable for direction and will not show the relationship to the great vessels that changes intraoperative risk tolerance.
- The interscapular bolster does more work than it might seem — properly positioned, it alone can reduce many posterior dislocations through arm abduction-traction without ever needing direct manipulation of the medial clavicle.
- When using a towel clip on the medial clavicle, clamp around the bone (not penetrating it) and apply steady, controlled anterior traction rather than a sudden jerk — a controlled pull is both safer and more often successful than a forceful, rapid maneuver.
- Keep thoracic/cardiothoracic surgery physically in the room (not merely "on call") for any posterior dislocation reduction attempt, closed or open — the entire rationale for treating this as an emergency is the speed with which a vascular injury during manipulation can become catastrophic.
- If closed reduction succeeds but "clunks" back out with gentle stress testing, do not simply accept it and hope for stability with immobilization alone — proceed to open stabilization, since an unstable reduction predicts early redislocation.
- Once the joint is open, reduce under direct vision rather than repeating blind closed maneuvers — direct visualization is materially safer for the retrosternal structures than a closed reduction attempt performed through intact soft tissue.
- For reconstruction, a figure-of-8 tendon graft construct (with or without internal brace augmentation) is currently the most widely reported and durable technique for chronic instability — plan graft harvest and tunnel sizing preoperatively rather than improvising intraoperatively.
- In any patient under 25 with a presumed SC dislocation, get the CT and specifically look for a physeal fracture line before consenting for surgery — this single check avoids operating on an injury that would have remodeled on its own.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Internal fixation with smooth pins across the SC joint is contraindicated due to migration risk into the mediastinum/great vessels — this remains one of the most important absolute prohibitions in this entire technique.
- In patients under 25, confirm true dislocation versus medial physeal fracture with CT before committing to open surgery — physeal fractures remodel and are managed nonoperatively.
- Posterior dislocation is a mediastinal compression emergency (dyspnea, dysphagia, venous congestion) requiring CT angiography workup and cardiothoracic surgery involvement.
- Blind posterior dissection during open exposure risks direct injury to the innominate vein/artery, trachea, or esophagus — stay controlled and superficial until the joint capsule is clearly identified.
- A towel-clip reduction performed with excessive force, or without vascular/thoracic backup immediately available, converts a controlled maneuver into a potential vascular emergency.
- Chronic SC joint instability/pain is more common after posterior dislocation with associated ligament disruption; mediastinal complications, while rare, are catastrophic and are the reason posterior dislocation is treated as an emergency.
- Reduction that appears successful but is unstable to gentle stress testing predicts early redislocation if simply immobilized — this finding should prompt open stabilization rather than reliance on a sling alone.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not specify a dedicated postoperative rehabilitation timeline for SC joint surgery; general activity modification and symptomatic management apply following reduction, with attention to the elevated risk of chronic instability/pain after posterior dislocations with associated ligament disruption. In practice, closed or open reduction is typically followed by a period of sling immobilization (commonly several weeks) with early elbow/wrist/hand motion, progressing to shoulder ROM and strengthening as pain and stability allow, and postoperative CT to confirm maintained reduction before advancing activity; return to contact sport or heavy labor should await confirmed joint stability on clinical exam.
