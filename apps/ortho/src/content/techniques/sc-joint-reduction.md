## Indications

Posterior SC dislocation is a surgical/reduction emergency because of proximity to the trachea, esophagus, great vessels, and vagus/phrenic nerves in the retrosternal space. Anterior dislocations are generally managed symptomatically without a reduction attempt, since closed reduction is often unsuccessful/unstable to hold and function is generally preserved despite residual subluxation.

## Positioning & setup

Transverse incision over the joint for open reduction. Thoracic surgery should be on standby given the risk of great vessel injury during manipulation. **Pediatric caveat**: in patients under 25, rule out a medial physeal (Salter-Harris type I/II) fracture with CT before consenting for open SC joint surgery — the medial clavicular physis is the last in the body to close (~22–25 years) and does not appear on plain radiographs until the late teens, so many apparent "SC joint dislocations" in this age group are actually physeal fractures, which remodel well and are usually managed nonoperatively even when displaced.

## Steps

1. **Attempt closed reduction under general anesthesia**: traction/abduction of the arm with a bolster placed between the scapulae, combined with direct posteriorly-directed pressure or towel-clip reduction to disengage and reduce the clavicle.
2. Have thoracic surgery on standby throughout, given the risk of great vessel injury during manipulation.
3. **If closed reduction fails, or the joint is unstable after reduction**, proceed to open reduction with ligament/meniscal disc repair or reconstruction (e.g., figure-of-8 free tendon graft reconstruction).
4. **Avoid internal fixation with smooth pins across the SC joint** — historically associated with catastrophic pin migration into the mediastinum/great vessels, now considered obsolete and contraindicated.

## Pitfalls

- Internal fixation with smooth pins across the SC joint is contraindicated due to migration risk into the mediastinum/great vessels.
- In patients under 25, confirm true dislocation versus medial physeal fracture with CT before committing to open surgery — physeal fractures remodel and are managed nonoperatively.
- Posterior dislocation is a mediastinal compression emergency (dyspnea, dysphagia, venous congestion) requiring CT angiography workup and cardiothoracic surgery involvement.
- Chronic SC joint instability/pain is more common after posterior dislocation with associated ligament disruption; mediastinal complications, while rare, are catastrophic and are the reason posterior dislocation is treated as an emergency.

## Post-op protocol

The monograph does not specify a dedicated postoperative rehabilitation timeline for SC joint surgery; general activity modification and symptomatic management apply following reduction, with attention to the elevated risk of chronic instability/pain after posterior dislocations with associated ligament disruption.
