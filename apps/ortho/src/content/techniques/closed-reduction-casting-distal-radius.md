## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: extra-articular distal radius fracture (DRF) with acceptable alignment (either non-displaced or reducible to an acceptable position), and unstable or elderly/low-demand patients with poor alignment tolerance managed via closed reduction with weekly radiographic surveillance. Acceptable alignment thresholds should be defined **in advance** based on patient age and functional demand — the same displacement tolerated in a low-demand 80-year-old is an indication for surgery in an active 45-year-old. Simple intra-articular patterns with step/gap ≤2 mm after reduction in a low-demand patient are also reasonable candidates.

**Contraindications**:
- Open fracture — requires irrigation/debridement per open-fracture protocol before any definitive immobilization decision; temporary splinting only.
- Grossly unstable or highly comminuted metaphyseal pattern (Fernandez V, AO/OTA 23-C3) where closed reduction is very unlikely to be maintained — reasonable to attempt a single reduction/splint as a bridge, but definitive plan should anticipate surgery.
- Marginal shear (Barton-type) fractures — inherently unstable due to the shear mechanism and carpal subluxation risk; closed treatment reliably fails.
- Active acute carpal tunnel syndrome (ACTS) or forearm compartment syndrome — these need emergent decompression, not casting, as the primary intervention.
- Segmental/"floating wrist" or ipsilateral forearm shaft injury — needs surgical stabilization.
- Relative: cognitive impairment or social circumstances precluding reliable cast care and weekly follow-up attendance — consider surgical fixation to reduce the burden of surveillance.
- Relative: significant dorsal or volar skin blistering/soft-tissue compromise at presentation — may still splint provisionally, but circumferential casting should wait until skin is safe.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: PA, lateral, and oblique wrist radiographs pre-reduction. Measure radial inclination, radial height, volar/dorsal tilt, articular step-off/gap, and ulnar variance; compare to normal values (radial inclination 22–23°, radial height 11–12 mm, volar tilt 11° [range 0–22°], ulnar variance neutral ±2 mm) and, if baseline anatomy is uncertain, to the contralateral wrist.
- **Decide the reduction target before touching the patient**: for a functional-demand adult, aim for radial inclination >15°, radial height loss <5 mm, dorsal tilt <10°, articular step/gap ≤2 mm; in a low-demand elderly patient a more permissive target (dorsal angulation up to 10–20°, radial shortening up to 5 mm) is acceptable, since radiographic alignment correlates poorly with functional outcome in this group compared with grip strength and ROM.
- **Anesthesia plan**: hematoma block is adequate for most closed reductions and avoids sedation risk in elderly/comorbid patients; reserve Bier block or procedural sedation for more painful, markedly displaced, or anxious presentations, or when a prolonged/repeated manipulation is anticipated.
- **Equipment/consumables checklist**: hematoma block kit (1% plain lidocaine, 10–20 mL, 21–23G needle), finger traps (Chinese finger traps) with a traction stand or IV pole and 5–10 lb of hanging weight, stockinette, orthopedic wool/cast padding, plaster or fiberglass slab material for the initial splint, definitive short-arm (or long-arm/Muenster, if rotational control anticipated) casting material for the follow-up visit, cast saw and spreader for later adjustments.
- **Case-specific planning**: confirm whether concomitant DRUJ instability or ulnar styloid base fracture is present on the pre-reduction films — this changes the immobilization position and follow-up plan; pre-book weekly radiography for the first 3 weeks so surveillance is not left to chance; set explicit expectations with the patient that ~13% of cast-treated adults in the DRAFFT2 trial required rescue surgical fixation for loss of position, which is part of informed consent for the nonoperative pathway.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Hematoma block**: prep the dorsal wrist with antiseptic. Palpate the fracture site (usually 2–3 cm proximal to the radiocarpal joint dorsally, at the point of maximal tenderness/deformity). Insert the needle into the fracture hematoma and confirm placement by aspirating dark, non-clotting blood. Inject 10 mL of 1% plain lidocaine directly into the hematoma; allow 5–10 minutes for onset before manipulating. Avoid injecting into intact soft tissue only — a "miss" gives poor analgesia and a false sense that the block has failed.
- **Alternative anesthesia**: Bier block (IV regional anesthesia) or procedural sedation (e.g., fentanyl/midazolam or ketamine) with full monitoring per departmental sedation protocol for more displaced fractures or when repeat manipulation is likely.
- **Position**: patient seated or supine with the arm supported on a hand table or arm board, shoulder abducted approximately 90°, elbow flexed 90°.
- **Countertraction**: apply finger traps to the index and middle (± ring) fingers and suspend 5–10 lb of weight over the flexed elbow for 5–10 minutes before manipulation. This fatigues the forearm musculature, disimpacts the fracture, and restores length, substantially reducing the force needed for manual reduction — a step that is easy to skip under time pressure but pays off in reduction quality.
- **Imaging setup**: perform reduction under mini-fluoroscopy where available to confirm alignment in real time; otherwise obtain post-reduction/post-splint radiographs in the department before the patient leaves.
- **Landmarks to identify and mark/palpate before manipulating**: radial styloid, Lister's tubercle, ulnar styloid, and the dorsal step-off at the fracture site — these guide both the direction of the reduction force and where to apply the three-point mold.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the diagnosis and displacement direction (dorsal/Colles vs volar/Smith vs marginal shear) on pre-reduction radiographs, and document a full baseline neurovascular exam, explicitly including median nerve sensory (2-point discrimination, light touch) and motor (thenar/APB) status — this baseline matters both clinically and medicolegally.
2. Obtain IV access if sedation is planned; otherwise proceed directly to hematoma block.
3. Perform the hematoma block (or induce sedation/Bier block) and allow adequate onset time before manipulating.
4. Apply finger-trap traction with 5–10 lb of countertraction for 5–10 minutes to disimpact the fracture and restore length.
5. Perform the reduction maneuver specific to the displacement pattern:
   - **Dorsally displaced (Colles)**: with traction maintained, place both thumbs over the dorsal distal fragment and push it volarly while the fingers support and counter-pressure the volar/proximal shaft; correct the dorsal tilt by then pronating the forearm and gently flexing/ulnarly deviating the wrist. Avoid extreme flexion.
   - **Volarly displaced (Smith)**: reverse the maneuver — with traction maintained, apply a dorsally directed force on the distal fragment while extending and supinating the wrist.
   - **Marginal shear (Barton) patterns**: closed reduction rarely holds durably; a single attempt to improve position for comfort/splinting is reasonable, but plan definitive surgical fixation.
6. Maintain the reduction (traction plus manual pressure) while an assistant applies the splint — do not release the reduction to apply padding yourself.
7. Apply stockinette and cast padding over the forearm, keeping padding modest at the fracture site itself so the subsequent mold is effective (excess padding blunts the three-point mold).
8. Apply a well-padded sugar-tong or volar/dorsal slab splint — avoid a circumferential cast in the first 24–48 hours given this is a swelling-prone injury.
9. Mold actively with three-point pressure while the plaster/fiberglass is still workable: dorsal pressure directly at the fracture level, opposing volar pressure proximal and distal to it, and an interosseous mold along the forearm shaft; hold until the material sets.
10. Position the wrist in slight flexion (roughly 10–15°) and ulnar deviation. Do not use the Cotton-Loder position (extreme flexion) — it is obsolete and associated with median nerve compression and stiffness.
11. Obtain post-reduction radiographs in at least two planes; remeasure radial inclination, height, tilt, and step/gap against the pre-agreed acceptable thresholds.
12. If alignment remains unacceptable, a second reduction attempt is reasonable; limit to two attempts before referring for surgical fixation rather than repeatedly re-manipulating a fracture that will not hold.
13. Provide splint care instructions (elevation, digit motion, signs of a tight cast) and confirm the weekly radiograph follow-up schedule for the first 3 weeks before the patient leaves.
14. At the first follow-up visit (typically 5–10 days, once acute swelling has plateaued), convert the splint to a definitive, well-molded three-point short-arm cast (or long-arm/Muenster cast if rotational control is needed for associated DRUJ instability); repeat radiographs after the cast change, since the change in mold can itself alter alignment.
15. Continue weekly radiographs through week 3 (the highest redisplacement-risk window), then check again at 6 weeks for union.
16. Transition cooperative, radiographically stable patients to a removable orthosis around week 4–5, beginning gentle active wrist ROM within the orthosis.
17. Discontinue immobilization at 4–6 weeks total once clinical and radiographic healing is adequate, and progress into the formal rehabilitation phases below.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Apply finger-trap traction with hanging weight for a full 5–10 minutes before attempting manipulation — muscle fatigue markedly eases the reduction and reduces the force required, which in turn reduces the risk of over-vigorous manipulation causing further soft-tissue injury.
- Confirm hematoma block needle placement by aspirating blood before injecting; injecting into soft tissue alone gives poor analgesia and can be mistaken for "block failure" when the real issue is needle position.
- Mold with an intentionally non-circular cast cross-section — a flattened dorsal-volar profile with a well-defined interosseous crease resists redisplacement substantially better than a round cast shell.
- Keep the cast trim line proximal to the MCP joints so fingers can flex to at least 90° at the MCPs, and leave the thumb IP/finger DIP-PIP joints free — this protects grip and prevents iatrogenic stiffness.
- Use a genuine two-person technique for the definitive cast: one person sustains the reduction, the second applies and molds the material — trying to do both single-handedly compromises the mold.
- Re-image immediately after any cast change (not just at the next scheduled interval), since remolding, swelling changes, or cast loosening can silently lose position between visits.
- In elderly, low-demand patients, resist the urge to chase perfect radiographic parameters with repeated aggressive manipulations — grip strength and ROM are better predictors of patient-reported outcome than strict alignment in this population.
- Educate the patient explicitly on tight-cast warning signs (increasing pain, paresthesia, pallor) and have a low threshold to bivalve or split the cast promptly if these occur, rather than waiting for the next visit.
- When converting from splint to cast, reassess for a small volar or dorsal marginal fragment that may not have been obvious on the initial films — a pattern that looked "simple" acutely can declare itself as unstable once swelling resolves.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- The Cotton-Loder position is obsolete and should not be used — it is associated with median nerve compression and stiffness.
- Circumferential casting in the first 24–48 hours risks compartment syndrome/acute carpal tunnel syndrome (ACTS) in a swelling-prone injury; use an initial splint and convert once swelling plateaus.
- Weekly films through week 3 are standard practice because this is the highest-risk window for early redisplacement; skipping this surveillance is the most common preventable cause of a missed rescue-fixation window.
- DRAFFT2 found 13% of cast-treated patients required rescue fixation for loss of position, versus ~0% in the K-wire arm — an important counseling point when choosing cast-first management, and a reminder to act promptly on redisplacement rather than accepting it retrospectively.
- Acute carpal tunnel syndrome complicates 4.5–9% of DRFs; the median nerve lies immediately volar to the fracture site and is vulnerable to direct contusion, traction, hematoma, or compartment pressure — progressive deficit or disproportionate pain after reduction/casting demands emergent carpal tunnel release, not observation or repeat imaging.
- An overly tight primary circumferential cast is a recognized, avoidable contributor to both compartment syndrome and ACTS — this is the rationale for splinting first and casting only after the swelling-prone window has passed.
- Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is reported in a meaningful minority of DRF patients; early mobilization of unaffected joints (digits, elbow, shoulder) and adequate analgesia are the principal modifiable mitigations.
- In DRUJ co-management, forearm rotation should be immobilized in the arc of maximal stability determined by clinical or intraoperative stress testing, not a fixed default position — casting in an arbitrary position risks recurrent DRUJ instability.
- Malunion (dorsal angulation, radial shortening) is the most common complication of nonoperative treatment; symptomatic malunion in a functionally demanding patient is addressed later with corrective osteotomy rather than prevented by over-aggressive initial reduction attempts.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: immobilized; digit/elbow/shoulder AROM from day 1 regardless of treatment method; cryotherapy; strict avoidance of forearm rotation and wrist loading; weekly radiographs for redisplacement surveillance; continued median nerve status documentation.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: maintain immobilization; weekly films to week 3; transition to removable orthosis around week 4–5 in cooperative patients with stable radiographic position, beginning gentle active wrist ROM within the orthosis.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: progressive active and active-assisted wrist flexion/extension, radial/ulnar deviation, and forearm pronosupination; light functional ADLs; gentle grip strengthening from ~week 6–8 once union is radiographically progressing.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: progressive resistive strengthening, proprioceptive/weight-bearing exercises, sport- or occupation-specific reconditioning.
- **Imaging**: post-reduction, then weekly to 3 wk, then 6 wk (union check), then as clinically indicated.
- **Loading milestones**: light functional loading (typing, light ADLs) generally from ~6 weeks once early union present; unrestricted loading/impact activity deferred to ~12 weeks with confirmed union.
- **Return to work**: sedentary/desk work often feasible within 1–2 weeks (non-dominant hand) or once splinted comfortably; manual labor/heavy lifting deferred to ~12 weeks with radiographic union and functional grip strength (commonly ≥70–80% of contralateral) and near-full pronosupination.
- **Return to sport**: pain-free full composite ROM, grip strength ≥85–90% of contralateral side, radiographic union, and sport-specific functional testing — generally not before 3 months for high-demand/impact sport.
