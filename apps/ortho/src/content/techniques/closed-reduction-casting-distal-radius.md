## Indications

Extra-articular distal radius fracture (DRF) with acceptable alignment (displaced or reducible to an acceptable position), and unstable or elderly/low-demand patients with poor alignment tolerance managed via closed reduction with weekly radiographic surveillance. Acceptable alignment thresholds should be defined in advance based on patient age and functional demand — the same displacement tolerated in a low-demand 80-year-old is an indication for surgery in an active 45-year-old.

## Positioning & setup

Hematoma block (intra-fracture lidocaine injection) is adequate for most closed reductions and avoids sedation risk, particularly relevant in elderly, comorbid patients; Bier block/procedural sedation is an alternative for displaced fractures requiring reduction in the ED.

## Steps

1. Anesthetize with hematoma block or, alternatively, Bier block/procedural sedation.
2. Perform closed reduction of the displaced fracture.
3. Apply a well-padded sugar-tong or volar/dorsal splint initially — avoid circumferential cast application in the first 24–48 hours in a swelling-prone injury.
4. Convert to a definitive, well-molded three-point (interosseous, dorsal, volar) short-arm cast once acute swelling has plateaued, typically 5–10 days; this timing also reduces compartment syndrome/acute carpal tunnel syndrome (ACTS) risk from an overly tight primary cast.
5. Mold the wrist in slight flexion and ulnar deviation; avoid extreme flexion — the "Cotton-Loder" position is obsolete and associated with median nerve compression and stiffness.
6. Reserve long-arm/Muenster casting for cases needing rotational control (e.g., DRUJ instability co-management), since forearm rotation control — not elbow immobilization — is the actual biomechanical goal; a well-molded short-arm cast is as effective as a long-arm cast for most extra-articular fractures and is better tolerated.
7. Obtain weekly radiographs for the first 3 weeks (the highest redisplacement-risk window), then at 6 weeks.
8. Total immobilization typically 4–6 weeks, transitioning to a removable orthosis for the final 1–2 weeks in cooperative, stable patients to permit early elbow/finger motion.

## Pitfalls

- The Cotton-Loder position is obsolete and should not be used — it is associated with median nerve compression and stiffness.
- Circumferential casting in the first 24–48 hours risks compartment syndrome/ACTS in a swelling-prone injury; use an initial splint and convert once swelling plateaus.
- Weekly films through week 3 are standard practice because this is the highest-risk window for early redisplacement.
- DRAFFT2 found 13% of cast-treated patients required rescue fixation for loss of position, versus ~0% in the K-wire arm — an important counseling point when choosing cast-first management.
- In DRUJ co-management, forearm rotation should be immobilized in the arc of maximal stability determined by clinical or intraoperative stress testing, not a fixed default position.

## Post-op protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: immobilized; digit/elbow/shoulder AROM from day 1 regardless of treatment method; cryotherapy; strict avoidance of forearm rotation and wrist loading; weekly radiographs for redisplacement surveillance; continued median nerve status documentation.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: maintain immobilization; weekly films to week 3; transition to removable orthosis around week 4–5 in cooperative patients with stable radiographic position, beginning gentle active wrist ROM within the orthosis.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: progressive active and active-assisted wrist flexion/extension, radial/ulnar deviation, and forearm pronosupination; light functional ADLs; gentle grip strengthening from ~week 6–8 once union is radiographically progressing.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: progressive resistive strengthening, proprioceptive/weight-bearing exercises, sport- or occupation-specific reconditioning.
- **Imaging**: post-reduction, then weekly to 3 wk, then 6 wk (union check), then as clinically indicated.
- **Loading milestones**: light functional loading (typing, light ADLs) generally from ~6 weeks once early union present; unrestricted loading/impact activity deferred to ~12 weeks with confirmed union.
- **Return to work**: sedentary/desk work often feasible within 1–2 weeks (non-dominant hand) or once splinted comfortably; manual labor/heavy lifting deferred to ~12 weeks with radiographic union and functional grip strength (commonly ≥70–80% of contralateral) and near-full pronosupination.
- **Return to sport**: pain-free full composite ROM, grip strength ≥85–90% of contralateral side, radiographic union, and sport-specific functional testing — generally not before 3 months for high-demand/impact sport.
