## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- High-energy, comminuted (Rüedi III / AO 43-C) pilon fractures — the staged protocol (Sirkin et al., 1999) remains the accepted standard, driven by the fragility of the thin, poorly vascularized anterior soft-tissue envelope of the distal tibia (minimal muscle coverage, watershed skin perfusion), which is the rate-limiting factor for surgical timing, not the bone injury itself.
- Rüedi II fractures with significant swelling, fracture blisters, or an open wound — soft-tissue status, not the articular pattern alone, drives the staging decision.
- Open pilon fractures requiring staged debridement and soft-tissue management before definitive plating.
- Immediate ORIF of high-energy patterns historically carried wound complication rates as high as 25–40%, the entire rationale for staging.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- A true Rüedi I non-displaced fracture — treat nonoperatively (well-molded splint/cast, strict non-weight-bearing, close radiographic follow-up) rather than committing to a staged surgical pathway.
- Active soft-tissue infection or cellulitis over the planned incision — treat the infection first; do not proceed to definitive fixation through infected tissue.
- Uncontrolled peripheral vascular disease or severe microvascular compromise (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes with PAD) — wound healing risk is substantially elevated; consider a definitive circular external fixator with limited internal fixation as an alternative to open plating.
- Medically unstable polytrauma patients who cannot tolerate a prolonged definitive procedure — the spanning fixator can remain in place longer than the typical 10–21 day window if the patient's overall status dictates.
- Severe, unreconstructable comminution with bone loss precluding a stable plate construct — consider circular (Ilizarov/hexapod) frame as the definitive strategy rather than forcing an unstable plate fixation.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** definitive CT (2–3 mm cuts, coronal/sagittal reconstruction) obtained *after* the spanning external fixator is applied, not before — the ligamentotaxis reduction improves fragment visualization and reduces artifact from gross displacement, and this is what enables articular fragment counting and approach planning.
- **Fragment mapping:** identify the major articular fragments before the case — anterolateral (Chaput), posterolateral (Volkmann), medial malleolar, and central impacted fragments — and plan the sequence of reduction and the approach(es) needed to reach each.
- **Implant templating:** anatomically contoured distal tibia locking plate options (anterolateral, anteromedial, medial) sized against the contralateral limb; have more than one plate footprint available given the variability of fracture geometry.
- **Graft/void-filler planning:** synthetic calcium-phosphate/calcium-sulfate substitute for straightforward metaphyseal voids; reserve iliac crest autograft for larger defects or compromised bone quality — plan and consent for a graft harvest site if autograft is anticipated.
- **Fibular status:** confirm whether the fibula was fixed at stage 1 or remains to be fixed at stage 2 — this changes the approach sequence, since a well-reduced fibula restores the length/rotation reference for the tibial reduction.
- **Incision planning:** plan stage 2 incisions to preserve a skin bridge of at least 7 cm from any fibular incision, and to avoid incorporating prior external fixator pin tracts directly into the wound.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** large and small fragment sets, distal tibia locking plate system, mini-fragment set for articular fragments, pointed (Weber) reduction clamps, K-wires, femoral distractor or spare external fixator components for provisional distraction, mini C-arm or standard fluoroscopy, radiolucent table, tourniquet (available but used sparingly), loupe magnification.
- **Vascular review:** repeat neurovascular exam; obtain CT angiography if any pulse asymmetry or concern for vascular injury was noted at initial presentation.
- **Plastics coordination:** arrange plastic surgery availability in advance for cases with marginal soft-tissue coverage anticipated at closure.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia or spinal/regional anesthesia (popliteal sciatic + saphenous block, or combined sciatic-femoral) — regional block is valuable for postoperative analgesia given the prolonged recovery.
- Supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the ipsilateral hip/buttock to bring the limb to neutral rotation.
- Thigh tourniquet applied but used judiciously — many pilon surgeons avoid prolonged inflation given the already-compromised soft-tissue envelope, reserving it for discrete portions of the dissection rather than the whole case.
- Foot free at the end of the table (or on a radiolucent extension) to allow manipulation and fluoroscopic imaging in all planes.
- If a posterior malleolar (Volkmann) fragment requires a separate posteromedial or posterolateral approach, plan positioning in advance — this may mean intraoperative repositioning (supine to prone, or a lateral decubitus/"floppy lateral" position) and should be discussed with the anesthesia and OR team before draping.
- Prep and drape the entire leg circumferentially from above the knee to the toes, allowing full visualization, manipulation, and side-to-side comparison.
- Mark surface landmarks before incision: tibial crest, medial malleolus, fibula, anterior tibial tendon, extensor hallucis longus, and the planned incision lines relative to the prior external fixator pin sites.
- Confirm fluoroscopic access (AP, lateral, and mortise views of the ankle) before final draping.
- Remove the external fixator at the start of the case (or the day before); inspect pin sites for drainage or infection and curette/treat as needed before proceeding — treat any actively draining pin tract as contaminated tissue to be avoided by the definitive incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Stage 1 (urgent, within hours of injury):** apply a spanning external fixator using calcaneal and proximal tibial pins crossing the ankle, restoring length, alignment, and rough reduction via ligamentotaxis under fluoroscopy. Fix the fibula only if it will not compromise later soft-tissue planning — many surgeons defer fibular fixation to the definitive stage to avoid a second incision through swollen tissue.
2. Elevate the limb, apply ice, splint as needed, and monitor the soft-tissue envelope with serial exams at each dressing change or clinic visit.
3. Obtain the definitive CT (2–3 mm cuts, coronal/sagittal reconstruction) after the spanning fixator is in place.
4. **Wait for the wrinkle sign** (skin folds return, fracture blisters re-epithelialize, edema subsides) before proceeding to definitive fixation — typically 10–21 days, occasionally 3+ weeks for the most severe injuries.
5. Hold a dedicated preoperative planning session with the CT reconstructions: identify the major fragments, decide on approach(es) and sequence, and template plate position.
6. **Stage 2 (definitive):** remove the external fixator, prep and drape.
7. **Fix the fibula first** if it was not addressed at stage 1 and length/rotation is not otherwise restored, via a direct lateral approach with a plate — this re-establishes the length and rotation reference before tackling the tibial articular reduction. If already fixed, confirm the reduction is still adequate.
8. Approach the tibia through a direct anterior, anteromedial, or anterolateral incision based on the fracture pattern and planned plate position. For the anterolateral approach, work in the interval between tibialis anterior and the extensor tendons; elevate periosteum minimally and protect the anterior neurovascular bundle and superficial peroneal nerve.
9. Identify and mobilize the major articular fragments; use a femoral distractor (or residual fixator pins) for provisional longitudinal distraction if additional visualization is needed.
10. Reduce the articular surface first — generally working posterior to anterior and medial to lateral, reducing large fragments to one another and to the fibula/talus as a single reduced block, using pointed reduction clamps and provisional K-wires; confirm joint congruity on AP, lateral, and mortise fluoroscopic views.
11. Elevate central impacted/depressed fragments through a metaphyseal cortical window (or via the articular fracture lines) with a bone tamp; support the elevated subchondral bone with bone graft or void filler.
12. Provisionally pin the reduced articular block and confirm reduction with fluoroscopy.
13. Apply the anatomically contoured locking plate along the reduced articular block, positioned to buttress the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction — use minimally invasive plate osteosynthesis (submuscular plate passage with limited additional soft-tissue stripping) where the fracture pattern allows.
14. Place subchondral raft screws just beneath the joint surface to support the articular fragments, then metaphyseal/diaphyseal screws to secure the plate to the shaft.
15. Perform a final fluoroscopic check in AP, lateral, and mortise views, assessing articular congruity, plate/screw position, and ankle joint space.
16. If the posterior malleolar (Volkmann) fragment is not adequately reduced/fixed from the anterior approach, address it through a separate posteromedial or posterolateral approach (with repositioning or external limb rotation as planned), placing a separate plate as needed.
17. Irrigate copiously; close in layers without tension — if tension is present, consider an incisional negative-pressure dressing or delayed closure/local flap rather than forcing a tight primary closure.
18. Splint the limb in a neutral position and elevate postoperatively.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Fix the fibula first when length or rotation is in question — it is the most reliable reference for restoring tibial length and rotation before the articular work begins.
- Use the "fracture reduces to itself" principle: articular fragments typically key together in only one orientation; identify the least-displaced fragment as a stable reference point and build the reduction around it.
- A femoral distractor spanning from the calcaneus to the proximal tibia (or a residual external fixator pin left in place until reduction is complete) gives excellent longitudinal traction for articular visualization without an assistant holding manual traction.
- Preserve every viable soft-tissue attachment to articular fragments during elevation — devascularized free fragments have poor healing potential and are prone to late collapse.
- Elevate central depression through the metaphyseal window with a small curved bone tamp or dental pick under direct/fluoroscopic guidance rather than blindly punching upward, which risks over- or under-correction.
- Contour the plate using the uninjured contralateral ankle radiograph as a template when the local anatomy is too disrupted to judge normal contour.
- Where the articular block has been separately reduced and fixed, consider a closed or limited-open technique for the metaphyseal-diaphyseal component (using the plate itself as a reduction tool) to minimize further soft-tissue stripping.
- Avoid prolonged tourniquet inflation — operating without inflation for the majority of the case reduces additional ischemic stress on an already compromised soft-tissue envelope.
- Arrange plastic surgery involvement proactively for any case with marginal soft-tissue coverage anticipated at closure, rather than reacting after the wound cannot be closed.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Nonoperative management is essentially never appropriate for a displaced high-energy pilon fracture — reserve it for a true Rüedi I non-displaced fracture in a medically unfit or very low-demand patient.
- Randomized and network meta-analysis data through 2025 continue to support staged management as reducing wound complications and deep infection versus early single-stage ORIF in high-energy patterns — do not skip staging reflexively based on isolated favorable-soft-tissue case series from other injury patterns.
- The national ENFORCE audit (UK, 2024; 656 patients/27 centres) documents wide real-world variation in how strictly the classic staged protocol is followed in practice, underscoring that this is a judgment-driven pathway rather than a rigid algorithm.
- Missing a posterior malleolar (Volkmann) fragment on the anterior approach and failing to address it separately is a common cause of persistent articular incongruity — reassess the CT for a posterior shear component before closing.
- Wound dehiscence or marginal skin necrosis at the anterior incision is the classic early postoperative complication — watch closely in the first 2–3 weeks, particularly at the apex of anterior flap incisions.
- Deep surgical site infection/osteomyelitis is the dominant complication historically associated with early ORIF through compromised soft tissue (wound complication rates up to 25–40% in the pre-staged-protocol era); the staged approach substantially mitigates but does not eliminate this risk.
- Structures at risk: the superficial peroneal nerve and anterior neurovascular bundle with anterolateral/anterior dissection; the saphenous nerve and vein with anteromedial/medial dissection.
- Hardware prominence is common at the subcutaneous distal tibia and frequently requires later removal once union is secure.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is the dominant long-term morbidity of pilon fractures; risk scales with initial articular step-off/gap, comminution grade, and quality of reduction — pilon fractures carry substantial rates of radiographic OA by 5–10 years even with technically sound fixation, given the severity of initial cartilage injury.
- Chronic swelling is common after pilon fixation and may persist a year or more — set expectations accordingly.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** strict non-weight-bearing, splint/boot immobilization, elevation, and close wound surveillance — wound complication rates are historically highest in this fracture group.
- **2–6 weeks:** early protected ROM of the ankle once wound healing is secure (typically starting 2–3 weeks), continued non-weight-bearing.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive partial weight-bearing beginning around 8–12 weeks depending on comminution and radiographic union — pilon fractures generally require a longer period of protected weight-bearing than plateau or shaft fractures given the articular comminution and metaphyseal healing demands.
- **3–6 months:** progression to full weight-bearing, ankle strengthening, gait normalization, edema management (chronic swelling is common and may persist a year or more).
- **Return to work:** sedentary 6–10 weeks, manual/standing labor 4–8 months.
- **Return to sport/impact activity:** highly individualized, often 6–12 months; a meaningful proportion of high-energy Rüedi III/AO 43-C3 patients will not return to pre-injury impact activity due to post-traumatic osteoarthritis.
