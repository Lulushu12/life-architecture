## Indications

The staged protocol (Sirkin et al., 1999) remains the accepted standard for high-energy, comminuted (Rüedi III / AO 43-C) pilon fractures. It is driven by the fragility of the thin, poorly vascularized anterior soft-tissue envelope of the distal tibia (minimal muscle coverage, watershed skin perfusion), which is the rate-limiting factor for surgical timing — not the bone injury itself. Immediate ORIF of high-energy patterns historically carried wound complication rates as high as 25–40%, the entire rationale for staging.

## Positioning & setup

Stage 1 uses calcaneal and proximal tibial external fixator pins crossing the ankle. Stage 2 incisions are planned to preserve a skin bridge of at least 7 cm from any fibular incision, typically through a direct anterior or anteromedial/anterolateral approach, sometimes combined with a posterolateral approach for posterior malleolar fragments.

## Steps

1. **Stage 1 (urgent):** apply a spanning external fixator (calcaneal and proximal tibial pins, crossing the ankle), restoring length, alignment, and rough reduction via ligamentotaxis. Fix the fibula only if it will not compromise later soft-tissue planning — some surgeons defer fibular fixation to the definitive stage to avoid a second incision through swollen tissue. Elevate the limb, apply ice, and monitor the soft-tissue envelope serially.
2. **Obtain definitive CT (2–3 mm cuts, coronal/sagittal reconstruction) after the spanning ex-fix**, not before — the ligamentotaxis reduction improves fragment visualization and reduces artifact from gross displacement, and this is what enables articular fragment counting for surgical planning.
3. **Wait for the wrinkle sign to return** before definitive fixation — typically 10–21 days, occasionally 3+ weeks for the most severe injuries.
4. **Stage 2 (definitive):** perform ORIF through incisions preserving a ≥7 cm skin bridge from any fibular incision, achieving anatomic articular reduction (frequently via a direct anterior or anteromedial/anterolateral approach, sometimes combined posterolateral for posterior malleolar fragments), bone graft/void-filler the metaphyseal defects, and fix with anatomically contoured locking plates positioned to minimize additional soft-tissue stripping — minimally invasive plate techniques are favored where the fracture pattern allows.
5. **For the most severe soft-tissue injuries or medically fragile patients**, consider definitive circular external fixation (Ilizarov/hexapod frame) with limited internal fixation of the articular block as an alternative to plating — it substantially reduces wound complication risk at the cost of pin-site care burden and frame time.
6. **Consider single-stage definitive external fixation** (not just fibular fixation) as an alternative to two-stage ORIF in select comminuted patterns — a 2024–2025 RCT found this viable, broadening the definitive-treatment menu beyond plating once soft tissue is deemed unfavorable for open surgery.

## Pitfalls

- Nonoperative management is essentially never appropriate for a displaced high-energy pilon fracture — reserve it for a true Rüedi I non-displaced fracture in a medically unfit or very low-demand patient.
- Randomized and network meta-analysis data through 2025 continue to support staged management as reducing wound complications and deep infection versus early single-stage ORIF in high-energy patterns — do not skip staging reflexively based on isolated favorable-soft-tissue case series from other injury patterns.
- The national ENFORCE audit (UK, 2024; 656 patients/27 centres) documents wide real-world variation in how strictly the classic staged protocol is followed in practice, underscoring that this is a judgment-driven pathway rather than a rigid algorithm.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is the dominant long-term morbidity of pilon fractures; risk scales with initial articular step-off/gap, comminution grade, and quality of reduction — pilon fractures carry substantial rates of radiographic OA by 5–10 years even with technically sound fixation, given the severity of initial cartilage injury.
- Chronic swelling is common after pilon fixation and may persist a year or more — set expectations accordingly.

## Post-op protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** strict non-weight-bearing, splint/boot immobilization, elevation, and close wound surveillance — wound complication rates are historically highest in this fracture group.
- **2–6 weeks:** early protected ROM of the ankle once wound healing is secure (typically starting 2–3 weeks), continued non-weight-bearing.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive partial weight-bearing beginning around 8–12 weeks depending on comminution and radiographic union — pilon fractures generally require a longer period of protected weight-bearing than plateau or shaft fractures given the articular comminution and metaphyseal healing demands.
- **3–6 months:** progression to full weight-bearing, ankle strengthening, gait normalization, edema management (chronic swelling is common and may persist a year or more).
- **Return to work:** sedentary 6–10 weeks, manual/standing labor 4–8 months.
- **Return to sport/impact activity:** highly individualized, often 6–12 months; a meaningful proportion of high-energy Rüedi III/AO 43-C3 patients will not return to pre-injury impact activity due to post-traumatic osteoarthritis.
