## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Added to MPFL reconstruction within the ESSKA 2024 "à-la-carte" algorithm when a bony risk factor drives instability:
  - **Medialization** for TT-TG >20 mm, or 15–20 mm combined with other risk factors (dysplasia, alta, rotational malalignment).
  - Add **distalization** when Caton-Deschamps index is >1.2–1.3 (patella alta).
- **Anteromedialization (Fulkerson)** when concurrent chondral offloading of the lateral facet/distal patella is also desired — e.g., patients with combined instability and established patellofemoral chondral wear.
- Recurrent instability with a persistently elevated TT-TG despite adequate soft-tissue reconstruction, or revision cases where an index MPFL-R failed due to an unaddressed bony risk factor.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Skeletally immature patients with an open tibial tubercle apophysis — a standard TTO risks growth disturbance; use a physeal-sparing alternative (e.g., **Roux-Goldthwait**, a tendon-transfer technique that does not violate the apophysis) instead.
- TT-TG elevation driven predominantly by **tibial internal rotation** rather than true tubercle lateralization — recent data show this pattern predicts worse outcomes from isolated TTO and may be better addressed with a derotational osteotomy; measure and consider this before defaulting to TTO for any TT-TG >20 mm.
- Poor bone quality/osteoporosis significant enough to compromise screw fixation — plan augmented fixation or reconsider staging if present.
- Active infection or compromised anterior soft-tissue envelope over the tibial crest.
- Advanced tibiofemoral or patellofemoral arthritis where realignment is unlikely to meaningfully improve symptoms — reassess the treatment goal in this population.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: CT is preferred for precise TT-TG measurement and rotational profiling (femoral anteversion, tibial torsion); true lateral radiograph (condyles overlapped) for Caton-Deschamps index; Merchant/skyline view for dysplasia grade and facet wear pattern, which informs whether anteromedialization (Fulkerson) is preferable to pure medialization.
- **Magnitude planning**: calculate the planned medialization distance directly from the measured TT-TG (target a corrected TT-TG in the normal range, generally <15 mm, without overcorrecting into medial overload) and the planned distalization distance from the Caton-Deschamps index (target CD index approximately 1.0). Plan these numerically before the day of surgery rather than adjusting by intraoperative visual estimation.
- **Technique selection**: Elmslie-Trillat (pure medialization) vs. Fulkerson (oblique anteromedialization, adds anterior translation to offload chondral contact pressure) vs. Roux-Goldthwait (pediatric, tendon transfer sparing the apophysis) — decide based on skeletal maturity, chondral status, and the magnitude/direction of correction needed.
- **Osteotomy dimensions**: plan for an osteotomy fragment of at least **~60 mm length and ~20 mm width** to accommodate two-screw fixation, with a fragment thickness of **10–15 mm** to resist screw compression and fracture; plan to preserve a **proximal bony buttress/hinge of approximately 10 mm** at the proximal extent of the cut to provide primary stability and prevent proximal migration ("tubercle ascension") of the fragment.
- **Fixation planning**: two bicortical screws (commonly 4.5 mm) placed slightly ascending/divergent is the standard, biomechanically strongest construct; confirm screw length availability for the patient's tibial diameter preoperatively.
- **Sequencing with MPFL-R**: when combined with MPFL reconstruction in the same setting, plan to complete the bony correction first and perform final MPFL graft tensioning afterward, since the corrected tubercle position changes the resting tension the graft needs.
- **Equipment**: oscillating saw, osteotomes, K-wires/guide pins for the oblique freehand cutting template, drill and screws sized per plan, fluoroscopy for intraoperative confirmation of fragment position and screw trajectory, and calipers or a ruler for direct intraoperative measurement of the shift achieved.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or spinal/regional anesthesia, often with a femoral/adductor canal nerve block for postoperative analgesia.
- Supine on a standard radiolucent table with a sterile thigh tourniquet (sterile tourniquet is preferred when the field extends distally onto the tibia) or a well-padded non-sterile tourniquet positioned to allow adequate proximal exposure.
- A bump or leg holder allowing knee flexion to at least 90° for exposure and for the post-fixation ROM/tracking check.
- Fluoroscopy C-arm positioned for both AP and lateral imaging of the proximal tibia and patellofemoral joint, tested for clear access before draping.
- Prep and drape the leg free from mid-thigh to foot, with wide exposure of the anterior tibial crest from the joint line to well below the planned distal extent of the osteotomy.
- Mark surface landmarks: tibial tubercle, patellar tendon borders, anterior tibial crest, and the planned longitudinal skin incision (typically a lateral parapatellar or anterior midline incision extending from the inferior pole of the patella to several centimeters below the tubercle, chosen to allow extension for a concomitant MPFL-R patellar-side exposure if combined).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make the skin incision over the anterolateral tibia, extending from the region of the joint line/inferior patellar pole to several centimeters distal to the tibial tubercle; expose the anterior compartment fascia and the patellar tendon insertion.
2. Elevate the anterior tibialis origin subperiosteally from the lateral aspect of the planned osteotomy to protect the anterior compartment musculature and allow safe saw/osteotome passage.
3. Select the technique based on the deformity being corrected:
   - **Elmslie-Trillat** — medialization only, osteotomy oriented in the coronal plane.
   - **Fulkerson** — oblique anteromedialization; the oblique cut angle can be titrated to change the balance of medial vs. anterior translation, favored when concurrent chondral offloading is also desired.
   - **Roux-Goldthwait** — pediatric technique using a hemi-patellar-tendon transfer without violating the tibial tubercle apophysis, used when the apophysis must be protected in a skeletally immature patient.
4. Predrill a series of guide holes along the medial side of the anterior tibial crest to serve as a freehand cutting template; for the oblique (Fulkerson-type) osteotomy, direct these holes posterolaterally so they just penetrate the proximal lateral tibial cortex, defining the plane of the cut before committing with the saw.
5. Make a transverse proximal cut just deep to the patellar tendon insertion, then connect it to the main longitudinal/oblique osteotomy using an oblique proximal-medial cut, following the predrilled guide-hole plane.
6. Extend the osteotomy distally to a total fragment length of approximately 60 mm (minimum), maintaining a fragment width of approximately 20 mm and thickness of 10–15 mm — a longer, adequately wide fragment provides a broader bone-to-bone healing surface and more room for two well-spaced screws.
7. Leave a distal soft-tissue/periosteal hinge intact, and taper the distal end of the osteotomy to a smooth, gradually thinning exit rather than an abrupt step — this reduces the risk of a stress-riser tibial shaft fracture at the distal tip of the cut.
8. Preserve a proximal bony buttress of approximately 10 mm at the top of the cut (do not extend the transverse cut fully across the tubercle) — this buttress resists proximal migration ("ascension") of the fragment and improves primary rotational stability of the construct.
9. Mobilize the osteotomized fragment (with the patellar tendon still attached) and shift it to the planned position:
   - For **medialization**, slide the fragment along the oblique cut plane in an anteromedial direction to the degree calculated from the preoperative TT-TG correction target.
   - For **distalization**, additionally shift the fragment distally (removing a corresponding wedge or advancing along a vertically oriented component of the cut) to correct the Caton-Deschamps index toward approximately 1.0.
10. Provisionally hold the fragment in the corrected position with a K-wire, and confirm the shift fluoroscopically and by direct measurement (calipers) against the preoperative plan before definitive fixation — do not rely on visual estimation.
11. Place the **distal screw first**, without fully tightening it — this allows the fragment to rotate/pivot around the distal screw as needed to fine-tune final medial translation, before the proximal screw locks the fragment's final rotational position.
12. Place the second (proximal) bicortical screw, oriented slightly ascending/divergent from the first, and tighten both screws to compress the fragment against the native tibia — two bicortical screws (commonly 4.5 mm) is the standard, strongest fixation construct.
13. Take the knee through a full ROM arc and directly assess patellar tracking; confirm no residual lateral maltracking or apprehension, and confirm no bony impingement of the fragment against the native tibia at terminal flexion.
14. If instability persists after bony correction (residual laxity on manual patellar glide testing), proceed to complete MPFL-R in the same setting, tensioning the graft only after the bony correction is finalized.
15. Repair the anterior compartment fascia over the construct, close the retinaculum/subcutaneous layers, and close skin in standard fashion.
16. Apply a hinged knee brace and place the patient on protected weight-bearing per the fixation strength and surgeon protocol before leaving the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Plan the medialization and distalization magnitude numerically from the measured TT-TG and Caton-Deschamps index before the day of surgery, then confirm the achieved shift intraoperatively with calipers and fluoroscopy — do not correct "by eye."
- Predrill the freehand cutting-guide holes along the medial crest before committing with the saw — this template makes the oblique cut angle reproducible and lets you adjust the planned trajectory before an irreversible cut is made.
- Keep the fragment at least ~60 mm long and ~20 mm wide with 10–15 mm thickness — an undersized fragment is difficult to fix with two well-spaced screws and heals less predictably.
- Leave a proximal bony buttress of about 10 mm — this small detail meaningfully improves primary stability and resists proximal migration of the fragment, which otherwise re-elevates the corrected patellar height.
- Taper the distal end of the osteotomy to a smooth exit rather than a sharp step — an abrupt distal termination is a stress riser and a recognized setup for a tibial shaft fracture.
- Place the distal screw first, snug but not fully tightened, and use it as a pivot point to fine-tune final medial translation before locking the proximal screw — this two-step tightening sequence gives much finer control over the final position than tightening both screws simultaneously.
- Orient both screws slightly ascending/divergent (rather than parallel) — this configuration is biomechanically the strongest and most resistant to fragment rotation under load.
- When combining with MPFL-R, finish the bony correction and confirm fixation before tensioning the ligament graft — sequencing the soft-tissue step last avoids tensioning against a position that is about to change.
- Directly visualize patellar tracking through a full ROM arc after fixation, before closing — this is the last chance to catch residual maltracking or unexpected fragment impingement while still able to revise the construct.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Excessive medialization overloads the medial facet — plan strictly from measured TT-TG, and confirm intraoperatively rather than trusting visual impression.
- Inadequate fixation risks fracture through the osteotomy site — undersized fragments, screws placed too close to the fragment edge, or omission of the proximal buttress all increase this risk.
- **Nonunion/delayed union** is uncommon but serious — protect weight-bearing until radiographic union is confirmed (~8–12 weeks).
- **Tibial fracture** can occur from a screw stress riser or from early aggressive loading before adequate healing — a sharply tapered/abrupt distal osteotomy exit is a recognized contributing technical factor; taper it smoothly.
- **Compartment syndrome** is rare but a recognized surgical emergency from anterior compartment bleeding — maintain a high index of suspicion postoperatively (pain out of proportion, tense compartment, pain with passive stretch) and have a low threshold to measure compartment pressures or return to the OR.
- Hardware irritation over the subcutaneous anterior tibial surface may require later screw removal once union is confirmed.
- Overcorrection (excessive medialization or distalization) produces medial patellofemoral overload or, with excessive distalization, iatrogenic patella baja with restricted flexion.
- Failing to recognize that an elevated TT-TG is driven by tibial internal rotation rather than true tubercle lateralization leads to a poorly-indicated isolated TTO with a worse outcome — consider derotational osteotomy in this pattern instead of or in addition to TTO.
- Fragment ascension (proximal migration under patellar tendon pull) if the proximal buttress is inadequate or fixation loosens — recheck radiographic position at early follow-up, not only at final union.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing is typically restricted to touch-down/protected weight-bearing for **4–6 weeks** pending union when TTO is combined with MPFL-R.
- Radiographic union is typically achieved by **8–12 weeks**; union is required before impact/pivoting sport is permitted.
- When combined with MPFL-R, the broader rehabilitation timeline (ROM progression, brace weaning, strengthening, return-to-sport criteria at 6–9 months) follows the same instability rehabilitation protocol, gated by the TTO-specific weight-bearing and union restrictions above.
