## Indications

Isolated medial (far more common) or lateral compartment OA, intact ACL, correctable/passively correctable deformity, minimal flexion contracture (<10–15°), flexion arc >90–100°. Based on the classic Kozinn-Scott criteria, substantially liberalized in modern high-volume series: obesity and age extremes are now considered relative rather than absolute contraindications, though outcomes remain best in appropriately selected patients.

Favor UKA over HTO when the patient is older (>60), has lower-impact activity goals, wants faster and more reliable pain relief and recovery, and has less tolerance for a staged surgical strategy. ACL insufficiency with instability is a relative contraindication (some high-volume centers proceed with concomitant/staged ACL reconstruction). Full-thickness cartilage loss found in the "spared" compartment on MRI/arthroscopy converts a UKA candidate to TKA.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed in the monograph beyond the general operative setup for compartment-sparing knee arthroplasty; pre-operative planning increasingly uses CT-based 3D bone models for robotic-assisted cases.

## Steps

1. **Confirm compartmental candidacy**: confirm full-thickness cartilage loss confined to one compartment (MRI or diagnostic arthroscopy if radiographs are equivocal); document ACL status clinically (Lachman/pivot-shift), since MRI ACL assessment can be unreliable in an arthritic, osteophyte-laden joint.
2. **Select bearing design:**
   - **Fixed-bearing**: more forgiving of positioning error; dominates US practice.
   - **Mobile-bearing** (e.g., Oxford): theoretically reduces polyethylene wear via conforming contact, but carries a specific risk of bearing dislocation (historically 1–2%), requiring surgeon experience with the technique.
3. **Select fixation**: cemented (standard) vs cementless UKA — cementless fixation (porous/HA-coated) has grown in adoption, with registry data showing comparable or improved aseptic loosening rates versus cemented fixation in some cohorts, addressing UKA's historically higher tibial component loosening rate.
4. **Consider robotic assistance**: among the earliest and most validated robotic arthroplasty applications; improves component positioning precision and soft-tissue balance reproducibility versus manual technique, with registry/comparative series suggesting lower revision rates in robotic cohorts (long-term >10-year comparative survivorship data still maturing).
5. **Plan for conversion pathway if needed**: UKA conversion to TKA is generally more straightforward than HTO-to-TKA conversion — a relevant counseling point for younger patients weighing lifetime surgical burden.

## Pitfalls

- **Aseptic loosening**, historically the leading UKA failure mode (particularly the tibial component), is improving with cementless designs but remains the dominant long-term concern.
- **Bearing dislocation** is specific to mobile-bearing designs.
- Watch for **progression of OA in unresurfaced compartments**, polyethylene wear, periprosthetic fracture, and infection (lower absolute rate than TKA given smaller implant/less bone exposure).
- **Unexplained pain** is a recognized, sometimes idiopathic, cause of early revision — counsel patients accordingly.
- Registry pooled data show UKA revision roughly **~2x** TKA at comparable follow-up (aggregate ~11% vs ~5.6%), counterbalanced by lower morbidity, faster recovery, and easier revision-to-TKA if it occurs.
- Do not proceed with medial UKA in ACL-insufficient knees with instability without addressing the ACL (concomitant/staged reconstruction), since outcomes are implant/surgeon-dependent.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acute | 0–2 weeks | Weight-bearing as tolerated immediately (cemented and most cementless designs) | 0–90° by 2 weeks | Quad activation, cryotherapy, gait training off walker/cane |
| Early recovery | 2–6 weeks | Full weight-bearing, wean assistive device | Progress toward full flexion | Closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike |
| Functional recovery | 6–12 weeks | Full | Full functional ROM (typically ≥120°) | Return to low-impact recreational activity, driving by 2–4 weeks |
| Return to work/sport | 6–12 weeks (desk work often 1–2 weeks) | — | — | Manual labor 8–12 weeks; low-impact sport (golf, cycling, doubles tennis) 6–12 weeks — UKA's faster recovery profile versus TKA is one of its principal selling points to active patients |
