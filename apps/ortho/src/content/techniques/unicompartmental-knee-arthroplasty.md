## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Isolated medial (far more common) or lateral compartment OA, intact ACL, correctable/passively correctable deformity, minimal flexion contracture (<10–15°), flexion arc >90–100°. Based on the classic Kozinn-Scott criteria, substantially liberalized in modern high-volume series: obesity and age extremes are now considered relative rather than absolute contraindications, though outcomes remain best in appropriately selected patients.

Favor UKA over HTO when the patient is older (>60), has lower-impact activity goals, wants faster and more reliable pain relief and recovery, and has less tolerance for a staged surgical strategy.

**Contraindications**:
- Inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic, crystal disease).
- Full-thickness cartilage loss in the "spared" compartment on MRI or diagnostic arthroscopy — converts the candidate to TKA.
- Fixed varus/valgus deformity not passively correctable, flexion contracture >15–20°, or flexion arc <90°.
- Active or recent joint sepsis, uncontrolled diabetes, active local skin/soft-tissue infection.
- A varus (or valgus) thrust on gait that does not correct with stress testing, suggesting a coronal instability the implant will not control.

**Relative contraindications**: ACL insufficiency with instability (some high-volume centers proceed with concomitant/staged ACL reconstruction, but outcomes are implant/surgeon-dependent); chondrocalcinosis on the affected compartment's articular surface is not an absolute contraindication if the remaining cartilage is intact but should prompt closer inspection of the "spared" side; morbid obesity and inflammatory-pattern chondrocalcinosis both correlate with somewhat higher revision rates even though they are no longer absolute exclusions.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Weight-bearing AP and Rosenberg (45° flexion PA) views** to confirm the disease is truly confined to one compartment; the Rosenberg view is more sensitive for early joint-space narrowing and frequently changes the plan versus the standing AP alone.
- **Full-length hip-to-ankle standing radiograph** to measure HKA, MPTA, and LDFA, and to confirm the deformity is passively correctable to neutral or slightly under-corrected (UKA is not intended to overcorrect the mechanical axis the way HTO is).
- **Skyline/lateral views** to screen the patellofemoral compartment for disease that would argue against isolated compartment replacement.
- **MRI or diagnostic arthroscopy** when radiographs are equivocal, specifically to confirm full-thickness cartilage loss is confined to the target compartment and to assess meniscal and ACL status; note that MRI ACL assessment can be unreliable in an arthritic, osteophyte-laden joint, so clinical exam (Lachman, pivot-shift) remains the primary means of confirming ACL competence.
- **CT-based 3D bone modeling** for robotic-assisted planning where available — used to plan component size, position, and depth of resection pre-operatively and to simulate gap balance.
- **Implant selection**: fixed-bearing vs mobile-bearing design, and cemented vs cementless fixation (see Step-by-Step Technique for trade-offs); have the full range of femoral, tibial, and bearing/insert sizes available, plus trial components spanning at least one size above and below the templated size.
- **Tourniquet and instrumentation**: standard UKA instrument tray (compartment-specific femoral and tibial cutting jigs, spacer blocks/tensioners, extraction and cement instruments) or the robotic arm/navigation system and its registration array if using robotic assistance.
- Counsel and consent for intra-operative conversion to TKA if unexpected disease is found in the "spared" compartment at arthrotomy — this should be an explicitly discussed possibility, not a surprise.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: spinal or general anesthesia, typically combined with an adductor canal block (preferred over femoral nerve block to spare quadriceps strength for early mobilization) and/or peri-articular local infiltration analgesia.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard or radiolucent table (radiolucent table required if using fluoroscopy or an intra-operative imaging-based robotic system). A lateral post at the mid-thigh and a foot support or leg holder allow the knee to be flexed to 90–120° for exposure and held in varus/valgus stress positions for gap assessment. Some surgeons use a leg-holding device that also permits a hanging-leg position for extension-gap checks.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh tourniquet, typically inflated after exsanguination; many surgeons perform UKA with the tourniquet deflated for part or all of the case to better assess cement penetration and bleeding, particularly for cementless technique.
- **Robotic/navigation setup**: if used, position the tracking array pins in the femur and tibia away from the planned implant footprint and incision, and perform registration (bony landmark and range-of-motion mapping) before the arthrotomy is extended, per the specific system's workflow.
- **Draping**: standard extremity drape exposing from mid-thigh to the foot, leaving the ankle free if using a distal alignment guide or leg-holder that manipulates the foot.
- **Surface landmarks marked pre-incision**: tibial tubercle, patellar borders, joint line, and planned incision (typically a short medial or lateral parapatellar incision, often 8–10 cm, centered over the affected compartment — considerably shorter than a standard TKA incision given the minimally invasive philosophy of most modern UKA techniques).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm compartmental candidacy at arthrotomy**: perform a limited medial (or lateral) parapatellar arthrotomy without everting the patella. Directly inspect the affected compartment, the ACL, and — critically — the "spared" compartment and patellofemoral joint before committing to any bone cuts; convert to TKA if unexpected full-thickness disease is found elsewhere.
2. **Remove peripheral osteophytes**: osteophytes on the tibial spine, femoral condyle, and intercondylar notch are removed early, since they cause a false sense of tightness in extension and, if left, cause post-operative impingement pain.
3. **Perform the tibial cut** using the compartment-specific cutting jig, referencing the intact "healthy" side of the joint and the pre-operative plan for resection depth and slope; the tibial cut is generally more conservative (less bone removed) than in TKA, matching implant thickness closely to preserve bone stock.
4. **Perform the femoral preparation** — sequence depends on technique philosophy: **tibia-first** references the finished tibial cut to size and position the femoral component; **femur-first** (used in some mobile-bearing systems) drills and positions the femoral component first, referencing the posterior condyle, then finishes the tibial cut to match. For mobile-bearing femur-first technique, make a preliminary cut of the posterior tibial cartilage to allow the femoral drill guide to seat under the condyle, and center the drill guide on the medial femoral condyle before drilling the peg holes.
5. **Balance flexion and extension gaps**: insert trial spacers/tensioners in extension and at 90° flexion to confirm the gaps are equal and appropriately tensioned (not over-stuffed, not lax) — this is the central technical goal of the case, since both under- and over-correction predict failure. Adjust with additional tibial resection, femoral component sizing, or (in mobile-bearing systems) insert thickness rather than soft-tissue release, which is used sparingly if at all in UKA compared with TKA.
6. **Check component rotation and sagittal alignment**: align the tibial sagittal cut with the femoral trial component to avoid bearing/component impingement through range of motion, and confirm no lift-off or impingement at full extension and deep flexion with the trial components and trial bearing/insert in place.
7. **Reconfirm limb alignment**: take the trial construct through a full range of motion, apply gentle varus/valgus stress, and confirm the corrected mechanical axis is appropriately positioned (generally corrected toward, but deliberately not past, neutral — overcorrection into the opposite compartment is a recognized cause of accelerated wear/failure in UKA, unlike the deliberate overcorrection strategy in HTO).
8. **Prepare and cement (or press-fit) final components**: pulse-lavage the cut bone surfaces thoroughly and dry the field before cementing — meticulous cementing technique in a dry field is repeatedly emphasized in the literature as central to avoiding the historically dominant failure mode of tibial component loosening. For cementless components, confirm rigid initial press-fit stability of both femoral and tibial components before proceeding.
9. **Insert the final bearing/insert**: for mobile-bearing designs, confirm the bearing tracks appropriately through range of motion without impingement on the tibial component wall — a wide gap between the bearing margin and the tibial component's lateral/medial wall predisposes to bearing rotation and subsequent dislocation, so component positioning that keeps this gap tight is a specific technical checkpoint at this step.
10. **Final range-of-motion and stability check**: cycle the knee through full flexion-extension with the definitive components in place, confirm no bearing impingement, subluxation, or gap asymmetry, and remove all cement extrusion (especially posteriorly, where retained cement can cause later loose-body symptoms or neurovascular irritation).
11. **Closure**: repair the arthrotomy in layers, consider a drain if bleeding is significant, and close the shorter UKA incision in standard fashion. Release the tourniquet before final closure if it was inflated for the case, to check hemostasis and cement extrusion sites.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Do not evert the patella — a limited arthrotomy without patellar eversion is both the hallmark of the minimally invasive UKA approach and a practical safeguard, since a fully everted patella distorts the flexion gap assessment.
- Remove peripheral osteophytes early and completely, especially on the tibial spine and intercondylar notch — residual osteophytes create a false extension-gap tightness during trialing and are a common, avoidable cause of post-operative pain from impingement.
- Under-correct rather than overcorrect the mechanical axis — unlike HTO, UKA is not meant to shift substantial additional load onto the unresurfaced compartment; aim for a corrected but still slightly deformed axis, and treat overcorrection as a technical error to avoid, not a goal.
- Keep tibial resection conservative — match resection depth closely to implant thickness rather than resecting extra bone "to be safe"; this preserves the deformability/bone stock that made UKA attractive in the first place and eases any eventual conversion to TKA.
- For mobile-bearing designs, be deliberate about component positioning to minimize the peripheral gap between the bearing rim and the tibial tray wall — a tight, well-aligned gap is the single most controllable technical factor in preventing bearing dislocation.
- Use pulsed lavage and a genuinely dry field before cementing, and consider adjuncts (thin cement mantle technique, pressurization) to maximize cement interdigitation on the tibial cut surface — since tibial component loosening has historically been UKA's dominant failure mode, treat cementing technique as a critical step rather than a routine one.
- Address ACL competence explicitly during trialing — if there is any question about ACL integrity intra-operatively (e.g., a lax Lachman under anesthesia that wasn't apparent pre-operatively), this changes the balancing target and may prompt concomitant or staged reconstruction rather than proceeding with a standard UKA gap-balance target.
- Recognize the learning curve: published series suggest a minimum of 10–15 UKA cases per year to maintain proficiency, and mobile-bearing (Oxford-type) technique in particular is best performed in a higher-volume practice given its specific instrumentation and bearing-tracking nuances.
- Have a low threshold to convert to TKA intra-operatively if compartment disease turns out more extensive than pre-operative imaging suggested — this is a planned contingency, not a failure, and should be consented for pre-operatively.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Aseptic loosening**, historically the leading UKA failure mode (particularly the tibial component), is improving with cementless designs but remains the dominant long-term concern. Meticulous cementing technique (dry field, pulsed lavage, adequate cement mantle) is the primary intra-operative mitigation for cemented components.
- **Bearing dislocation** is specific to mobile-bearing designs (historically reported around 1–2%); risk rises with component malposition (a wide gap between the bearing margin and tibial tray wall predisposing to bearing rotation and subsequent dislocation), trauma to the operated leg, and high-flexion activities post-operatively — reinforced by more recent case series on mobile-bearing dislocation risk factors.
- Watch for **progression of OA in unresurfaced compartments**, polyethylene wear, periprosthetic fracture, and infection (lower absolute rate than TKA given smaller implant/less bone exposure).
- **Unexplained pain** is a recognized, sometimes idiopathic, cause of early revision — counsel patients accordingly pre-operatively.
- Registry pooled data show UKA revision roughly **~2x** TKA at comparable follow-up (aggregate ~11% vs ~5.6%), counterbalanced by lower morbidity, faster recovery, and easier revision-to-TKA if it occurs.
- Do not proceed with medial UKA in ACL-insufficient knees with instability without addressing the ACL (concomitant/staged reconstruction), since outcomes are implant/surgeon-dependent.
- **Component malposition/rotational error** is a preventable cause of both bearing impingement (fixed- and mobile-bearing designs) and dislocation (mobile-bearing designs specifically) — careful sagittal alignment of the tibial cut with the femoral trial, and a conservative "+2" cutting guide philosophy where applicable, reduce this risk.
- **Overcorrection of the mechanical axis** transfers excess load to the contralateral compartment and predicts accelerated progression there — a distinct failure mode from HTO, where overcorrection is deliberately targeted.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acute | 0–2 weeks | Weight-bearing as tolerated immediately (cemented and most cementless designs) | 0–90° by 2 weeks | Quad activation, cryotherapy, gait training off walker/cane |
| Early recovery | 2–6 weeks | Full weight-bearing, wean assistive device | Progress toward full flexion | Closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike |
| Functional recovery | 6–12 weeks | Full | Full functional ROM (typically ≥120°) | Return to low-impact recreational activity, driving by 2–4 weeks |
| Return to work/sport | 6–12 weeks (desk work often 1–2 weeks) | — | — | Manual labor 8–12 weeks; low-impact sport (golf, cycling, doubles tennis) 6–12 weeks — UKA's faster recovery profile versus TKA is one of its principal selling points to active patients |
