## Indications

Surgical fixation is indicated for unacceptable angulation/shortening, malrotation, multiple or open fractures, intra-articular fractures with step-off, or unstable fracture patterns. Rotational alignment must be assessed clinically (fingernail plane symmetry, composite fist for scissoring), since rotation is invisible on plain radiographs. Key practical thresholds: boxer's fracture (5th MC neck) has a widely used practical angulation ceiling of roughly 30° (index/long), 40° (ring), 50-70° (small), though rotational malalignment and pseudoclawing are absolute indications to fix regardless of angle; metacarpal shaft shortening up to 2-5 mm is tolerated before extensor lag becomes clinically relevant; any clinical malrotation on composite fist is not acceptable and should be fixed.

## Positioning & setup

Standard AP/lateral/oblique hand radiographs guide fracture pattern assessment; CT is used pre-operatively for intra-articular comminution (base fractures, Bennett/Rolando thumb fractures).

## Steps

1. **Closed reduction and splinting/casting** for stable fractures with acceptable alignment.
2. **Percutaneous K-wire fixation** (intramedullary or crossed) for minimally invasive fixation that avoids extensive soft-tissue stripping.
3. **Plate/screw fixation** for comminuted/unstable/articular fractures — provides rigid fixation allowing earlier motion, at the cost of higher risk of tendon adhesion/irritation from hardware.
4. **Intramedullary headless screw fixation** — increasingly popular for metacarpal neck/shaft fractures, allowing earlier active motion with less soft-tissue disruption than plating.
5. **Boxer's fracture:** closed reduction (Jahss maneuver) and buddy taping/short-arm ulnar gutter cast for acceptable alignment; percutaneous pinning (antegrade intramedullary or transverse) for unacceptable angulation, malrotation, or multiple metacarpal fractures.
6. **Thumb Bennett/Rolando fractures:** closed reduction and percutaneous pinning for a simple Bennett fracture; ORIF or external fixation/CT-guided fixation for comminuted Rolando fractures, given the deforming pull of APL.

## Pitfalls

- Rotational malunion is the functionally most significant type of malunion — clinical rotational assessment before and after fixation is essential since rotation is not visible on plain films.
- Nonunion is uncommon in hand fractures given rich vascularity but is more common with open/comminuted injuries or infection.
- Hardware irritation/tendon adhesion is a particular risk with dorsal plating placed under the extensor tendons.
- Stiffness is the most common overall complication of hand fractures, related to both the injury itself and prolonged immobilization — favor techniques that allow earlier motion when the fracture pattern permits.
- K-wire fixation carries pin tract infection risk and typically requires protection until pin removal (~3-4 weeks) before more aggressive mobilization.
- Extensor lag can result from shaft shortening beyond the 2-5 mm tolerance threshold.

## Post-op protocol

- **Non-operative, stable fractures:** protected motion within a splint/buddy tape from the outset when possible (early motion reduces stiffness — a major complication of hand fractures), progressing to unrestricted motion by 4-6 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing.
- **Operative fixation (K-wire/plate/IM screw):** early protected active motion is a major advantage of rigid fixation (screw/plate), allowing therapy to begin within days; K-wire fixation typically requires protection until pin removal (~3-4 weeks) before more aggressive mobilization.
- Return to contact sport/heavy manual labor is generally allowed at 6-8 weeks with radiographic union.
