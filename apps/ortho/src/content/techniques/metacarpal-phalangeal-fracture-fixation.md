## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Surgical fixation is indicated for unacceptable angulation/shortening, malrotation, multiple or open fractures, intra-articular fractures with step-off, or unstable fracture patterns. Rotational alignment must be assessed clinically (fingernail plane symmetry, composite fist for scissoring), since rotation is invisible on plain radiographs. Key practical thresholds: boxer's fracture (5th MC neck) has a widely used practical angulation ceiling of roughly 30° (index/long), 40° (ring), 50-70° (small), though rotational malalignment and pseudoclawing are absolute indications to fix regardless of angle; metacarpal shaft shortening up to 2-5 mm is tolerated before extensor lag becomes clinically relevant; any clinical malrotation on composite fist is not acceptable and should be fixed.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- Stable, minimally displaced fractures within the accepted angulation/shortening thresholds and without rotational deformity should be managed non-operatively — surgery adds hardware-related and stiffness risk without a clear benefit in these patterns.
- Active infection or heavily contaminated open wounds may require staged management (initial debridement and temporizing external fixation or splinting) rather than immediate definitive internal fixation.
- Severe comminution with inadequate bone stock for screw purchase is a relative contraindication to isolated screw fixation and favors plate-spanning, external fixation, or combined techniques.
- Poor soft-tissue envelope (crush injury, degloving) over the planned plate site is a relative contraindication to dorsal plating until the soft tissue is judged adequate, favoring K-wire or IM screw fixation with less soft-tissue disruption in the interim.
- Patient factors precluding reliable postoperative pin care/follow-up are a relative contraindication to percutaneous K-wire fixation specifically, given the need for pin-site care and eventual pin removal.

## Preoperative Planning

- Obtain standard AP/lateral/oblique hand radiographs to characterize the fracture pattern (transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted, intra-articular); obtain CT for intra-articular comminution, particularly thumb Bennett/Rolando fracture-dislocations, to fully map fragment size/number before choosing a fixation strategy.
- Document clinical rotation preoperatively (fingernail plane, composite fist scissoring) as a baseline and confirm correction is achieved intraoperatively before any fixation is finalized — this must be checked with the tourniquet down or without traction distorting the normal cascade, and is best confirmed dynamically rather than from a single static position.
- Select implant/technique based on fracture location and pattern: closed reduction/splinting for stable acceptable-alignment fractures; percutaneous K-wires (intramedullary, e.g., antegrade/retrograde, or crossed) for minimally displaced-to-moderately unstable patterns needing minimally invasive stabilization; plates/screws for comminuted, unstable, or articular fractures needing rigid fixation and early motion; intramedullary headless screw fixation for many metacarpal neck/shaft fractures amenable to a percutaneous or mini-open approach.
- For thumb Bennett fractures, plan percutaneous pinning technique and confirm adequate closed reduction is achievable; for Rolando (comminuted) fractures, plan for ORIF or a spanning/external fixation construct given the deforming pull of APL and the difficulty of maintaining a closed reduction in a comminuted pattern.
- Equipment: mini fragment/hand fracture set (K-wires of appropriate diameter, mini plates and screws, headless compression screws in a range of sizes), fluoroscopy (mini C-arm) available for all percutaneous and most open fixation, wire driver, image intensifier drape.
- Template screw/plate length where feasible from preoperative imaging, though final sizing is typically confirmed intraoperatively under fluoroscopy given the small working dimensions involved.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** WALANT (local lidocaine-epinephrine, no tourniquet) is increasingly used for percutaneous pinning and even some plate/screw fixation of hand fractures, allowing active motion confirmation of stable fixation and tendon gliding intraoperatively; regional block (Bier/axillary) or general anesthesia remains standard for more complex multi-fracture, comminuted, or intra-articular reconstructions requiring longer operative time.
- **Positioning:** supine, arm extended on a hand table with the hand positioned for the specific fracture (e.g., ulnar border down for 5th metacarpal work, thumb ray positioned for Bennett/Rolando access).
- **Tourniquet:** standard arm tourniquet after Esmarch exsanguination if not using WALANT; for percutaneous pinning alone under WALANT, a tourniquet is often omitted.
- **Fluoroscopy setup:** a mini C-arm is positioned to allow AP, lateral, and oblique views of the hand without repositioning the arm excessively; confirm the image intensifier can capture true orthogonal views of the specific ray being fixed before starting, since foreshortened oblique views are a common source of missed malreduction.
- **Landmarks marked before incision/pin entry:** the dorsal metacarpal head/neck entry point for antegrade IM nailing or pinning, the extensor tendon course (to plan an approach that splits or retracts rather than transects the extensor mechanism), and the radial sensory nerve branches over the thumb/index metacarpal for Bennett fracture pinning.
- **Draping:** standard sterile hand drape allowing full fluoroscopic access; keep the forearm draped free if proximal extension of exposure becomes necessary.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Attempt closed reduction first** for all displaced fractures amenable to it: for metacarpal neck fractures use the Jahss maneuver (flex the MCP joint to 90° and push the proximal phalanx dorsally to lever the metacarpal head into extension/reduced position); for shaft fractures use longitudinal traction with correction of angulation and rotation under direct visualization of the fingernail alignment.
2. **Confirm reduction under fluoroscopy** in orthogonal planes; if acceptable alignment/rotation is achieved and the pattern is stable, proceed to splint/cast immobilization (short-arm ulnar gutter cast for boxer's fracture, buddy taping for stable phalangeal fractures) rather than surgery.
3. **Percutaneous K-wire fixation (unacceptable angulation, malrotation, or multiple metacarpal fractures):** under fluoroscopy, pass one or more K-wires either as an antegrade intramedullary construct (entry at the metacarpal base, wire directed down the shaft across the fracture into the head, "bouquet" technique with multiple thin wires for neck fractures) or as crossed/transverse wires stabilizing the fracture and, if needed, transfixing to an adjacent stable metacarpal for added stability in multiple fracture patterns. Confirm reduction and rotation fluoroscopically and clinically (fingernail cascade, composite fist) before bending/cutting wires and leaving them either buried subcutaneously or protruding through the skin for later removal.
4. **Plate/screw fixation (comminuted/unstable/articular fractures):** approach dorsally, splitting or retracting the extensor mechanism (avoid transecting the sagittal bands/extensor tendon); reduce the fracture under direct vision, provisionally hold with pointed reduction forceps or K-wires, then apply a mini fragment plate contoured to the dorsal metacarpal/phalangeal surface with bicortical screws proximal and distal to the fracture (or lag-screw technique alone for a long oblique/spiral pattern with adequate length for 2-3 screws). Confirm articular reduction under direct vision and fluoroscopy for intra-articular fractures, and take the finger through a passive range of motion on the table to confirm the fixation does not impinge tendon excursion.
5. **Intramedullary headless screw fixation (metacarpal neck/shaft):** through a small dorsal entry portal at the metacarpal head (typically just off the articular surface, following manufacturer-specific entry point guidance), pass a guidewire down the intramedullary canal across the reduced fracture, then place a cannulated headless compression screw of appropriate length/diameter over the wire, achieving interfragmentary compression while avoiding screw prominence that would impinge the MCP joint or extensor mechanism; confirm screw position and joint clearance fluoroscopically and by passive MCP motion before closing.
6. **Boxer's fracture (5th metacarpal neck):** if closed reduction/casting achieves and maintains acceptable alignment, proceed non-operatively; for unacceptable angulation, malrotation, or multiple metacarpal fractures, proceed with antegrade intramedullary pinning (bouquet technique) or transverse pinning, confirming reduction and rotation before final wire fixation.
7. **Thumb Bennett fracture (simple 2-part intra-articular base fracture-dislocation):** reduce the metacarpal base onto the retained volar ulnar (Beak ligament-attached) fragment by longitudinal traction, pronation, and direct pressure over the metacarpal base, then place percutaneous K-wires from the metacarpal shaft/base across the fracture into the trapezium and/or the index metacarpal to hold the reduction, confirming articular congruity fluoroscopically.
8. **Thumb Rolando fracture (comminuted 3-part or more intra-articular base fracture):** given the deforming pull of APL and the difficulty maintaining closed reduction in a comminuted pattern, plan for ORIF with a mini fragment plate/screws restoring the articular surface where fragment size allows, or a spanning/external fixation construct (or combined K-wire plus external fixation) when fragments are too small/numerous for direct fixation; accept that anatomic articular restoration may not be fully achievable in severely comminuted patterns and that ligamentotaxis via spanning fixation may be the more realistic goal.
9. **Close and dress:** close the extensor-splitting approach in layers, protect any percutaneous wire sites with a sterile dressing and a well-padded splint (e.g., ulnar or radial gutter, or intrinsic-plus position splint depending on the ray involved) that protects the fixation while permitting motion of uninvolved joints where possible.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Check rotation clinically at every step — after closed reduction, after provisional fixation, and after final fixation — using the fingernail plane and composite fist scissoring test; rotation is the one deformity radiographs will not reveal, and it is far easier to correct before the wires/screws are final.
- For the Jahss maneuver, flex the MCP to 90° before applying dorsal pressure on the proximal phalanx — attempting the maneuver with the MCP extended loses mechanical advantage and risks inadequate reduction.
- When placing an antegrade "bouquet" of intramedullary K-wires for a metacarpal neck fracture, use multiple thin wires fanned within the canal rather than a single thick wire — this improves rotational control and fill of the canal without requiring an oversized single wire.
- Approach dorsal plating by splitting (not transecting) the extensor mechanism, and always take the digit through passive motion on the table before closing to confirm the plate/screws do not impinge tendon excursion — hardware-related extensor adhesion is one of the most common and most avoidable complications of hand plating.
- For intramedullary headless screw fixation, respect the manufacturer-specific entry point precisely — an eccentric entry point is a common cause of malreduction or screw-head prominence against the MCP joint.
- Use fluoroscopy in true orthogonal (not oblique) views before accepting any reduction — a foreshortened oblique view can mask residual angulation or malrotation that looks acceptable on a single imperfect image.
- For Bennett fractures, remember the deforming force is APL pulling the metacarpal shaft proximally and radially while the ulnar volar (Beak) fragment stays anchored by the anterior oblique ligament — reduction is achieved by realigning the shaft to the fragment, not the other way around.
- Favor fixation techniques that allow earlier active motion (screw/plate, or IM screw) over prolonged K-wire immobilization whenever the fracture pattern and bone quality reasonably support it, since stiffness is the single most common complication of hand fractures overall and is strongly related to duration of immobilization.
- In multiple adjacent metacarpal fractures, consider transfixing wires to a stable neighboring metacarpal for added rotational and length stability rather than relying on intramedullary fixation of each fracture in isolation.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Rotational malunion is the functionally most significant type of malunion — clinical rotational assessment before and after fixation is essential since rotation is not visible on plain films.
- Nonunion is uncommon in hand fractures given rich vascularity but is more common with open/comminuted injuries or infection.
- Hardware irritation/tendon adhesion is a particular risk with dorsal plating placed under the extensor tendons.
- Stiffness is the most common overall complication of hand fractures, related to both the injury itself and prolonged immobilization — favor techniques that allow earlier motion when the fracture pattern permits.
- K-wire fixation carries pin tract infection risk and typically requires protection until pin removal (~3-4 weeks) before more aggressive mobilization.
- Extensor lag can result from shaft shortening beyond the 2-5 mm tolerance threshold.
- Eccentric intramedullary screw entry point or an oversized screw can leave symptomatic prominence against the MCP joint or articular surface, requiring revision.
- Attempting to force an anatomic reduction of a severely comminuted Rolando fracture with small fragment screws alone, rather than accepting a ligamentotaxis-based spanning construct, risks fragment devascularization and failure of fixation.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Non-operative, stable fractures:** protected motion within a splint/buddy tape from the outset when possible (early motion reduces stiffness — a major complication of hand fractures), progressing to unrestricted motion by 4-6 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing.
- **Operative fixation (K-wire/plate/IM screw):** early protected active motion is a major advantage of rigid fixation (screw/plate), allowing therapy to begin within days; K-wire fixation typically requires protection until pin removal (~3-4 weeks) before more aggressive mobilization.
- Return to contact sport/heavy manual labor is generally allowed at 6-8 weeks with radiographic union.
