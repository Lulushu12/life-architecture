## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications** — selection across the ladder is driven by lesion size, containment (contained vs. shoulder/bipolar/kissing), depth (chondral-only vs. osteochondral with bone loss), and patient age/activity demand:

- **<2 cm²**: microfracture (declining favor) or OAT.
- **2–4 cm²**: OAT (if donor site adequate) or MACI/ACI.
- **>4 cm² or osteochondral, contained**: osteochondral allograft (OCA); MACI for chondral-only lesions.
- Overall threshold zone: ICRS grade 3B–4 (Outerbridge III–IV), focal and contained, symptomatic.
- **Alignment as prerequisite**: obtain standing long-leg (hip-knee-ankle) films whenever a biologic procedure is planned in a compartment with degenerative change or in a patient with clinical varus/valgus thrust. If the mechanical axis passes outside the central third of the affected compartment (or clinical/radiographic varus/valgus exceeds ~5°), plan concurrent or staged high tibial osteotomy (medial-sided pathology) or distal femoral osteotomy (lateral-sided pathology) rather than proceeding with an isolated graft.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:

- Diffuse, bipolar, or "kissing" cartilage lesions — poor candidate for any focal restoration technique; consider unloading osteotomy, arthroplasty pathway, or (for MACI in select bipolar cases) a staged, case-by-case discussion.
- Uncorrected coronal malalignment >5° in the affected compartment, or uncorrected ligamentous instability — correct first or stage concurrently.
- Inflammatory arthropathy, active joint infection, or uncontrolled crystalline arthropathy.
- Advanced diffuse osteoarthritis (Kellgren-Lawrence ≥3 outside the focal lesion) — restoration procedures are not salvage for a degenerative joint.
- Uncontrolled obesity (relative — most protocols use a BMI threshold, commonly ~35, beyond which graft survivorship data are limited and load on the graft is higher).
- Smoking (relative) — impairs bone and soft-tissue healing; strongly encourage cessation before OAT/OCA/MACI.
- Skeletal immaturity is not an absolute contraindication but changes graft-site selection and fixation planning (avoid violating the physis).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to characterize lesion size, depth, containment, subchondral bone integrity, and to screen for a bipolar/kissing lesion. Standing long-leg alignment films in every case where degenerative change or clinical malalignment is present. Recognize that MRI systematically underestimates true lesion size and depth relative to arthroscopic and intraoperative findings — plan for a graft size roughly one size class larger than the MRI measurement and confirm the true footprint with a sizing trial/sterile ruler intraoperatively before committing to a graft order.
- **Diagnostic arthroscopy** (staged or same-anesthetic) is often used to confirm containment, grade the lesion (ICRS/Outerbridge), and assess the menisci and ligaments before committing to a two-stage cell-based procedure or ordering allograft tissue.
- **Implant/graft selection and sizing**:
  - *Microfracture*: no implant; awl/pick set of standard angulations (0°, 30°, 45°, 90°) to reach the full defect perpendicular to the subchondral plate.
  - *OAT/mosaicplasty*: cannulated harvester/recipient sizing system (typically 6, 8, 10 mm diameter plugs); plan donor-site mapping preoperatively (lateral trochlea, intercondylar notch periphery, superolateral/superomedial "sulcus terminalis" zones) to avoid harvesting from a weight-bearing surface.
  - *MACI*: requires a first-stage arthroscopic biopsy (typically from a minimally weight-bearing area such as the superomedial or superolateral trochlear edge or intercondylar notch, ~200–300 mg of cartilage) sent to the processing laboratory; manufacturing turnaround is typically 4–6 weeks, so the implantation stage must be scheduled and the OR/graft logistics coordinated in advance.
  - *OCA*: requires ordering a size-matched fresh (or fresh-frozen) allograft from a tissue bank based on MRI/CT measurements (condyle width, radius of curvature) and lesion location; fresh allograft has a limited shelf viability window (commonly used within ~28 days of procurement per most tissue-bank protocols), so surgical scheduling must be coordinated tightly with graft availability — have a contingency plan (reschedule vs. proceed with an alternate technique) if the graft does not arrive matched or viable.
- **Equipment/instrumentation checklist**: standard arthroscopy tower plus, depending on technique — microfracture awl set; OAT harvester/dilator/tamp system; mini-arthrotomy tray and fibrin glue/sealant for MACI; oscillating saw, reamers/dowel cutters, and a graft-preparation back table (separate sterile setup) for OCA.
- **Concurrent procedures to plan for**: HTO/DFO instrumentation on standby if alignment correction is being staged or combined; meniscal repair or meniscal allograft transplant instrumentation if meniscal deficiency coexists.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia, often combined with a femoral or adductor canal nerve block for postoperative analgesia, particularly for open/mini-arthrotomy stages (MACI implantation, OCA, larger mosaicplasty).
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard or arthroscopic table with a leg holder or lateral post; the leg holder should allow the knee to be flexed to at least 110–120° for posterior condyle access and extended for anterior/trochlear lesions. A tourniquet is placed high on the thigh and kept available (inflated as needed rather than routinely, particularly for the open stages where a bloodless field aids graft visualization and fibrin glue adherence).
- **Setup**: standard sterile arthroscopy drape with a fluid inflow/outflow system; for OAT and OCA, have a separate sterile back table for graft preparation to avoid contaminating the main field, and dedicate an assistant to graft handling. For MACI/mini-arthrotomy stages, prep and drape to allow conversion from arthroscopic portals to a limited medial or lateral parapatellar arthrotomy centered over the defect.
- **Fluoroscopy**: not routinely required for the knee cartilage ladder (unlike OCD retroarticular drilling); have it available if a concurrent osteotomy is planned in the same setting.
- **Landmarks**: mark standard anteromedial/anterolateral arthroscopy portals, the joint line, and the projected arthrotomy incision (medial or lateral parapatellar, centered over the defect location determined by preoperative MRI) before draping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**A. Diagnostic arthroscopy (all techniques)**
1. Establish standard anterolateral viewing and anteromedial working portals.
2. Perform a full diagnostic sweep — confirm lesion size, depth, containment, and rule out a second (bipolar/kissing) lesion.
3. Probe the lesion margins to assess a stable cartilage rim versus a shoulder that needs debridement back to healthy tissue.
4. Confirm meniscal and ligamentous status; address any concurrent meniscal tear or instability per the relevant technique guide before or during the same anesthetic episode.

**B. Microfracture**
5. Debride all unstable/calcified cartilage back to a stable, vertical, healthy cartilage rim using a curette or shaver — do not leave a beveled or feathered edge, which predisposes to further delamination.
6. Remove the calcified cartilage layer completely without violating the subchondral plate — this layer, if left, blocks marrow-derived cell migration into the defect.
7. Perforate the subchondral bone with an awl or pick, working from the periphery toward the center, spacing holes 3–4 mm apart to a depth of 2–4 mm — enough to see fat droplets and marrow bleeding ("fat droplets sign") without fracturing the intervening bone bridges.
8. Deflate the tourniquet (if inflated) and reduce pump pressure to confirm marrow bleeding from each hole before completing the case.

**C. OAT/mosaicplasty**
5. Prepare the recipient site with a sizing guide/cannulated cutter perpendicular to the articular surface, creating a socket matched to the plug diameter, typically 10–15 mm deep.
6. Harvest donor plug(s) from the selected non-weight-bearing donor site using the harvester perpendicular to the articular surface, taking care to match the native cartilage curvature/thickness as closely as possible to the recipient site.
7. Press-fit each plug flush with (or 0.5–1 mm recessed relative to) the surrounding cartilage surface using gentle manual pressure or a tamp — avoid mallet impaction directly on the articular surface, which causes chondrocyte death at the impact zone.
8. For lesions requiring multiple plugs (mosaicplasty), place the largest/central plug first, then fill the periphery, minimizing gaps between plugs (aim for <1 mm gaps, which fill with fibrocartilage) and avoiding plug-to-plug contact that fractures the cartilage edges.
9. Confirm congruity and stability of each plug through a full range of motion before closing.

**D. MACI (two-stage)**
5. *Stage 1 (arthroscopic)*: harvest a full-thickness cartilage biopsy (~200–300 mg) from a minimally loaded area (superomedial/superolateral trochlear edge or intercondylar notch), avoiding the weight-bearing surfaces and the lesion itself; ship per manufacturer protocol for chondrocyte expansion onto the collagen membrane.
6. *Stage 2 (open/mini-arthrotomy, typically 4–6 weeks later)*: expose the defect through a limited arthrotomy centered over the lesion.
7. Debride the defect to a stable, healthy cartilage rim and completely remove the calcified cartilage layer without breaching the subchondral bone — bleeding through the subchondral plate contaminates the implantation site with blood, which impairs cell-scaffold adherence, so achieve meticulous hemostasis (epinephrine-soaked sponges, fibrin sealant "tamponade," or a brief tourniquet inflation) before implantation.
8. Template the defect with a sterile foil template, cut the collagen membrane to match, and orient the cell-seeded side toward the subchondral bone as directed by the manufacturer.
9. Secure the membrane with fibrin glue around the entire perimeter (periosteal sutures are not used in the current MACI generation); test containment by cycling the knee through a range of motion and probing the margin gently.
10. Layered closure of the arthrotomy, capsule, and skin.

**E. Osteochondral allograft (OCA)**
5. Confirm graft match (size, condyle laterality, orientation) against the recipient site immediately before opening the graft package.
6. Expose the defect via a limited arthrotomy; ream or use a coring reamer to create a cylindrical (dowel technique) or shaped (shell technique) recipient socket with a healthy, bleeding cancellous bed and measured depth (commonly 4–6 mm of subchondral bone, total plug depth ~5–8 mm including cartilage) — deeper bone inclusion increases immunogenic marrow-element load without improving incorporation.
7. On the back table, prepare the donor graft using a matching coring reamer/saw system, keeping the graft submerged in room-temperature saline throughout cutting to limit thermal necrosis of chondrocytes and to allow the graft to rewarm from cold storage.
8. Trim the graft to the measured recipient depth, then press-fit it into the socket by hand or with minimal, gentle mallet taps on a tamp — never strike the articular cartilage directly; excessive impaction force is a recognized cause of chondrocyte death at the graft surface.
9. Confirm a flush, stable fit without shims or fixation hardware in the standard dowel technique; use peripheral bioabsorbable pins or compression screws only if primary press-fit stability is inadequate (more common with shell grafts or irregular/shoulder lesions).
10. Irrigate thoroughly to clear marrow debris, then close in layers.

**F. Selection and concurrent procedures (all techniques)**
11. Before closing, reassess meniscal integrity and coronal alignment; if a concurrent HTO/DFO or meniscal repair/transplant is planned, sequence it per the surgical plan (osteotomy typically staged or done in the same setting depending on surgeon preference and construct stability).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always confirm true lesion size and containment arthroscopically/intraoperatively before finalizing graft order or plug count — MRI reliably underestimates both size and depth.
- For OAT, harvest donor plugs from the periphery of the lateral trochlea or intercondylar notch (the classic "sulcus terminalis" zone) — never from a directly weight-bearing surface, even if convenient.
- Match plug/graft orientation to native cartilage curvature; a plug placed with even a 1–2 mm step-off relative to the surrounding surface concentrates shear stress at the edge and predisposes to early wear or graft subsidence.
- For mosaicplasty, place plugs in a "brick-laying" pattern working from the defect periphery inward, and angle the harvester slightly to match the local articular curvature rather than harvesting all plugs perpendicular to a flat reference plane.
- For MACI, achieving a dry, bloodless implantation bed is as important as accurate debridement — take the extra few minutes for hemostasis before placing the scaffold, since blood at the interface is a common cause of early delamination.
- For OCA, keep the graft immersed and cool the reaming/cutting process with irrigation to minimize thermal chondrocyte injury; studies of graft-recipient AP dimensional mismatch suggest that modest mismatch is not strongly associated with failure, so do not over-prioritize a perfect dimensional match at the expense of a well-seated, stable press-fit.
- Use minimal manual or gentle tamp pressure — not a mallet directly on cartilage — for both OAT and OCA plug seating; excess impaction force is one of the most under-recognized causes of early chondrocyte death and graft failure.
- Address concomitant malalignment and meniscal deficiency at the time of, or in a planned staged sequence with, the cartilage procedure — grafts placed into an unloaded, biomechanically sound compartment have measurably better survivorship.
- When ordering fresh OCA, build in a realistic buffer for graft-matching and shipping delays, and have a documented contingency (reschedule vs. an alternate technique) discussed with the patient preoperatively.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **OAT**: donor-site morbidity in up to ~10% of patients (usually mild); plug-fill gaps/incongruity between grafts if spacing and angulation are not carefully controlled.
- **MACI**: delamination is less common than with periosteal-patch first-generation ACI but still occurs, most often from an inadequately hemostatic bed or incomplete perimeter fibrin-glue seal; graft hypertrophy and incomplete integration at the graft-host margin can occur; two-stage nature and cost are the principal drawbacks.
- **OCA**: graft resorption/collapse or, rarely, immunologic non-incorporation; graft availability, cost, and a theoretical (very low but real) disease-transmission risk are trade-offs. Excessive impaction force during seating is a preventable cause of surface chondrocyte death.
- MRI tends to underestimate true lesion size and depth compared with arthroscopic findings — confirm intraoperatively before committing to a graft size; under-sizing leads to peripheral gapping and fibrocartilage fill at the margin.
- Any procedure on this ladder performed without correcting a concurrent >5° malalignment, meniscal deficiency, or ligament instability is set up to fail and should instead be staged or combined with realignment osteotomy or meniscal repair/transplant.
- Failure to remove the calcified cartilage layer completely (microfracture and MACI alike) blocks marrow-derived cell access to the defect and is a common, avoidable cause of poor fill.
- Harvesting OAT donor plugs from a truly weight-bearing zone (rather than the intended low-load periphery) converts a minor donor-site issue into a second symptomatic lesion.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weight-bearing**: non-weight-bearing to toe-touch for 4–6 weeks for femoral condyle lesions (6–8 weeks for larger OCA or tibial plateau/patellofemoral lesions), progressing gradually to full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks.
- **CPM**: continuous passive motion is commonly prescribed for MACI/ACI (several hours daily for 4–6 weeks) to promote graft nutrition/maturation; used less consistently after OAT/OCA.
- Avoid impact loading (running, jumping) for 4–6 months; introduce low-impact conditioning (stationary bike, pool) progressively from ~6–8 weeks.
- **Return to pivoting sport**: typically 9–12 months for MACI (graft maturation is slow — hyaline-like tissue continues remodeling well past 12 months), 6–9 months for OAT, 9–12 months for OCA; criteria-based on absence of effusion, full strength symmetry, and (where available) imaging evidence of graft incorporation/fill.
