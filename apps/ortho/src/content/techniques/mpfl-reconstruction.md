## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Recurrent patellar instability (≥2 dislocation/subluxation episodes) with clinical and/or MRI evidence of MPFL insufficiency.
- High-risk first-time dislocators with identifiable risk factors — high-grade trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, elevated TT-TG, young age, contralateral dislocation history, or associated osteochondral injury — reflecting the practice shift away from the older "two-strikes" (wait for recurrence) paradigm.
- Persistent apprehension or subjective instability despite a completed structured quadriceps/hip rehabilitation program.
- As the base soft-tissue procedure in the ESSKA 2024 "à-la-carte" algorithm, to which bony procedures (tibial tubercle osteotomy, trochleoplasty, derotational or distal femoral varus osteotomy) are added according to the patient's individual risk-factor profile.
- Patellar-sided avulsion MPFL tears — these have a higher failure rate with repair alone and generally favor formal reconstruction over primary repair.

**Contraindications**
- Active or recent knee infection.
- Isolated MPFL-R as the *sole* procedure in the face of a major uncorrected bony risk factor (TT-TG >20 mm, severe trochlear dysplasia, Caton-Deschamps >1.3) — proceeding without the indicated concomitant bony correction is a relative contraindication because isolated reconstruction is either overconstrained trying to compensate, or fails.
- Diffuse patellofemoral arthritis with fixed maltracking — soft-tissue reconstruction alone will not address an arthritic, non-congruent joint and may accelerate symptomatic overload.
- Open physes without adopting a physeal-sparing technique (relative — not a bar to surgery, but mandates trajectory modification).
- Uncorrected severe coronal/torsional malalignment (marked genu valgum, significant femoral anteversion or external tibial torsion) driving the instability — address or plan to address concurrently; isolated MPFL-R against a large uncorrected deforming force is prone to recurrence or overload failure.
- Skin compromise or active dermatologic infection over the planned incisions.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Radiographs**: true lateral (femoral condyles overlapped) for Caton-Deschamps index; Merchant/skyline view at 30–45° flexion for sulcus angle and dysplasia signs; full-length standing alignment films if genu valgum is suspected.
- **MRI**: confirms MPFL tear location and pattern (femoral origin vs. mid-substance vs. patellar avulsion — the last favors reconstruction over repair), documents trochlear morphology (Dejour grade), screens for osteochondral injury of the medial patellar facet/lateral femoral condyle, and assesses hamstring donor tendon caliber if autograft harvest is planned.
- **CT**: preferred for precise TT-TG measurement and rotational profiling (femoral anteversion, tibial torsion) when a concomitant bony procedure is under consideration — order this whenever the risk-factor menu (dysplasia grade, alta, TT-TG, rotation) is being formally assessed, not only when TTO is already decided.
- **Graft selection and sizing**: gracilis autograft alone or gracilis + semitendinosus (doubled/quadrupled to a target 5–6 mm diameter), partial-thickness quadriceps tendon autograft (avoids a second harvest-site incision, preserves the hamstrings for concomitant procedures), or allograft (gracilis, semitendinosus, or tibialis anterior — favored in revision settings or when hamstrings are needed elsewhere). Confirm graft diameter matches planned tunnel/socket and fixation device sizing before draping.
- **Equipment**: fluoroscopy C-arm capable of a strict lateral distal-femur view, long guide pins, cannulated drills matched to graft diameter, interference screws (sized to graft), 2.4–3.5 mm suture anchors as an alternative patellar-side fixation option, suspensory button device if using socket rather than tunnel technique, standard arthroscopy tower for a diagnostic look before open work.
- **Case-specific planning**: use the Dejour grade, Caton-Deschamps index, and TT-TG value together (the "menu" of risk factors) to decide before the day of surgery whether TTO, trochleoplasty, or derotational osteotomy will be combined — this changes both sequencing (bony correction before final graft tensioning) and consent. In skeletally immature patients, template the femoral tunnel trajectory against the distal femoral physis on preoperative imaging and plan a physeal-sparing fixation strategy.
- Consent for an examination under anesthesia (EUA) of both knees, and for possible arthroscopic treatment of concomitant chondral lesions or loose bodies found at the time of surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia or spinal/regional anesthesia, typically combined with an adductor canal or femoral nerve block for postoperative analgesia (femoral block should be used judiciously if early postoperative quadriceps activation is desired).
- Supine on a standard radiolucent OR table with a well-padded, non-sterile thigh tourniquet (sterile tourniquet if a concomitant tibial-level procedure is planned in the same field).
- A lateral post or leg holder that allows the knee to be taken through a full flexion arc (0–120°) intraoperatively without repositioning, since isometry/tracking checks require dynamic motion.
- Position and test the fluoroscopy C-arm **before** prepping: confirm a strict lateral view of the distal femur with the posterior femoral condyles precisely superimposed is obtainable in the final leg position — this view is the single most important technical prerequisite for the case.
- Perform EUA on both knees before prep and drape: patellar glide/quadrant test, apprehension test at 20–30° flexion, generalized ligamentous laxity (Beighton score) — compare directly to the contralateral, asymptomatic knee.
- Prep and drape the entire leg free from mid-thigh to foot to allow full ROM assessment and fluoroscopic imaging throughout the case.
- Mark surface landmarks before incision: medial femoral epicondyle, adductor tubercle, medial and lateral borders of the patella, tibial tubercle, planned hamstring harvest incision (~3 cm over the pes anserinus, roughly 2 finger-breadths distal to the joint line), and the medial parapatellar patellar-sided incision (3–4 cm).
- Diagnostic arthroscopy through standard anterolateral/anteromedial portals is commonly performed first to assess cartilage status, remove loose bodies, address concomitant chondral lesions (fix fragments ≥1 cm² when feasible), and confirm the instability pattern dynamically before proceeding to the open reconstruction.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Perform EUA and diagnostic arthroscopy; treat concomitant intra-articular pathology (loose body removal, chondral fixation, synovial pathology) before proceeding.
2. Harvest the graft: gracilis (± semitendinosus) through the pes anserinus incision, or a partial-thickness quadriceps tendon strip (central third, superficial layer, roughly 7–9 cm long × 8–10 mm wide, leaving the deep fibers and patellar attachment intact for a pedicled technique), or thaw and prepare allograft on the back table with whipstitched/looped ends sized to the planned tunnel diameter.
3. Make the medial parapatellar incision and develop the interval for the soft-tissue tunnel in the "second layer" plane — deep to the subcutaneous fat, superficial to the joint capsule/synovium — running from the proximal-medial patella to the region of the medial femoral epicondyle.
4. Prepare patellar-side fixation: two divergent bone tunnels in the proximal one-half to two-thirds of the medial patellar border (angled obliquely, kept ≥15 mm apart, and clear of the anterior cortex and articular surface), or a docking construct with suture anchors, or a single patellar tunnel with the graft looped and secured with an interference screw or suspensory button. Choose anchors over tunnels in a thin or small patella.
5. Identify the femoral attachment site using Schöttle's fluoroscopic method. Obtain a strict lateral of the distal femur with the posterior condyles precisely superimposed — even a few degrees of obliquity meaningfully shifts the apparent point, so re-shoot rather than accept an approximate view. The point lies roughly 1 mm anterior to the posterior femoral cortex extension line, at the level of the posterior origin of Blumensaat's line, and just proximal/anterior to the posterior point of the medial femoral condyle. A lateral-to-medial beam orientation has been shown to improve accuracy over the traditional medial-to-lateral view (acceptable tunnel placement in roughly 91% vs. 66% of cases in comparative fluoroscopic studies) — use this orientation when C-arm access allows.
6. Place a guide pin at the fluoroscopically confirmed point and check it on both the true lateral (Schöttle point) and an AP view — the pin should sit at approximately the level of the posterior aspect of the medial epicondyle and, in skeletally immature patients, be aimed away from the distal femoral physis.
7. Cross-check the fluoroscopic point against the palpable anatomic landmark — the soft-tissue "saddle" between the adductor tubercle and the medial epicondyle — before committing to drilling.
8. Drill the femoral tunnel or socket over the guide pin to a depth appropriate for the chosen fixation device (commonly a 25–30 mm socket for suspensory or interference fixation). Avoid full transphyseal drilling in the skeletally immature femur.
9. Pass the graft through the second-layer soft-tissue tunnel from the patella to the femoral site, confirming it remains extra-articular (deep to subcutaneous tissue, superficial to capsule) and lies without kinking or excessive tension.
10. Provisionally fix the femoral end with a temporary suture or pin and take the knee through a full ROM arc (0–120°) before committing to final fixation. Check isometry: graft length change from extension to flexion should be minimal — a graft that tightens progressively with flexion signals an anterior/proximal tunnel and predicts overconstraint; some graft laxity in deeper flexion is expected and appropriate, since the trochlear groove — not the MPFL — becomes the dominant restraint past 30–40°.
11. With the knee flexed to approximately 30–45° and the patella manually centered in the trochlea (not held over-medialized), tension the graft and secure the femoral side with an interference screw, a suspensory button, or a tied suture over a post — never tension in full extension.
12. Cycle the knee through full ROM again and directly assess patellar tracking and glide: roughly one quadrant of lateral translation at 20–30° flexion should remain (confirming the reconstruction is not overconstrained) with no residual lateral apprehension.
13. Confirm restored central patellar tracking through the arc of motion, either by direct visualization or fluoroscopically; if residual maltracking persists and a bony procedure was not already planned, reassess the risk-factor profile before closing.
14. If a concomitant bony procedure (TTO, trochleoplasty, derotational osteotomy) is being performed in the same setting, complete the bony correction **before** final MPFL graft tensioning — bony correction changes the resting length/tension needed from the graft.
15. Close the soft-tissue tunnel and retinaculum, close the patellar-side and graft-harvest incisions in layers, apply a sterile dressing, and place a hinged knee brace locked in extension before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a lateral-to-medial (rather than medial-to-lateral) fluoroscopic beam when localizing Schöttle's point — comparative data show meaningfully higher accuracy with this orientation.
- Insist on a truly superimposed-condyle lateral before drilling; re-shoot rather than accept a "close enough" image, since even minor obliquity produces a clinically significant tunnel error.
- Perform the isometry/excursion check with a temporary suture before committing to final femoral fixation — this is the cheapest insurance against overconstraint and is far easier to correct at this stage than after definitive fixation.
- Fix the patellar side first (it tolerates small positional variance better) and save the femoral side — the accuracy-critical step — for last, with an unhurried final fluoroscopic check.
- Reduce the patella to a centered position during tensioning rather than holding it over-medialized; a common intraoperative error is to tension the graft while manually reducing the patella too far medially, which reproduces a falsely tight construct.
- Tension at 30–45° flexion, never in extension — grafts tensioned in extension are reliably tighter than intended once the knee flexes and the native anatomy takes over load-sharing.
- When harvesting a quadriceps tendon autograft, keep the strip partial-thickness and leave the deep fibers and patellar attachment intact when using a pedicled construct — this preserves extensor mechanism strength and avoids opening a separate hamstring harvest site.
- Stay strictly in the second-layer plane (deep to subcutaneous fat, superficial to capsule/synovium) when creating the soft-tissue tunnel — straying intra-articular risks synovitis/effusion, and straying too superficial produces a visible or palpable graft ridge.
- In combined procedures, sequence bony correction (TTO, trochleoplasty) before final graft tensioning — tensioning first and correcting bone afterward risks a mismatched, retrospectively over- or under-tensioned graft.
- Consider suture-tape internal-brace augmentation alongside the primary graft in revision cases, higher-demand athletes, or when graft tissue quality is marginal — early biomechanical and clinical data support decreased gap formation without adverse effect on patellofemoral contact pressure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Anterior/proximal femoral tunnel malposition** is the leading technical cause of failure and revision surgery. It causes progressive graft tightening in flexion, overconstraint, loss of terminal flexion, elevated medial patellofemoral contact pressure, and accelerated medial-facet arthrosis; posterior/distal malposition under-constrains and risks recurrent instability. Always confirm the Schöttle point fluoroscopically before final fixation.
- **Saphenous nerve (infrapatellar branch) injury** is a recognized risk during hamstring harvest or the patellar-side incision — anteromedial knee numbness is common and usually transient; protect by staying anterior to the sartorius and using blunt dissection near the pes.
- **Patellar fracture** from the graft tunnel (stress riser) is a rare but recognized complication, more likely with tunnels placed too close together, too close to the anterior cortex, or in a thin patella — keep tunnels ≥15 mm apart, angle them obliquely, and favor suture-anchor fixation over tunnels in a small or osteopenic patella.
- Pooled recurrent instability after MPFL-R is **~3–8%**, but this rate is meaningfully higher when bony risk factors (high-grade dysplasia, significant alta, TT-TG >20 mm) are left unaddressed — isolated MPFL-R is not always sufficient.
- **Overconstraint** from tensioning too tightly or in extension presents as loss of terminal flexion, persistent medial-sided pain, or (over years) accelerated medial patellofemoral arthrosis. Recognize it by comparing passive flexion to the contralateral knee intraoperatively and at follow-up; severe cases may require graft release or revision.
- **Stiffness/arthrofibrosis** and **persistent anterior knee pain** are recognized complications independent of tunnel position — evaluate for overconstraint, an unaddressed bony risk factor, or concomitant chondral pathology before attributing either to "just a stiff knee."
- In skeletally immature patients, physeal injury from a transphyseal trajectory risks growth arrest or angular deformity — use a physeal-sparing technique (epiphyseal fixation or a fluoroscopically confirmed trajectory that stays distal/anterior to the physis).

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: hinged brace locked in extension for ambulation (unlocked for supervised ROM exercises); weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches (WBAT if isolated MPFL-R; touch-down/protected WB for ~4–6 weeks if a concurrent TTO is pending union); quad sets, straight-leg raises, patellar mobilization; ROM 0–90° by end of week 2.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progress ROM to full flexion; wean brace/crutches as quad control returns (typically able to perform SLR without lag before weaning brace); begin closed-chain strengthening (mini-squats, limited-arc leg press); stationary bike once ROM >100°.
- **Weeks 6–12**: full ROM expected; progressive strengthening (closed then open chain as tolerated); proprioceptive/balance training; light jogging around week 8–12 if quad strength ≥80% contralateral and no effusion.
- **Months 3–6**: sport-specific agility, plyometrics, cutting/pivoting drills introduced progressively from month 4.
- **Return to sport**: typically **6–9 months**, criteria-based — quadriceps strength ≥90% contralateral limb, single-leg hop test symmetry ≥90%, no apprehension on exam, completion of sport-specific agility progression without symptoms. If a bony procedure (TTO) was combined, radiographic union (typically by 8–12 weeks) is required before impact/pivoting sport.
