## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: failure to achieve/maintain acceptable alignment by closed means; Fernandez II/III/IV/V patterns; AO/OTA 23-B3 (volar Barton) and most 23-C patterns; articular step/gap >2 mm in an active patient; dorsally comminuted fractures with volar cortical continuity. Volar locking plate (VLP) is the workhorse fixation for unstable/displaced distal radius fractures (DRF), and recent meta-analyses (2024–2025) comparing VLP to external fixation generally favor VLP for earlier functional recovery and fewer pin-site complications.

**Contraindications**:
- Active infection over the volar wrist or a compromised soft-tissue envelope at the planned incision site — address the soft-tissue problem first, or consider a staged/external-fixation approach.
- Severely comminuted dorsal and volar cortices with no stable column for plate purchase — may need combined dorsal/volar fixation, fragment-specific plating, or bridging external fixation rather than VLP alone.
- Very low-demand, non-ambulatory, or medically unfit patients where the risks of surgery outweigh the modest functional advantage over casting — the evidence base (VOLCON, WRIST, DRIFT, DART) supports individualized, activity/comorbidity-based rather than purely displacement-based decision-making in this group.
- Open fractures with gross contamination — irrigate and debride first; definitive VLP fixation may be staged rather than performed at the index procedure depending on wound status.
- Relative: a small, isolated volar-ulnar (lunate facet) fragment too distal/small for standard plate purchase — plan for a fragment-specific implant or dorsal augmentation preoperatively rather than discovering intraoperatively that the standard plate cannot control it.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/templating**: PA, lateral, and oblique wrist radiographs with measurement of radial inclination, radial height, volar/dorsal tilt, articular step/gap, and ulnar variance; CT for comminuted, die-punch, or complex intra-articular patterns to plan fragment-specific fixation and to identify a small volar-ulnar (lunate facet) fragment that a standard plate's distal row may not adequately control.
- **Implant selection/sizing**: select plate length/width based on templating against the contralateral or pre-injury films; have both a standard volar locking plate and fragment-specific/dorsal spanning plate options available for die-punch (Melone medial complex) patterns identified on CT. Distal (subchondral) row screws are typically locking screws to maximize purchase in osteoporotic/comminuted metaphyseal bone; proximal shaft screws are usually a mix of locking and non-locking depending on plate design and reduction needs.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: volar locking plate system with variable-angle or fixed-angle distal screw options per surgeon preference, provisional K-wires for fragment holding, a distraction/traction tower or finger traps with weighted countertraction for ligamentotaxis, mini fluoroscopy, depth gauge, and — if a die-punch pattern is anticipated — a fragment-specific plate set or small dorsal buttress plate.
- **Case-specific planning**: review the watershed line anatomy on lateral imaging/templating — the width of this line (averaging roughly 28–29 mm in cadaveric studies) has been proposed as an anatomic guide to conservative initial distal screw-length selection by plate size; plan distal screw lengths conservatively (many series recommend not exceeding ~24 mm) and expect to trim intraoperatively based on direct fluoroscopic and tactile feedback rather than relying on preoperative templating alone. Plan the DRUJ stress-test step into the case for any Fernandez IV/V or AO/OTA 23-B/C pattern, and have DRUJ transfixion equipment available on the back table.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or regional (axillary/supraclavicular block) anesthesia; regional block provides excellent intraoperative conditions and extended postoperative analgesia for this typically 60–120 minute procedure.
- **Position**: supine, arm abducted ~90° on a radiolucent hand table.
- **Tourniquet**: pneumatic upper-arm tourniquet, inflated for the exposure and fixation; can be released before final closure if the case has run long, once hemostasis is confirmed.
- **Traction setup**: finger traps on the index and middle (± ring) fingers with a traction tower or weighted countertraction available for ligamentotaxis-assisted reduction of length and inclination before addressing tilt.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm so PA, lateral, and — critically — a "skyline"/tangential distal view can all be obtained without repositioning the arm; the skyline view is essential for confirming distal screws have not penetrated the dorsal cortex and is easy to omit under time pressure if not planned into the setup.
- **Draping**: prep and drape the hand and forearm free to allow intraoperative forearm rotation for the DRUJ stress test at the end of the case.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: the flexor carpi radialis (FCR) tendon (palpate with resisted wrist flexion), the radial artery pulse (stay ulnar to it), and the surface projection of the fracture line/watershed level on the volar wrist.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Mark the FCR tendon and plan the incision along or just radial to the FCR sheath, extending from the wrist flexion crease proximally as needed for plate length.
2. Incise and open the FCR sheath, retract the FCR tendon ulnarly (or radially, per surgeon preference) along with the median nerve and remaining flexor tendons, and retract the radial artery radially — this is the modified Henry interval.
3. Elevate the pronator quadratus from its radial insertion, preserving as much of the muscle as feasible for later repair, and reflect it ulnarly to expose the fracture.
4. Débride the fracture hematoma and clear the fracture site of interposed callus/soft tissue to allow an accurate reduction.
5. Restore radial length and inclination first, often assisted by longitudinal traction (finger traps/traction tower) achieving ligamentotaxis through the intact volar and dorsal ligament complex, before addressing volar tilt.
6. Reduce the articular surface under direct vision and fluoroscopy; use provisional K-wires to hold key fragments (particularly the volar-ulnar/lunate facet fragment, which is small and easily under-recognized) before applying the plate.
7. Address volar tilt by correcting any residual dorsal angulation, typically with a combination of direct manipulation and the buttressing effect of the plate itself once provisionally applied.
8. Apply the volar locking plate, positioned proximal to the watershed line — the transverse ridge marking the distal extent of safe plate placement — to avoid later flexor tendon irritation/rupture; a plate edge sitting distal to this line against the flexor tendons is the classic mechanism for late FPL rupture.
9. Provisionally fix the plate to the shaft with a non-locking screw or K-wire through an oblong/compression hole, then confirm plate position and fracture reduction on fluoroscopy before committing to the distal locking screws.
10. Place distal (subchondral) row locking screws, prioritizing purchase in the strongest subchondral bone and, where relevant, direct fixation of the volar-ulnar (lunate facet) fragment; use locking screws preferentially here given the typically comminuted/osteoporotic nature of this bone.
11. Confirm distal screw length under multiple fluoroscopic views, including a dedicated "skyline"/tangential distal view, to avoid dorsal cortex penetration and extensor tendon injury — a useful adjunct technique is to palpate the drill/depth-gauge hook tip against the dorsal cortex and subtract several millimeters (roughly 7 mm in published technique descriptions) from the measured length as a cross-check against fluoroscopic measurement alone.
12. Place proximal shaft screws to secure the plate to the diaphysis, using the compression/oblong hole first if not already done, to fine-tune fracture reduction before locking.
13. For die-punch (Melone medial complex) fragments not adequately controlled by the volar plate's distal row, add a fragment-specific plate or a dorsal buttress/spanning plate as planned preoperatively.
14. Take final PA, lateral, and skyline fluoroscopic views to confirm articular reduction, radial inclination/height/tilt restoration, and absence of any intra-articular or dorsally penetrating screws.
15. Passively and actively (where feasible under anesthesia) move the wrist and check for tendon catching or crepitus against the plate/screws before closing.
16. Actively stress-test DRUJ stability in pronation, neutral, and supination under fluoroscopy before closing — this is the operative moment that converts occult instability into a treated diagnosis, and should be performed after any DRF or ulnar styloid fixation; proceed to DRUJ stabilization technique if instability is found.
17. Repair the pronator quadratus over the plate where tissue quality allows, providing an additional soft-tissue barrier between the plate and the flexor tendons.
18. Close the FCR sheath, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers; apply a well-padded volar splint with the wrist in a neutral-to-slightly-extended position.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use ligamentotaxis (longitudinal traction through intact ligaments) to restore length and inclination before fighting to correct tilt directly — length restoration often makes tilt correction technically easier.
- Provisionally pin the volar-ulnar (lunate facet) fragment before applying the plate — this fragment is small, often displaces independently, and is the classic piece that a standard plate's distal row fails to control if not specifically addressed.
- Plan distal screw length conservatively rather than maximally — published biomechanical and clinical data suggest full-length distal screws are often unnecessary for adequate subchondral support, and shorter (roughly 75%-length) screws can reduce the risk of dorsal penetration while still providing solid fixation; a widely cited rule of thumb is not to exceed roughly 24 mm in the distal row regardless of what the depth gauge suggests, and to cross-check with a tangential/skyline fluoroscopic view.
- The watershed line's width (averaging roughly 28–29 mm in cadaveric series) has been proposed as a rough anatomic proxy for expected distal screw length by plate size — useful as a sanity check against the depth gauge reading, not a replacement for fluoroscopic confirmation.
- Always obtain a true tangential/skyline distal view before finishing — this is the single most reliable way to catch a dorsally prominent screw, and standard PA/lateral views alone can miss penetration that the skyline view reveals.
- Move the wrist through a full passive arc intraoperatively before closing to feel/see for any screw or plate impingement on the flexor or extensor tendons.
- Repair the pronator quadratus over the plate when tissue quality permits — this adds a soft-tissue buffer between hardware and the flexor tendons and is associated with lower rates of late tendon irritation in many surgeons' experience, even though the evidence base for this specific step is mixed.
- Keep the plate's most proximal-distal edge strictly proximal to the watershed line — when in doubt intraoperatively about whether the plate sits too distal, err proximal; a plate positioned slightly more proximal than ideal is a far smaller problem than one that abrades the flexor tendons.
- Do not skip the intraoperative DRUJ stress test simply because the radius reduction "looks anatomic" — instability can be present even after a technically excellent radius fixation, and this is the only reliable moment to catch and treat it.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Plate placement distal to the watershed line predisposes to FPL rupture from mechanical abrasion against the tendon — a well-documented late complication of distal plate malposition.
- EPL rupture is a recognized delayed complication even with correctly measured screws, from direct trauma or attritional wear over a healing dorsal ridge or a prominent dorsal screw; it remains the most commonly cited complication after variable-angle volar locking plate fixation, and dorsal cortex screw penetration is its most common identifiable cause — reinforcing the importance of the skyline fluoroscopic view.
- Over-reliance on VLP alone for dorsally comminuted, volarly deficient fractures without addressing the dorsal defect risks loss of volar tilt.
- Missing a small volar-ulnar (lunate facet) fragment too distal/small for standard plate purchase is a leading cause of early fixation failure and carpal subluxation.
- Recent meta-analyses (2024–2025) comparing VLP to external fixation generally favor VLP for earlier functional recovery and fewer pin-site complications.
- Median nerve injury/acute carpal tunnel syndrome (4.5–9% incidence in DRF overall) requires vigilant serial exam through the postoperative period and a low threshold for urgent carpal tunnel release if progressive.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis is strongly correlated with residual articular incongruity (>2 mm step/gap) — the principal justification for anatomic articular reduction, particularly in 23-B/C, Fernandez II/III, and Melone II–V patterns.
- Delayed union/nonunion is uncommon in metaphyseal DRF given the rich cancellous bone bed, but should be suspected with persistent pain and lack of radiographic progression beyond 3–4 months, particularly in smokers, uncontrolled diabetics, or after high-energy comminuted patterns.
- Extensor/flexor tenosynovitis short of frank rupture is common with prominent hardware; monitor clinically and consider elective hardware removal (typically not before 1 year, once union is secure) if symptomatic.
- Malposition of the plate too proximal can leave the distal fragment/articular surface inadequately supported, particularly in comminuted metaphyseal patterns — balance the need to stay proximal to the watershed line against maintaining adequate distal fragment control.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 0 (0–2 wk)**: protected splint; digit AROM from day 1; strict avoidance of forearm rotation and wrist loading; elevation/edema control; continued median nerve status documentation.
- **Phase 1 (2–6 wk)**: given rigid fixation, early protected active wrist ROM typically begins at 1–2 weeks post-op (surgeon/construct-dependent), progressing gently — this early-motion advantage is a practical benefit of internal fixation over casting.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 wk)**: progressive strengthening and scar mobilization; gentle grip strengthening from ~week 6–8 once union is radiographically progressing; per AAOS/APTA 2024 CPG, track PRWE/DASH/QuickDASH and grip dynamometry.
- **Phase 3 (12–16+ wk)**: progressive resistive strengthening, proprioceptive/weight-bearing exercises, sport- or occupation-specific reconditioning.
- **Imaging**: post-op, 2 wk, 6 wk, 3 mo (union confirmation); further imaging only if clinically indicated.
- **Loading milestones**: light functional loading (typing, light ADLs) generally from ~6 weeks once early union present; unrestricted loading/impact activity deferred to ~12 weeks with confirmed union and adequate strength/ROM.
- **Return to work**: sedentary/desk work often feasible within 1–2 weeks (non-dominant hand); manual labor/heavy lifting deferred to ~12 weeks with radiographic union and functional grip strength (commonly ≥70–80% of contralateral) and near-full pronosupination.
- **Return to sport**: pain-free full composite ROM, grip strength ≥85–90% of contralateral side, radiographic union, and sport-specific functional testing — generally not before 3 months for high-demand/impact sport.
