## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Subaxial cervical fracture-dislocation with SLIC ≥5, or AO Spine type C morphology, or B2/B3 morphology with a neurologic (N) deficit.
- Unifacet or bifacet dislocation, facet fracture-dislocation, and multi-column subaxial instability.
- Fractures in patients with ankylosing spondylitis/DISH (M3/M2 modifier) — these require a distinct, more cautious management approach given the brittle, poorly vascularized, long-lever-arm nature of the ankylosed spine.
- Progressive neurologic deterioration or persistent cord/root compression despite closed reduction.
- Failed or incomplete closed reduction in an injury pattern otherwise amenable to it.

**Contraindications / cautions**
- **Manual reduction/extension maneuvers in ankylosing spondylitis/DISH** — contraindicated; these can worsen displacement in brittle, poorly vascularized ankylosed bone. Reduction (if attempted at all) should be done with extreme caution, typically in the operating room under controlled conditions rather than at the bedside.
- **Awake closed reduction in an obtunded, uncooperative, or non-examinable patient** — relative contraindication; pre-reduction MRI is generally preferred in this population to exclude a large disc herniation that could be pushed into the canal during reduction.
- **Uncorrected coagulopathy or hemodynamic instability** precluding safe positioning/anesthesia — stabilize first.
- **Distracted/unstable segments with an associated vertebral artery injury** on CTA — modify approach/instrumentation planning accordingly and involve vascular/neurointerventional consultation as needed.
- **Grossly comminuted vertebral body precluding safe anterior plate/screw purchase** — favor a posterior or combined approach instead of anterior-only fixation.

## Preoperative Planning

- **CT (thin-cut, sagittal/coronal reconstructions)** of the full cervical spine to characterize fracture morphology, facet perch/dislocation, and to screen for non-contiguous injuries — essential in any high-energy mechanism and mandatory in suspected AS/DISH.
- **MRI**, particularly pre-reduction in obtunded or non-examinable patients, to assess disc herniation, cord signal/compression, PLC/DLC integrity, and epidural hematoma; also used post-reduction to confirm adequacy of decompression when reduction is achieved by traction alone.
- **CTA of the neck** when the fracture involves the transverse foramen, in facet dislocation, or in C1-C3 injuries, to screen for blunt cerebrovascular/vertebral artery injury (Denver/modified Denver criteria).
- **Decide approach preoperatively** based on the dominant column of injury: anterior (ACDF ± corpectomy) for primary disc/body pathology; posterior (lateral mass screw-rod fixation) for primary facet/posterior tension-band pathology; combined for three-column (AO type C) injuries or when either approach alone is judged insufficient.
- **Implant selection**: anterior cervical plate and interbody graft/cage or corpectomy strut graft/cage sized to the involved level(s) for the anterior construct; a lateral mass screw-rod system (or, for AS/DISH, a long-segment construct spanning well above and below the injury) for the posterior construct.
- **For AS/DISH specifically**: image the entire spine given the disproportionately high rate of non-contiguous fractures in this population, and plan a long-segment construct from the outset rather than a standard short-segment fixation.
- **Have Gardner-Wells tongs/traction equipment available** if closed reduction is planned as a first step, with a predetermined traction weight protocol and neurologic-exam checkpoints between weight increments.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Awake closed reduction (when performed) precedes anesthesia**: performed in a monitored setting (ICU or OR) with the patient awake and cooperative, using Gardner-Wells tongs and serial traction weight increases under continuous fluoroscopy, with a neurologic exam repeated after each increment — stop and reassess immediately if any new deficit appears.
- **General anesthesia with awake fiberoptic intubation** (or in-line stabilization) for the definitive fixation procedure, regardless of approach, given the unstable segment; avoid neck manipulation during induction and transfer.
- **Anterior approach positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table, shoulder roll, head in a horseshoe headrest or gel doughnut, shoulders taped caudally for fluoroscopic visualization of lower cervical/upper thoracic levels if needed.
- **Posterior approach positioning**: prone with the head rigidly fixed in a Mayfield three-pin clamp, allowing controlled intraoperative reduction under direct vision and fluoroscopy; neutral-to-slightly-flexed positioning is typical to open the interlaminar space, adjusted per injury pattern.
- **AS/DISH-specific positioning caution**: position the patient in whatever alignment matches their fixed baseline kyphotic deformity — do not force a neutral or extended position on a table, since this itself can create catastrophic displacement in a rigid, ankylosed spine; positioning should replicate, not correct, the patient's baseline sagittal alignment unless correction is a deliberate, controlled part of the planned reduction.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: true AP and lateral views of the injured level(s) obtainable without repositioning; confirm before draping.
- **Prep and drape** the field appropriate to the planned approach (anterior cervical field, posterior midline field, or both if a combined procedure is planned or anticipated as a possible intraoperative conversion).

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Attempt awake closed reduction** (if indicated and the patient is a suitable candidate) with serial neurologic exams under fluoroscopy using Gardner-Wells tongs/traction, incrementally increasing weight while reassessing alignment and neurologic status after each increment; stop immediately if a new deficit develops and obtain urgent MRI before any further reduction attempt.
2. **Confirm final reduction (or document irreducibility) on fluoroscopy** before proceeding to definitive fixation, and obtain post-reduction MRI if not already done, particularly if reduction was achieved by traction in a patient who could not be reliably examined throughout.

### Anterior approach (ACDF ± corpectomy)
3. **Standard Smith-Robinson anterior cervical exposure** to the involved level(s), developing the interval between the carotid sheath and trachea/esophagus.
4. **Perform discectomy** at the injured level(s), removing disrupted disc material and directly decompressing any retropulsed disc/bone fragments compressing the cord.
5. **If a comminuted vertebral body fracture (AO A3/A4) is present**, extend to corpectomy, removing the fractured body back to healthy bone margins and preparing endplates for a structural strut graft or cage.
6. **Reduce residual translation/angulation** under fluoroscopy using distraction and gentle manipulation across the disc/corpectomy space.
7. **Place interbody graft/cage or corpectomy strut graft/cage**, restoring height and lordosis, then apply an anterior cervical plate spanning the construct with screws into the involved vertebral bodies.
8. **Confirm alignment and hardware position on final AP/lateral fluoroscopy.**

### Posterior approach (lateral mass screw-rod fixation)
9. **Posterior midline exposure**, subperiosteal dissection to the lateral masses of the involved and adjacent levels.
10. **Directly visualize and reduce the dislocated/perched facet(s)** using a facet distractor or similar instrument, under direct vision — this direct facet access is the principal advantage of the posterior approach for predominantly posterior injuries.
11. **Place lateral mass screws bilaterally** at the involved and adjacent levels using a standard entry point and trajectory (e.g., Magerl or Roy-Camille technique per surgeon preference), confirming trajectory under fluoroscopy and staying mindful of the exiting nerve root and vertebral artery (more laterally at risk at the more cephalad levels).
12. **Contour and secure rods**, applying compression or neutral loading across the construct as the injury pattern dictates, and decorticate/graft the posterior elements to promote fusion.
13. **Confirm alignment and hardware position on final fluoroscopy.**

### Combined anterior-posterior approach
14. **For highly unstable, multi-column (AO type C) injuries**, perform both anterior and posterior stages (order per surgeon preference and injury pattern — posterior facet reduction/fixation first when facet dislocation dominates and needs direct reduction, or anterior decompression first when significant cord compression from disc/bone requires urgent anterior clearance) to achieve circumferential stability.

### AS/DISH-specific technique notes
15. **Avoid intraoperative extension/reduction maneuvers** beyond gentle, image-guided correction; favor long-segment posterior fixation spanning well above and below the fracture (typically several levels beyond what would be used in a non-ankylosed spine) given the inherently unstable, poorly vascularized bone and long lever arms involved.
16. **Confirm no non-contiguous fracture** was missed on whole-spine imaging before considering the case complete.

### Closure (any approach)
17. **Irrigate, achieve hemostasis, and close in layers**, with a drain for the posterior approach if extensive dissection or epidural bleeding occurred.
18. **Obtain postoperative CT** to confirm hardware position and alignment before mobilization.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Increase traction weight in small, deliberate increments during closed reduction, reassessing the neurologic exam after each step — a new deficit during reduction is a stop-and-reassess event, not a reason to push through with more weight.
- Choose the approach based on which column dominates the injury pattern (anterior for disc/body compression, posterior for tension-band/facet injury, combined for three-column AO type C injuries) rather than defaulting to a single approach for every case.
- For posterior facet reduction, a dedicated facet distractor under direct vision gives more controlled reduction than attempting reduction purely through screw/rod compression after instrumentation is placed.
- Extend lateral mass screw fixation at least one level above and below the primary injury in most posterior constructs for adequate biomechanical purchase, and further in AS/DISH given the long, rigid lever arms involved.
- In AS/DISH, position the patient to match their fixed baseline sagittal alignment rather than forcing a neutral position on the table — attempting to "correct" the deformity through positioning alone, outside a controlled and deliberate reduction maneuver, is a recognized cause of iatrogenic displacement.
- Obtain whole-spine imaging early in any suspected AS/DISH fracture — a second, non-contiguous fracture is common enough that it should be actively excluded rather than assumed absent.
- When corpectomy is required anteriorly, prepare adjacent endplates meticulously and select strut graft/cage height carefully — under- or over-distraction of the corpectomy space affects both construct stability and segmental alignment.
- In combined procedures, decide staging order based on which pathology is more urgent/threatening (cord compression from disc/bone favors early anterior decompression; facet dislocation that needs direct reduction favors posterior-first) rather than a fixed sequence.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **In an ankylosed spine (AS/DISH), avoid manual reduction/extension maneuvers** — these can worsen displacement in brittle, poorly vascularized bone; use long-segment fixation instead of standard-length constructs.
- **Whole-spine imaging should be obtained with a low threshold in AS/DISH patients**, given the disproportionately high rate of missed or under-treated fractures and epidural hematoma in this population.
- **Choose the approach (anterior, posterior, or combined) based on which column dominates the injury pattern** — anterior for disc/body compression, posterior for tension-band/facet injury, combined for three-column AO type C injuries or when anterior-only fixation is judged insufficient.
- **Pre-reduction MRI practice varies**; obtunded patients or those who cannot be reliably examined during traction generally warrant pre-reduction MRI when readily available.
- **New or worsening neurologic deficit during closed reduction** — stop immediately, obtain urgent MRI, and reassess the plan; do not continue increasing traction weight through a new deficit.
- **Vertebral artery injury** during posterior lateral mass screw placement, particularly at more cephalad subaxial levels — careful trajectory and awareness of individual anatomic variation are the primary mitigations.
- **Inadequate decompression** if a retropulsed fragment or herniated disc is not fully addressed anteriorly before relying on posterior-only fixation/ligamentotaxis for indirect decompression.
- **Construct failure/pseudarthrosis** if fixation length or approach does not match the injury's instability pattern — an underpowered construct in a highly unstable AO type C injury is a recognized cause of early hardware failure.
- **Esophageal/tracheal injury** from anterior retraction, as with any anterior cervical exposure.
- **Missed non-contiguous fracture in AS/DISH**, given the disproportionately high rate of missed injury and epidural hematoma in this population — a low threshold for whole-spine imaging mitigates this.

## Postoperative Protocol

Post-op fixation patients typically mobilize with brace support per surgeon protocol, with formal ROM restrictions (avoid combined flexion-rotation for facet injuries, avoid extension in extension-type injuries) until early fusion consolidation (~6–8 weeks). Interval imaging at 6–12 weeks confirms healing/stability. Progressive resistance exercise and dynamic core stabilization follow once fusion is trending (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable fixation; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months, longer if fusion levels are extensive or a neurologic deficit persists. Return-to-sport/contact-activity clearance requires solid radiographic fusion (≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, and normalized strength (≥90% of contralateral/baseline), and clearance for contact/collision sports remains individualized and controversial, particularly for constructs involving C1-C2.
