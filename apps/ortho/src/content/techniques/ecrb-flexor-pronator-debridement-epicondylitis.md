## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Lateral epicondylitis (ECRB origin) or medial epicondylitis (common flexor-pronator origin) refractory to ≥6-12 months of conservative management, including a documented, adequately dosed trial of eccentric/isotonic loading and at least a considered trial of PRP or corticosteroid injection.
- Persistent, functionally limiting pain with imaging (MRI/US) corroborating tendinosis at the expected origin.
- Suspected concomitant intra-articular pathology (plica, loose body, osteochondral lesion) on the lateral side — favors an arthroscopic approach that can treat both simultaneously.

**Contraindications**
- Symptom duration <6 months without a fully completed structured conservative trial — given that ~80-90% of epicondylitis resolves within 12 months regardless of treatment, premature surgery exposes patients to operative risk for a condition likely to resolve on its own.
- Active infection, or diagnostic uncertainty about the source of lateral/medial elbow pain (e.g., unaddressed cervical radiculopathy, radial tunnel syndrome as the true pain generator) — clarify the diagnosis before committing to debridement.
- Medial epicondylitis with unaddressed, symptomatic ulnar neuropathy that has not been evaluated/planned for concurrently — decide pre-operatively whether concomitant ulnar nerve transposition is needed.
- Uncontrolled expectations — patients seeking a "quick fix" without having exhausted conservative measures are poor surgical candidates both clinically and from a shared-decision-making standpoint.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** MRI or ultrasound to confirm the diagnosis, characterize the degree of tendinosis versus partial/full-thickness tearing, and screen for coexisting intra-articular pathology (lateral: plica, osteochondral lesion, loose bodies; medial: ulnar collateral ligament attenuation, ulnar nerve subluxation).
- **Approach decision:** choose open versus arthroscopic technique before the day of surgery based on imaging findings — arthroscopic approach is favored when intra-articular pathology is suspected, since it allows diagnostic and therapeutic treatment of both problems in one setting; open approach remains standard for isolated extra-articular debridement, particularly on the medial side where the ulnar nerve makes arthroscopic ECRB-equivalent work impractical.
- **Ulnar nerve planning (medial cases):** given up to 50% coexistence of ulnar neuritis with medial epicondylitis, examine and document ulnar nerve status (Tinel at the cubital tunnel, subjective paresthesias, motor exam) pre-operatively, and have a low threshold to plan concomitant in-situ decompression or transposition if there is any pre-operative ulnar nerve irritability.
- **Equipment:** standard elbow arthroscopy tower/instrumentation (for lateral arthroscopic cases) or an open soft-tissue instrument set (for open ECRB or flexor-pronator debridement); have suture anchors available if a formal tendon repair/reattachment (rather than debridement alone) is anticipated based on the degree of pathology seen on imaging.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or a supraclavicular/infraclavicular brachial plexus block, often combined for post-operative analgesia; regional block is particularly helpful for same-day discharge comfort.
- **Positioning — open technique:** supine with the arm on a hand table, or lateral decubitus with the arm draped over a padded bolster for better access to the lateral elbow.
- **Positioning — arthroscopic technique (lateral cases):** lateral decubitus or supine with a arm holder/traction tower, allowing standard anteromedial/anterolateral (and posterior, if needed) portal placement; a sterile or non-sterile tourniquet is used, and the surgeon should confirm free elbow flexion/extension range before final draping.
- **Landmarks to mark:** the lateral epicondyle and the point of maximal tenderness ~1 cm distal/anterior to it (ECRB origin) for lateral cases; the medial epicondyle and the course of the ulnar nerve (mark the cubital tunnel) for medial cases — critical to mark the nerve course before incision given its proximity to the working field.
- **Tourniquet:** standard arm tourniquet, exsanguinated and inflated per limb-appropriate protocol; plan to release before final closure to confirm hemostasis, particularly around the ulnar nerve field on the medial side.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm failure of ≥6-12 months conservative management, including a documented PRP or corticosteroid injection trial, and reconfirm point tenderness matches the expected ECRB or flexor-pronator origin before incision.
2. **Open lateral (ECRB) approach:** make a longitudinal or slightly curved incision centered over the lateral epicondyle, extending distally along the extensor mass. Incise the fascia and identify the interval between extensor carpi radialis longus (anterior, normal) and the extensor digitorum communis/ECRB complex (posterior, involved) — this interval is often marked by a visible color/texture change between healthy ECRL and the grayish, friable ECRB origin.
3. Elevate the extensor carpi radialis longus anteriorly to expose the ECRB origin. Incise longitudinally through the ECRB origin and inspect: pathological tissue is typically dull gray, friable, and lacks the normal glistening white fibrillar appearance of healthy tendon ("angiofibroblastic hyperplasia").
4. Sharply excise the degenerative tissue back to a bleeding, healthy tendon and bone margin — bleeding bone at the epicondyle is a useful visual endpoint indicating adequate debridement into a vascularized bed that can support healing.
5. Throughout debridement, stay anterior/superficial relative to the lateral ulnar collateral ligament, which lies deep to the ECRB origin and inserts on the supinator crest — do not carry dissection posterior/inferior to the equator of the radial head or deep past the ECRB footprint, as this is where inadvertent LUCL violation occurs.
6. If a formal footprint reattachment is elected (versus debridement alone), place 1-2 small suture anchors at the lateral epicondyle and repair the ECRB/extensor origin back to bone; otherwise, simply repair the split in the ECRL/ECRB interval with absorbable suture.
7. **Arthroscopic lateral approach (alternative):** establish standard anteromedial viewing and anterolateral working portals; perform a diagnostic survey of the joint, addressing any plica, loose bodies, or osteochondral lesions identified.
8. Identify the undersurface of the ECRB origin from within the joint (visible as capsular/synovial irregularity overlying the lateral epicondyle just proximal to the radiocapitellar joint) and debride it with a shaver or radiofrequency device, working from inside-out; take care to stay superficial to the lateral collateral ligament complex, which is deep to this working zone and must not be violated.
9. **Open medial (flexor-pronator) approach:** make a longitudinal incision centered over the medial epicondyle, staying anterior enough to avoid the ulnar nerve, which should be identified and protected (with a vessel loop for retraction/reference) before any deep dissection proceeds.
10. Split the flexor-pronator mass (typically between the flexor carpi radialis and pronator teres, or through the substance of the common origin depending on the site of maximal pathology) to expose the deep, degenerative portion of the origin.
11. Excise the degenerative tissue back to a healthy, bleeding margin, as on the lateral side; if the ulnar collateral ligament is visualized deep to the flexor-pronator mass, avoid violating it — stay superficial to the ligament plane throughout debridement.
12. Repair the split flexor-pronator fascia/origin with absorbable suture, or reattach to bone with suture anchors if a formal footprint takedown was performed for extensive disease.
13. If pre-operative ulnar nerve irritability was documented, perform in-situ decompression (release the cubital tunnel retinaculum/Osborne's ligament) or formal anterior transposition as planned, protecting the nerve throughout the remainder of the case.
14. Release the tourniquet, achieve hemostasis (particularly important near the ulnar nerve on the medial side), and close the fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers.
15. Apply a soft dressing and a light splint or sling for comfort in the immediate post-operative period; formal immobilization is typically brief given the goal of early protected motion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use the visible tissue-quality difference (glistening white ECRL versus dull gray, friable ECRB) as your primary intraoperative guide to the extent of debridement, rather than a fixed distance or percentage.
- Debride to a bleeding bone/tendon margin at the epicondyle — this is a reliable, reproducible endpoint that signals you have reached a vascularized bed capable of supporting healing.
- On the lateral side, keep dissection anterior to and superficial relative to the lateral ulnar collateral ligament at all times; if you are working posterior/inferior to the equator of the radial head, stop and reorient — you are at risk of the LUCL.
- For arthroscopic ECRB debridement, work strictly from inside-out and stay superficial to the capsule/LUCL complex; the intra-articular vantage point makes it easier, not harder, to stay in the correct superficial plane once oriented.
- On the medial side, identify and loop the ulnar nerve before any deep dissection, even when a transposition is not planned — having it tagged and retractable dramatically de-risks the rest of the case.
- Favor the arthroscopic approach whenever pre-operative imaging raises any suspicion of intra-articular pathology — it converts a single debridement procedure into a combined diagnostic-and-therapeutic case without a second surgery.
- Reserve formal suture-anchor reattachment for cases with extensive footprint takedown; for more limited debridement, side-to-side repair of the split origin is usually sufficient and avoids added hardware-related risk.
- Counsel patients pre-operatively (and re-confirm intraoperatively that criteria are met) that the natural history is favorable in most untreated patients — this keeps the surgical indication appropriately conservative and reduces the population exposed to operative risk without benefit.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Aggressive ECRB debridement risks inadvertent violation of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament, producing iatrogenic lateral elbow instability — a critical technical pitfall to avoid during lateral-sided debridement; stay anterior/superficial and avoid dissecting posterior/inferior to the radial head equator.
- Always examine the ulnar nerve pre-operatively (Tinel at cubital tunnel) with medial epicondylitis given the high coexistence of ulnar neuritis, and identify/protect it early intraoperatively before deep dissection on the medial side.
- Persistent/recurrent pain occurs in an estimated ~10-15% of surgical patients — counsel accordingly, especially since natural history without surgery is favorable in the majority.
- Reserve surgery for genuinely refractory cases (≥6-12 months failed conservative care) given the strong self-limited natural history (~80-90% resolve by 12 months regardless of treatment).
- Under-debridement (leaving grossly diseased tissue behind because the surgeon is reluctant to violate the "normal-looking" fascia) is a recognized cause of persistent symptoms — trust the bleeding-bone endpoint rather than stopping early.
- Failure to identify concomitant intra-articular pathology pre-operatively (missed plica, loose body, OCD) can leave a source of residual symptoms untreated after an otherwise well-performed extra-articular debridement.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Postoperative course generally mirrors the conservative rehabilitation framework, escalated once surgical healing allows: brief period of splint/sling comfort, then activity/ergonomic modification, progressive isotonic strengthening, and grip work.
- **Weeks 0-2:** soft dressing/light splint for comfort, gentle active ROM as tolerated, avoid resisted extension (lateral)/flexion-pronation (medial) and heavy gripping.
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive active ROM, initiate gentle isometric then isotonic strengthening of the extensor (lateral) or flexor-pronator (medial) group.
- **Weeks 6-12:** progressive resistance strengthening, grip work, gradual reintroduction of provocative/sport-specific activity.
- **Return to work/sport:** variable; post-surgical return to sport typically 4-6 months.
- Reported surgical success rate ~85-90% for symptom resolution.

Complications: persistent/recurrent pain (~10-15%), lateral elbow instability if the lateral ulnar collateral ligament is inadvertently violated during aggressive ECRB debridement, infection, incomplete relief.
