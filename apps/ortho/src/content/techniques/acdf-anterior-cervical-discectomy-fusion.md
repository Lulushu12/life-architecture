## Indications

ACDF is the workhorse for 1–2 level cervical radiculopathy, especially with concomitant kyphosis or central/paracentral pathology requiring direct anterior decompression. It is also a preferred approach for degenerative cervical myelopathy/OPLL limited to 1–3 levels, particularly with predominant anterior (disc-osteophyte or OPLL) compression and kyphotic or straight alignment, since posterior decompression relies on cord drift-back toward a lordotic curve and fails in kyphosis. Avoid ACDF alone for ≥4 levels (rising nonunion/dysphagia risk without a corpectomy strategy) or severe osteoporosis without an augmentation plan.

## Positioning & setup

Standard anterior cervical exposure and positioning principles apply across all anterior cervical procedures in this monograph: avoid excessive neck extension during intubation and positioning in patients with severe stenosis or myelopathy; consider awake fiberoptic intubation in severe myelopathy with unstable or high-grade stenosis to avoid a positioning-related cord injury. Consider baseline ENT/laryngoscopy assessment preoperatively in revision anterior surgery, prior contralateral approach, or pre-existing dysphonia, given cumulative recurrent laryngeal nerve risk with repeat anterior exposure. Intraoperative neuromonitoring (SSEP/MEP) is selectively used for severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery, or any case with a deformity-correction component.

## Steps

1. **Confirm approach selection** against alignment and compression pattern: anterior approach is favored for 1–3 level disease with predominant anterior compression and kyphotic/straight alignment, where direct decompression of the compressive pathology is desired.
2. **Perform discectomy and decompression**, including complete uncovertebral decompression for foraminal stenosis.
3. **Perform generous foraminotomy under microscope or loupes** when uncal spurs contribute to foraminal narrowing.
4. **Avoid over-distraction** during exposure and instrumentation — over-distraction risks adjacent segment strain.
5. **Perform meticulous endplate preparation** for the fusion bed before graft/cage placement and plating.
6. **For a wider single-level decompression need or long-segment anterior disease**, consider corpectomy with strut/cage reconstruction, or a hybrid ACDF-corpectomy construct, which balances decompression adequacy against construct stability; avoid ≥3-level corpectomy given very high graft/plate failure rates.
7. **For OPLL**, do not attempt to fully resect/float a densely adherent mass without assessing dural ossification risk (high CSF leak risk); for K-line-negative, high canal-occupying-ratio OPLL where posterior indirect decompression is predicted inadequate, combined anterior-posterior surgery or direct anterior corpectomy decompression is considered, accepting higher CSF leak/complication risk for more complete decompression.

## Pitfalls

- **Dysphagia**: most common early complication (20–60% short-term incidence depending on definition), usually self-limited by 6–12 weeks; multilevel constructs and prolonged retraction increase risk.
- **Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy/hoarseness**: usually transient traction neurapraxia; permanent injury is rare (<1%) but under-recognized without formal voice assessment.
- **Adjacent segment disease**: cumulative incidence roughly 2–3%/year, driving long-term reoperation rates — a principal rationale for considering cervical disc arthroplasty in appropriate candidates instead.
- **Pseudarthrosis/nonunion**: increases with number of levels fused and with smoking; smoking cessation should be mandated pre- and post-operatively. May require revision (anterior or posterior supplemental fixation).
- **Hardware failure/graft subsidence**: more relevant in multilevel/corpectomy constructs.
- **Epidural hematoma, CSF leak, vertebral artery injury, esophageal injury**: rare but serious; esophageal perforation is especially feared with prominent anterior hardware or revision surgery.
- **C5 palsy**: also occurs after anterior corpectomy, not exclusively after posterior surgery, though it is more classically described posteriorly.

## Post-op protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: soft cervical collar for comfort (not universally required — surgeon preference; many use it only for comfort/proprioceptive reminder rather than rigid immobilization for single/two-level plated constructs). Avoid heavy lifting (>2–4 kg) and no BLT (bending/lifting/twisting); no driving until off narcotics and comfortable without the collar. Swallowing precautions/soft diet if dysphagia present (common, usually transient 1–4 weeks, occasionally longer).
- **Weeks 2–6**: gentle active ROM as tolerated, gradual collar weaning, begin isometric neck exercises; wound check and assessment for delayed hematoma/dysphagia progression.
- **Weeks 6–12**: formal PT once early fusion stability is presumed — isometric progressing to isotonic strengthening, scapular stabilization, postural retraining. Flexion-extension radiographs around 8–12 weeks in select cases to assess early fusion progress.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive strengthening, return to most activities; confirm fusion (static + dynamic radiographs, occasionally CT if uncertain and symptomatic) before clearing high-risk activities.
- **Return to work/sport**: sedentary work ~1–2 weeks; manual labor ~6–12 weeks guided by radiographic fusion progress and symptom resolution; contact/high-risk sport is individualized, generally not before solid radiographic fusion (often ≥4–6 months), and relatively contraindicated in some contact sports even after solid fusion given adjacent-segment biomechanics.
- **Fusion rates**: single-level ACDF with plate ~95–97%+; rates decline with increasing levels and with smoking.
