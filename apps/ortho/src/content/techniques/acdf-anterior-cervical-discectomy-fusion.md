## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- 1–2 level cervical radiculopathy with concordant imaging, failed 6–12 weeks of conservative care (or progressive motor deficit sooner), especially with concomitant kyphosis or central/paracentral pathology requiring direct anterior decompression.
- Degenerative cervical myelopathy/OPLL limited to 1–3 levels with predominant anterior (disc-osteophyte or OPLL) compression and kyphotic or straight alignment — posterior decompression relies on cord drift-back toward a lordotic curve and fails in kyphosis.
- Cervical disc herniation with progressive/severe motor deficit or myelopathic signs.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- ≥4 contiguous levels via discectomy alone — rising nonunion and dysphagia risk; consider a corpectomy or hybrid strategy instead.
- Severe osteoporosis without a plan for augmentation (larger footprint plate, longer screws, or supplemental posterior fixation).
- Active local or systemic infection, unaddressed coagulopathy.
- Densely dural-adherent OPLL where attempted direct floating/resection risks a high-volume, difficult-to-repair CSF leak — favor posterior indirect decompression when K-line permits, or plan for a leak-management strategy in advance if anterior resection is unavoidable.
- Prior contralateral anterior neck surgery or pre-existing vocal cord palsy — not an absolute contraindication, but demands preoperative laryngoscopy since a second recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury risks bilateral palsy and airway compromise.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to define disc/osteophyte pathology, canal diameter, and cord signal; CT for bony anatomy, uncovertebral/facet osteophyte morphology, and vertebral artery course (especially in revision or anomalous anatomy); flexion-extension radiographs for instability and baseline cervical lordosis (C2–C7 Cobb, SVA).
- **Levels and construct**: mark levels precisely — confirm with intraoperative fluoroscopy/lateral X-ray against a counted reference (occiput or C2, not just "feels like C5-6") since wrong-level surgery is a recognized, largely preventable error.
- **Graft/implant selection**: allograft (structural fibula/iliac ring) vs. PEEK or titanium cage with local autograft/demineralized bone matrix; anterior plate (static vs. dynamic/translational) sized to levels involved without impinging adjacent disc spaces (keep plate edge ≥5 mm from the adjacent disc to limit adjacent-level ossification).
- **Corpectomy contingency**: if intraoperative findings (posterior vertebral body osteophyte, retrovertebral OPLL) demand wider decompression than discectomy alone permits, have strut graft (fibula allograft or expandable cage) and a longer plate available; plan against ≥3-level corpectomy given very high graft/plate failure rates — favor a hybrid ACDF-corpectomy construct instead.
- **Neuromonitoring**: book SSEP/MEP selectively for severe stenosis, OPLL, revision surgery, or any case with a deformity-correction component.
- **ENT/laryngoscopy**: baseline voice/swallow assessment for revision anterior surgery, prior contralateral approach, or pre-existing dysphonia.
- **Smoking cessation counseling** preoperatively — nonunion risk is substantially elevated in smokers and this is a modifiable factor worth addressing before consenting to fusion.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General endotracheal anesthesia; avoid excessive neck extension during intubation and positioning in patients with severe stenosis or myelopathy. Awake fiberoptic intubation is reasonable in severe myelopathy with unstable or high-grade stenosis to avoid a positioning-related cord injury.
- Supine position, shoulders taped caudally to improve lateral fluoroscopic visualization of lower cervical/upper thoracic levels, small bump/roll between the scapulae to allow gentle extension (only as much as the pathology tolerates), head on a horseshoe or gel donut, neutral rotation.
- Slight reverse Trendelenburg reduces venous engorgement/bleeding.
- Confirm the level with a lateral fluoroscopic shot before incision, marking the skin over the target disc space; a right-sided approach is traditional (some surgeons prefer left for reasons of surgeon handedness/RLN course debate — no strong outcome difference is proven either way, so use whichever side you are most experienced with).
- Free-running EMG leads placed if neuromonitoring is planned; baseline SSEP/MEP obtained before positioning changes and again after final positioning to detect any positioning-related signal change before incision.
- Prep and drape from chin to sternal notch, lateral to the trapezius border, with a transverse or oblique incision planned along a skin crease at the marked level.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and superficial exposure.** Transverse incision centered over the target level(s) along a skin crease; incise platysma transversely (or split it longitudinally) and develop the plane medial to the sternocleidomastoid and carotid sheath, lateral to the trachea/esophagus and strap muscles.
2. **Deep exposure along the avascular plane.** Bluntly develop the interval between the carotid sheath (retracted laterally) and the visceral column — trachea, esophagus, thyroid (retracted medially), staying anterior to the prevertebral fascia. Palpate the carotid pulse throughout retraction.
3. **Identify the longus colli muscles** bilaterally and elevate them subperiosteally off the vertebral bodies just enough to seat retractor blades under them — this protects the sympathetic chain (lateral to longus colli) and centers your exposure.
4. **Confirm the level** with a spinal needle or marker and lateral fluoroscopy before incising the disc — re-confirm again after any retractor repositioning.
5. **Place self-retaining retractor** under the longus colli bilaterally; avoid retractor blades riding up onto the trachea/esophagus, which is a major driver of postoperative dysphagia.
6. **Discectomy.** Incise the annulus, remove disc material with pituitary rongeurs and curettes down to the posterior longitudinal ligament (PLL), working from a wide anterior annulotomy toward the posterior third under loupe or microscope magnification.
7. **Uncovertebral decompression.** Complete bilateral uncovertebral joint (joints of Luschka) decompression with a high-speed burr and Kerrison rongeurs when foraminal stenosis contributes to the radiculopathy — this is frequently under-done and is a common cause of persistent arm pain if skipped.
8. **Posterior osteophyte/PLL management.** Thin the posterior vertebral body osteophytes with a burr; open the PLL with a fine nerve hook or Kerrison if central/paracentral disc material or OPLL is suspected to be trapped behind it, and remove residual disc/osteophyte from the foramen and behind the PLL under direct or microscope visualization.
9. **Foraminotomy.** Perform a generous foraminotomy under microscope or loupes when uncal spurs or facet osteophyte contribute to foraminal narrowing — decompress until the nerve root is visibly mobile and a nerve hook passes freely along its course.
10. **Endplate preparation.** Prepare the fusion bed meticulously: remove cartilaginous endplate down to bleeding subchondral bone with a curette or burr while preserving the cortical endplate contour (over-aggressive endplate violation predisposes to subsidence). Avoid over-distraction with the vertebral body distractor pins — over-distraction strains the facet joints and adjacent segments and can mask an appropriately-sized graft as too small.
11. **Graft/cage sizing and placement.** Trial sizers to select a cage/graft that restores disc height and segmental lordosis without overstuffing; pack with autograft/allograft or DBM as per surgeon preference; seat the graft under gentle distraction, then release distraction to load-share the construct.
12. **Corpectomy extension (if needed).** If wider decompression is required, extend to corpectomy with a high-speed burr, preserving lateral cortical walls as a guide, decompressing to the uncovertebral joints laterally; reconstruct with strut graft or expandable cage spanning to healthy endplates above and below.
13. **Plate application.** Select plate length to span the construct without overlapping adjacent disc spaces; contour to match cervical lordosis, provisionally fix with self-drilling/self-tapping screws, and confirm screw trajectory does not violate the endplate above/below or breach posteriorly into the canal.
14. **Fluoroscopic confirmation.** Obtain AP and lateral fluoroscopy to confirm graft/cage position, plate position, and screw length/trajectory before final tightening.
15. **Hemostasis and drain.** Achieve meticulous hemostasis, especially around the longus colli edges and epidural venous plexus; consider a small subfascial drain for multilevel or corpectomy constructs, particularly if any oozing persists.
16. **Closure.** Release retractors gradually to allow reactive hyperemia to settle and to inspect for bleeding; close platysma, subcutaneous layer, and skin (subcuticular closure is standard); some surgeons obtain a lateral X-ray in the room before leaving.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Keep the retractor blades directly under the longus colli, not on the trachea/esophagus — repositioning the retractor periodically during long multilevel cases reduces cumulative retraction-related dysphagia and airway edema.
- Deflate/loosen the endotracheal tube cuff briefly after retractor placement and re-inflate to a lower pressure — this is associated with reduced postoperative dysphagia and hoarseness in some series.
- Use a consistent counting method for level confirmation (count from C2 or the occiput on lateral fluoroscopy, not just visual gestalt) to avoid wrong-level surgery, particularly in patients with cervical ribs, transitional anatomy, or a short/obese neck.
- When treating OPLL or a large posterior osteophyte, thin the bone with a diamond burr until an eggshell remains, then remove the final layer with a fine curette or micro-Kerrison rather than levering — this reduces the chance of pushing fragments into the canal or against the cord.
- Slight under-distraction (rather than over-distraction) when trialing cage height tends to produce better segmental lordosis and less postoperative axial pain than maximal distraction.
- For multilevel constructs, alternate structural graft and cage levels only if biomechanically sound — mixing graft types across levels without a plan increases subsidence risk; keep the plan consistent across the construct.
- If a durotomy/CSF leak is encountered, address it primarily if accessible (fibrin sealant, muscle/fat patch, or direct repair if feasible) and consider a lumbar drain and head-of-bed elevation postoperatively rather than reflexively converting to a larger exposure.
- In revision anterior surgery, obtain a formal ENT laryngoscopy first — if a vocal cord palsy is already present on the side of prior surgery, approach from the ipsilateral (already palsied) side on Bettany's or a similar rationale to avoid bilateral palsy risk.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Dysphagia**: most common early complication (20–60% short-term incidence depending on definition), usually self-limited by 6–12 weeks; multilevel constructs and prolonged retraction increase risk. Manage with soft diet, reassurance, and swallow evaluation if severe or persistent.
- **Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy/hoarseness**: usually transient traction neurapraxia; permanent injury is rare (<1%) but under-recognized without formal voice assessment. New hoarseness beyond several weeks warrants ENT referral.
- **Adjacent segment disease**: cumulative incidence roughly 2–3%/year, driving long-term reoperation rates — a principal rationale for considering cervical disc arthroplasty in appropriate candidates instead.
- **Pseudarthrosis/nonunion**: increases with number of levels fused and with smoking; smoking cessation should be mandated pre- and post-operatively. May require revision (anterior or posterior supplemental fixation).
- **Hardware failure/graft subsidence**: more relevant in multilevel/corpectomy constructs; meticulous endplate preservation and correct graft sizing are the main preventive levers.
- **Epidural hematoma**: can present as delayed neck swelling, airway compromise, or new neurologic deficit — low threshold to open the wound at bedside/return to OR if suspected, as airway obstruction from expanding hematoma is a surgical emergency.
- **CSF leak**: from unrecognized durotomy during PLL/OPLL resection; repair primarily when possible, or manage with drain avoidance, lumbar drainage, and bed rest.
- **Vertebral artery injury**: rare, occurs with aberrant medial VA course or overly lateral dissection/uncovertebral resection; control with tamponade/hemostatic agents and vascular surgery or endovascular consultation — do not blindly clamp.
- **Esophageal injury**: especially feared with prominent anterior hardware or revision surgery; can present late as dysphagia, fever, or wound drainage — a high index of suspicion and early contrast swallow study/ENT-GI involvement is critical if suspected.
- **C5 palsy**: also occurs after anterior corpectomy, not exclusively after posterior surgery, though more classically described posteriorly.
- **Wrong-level surgery**: preventable with disciplined intraoperative level confirmation against a fixed anatomic reference.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–2**: soft cervical collar for comfort (not universally required — surgeon preference; many use it only for comfort/proprioceptive reminder rather than rigid immobilization for single/two-level plated constructs). Avoid heavy lifting (>2–4 kg) and no BLT (bending/lifting/twisting); no driving until off narcotics and comfortable without the collar. Swallowing precautions/soft diet if dysphagia present (common, usually transient 1–4 weeks, occasionally longer).
- **Weeks 2–6**: gentle active ROM as tolerated, gradual collar weaning, begin isometric neck exercises; wound check and assessment for delayed hematoma/dysphagia progression.
- **Weeks 6–12**: formal PT once early fusion stability is presumed — isometric progressing to isotonic strengthening, scapular stabilization, postural retraining. Flexion-extension radiographs around 8–12 weeks in select cases to assess early fusion progress.
- **Months 3–6**: progressive strengthening, return to most activities; confirm fusion (static + dynamic radiographs, occasionally CT if uncertain and symptomatic) before clearing high-risk activities.
- **Return to work/sport**: sedentary work ~1–2 weeks; manual labor ~6–12 weeks guided by radiographic fusion progress and symptom resolution; contact/high-risk sport is individualized, generally not before solid radiographic fusion (often ≥4–6 months), and relatively contraindicated in some contact sports even after solid fusion given adjacent-segment biomechanics.
- **Fusion rates**: single-level ACDF with plate ~95–97%+; rates decline with increasing levels and with smoking.
