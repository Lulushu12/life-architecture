## Indications

OA flare (any joint), inflammatory arthritis flare (adjunct, not primary therapy), adhesive capsulitis, subacromial impingement, trochanteric bursitis, de Quervain's, trigger finger, CMC1 OA, plantar fasciitis (judicious), epicondylitis (short-term only — inferior to PRP/eccentric loading beyond 6–12 weeks), carpal tunnel (bridge, not definitive).

## Positioning & setup

Site-specific positioning and entry points:

- **Knee (IA, superolateral approach):** Supine, knee extended, quadriceps relaxed. Entry ~1 cm superior and 1 cm lateral to the superolateral corner of the patella, directed posteromedially toward the intercondylar notch/patellofemoral joint under the patella. Alternative: anteromedial/anterolateral parapatellar approach with knee flexed 90° over the table edge (higher accuracy in some studies, useful when effusion present anteriorly).
- **Hip (IA, US-guided anterior approach — landmark technique is unreliable and should not be used):** Supine, hip neutral or slight external rotation. Linear/curvilinear probe placed longitudinally over the femoral neck to visualize the femoral head-neck junction and anterior synovial recess; needle in-plane from distal-lateral to proximal-medial, targeting the recess just lateral to the femoral vessels (identify and avoid the femoral neurovascular bundle medially with color Doppler before needling). Fluoroscopic guidance with contrast arthrogram is the alternative standard.
- **Shoulder — glenohumeral (posterior approach):** Seated or lateral, arm neutral at side. Entry 2 cm inferior and 2 cm medial to the posterolateral corner of the acromion, directed anteriorly toward the coracoid process. US guidance from a posterior short-axis view improves accuracy, especially in adhesive capsulitis where capsule compliance is reduced.
- **Shoulder — subacromial:** Seated, arm relaxed at side ("Crass position," hand on posterior hip, widens the subacromial space). Lateral approach: entry just inferior to the midpoint of the lateral acromial edge, directed medially and slightly posteriorly/superiorly under the acromion into the subacromial bursa. US guidance strongly recommended given the consequences of an intratendinous (vs bursal) injection.
- **CMC1 (thumb base):** Identify the joint by palpating the "anatomic snuffbox" distal recess or by traction/gliding the thumb metacarpal to open the joint line; entry dorsoradial at the joint line between trapezium and first metacarpal base, needle nearly perpendicular to skin, small volume (0.5–1 mL). Tight joint space — US guidance materially improves accuracy versus landmark technique.
- **Trigger finger (A1 pulley):** A1 pulley overlies the metacarpal head at the distal palmar crease (or proximal to it for the thumb). Needle introduced at 30–45° just proximal to the palpable nodule/pulley, into the flexor tendon sheath (feel for reduced resistance as the needle enters the sheath rather than the tendon substance), with injectate visibly tracking along the sheath. US guidance reduces intratendinous injection risk and improves single-injection success, particularly in diabetics.

## Steps

1. **Screen for red flags before proceeding**: active local/systemic infection, suspected septic arthritis (aspirate and send for cell count/culture/Gram stain before any steroid), uncontrolled coagulopathy for deep/vascular-adjacent targets, planned arthroplasty within the pre-op washout window, known allergy to injectate components, poorly controlled diabetes, or prior tendon rupture risk (avoid intratendinous injection, especially Achilles/patellar after ≥2 prior injections).
2. **Aspirate first if any effusion present** — improves accuracy, provides diagnostic fluid, and reduces post-injection pressure/pain.
3. **Select agent and dose** by joint/target: triamcinolone acetonide 40 mg knee/shoulder, 20 mg wrist/ankle/elbow, 10 mg small joints/tendon sheath (3–8 weeks effect); methylprednisolone acetate 40–80 mg knee, 20–40 mg subacromial, 20 mg small joints (2–6 weeks); betamethasone 6–12 mg (rapid onset); dexamethasone sodium phosphate (non-particulate) 4–10 mg — mandatory for transforaminal cervical/lumbar targets given embolic risk of particulate steroid near radicular vessels.
4. **Use image guidance** (US or fluoroscopy) for hip (landmark accuracy near 0% reliably), subacromial/GH shoulder in obese patients (landmark GH accuracy only ~65–80% blind), and any joint with a prior failed landmark-guided attempt. Landmark-guided knee accuracy is high (~70–90%) and does not mandate imaging.
5. **Inject peritendinous or bursal targets, never intratendinous**, for any soft-tissue/tendon-adjacent target.
6. **Mix with local anesthetic** for immediate diagnostic confirmation where relevant — pain relief within minutes implicates the injected structure; document the response as part of the diagnostic workup.

## Pitfalls

- Repeated intra-articular corticosteroid, particularly triamcinolone, has documented chondrotoxic effects — a landmark 2-year RCT (McAlindon et al., JAMA 2017) showed greater cartilage volume loss with quarterly triamcinolone vs saline in knee OA with no functional benefit difference. Steroid is for symptom control in flares, not a program of scheduled quarterly injections.
- Do not inject directly into tendon substance — peritendinous/bursal targets only; limit to ≤2–3 injections per tendon lifetime in weight-bearing tendons (Achilles, patellar, rotator cuff) given rupture risk.
- Do not proceed with elective IA corticosteroid within 3 months of planned ipsilateral THA (meta-analysis-level infection signal) or generally within a 4-week minimum before TKA (delaying beyond 4 weeks does not further reduce septic revision risk per registry data) — flag any injection within the planned pre-op window explicitly in the surgical planning note.
- Steroid injection frequency ceiling: generally ≤3–4 injections per joint per year; minimum 3-month interval between IA injections in the same joint.
- Do not perform blind (landmark-only) hip injections when image guidance is available — accuracy is unacceptably low and risks neurovascular injury.
- Do not treat presumed gout/pseudogout empirically with steroid in a new acute mono-arthritis without at least attempting aspiration and crystal analysis, given overlap in presentation with septic arthritis.
- Post-injection flare (~2–5%, crystal-induced synovitis from the steroid vehicle, self-limited 24–48h); skin/subcutaneous atrophy and hypopigmentation more common with superficial soft-tissue injections (lateral epicondyle, dorsal wrist) — counsel patients, especially with darker skin tones and thin subcutaneous tissue; transient hyperglycemia 24–72h in diabetics; facial flushing ~10% especially with triamcinolone; true septic arthritis after IA steroid is rare (~1:10,000–1:50,000) with sterile technique but risk rises sharply in an already-infected or bacteremic host.

## Post-op protocol

- **Phase 1 (Day 0–3):** Relative rest of the injected joint/structure for 24–48 hours; avoid high-impact loading and heavy resistance training in the treated region. Ice for symptomatic post-injection flare. NSAID use is not restricted after corticosteroid (unlike PRP/prolotherapy). Continue baseline analgesia (acetaminophen/paracetamol) as needed.
- **Phase 2 (Week 1–2):** Resume gentle active range of motion and low-load activities of daily living. Begin or resume formal physiotherapy targeting the underlying mechanical driver (e.g., quadriceps/hip abductor strengthening for knee OA, rotator cuff/scapular stabilizer program for subacromial pathology).
- **Phase 3 (Week 2–6):** Progress strengthening per underlying diagnosis; for knee/hip OA, emphasize closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining, and weight management counseling.
- **Return to work/sport:** Sedentary work typically same day to 2–3 days; manual labor/heavy lifting 3–7 days post steroid.
