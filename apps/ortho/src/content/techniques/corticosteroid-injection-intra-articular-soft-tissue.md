## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

OA flare (any joint), inflammatory arthritis flare (adjunct, not primary therapy), adhesive capsulitis, subacromial impingement, trochanteric bursitis, de Quervain's, trigger finger, CMC1 OA, plantar fasciitis (judicious), epicondylitis (short-term only — inferior to PRP/eccentric loading beyond 6–12 weeks), carpal tunnel (bridge, not definitive).

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Active local or systemic infection, or cellulitis overlying the planned injection site — absolute contraindication.
- Suspected septic arthritis — aspirate and send fluid for cell count/culture/Gram stain **before** any steroid; never inject empirically into an unexplained acute effusion.
- Uncontrolled coagulopathy or supratherapeutic anticoagulation for deep/vascular-adjacent targets (hip, spine) without a documented risk-benefit plan.
- Injection planned within the pre-arthroplasty washout window in the same joint (see below) — reschedule or use an alternative modality.
- Known allergy to injectate components (bovine-derived carriers, local anesthetic class, preservatives).
- Poorly controlled diabetes — relative contraindication; counsel on transient hyperglycemia (24–72h) and consider deferring elective injection if HbA1c is grossly uncontrolled.
- Overlying joint prosthesis with an unclear pain diagnosis — aspirate to rule out PJI before any therapeutic injection into a periprosthetic soft-tissue structure.
- Tendon rupture risk — avoid direct intratendinous injection, especially Achilles, patellar tendon, and after ≥2 prior injections into the same tendon; treat as a relative contraindication to a third injection in a weight-bearing tendon.
- Uncooperative patient or inability to maintain the position needed for a safe, accurate injection (e.g., hip injection requiring image guidance).

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the diagnosis and injection target clinically before booking — corticosteroid is a symptom-control tool, not a diagnostic fishing expedition; if the pain generator is genuinely unclear, plan a diagnostic block with local anesthetic mixed into the injectate rather than steroid alone.
- Review prior injection history for the same joint/tendon: cumulative injection count, interval since last injection (minimum 3-month interval between IA injections in the same joint), and any documented tendon injections (cap at ≤2–3 lifetime injections into a weight-bearing tendon).
- Check the surgical calendar: if arthroplasty is being considered or scheduled in the same joint, flag the case explicitly — avoid elective IA corticosteroid within 3 months of THA and within a 4-week minimum before TKA in the same joint.
- Select agent and dose in advance based on target (see table) and stock accordingly; for any transforaminal cervical or lumbar target, plan for non-particulate dexamethasone only — do not stock/substitute a particulate agent for these targets.
- Plan imaging guidance needs ahead of time: book ultrasound or fluoroscopy suite time for hip, subacromial/glenohumeral injections in obese patients, and any joint with a prior failed landmark-guided attempt.
- Screen for red flags at the planning visit, not on the table: active infection, suspected septic arthritis, coagulopathy, arthroplasty-window conflict, allergy, poorly controlled diabetes, and tendon-injection history.

**Agents and typical dosing**

| Agent | Relative potency | Typical joint dose | Duration of action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triamcinolone acetonide (particulate) | High, long-acting depot | 40 mg knee/shoulder; 20 mg wrist/ankle/elbow; 10 mg small joints/tendon sheath | 3–8 weeks clinical effect |
| Methylprednisolone acetate (particulate) | Moderate-high | 40–80 mg knee; 20–40 mg subacromial; 20 mg small joints | 2–6 weeks |
| Betamethasone (particulate/semi-soluble) | High | 6–12 mg | Rapid onset, moderate duration |
| Dexamethasone sodium phosphate (non-particulate) | Moderate | 4–10 mg | Shorter duration, but mandatory choice for transforaminal cervical/lumbar injections near radicular vessels (embolic risk with particulate steroids) |

## Anesthesia & Positioning

No general or regional anesthesia is required — these are performed under local skin anesthesia (ethyl chloride spray, or lidocaine wheal for larger joints) in a clinic or procedure room, with or without ultrasound/fluoroscopic guidance. Position and landmarks are site-specific:

- **Knee (IA, superolateral approach):** Supine, knee extended, quadriceps relaxed. Entry ~1 cm superior and 1 cm lateral to the superolateral corner of the patella, directed posteromedially toward the intercondylar notch/patellofemoral joint under the patella. Alternative: anteromedial/anterolateral parapatellar approach with knee flexed 90° over the table edge (higher accuracy in some studies, useful when effusion present anteriorly). The superolateral approach with the leg extended is the best-studied landmark technique, with pooled blind accuracy around 91%; a midpatellar portal has produced comparably high accuracy in some series (~93%) but more variability across studies — ultrasound guidance pushes accuracy for either approach toward 95–100% and is worth using whenever available, not just when a blind attempt has already failed.
- **Hip (IA, US-guided anterior approach — landmark technique is unreliable and should not be used):** Supine, hip neutral or slight external rotation. Linear/curvilinear probe placed longitudinally over the femoral neck to visualize the femoral head-neck junction and anterior synovial recess; needle in-plane from distal-lateral to proximal-medial, targeting the recess just lateral to the femoral vessels (identify and avoid the femoral neurovascular bundle medially with color Doppler before needling). Fluoroscopic guidance with contrast arthrogram is the alternative standard.
- **Shoulder — glenohumeral (posterior approach):** Seated or lateral, arm neutral at side. Entry 2 cm inferior and 2 cm medial to the posterolateral corner of the acromion, directed anteriorly toward the coracoid process. US guidance from a posterior short-axis view improves accuracy, especially in adhesive capsulitis where capsule compliance is reduced.
- **Shoulder — subacromial:** Seated, arm relaxed at side ("Crass position," hand on posterior hip, widens the subacromial space). Lateral approach: entry just inferior to the midpoint of the lateral acromial edge, directed medially and slightly posteriorly/superiorly under the acromion into the subacromial bursa. US guidance strongly recommended given the consequences of an intratendinous (vs bursal) injection.
- **CMC1 (thumb base):** Identify the joint by palpating the "anatomic snuffbox" distal recess or by traction/gliding the thumb metacarpal to open the joint line; entry dorsoradial at the joint line between trapezium and first metacarpal base, needle nearly perpendicular to skin, small volume (0.5–1 mL). Tight joint space — US guidance materially improves accuracy versus landmark technique.
- **Trigger finger (A1 pulley):** A1 pulley overlies the metacarpal head at the distal palmar crease (or proximal to it for the thumb). Needle introduced at 30–45° just proximal to the palpable nodule/pulley, into the flexor tendon sheath (feel for reduced resistance as the needle enters the sheath rather than the tendon substance), with injectate visibly tracking along the sheath. US guidance reduces intratendinous injection risk and improves single-injection success, particularly in diabetics.

Standard sterile skin prep (chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine, allowed to dry) at every site; no tourniquet is used. Drape only as needed to maintain a clean field around the entry point — full surgical draping is not required for an office-based injection.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Screen for red flags before proceeding: active local/systemic infection, suspected septic arthritis (aspirate and send for cell count/culture/Gram stain before any steroid), uncontrolled coagulopathy for deep/vascular-adjacent targets, planned arthroplasty within the pre-op washout window, known allergy to injectate components, poorly controlled diabetes, or prior tendon rupture risk (avoid intratendinous injection, especially Achilles/patellar after ≥2 prior injections).
2. Position the patient per the site-specific setup above and mark the entry point.
3. Prep the skin with antiseptic and allow it to dry; use sterile gloves and, for deeper/image-guided targets, a sterile probe cover.
4. Anesthetize the skin (ethyl chloride spray or a small lidocaine wheal) if not already incorporated into the injectate mixture.
5. Aspirate first if any effusion is present — improves accuracy, provides diagnostic fluid, and reduces post-injection pressure/pain. Send aspirate for analysis if there is any diagnostic uncertainty before proceeding to steroid.
6. Select agent and dose by joint/target: triamcinolone acetonide 40 mg knee/shoulder, 20 mg wrist/ankle/elbow, 10 mg small joints/tendon sheath (3–8 weeks effect); methylprednisolone acetate 40–80 mg knee, 20–40 mg subacromial, 20 mg small joints (2–6 weeks); betamethasone 6–12 mg (rapid onset); dexamethasone sodium phosphate (non-particulate) 4–10 mg — mandatory for transforaminal cervical/lumbar targets given embolic risk of particulate steroid near radicular vessels.
7. Use image guidance (US or fluoroscopy) for hip (landmark accuracy near 0% reliably), subacromial/GH shoulder in obese patients (landmark GH accuracy only ~65–80% blind), and any joint with a prior failed landmark-guided attempt. Landmark-guided knee accuracy is high (~85–93%) and does not mandate imaging, though ultrasound further improves it when available.
8. Advance the needle along the planned trajectory; if using ultrasound, keep the needle tip in-plane and visible throughout advancement rather than chasing it after losing the image.
9. Confirm intra-articular or bursal position before injecting: for IA targets, a low-resistance "give" as the needle enters the joint capsule and free aspiration of a small amount of synovial fluid (if present) are reassuring; for bursal/peritendinous targets, injectate should flow easily without the high resistance of an intratendinous injection.
10. Inject peritendinous or bursal targets, never intratendinous, for any soft-tissue/tendon-adjacent target — stop and reposition if injection meets high resistance or the patient reports a sharp, focal pain suggesting intratendinous placement.
11. Mix with local anesthetic for immediate diagnostic confirmation where relevant — pain relief within minutes implicates the injected structure; document the response as part of the diagnostic workup.
12. Withdraw the needle, apply a small dressing, and observe the patient briefly for any immediate reaction before discharge.
13. Document agent, dose, volume, laterality, image guidance used, and any aspirate sent, and counsel the patient on expected flare, activity restriction, and follow-up timing.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always aspirate before injecting into a joint with any palpable or visible effusion — it both improves diagnostic yield and materially improves subsequent injectate accuracy and distribution.
- For the knee, extend the leg fully and let the quadriceps relax completely before the superolateral approach — residual quadriceps tone narrows the patellofemoral space and is a common, fixable cause of a "bounce off bone" sensation.
- For subacromial injections, use the Crass position (hand on the posterior hip) to internally rotate and extend the arm — this measurably widens the subacromial space and reduces the chance of an inadvertent intratendinous rotator cuff injection.
- When using ultrasound for any target, identify and trace the relevant neurovascular structures with color Doppler before final needle advancement, not just at the start of the scan — vessel position can shift slightly with probe pressure.
- For CMC1 injections, gentle longitudinal traction on the thumb metacarpal while advancing the needle can open the joint line just enough to improve the feel of entry into a genuinely tight space.
- For trigger finger, inject slowly and watch/feel for the injectate tracking along the flexor sheath rather than ballooning focally under the skin — a visible bleb that doesn't move suggests subcutaneous rather than sheath placement.
- Warn patients proactively about the post-injection flare and expected timeline before they leave the room — a phone call about expected 24–48 hour discomfort is far more reassuring when it was predicted in advance than when it is not.
- Keep a simple per-joint injection log (date, agent, dose) in the chart — this is the only reliable way to enforce the ≤3–4 injections/year and ≤2–3 lifetime tendon-injection ceilings across a multi-visit relationship.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Repeated intra-articular corticosteroid, particularly triamcinolone, has documented chondrotoxic effects — a landmark 2-year RCT (McAlindon et al., JAMA 2017) showed greater cartilage volume loss with quarterly triamcinolone vs saline in knee OA with no functional benefit difference. Steroid is for symptom control in flares, not a program of scheduled quarterly injections.
- Do not inject directly into tendon substance — peritendinous/bursal targets only; limit to ≤2–3 injections per tendon lifetime in weight-bearing tendons (Achilles, patellar, rotator cuff) given rupture risk.
- Do not proceed with elective IA corticosteroid within 3 months of planned ipsilateral THA (meta-analysis-level infection signal) or generally within a 4-week minimum before TKA (delaying beyond 4 weeks does not further reduce septic revision risk per registry data) — flag any injection within the planned pre-op window explicitly in the surgical planning note.
- Steroid injection frequency ceiling: generally ≤3–4 injections per joint per year; minimum 3-month interval between IA injections in the same joint.
- Do not perform blind (landmark-only) hip injections when image guidance is available — accuracy is unacceptably low and risks neurovascular injury.
- Do not treat presumed gout/pseudogout empirically with steroid in a new acute mono-arthritis without at least attempting aspiration and crystal analysis, given overlap in presentation with septic arthritis.
- Post-injection flare (~2–5%, crystal-induced synovitis from the steroid vehicle, self-limited 24–48h); skin/subcutaneous atrophy and hypopigmentation more common with superficial soft-tissue injections (lateral epicondyle, dorsal wrist) — counsel patients, especially with darker skin tones and thin subcutaneous tissue; transient hyperglycemia 24–72h in diabetics; facial flushing ~10% especially with triamcinolone; true septic arthritis after IA steroid is rare (~1:10,000–1:50,000) with sterile technique but risk rises sharply in an already-infected or bacteremic host.
- Intratendinous injection is a technical error to actively guard against, not just a theoretical risk — high injection resistance or sharp focal pain during injection should prompt immediate repositioning rather than pushing through.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Phase 1 (Day 0–3):** Relative rest of the injected joint/structure for 24–48 hours; avoid high-impact loading and heavy resistance training in the treated region. Ice for symptomatic post-injection flare. NSAID use is not restricted after corticosteroid (unlike PRP/prolotherapy). Continue baseline analgesia (acetaminophen/paracetamol) as needed.
- **Phase 2 (Week 1–2):** Resume gentle active range of motion and low-load activities of daily living. Begin or resume formal physiotherapy targeting the underlying mechanical driver (e.g., quadriceps/hip abductor strengthening for knee OA, rotator cuff/scapular stabilizer program for subacromial pathology).
- **Phase 3 (Week 2–6):** Progress strengthening per underlying diagnosis; for knee/hip OA, emphasize closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining, and weight management counseling.
- **Return to work/sport:** Sedentary work typically same day to 2–3 days; manual labor/heavy lifting 3–7 days post steroid.
