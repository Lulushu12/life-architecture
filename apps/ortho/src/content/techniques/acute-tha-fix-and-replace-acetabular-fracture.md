## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications** — acute total hip arthroplasty combined with column fixation ("fix-and-replace") is favored over ORIF alone when:
- Severe articular comminution/impaction precludes a congruent, durable reduction of the dome.
- The pattern is both-column or transverse + posterior wall with marked osteoporosis and dome destruction.
- There is pre-existing ipsilateral hip osteoarthritis (the fracture is superimposed on an already-arthritic joint that would need arthroplasty regardless).
- There is femoral head injury (impaction fracture, chondral injury, or a fracture-dislocation with elevated osteonecrosis risk).
- Patient age/frailty is such that a second surgery for post-traumatic arthritis — which develops in a substantial proportion of elderly ORIF patients — would be poorly tolerated.

Contemporary evidence supports this combined approach in carefully selected patients ≥60–65 years with unreconstructible or high-PTOA-risk patterns.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- The fracture is reconstructible with good bone stock and a preservable dome — ORIF alone remains standard regardless of age, since outcomes depend far more on reduction quality and comminution than age itself.
- Active local infection or heavily contaminated/degloved soft tissue over the planned approach — stage or delay definitive arthroplasty.
- Physiologic instability precluding a combined, longer anesthetic — in the acute trauma patient this may argue for staged rather than same-anesthetic fix-and-replace.
- Very young, high-demand patients with reconstructible anatomy — arthroplasty durability concerns favor exhausting ORIF options first.
- Insufficient posterior column/wall or pelvic bone stock to support any cup construct — may require a more extensive reconstruction (cup-cage, custom triflange) beyond the scope of a routine fix-and-replace and should be anticipated preoperatively, not discovered intraoperatively.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: CT with 3D reconstruction is essential to characterize dome comminution/impaction, column integrity, and quadrilateral surface status, and to confirm this is truly an unreconstructible pattern rather than one amenable to ORIF alone — this distinction should be made preoperatively, not as an intraoperative rescue decision. Assess femoral head/neck and proximal femur for any deformity or prior implants that would affect femoral component choice.
- **Implant selection and templating**: template for an uncemented or, per recent RCT-level evidence in compromised bone, a cemented revision-type or dual-mobility acetabular construct. Have augments/buttress options available (a dedicated quadrilateral surface buttress plate, cup-cage construct, or jumbo/revision cup) in case intraoperative bone loss exceeds preoperative estimates. Plan the femoral component (often a standard primary stem is adequate; have a longer/revision stem available if proximal femoral bone quality is poor or if concurrent femoral pathology exists).
- **Liner selection**: plan for a dual-mobility or constrained liner given the elevated dislocation risk in this population from posterior wall/column deficiency and abductor compromise — decide this in advance rather than defaulting to a standard liner and reconsidering only if instability is seen intraoperatively.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: standard acetabular ORIF set (reconstruction plates, lag screws, quadrilateral surface buttress plate) plus a full primary/revision THA tray, trial components across a range of sizes, and cement if a cemented cup is planned.
- **Team and timing planning**: this is frequently a combined or closely staged procedure — confirm arthroplasty and trauma instrumentation, and appropriately experienced staff, are available for a single anesthetic when feasible, since combining reduces total anesthetic exposure in a frail patient population.
- **Medical optimization**: geriatric/orthogeriatric co-management preoperatively (cardiac risk stratification, anticoagulation reversal if applicable, nutritional status) given this population's vulnerability to perioperative complications.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia (occasionally combined with neuraxial/regional technique for postoperative analgesia) is standard; arterial line and large-bore access given the combined procedure's blood loss potential. Involve anesthesia/geriatric medicine early for frail patients — this population tolerates hypotension and prolonged anesthesia poorly.
- **Positioning**: dictated by the approach used for column fixation — lateral decubitus (or prone, per surgeon preference) for a posterior/Kocher-Langenbeck-based approach, which also conveniently allows a posterior approach to the hip for THA in the same position; supine for an anterior/Stoppa-based column fixation, which may require repositioning or an anterior-based THA approach depending on surgeon preference and fracture pattern.
- **Draping**: prep and drape widely enough to allow both the fracture-fixation exposure and the arthroplasty exposure without re-prepping; if repositioning between fixation and arthroplasty is anticipated, plan and communicate this explicitly with the team before starting.
- **Fluoroscopy**: set up for both fracture reduction confirmation (Judet views) and standard intraoperative AP pelvis/hip imaging for component positioning.
- **Landmarks**: mark the greater trochanter, PSIS/iliac crest, and ASIS as for standard acetabular ORIF exposures, plus standard THA landmarks (greater trochanter tip, anterior/posterior borders) relevant to the chosen arthroplasty approach.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Expose and reduce the fracture** using the approach dictated by the Judet-Letournel pattern (Kocher-Langenbeck for posterior-predominant patterns, ilioinguinal/Stoppa for anterior-predominant/both-column patterns) — see the acetabular ORIF approach-selection guide for exposure and reduction technique detail.
2. **Perform column fixation first**: restore the columns and posterior wall/quadrilateral surface with interfragmentary lag screws and neutralization/buttress plating, exactly as in a standard acetabular ORIF, with the specific goal of re-creating a stable, congruent bony platform capable of supporting an acetabular cup — perfect articular congruity of the native joint surface is not the goal here, since it will be replaced, but restoration of the columns' structural continuity and the quadrilateral surface is essential to avoid medial cup migration.
3. **Buttress the quadrilateral surface** with a spring plate or dedicated quadrilateral buttress plate whenever there is medial wall comminution or both-column involvement — an unsupported quadrilateral surface will not reliably support a cup under load, cemented or uncemented, and is a common mechanism of early failure if skipped.
4. **Confirm fixation with fluoroscopy** (Judet views) before proceeding to arthroplasty — it is far easier to revise a fixation construct at this stage than after cup implantation.
5. **Prepare the acetabulum**: ream conservatively and progressively, being mindful that the freshly fixed columns provide the structural support — avoid aggressive reaming that could displace fixation or perforate a thin, previously comminuted wall.
6. **Select and implant the acetabular component**: use a cementless (porous, high-friction) cup when host bone contact is adequate across a broad surface, or a cemented revision-type or dual-mobility cup when bone quality/contact is compromised — recent RCT-level evidence supports cementing in this specific compromised-bone setting for maximal early stability. Supplement fixation with multiple screws into the remaining native bone (ilium, ischium) where the fixation plates allow screw access, and consider augments or a cup-cage construct if intraoperative bone loss is greater than anticipated.
7. **Select the liner**: favor a dual-mobility or constrained liner given elevated dislocation risk from posterior wall/column deficiency and abductor compromise — this decision should already be planned preoperatively but confirm intraoperatively that the construct and soft tissue tensioning support the chosen liner.
8. **Prepare the femur and implant the femoral component** per standard THA technique for the chosen approach; a standard primary stem is usually adequate, but assess proximal femoral bone quality and use a longer/revision stem if there is any concern about the metaphyseal fit or concurrent femoral pathology.
9. **Trial reduction and assess stability**: check hip stability through a full range of motion, including the position most likely to be provoked by the fixation construct (e.g., posterior instability if a posterior wall deficiency was addressed) — this is the point at which to escalate to a more constrained liner if stability is marginal, not after closure.
10. **Final component implantation** and confirm final leg length, offset, and stability.
11. **Close in layers**, repairing the short external rotators (if a posterior approach was used) or the abdominal wall layers (if a Stoppa-based fixation exposure was used) as for standard ORIF closure; place a drain given the combined dissection.
12. **Obtain final fluoroscopic/radiographic images** confirming both fracture fixation position and component position before leaving the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Fix the columns to restore structural continuity and quadrilateral surface support, not to achieve perfect articular congruity — the arthroplasty replaces the joint surface, so the fixation's job is to give the cup a stable bony bed, which changes what "good enough" fixation looks like compared with ORIF alone.
- Do not skip the quadrilateral surface buttress in both-column/medial wall comminution cases — this is the single most common technical omission that leads to early medial cup migration in fix-and-replace constructs.
- Place cup fixation screws through the reconstruction plate or around it into remaining native bone wherever possible — added points of fixation matter more in compromised bone than in a routine primary THA.
- Have augments, a cup-cage construct, or a jumbo cup available even when the preoperative plan anticipates a routine construct — intraoperative bone loss during exposure/reduction in these comminuted patterns is frequently greater than CT alone predicts.
- Default toward a dual-mobility or constrained liner rather than a standard liner in this population — the threshold for "this construct is probably fine with a standard liner" should be high given the elevated baseline dislocation risk from column/wall deficiency and abductor injury.
- When feasible, perform fixation and arthroplasty under a single anesthetic in one position — this reduces total anesthesia time and blood loss in a population (elderly, often frail) that tolerates staged procedures poorly.
- Involve orthogeriatric/medical co-management from the preoperative period through discharge — this population's complication and mortality risk is driven as much by perioperative medical management as by the surgery itself.
- Counsel the patient and family explicitly before surgery about the elevated dislocation and infection risk relative to elective THA — this is a different risk-benefit conversation than a routine arthroplasty consent.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Dislocation risk after fix-and-replace THA is elevated relative to elective THA** due to posterior column/wall deficiency and abductor compromise — mitigate with dual-mobility or constrained liner selection, and confirm stability intraoperatively through relevant provocative positions before closing.
- **Contemporary fix-and-replace series report reoperation rates of roughly 8–9%**, predominantly for infection (~5–6%) or dislocation — counsel patients accordingly and set realistic expectations preoperatively.
- **Inadequate quadrilateral surface support** is a mechanism of early medial cup migration/protrusio — always buttress in both-column or medial wall comminution patterns.
- **Reserve this approach for unreconstructible or high-PTOA-risk patterns** — ORIF alone remains preferred when the joint is reconstructible, since reduction quality, not age, is the dominant driver of ORIF success (7-year joint survival ~80% with anatomic reduction vs. ~65–70% with imperfect reduction); performing fix-and-replace on a reconstructible fracture exposes the patient to arthroplasty-specific risks (dislocation, infection, revision surgery) unnecessarily.
- **Mid-term (minimum 5-year) comparative data favor fix-and-replace specifically in comminuted/impacted-dome patterns** — patient selection by fracture morphology, not a blanket age cutoff, is what the evidence supports; applying an age cutoff alone risks both over- and under-treating individual patients.
- **Blood loss and operative time** are additive when combining fixation and arthroplasty — anticipate and plan for this in frail patients rather than being surprised intraoperatively; have cell salvage available.
- **Femoral component malposition or leg-length discrepancy** can occur when the fracture reduction/reconstruction distorts normal proximal femoral/acetabular landmarks — use careful intraoperative measurement (contralateral limb comparison, offset/length checks) rather than relying solely on the distorted anatomy.
- **Infection** risk is compounded by the combined, longer procedure and often-compromised soft tissue envelope in trauma patients — meticulous technique, antibiotic timing, and drain use are important mitigants.

## Postoperative Protocol

Standard THA rehabilitation protocol, accelerated relative to ORIF-alone: weight-bearing as tolerated is typically permitted from the outset, since implant stability supersedes fracture-fixation concerns. Dislocation precautions are tailored to approach and construct — a dual-mobility or constrained liner may allow relaxed standard precautions. In comparative series, fix-and-replace is associated with earlier mobilization and lower rates of secondary arthroplasty conversion than staged ORIF-then-THA.
