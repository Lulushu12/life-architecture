## Indications

Both procedures are joint-preservation options for young, active patients strongly motivated to avoid or delay arthroplasty, and both are technically demanding niche techniques largely retained at specialized hip-preservation centers.

- **Vascularized bone grafting** (free vascularized fibular graft or vascularized iliac crest graft): larger precollapse or early postcollapse (ARCO IIIA) lesions.
- **Rotational osteotomy** (Sugioka transtrochanteric anterior/posterior rotational osteotomy, or angular intertrochanteric osteotomy): candidacy requires a small-to-moderate lesion, an intact posteroinferior (or anteroinferior) viable segment to rotate into the weight-bearing dome, and skeletal maturity (triradiate closed).

## Positioning & setup

Vascularized fibular grafting requires microsurgical setup for the vascular anastomosis. Rotational osteotomy is performed transtrochanteric and is largely practiced in Asian hip-preservation centers, which hold the most published experience with the technique.

## Steps

**A. Vascularized fibular grafting ("trap-door" technique)**
1. Harvest a free vascularized fibular graft (or use a pedicled vascularized iliac crest graft as an alternative).
2. Create access to the necrotic lesion via the "trap-door" technique.
3. Insert the vascularized graft into the necrotic defect.
4. Perform microsurgical anastomosis of the graft's vascular pedicle.

**B. Rotational osteotomy (Sugioka)**
1. Confirm candidacy: small-to-moderate lesion with an intact posteroinferior (or anteroinferior) viable segment and closed triradiate cartilage.
2. Perform a transtrochanteric anterior or posterior rotational osteotomy (or angular intertrochanteric osteotomy as an alternative).
3. Rotate the necrotic segment out of the primary weight-bearing zone, bringing the intact viable segment into the weight-bearing dome.

## Pitfalls

- Vascularized fibular graft donor-site morbidity: ankle instability, EHL weakness, sensory deficit, rare compartment syndrome.
- Nonunion of the graft or technical failure of the microvascular anastomosis are recognized failure modes of vascularized grafting.
- Rotational osteotomy is technically demanding and requires an intact viable segment to rotate into the weight-bearing zone — inadequate viable segment size or poor patient selection undermines the procedure's rationale.
- Both procedures convert to THA if unsuccessful; prior osteotomy in particular increases subsequent THA technical complexity due to altered proximal femoral anatomy and retained hardware.
- Vascularized grafting is technically demanding overall and has largely been superseded in general practice by core decompression + biologic augmentation given the morbidity/complexity trade-off, but is retained as an option at specialized centers for large lesions.

## Post-op protocol

The monograph does not detail a distinct phased rehabilitation protocol for these two procedures. Reported survivorship for vascularized fibular grafting in expert hands is 70–80% at 5–10 years in selected series — historically the best reported joint-preservation results for large lesions. General ONFH joint-preservation principles apply: protected weight-bearing during the early healing/decision window and serial surveillance imaging to detect interval collapse.
