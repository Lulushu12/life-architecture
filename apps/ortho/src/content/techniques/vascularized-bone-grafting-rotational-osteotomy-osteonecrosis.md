## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Both procedures are joint-preservation options for young, active patients strongly motivated to avoid or delay arthroplasty, and both are technically demanding niche techniques largely retained at specialized hip-preservation centers.
- **Vascularized bone grafting** (free vascularized fibular graft or pedicled/free vascularized iliac crest graft): larger precollapse (ARCO IIC) or early postcollapse (ARCO IIIA) lesions, particularly in lesions too large for core decompression to reliably prevent collapse.
- **Rotational osteotomy** (Sugioka transtrochanteric anterior or posterior rotational osteotomy, or angular intertrochanteric osteotomy): candidacy requires a small-to-moderate lesion, an intact posteroinferior (or anteroinferior) viable segment of adequate size to rotate into the weight-bearing dome, and skeletal maturity (triradiate closed).

**Contraindications**
- Advanced postcollapse disease with joint incongruity or significant secondary osteoarthritis (ARCO IIIB with substantial flattening, or ARCO IV) — neither procedure reliably improves outcomes once the joint surface is significantly deformed or arthritic; THA is indicated instead.
- Inadequate viable (non-necrotic) segment to rotate into the weight-bearing zone for rotational osteotomy candidacy — without sufficient viable articular cartilage to reposition, the procedure's biomechanical rationale fails.
- Active infection.
- For vascularized fibular grafting specifically: inadequate recipient vessel quality/caliber for microsurgical anastomosis, or patient factors that elevate microvascular failure risk (uncontrolled diabetes, heavy smoking, peripheral vascular disease) without optimization.
- For rotational osteotomy specifically: significant hip stiffness or pre-existing labral/acetabular pathology that would not tolerate altered femoral head-acetabular congruency after rotation.
- Relative: surgeon/center inexperience — both procedures have a steep learning curve and are best performed, or at minimum co-managed, at centers with specific experience; outcomes in less experienced hands do not match published results from expert centers.
- Relative: patient inability to comply with a prolonged period of protected weight-bearing, which both techniques require for graft incorporation/osteotomy union.

## Preoperative Planning

- **MRI (dedicated hip protocol)**: precisely define lesion size (Steinberg %, Kerboul/combined necrotic angle) and location (medial/central/lateral) — the extent and location of viable versus necrotic bone directly determines candidacy for rotational osteotomy (is there an adequate viable segment to rotate into the weight-bearing dome?) and informs planned graft volume for vascularized grafting.
- **CT** for precise 3D delineation of the necrotic segment boundary and subchondral bone quality, particularly useful in planning the "trap-door" access window for vascularized fibular grafting and in assessing the femoral neck-shaft geometry relevant to rotational osteotomy correction angles.
- **Vascular imaging/assessment** for vascularized fibular grafting: confirm three-vessel runoff to the lower leg (donor limb) with a pulse exam ± formal vascular studies if any concern, since the peroneal artery pedicle is harvested with the fibular graft and donor-limb vascular compromise is a recognized risk if runoff is inadequate; also plan and mark the recipient vessel (commonly branches of the medial femoral circumflex or lateral femoral circumflex system) for anastomosis.
- **Plan graft dimensions**: template the length of fibular strut needed to span from the lateral femoral cortex through the femoral neck into the subchondral necrotic defect, sized from preoperative imaging.
- **Rotational osteotomy angle planning**: measure the arc of necrotic involvement on preoperative imaging (anterior, posterior, and combined views) to calculate the degree of anterior or posterior rotation needed to bring a viable segment into the weight-bearing dome — this is a case-specific geometric calculation, not a fixed standard angle, and should be worked out preoperatively rather than judged purely intraoperatively.
- **Microsurgical team and equipment**: confirm availability of a microsurgical set (operating microscope or high-power loupes, microvascular instruments, appropriately sized vessel clamps and sutures) and a surgeon/team experienced in the anastomosis technique; this is typically a two-team (harvest + recipient site) approach to reduce total operative time.
- **Rotational osteotomy fixation implants**: a fixed-angle blade plate or angled compression device suited to the transtrochanteric osteotomy geometry, sized and templated preoperatively based on proximal femoral anatomy.
- **Discuss donor-site morbidity in detail with the patient preoperatively** (ankle instability, EHL weakness, sensory deficit) as part of informed consent for vascularized fibular grafting, since this is a healthy-donor-site procedure with its own dedicated complication profile.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard for both procedures given the length and complexity of the surgery (particularly vascularized grafting, which frequently exceeds 4–6 hours including harvest, preparation, and microsurgical anastomosis); regional block/catheter is often added for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position (vascularized fibular graft)**: supine with the donor leg (typically ipsilateral or contralateral per surgeon preference/vessel anatomy) prepped and draped separately and simultaneously with the hip, allowing a two-team approach — one team harvesting the fibular graft with its peroneal vascular pedicle while the second team prepares the proximal femoral access ("trap-door") and recipient vessels. A thigh tourniquet may be used on the donor leg during fibular harvest to improve visualization, released before pedicle assessment/graft transfer.
- **Position (rotational osteotomy)**: lateral decubitus position is standard for the transtrochanteric approach, providing access to the greater trochanter and proximal femur for the extended lateral exposure this procedure requires.
- **Fluoroscopy**: essential for both procedures — for vascularized grafting, to confirm the trap-door access trajectory and final graft position within the femoral neck/head under orthogonal views; for rotational osteotomy, to confirm the transtrochanteric cut level, the angle of rotation achieved, and final fixation position.
- **Draping**: for vascularized grafting, both the hip/proximal thigh field and the donor leg (if fibula) are prepped and draped as separate but simultaneously accessible sterile fields; for rotational osteotomy, standard extensile lateral hip drape from the iliac crest to mid-thigh.
- **Tourniquet**: used on the donor leg for fibular harvest (released before microvascular work); not used at the hip/recipient site for either procedure given the depth and extent of dissection required.
- **Surface landmarks marked**: greater trochanter and the planned lateral trap-door or osteotomy entry site for both procedures; for fibular harvest, the fibular head and lateral malleolus are marked to guide the longitudinal lateral leg incision, preserving a proximal and distal fibular segment (typically ~6–8 cm at each end) to protect the proximal tibiofibular joint and distal ankle mortise stability.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**A. Vascularized fibular grafting ("trap-door" technique)**

1. **Harvest the free vascularized fibular graft** through a longitudinal lateral leg incision, preserving proximal and distal fibular segments to maintain knee and ankle joint stability, carefully dissecting and preserving the peroneal artery and vena comitantes as the vascular pedicle along the length of the harvested segment, and protecting the common peroneal nerve proximally throughout.
2. **Prepare the recipient site concurrently** (second team): approach the proximal femur laterally, and create a cortical window ("trap-door") into the lateral femoral cortex below the greater trochanter, directed toward the necrotic lesion under fluoroscopic guidance.
3. **Debride the necrotic bone** through the trap-door window, curetting out the dead cancellous bone back to bleeding, viable margins, taking care to preserve the overlying subchondral plate and articular cartilage.
4. **Pack the resulting cavity with morselized cancellous autograft** (harvested locally, e.g., from the greater trochanter or iliac crest) around the planned graft position to support the subchondral plate and fill dead space.
5. **Trim the fibular graft to the templated length** and insert it through the trap-door and femoral neck tract into the subchondral defect, positioning it to provide structural support directly beneath the weight-bearing articular surface.
6. **Perform the microvascular anastomosis**, connecting the peroneal artery and vena comitantes of the graft pedicle to the prepared recipient vessels (commonly branches of the femoral circumflex system) using standard microsurgical technique (end-to-end or end-to-side, per vessel caliber match and surgeon preference).
7. **Confirm graft perfusion** after anastomosis (visual inspection of bleeding from the graft's cut cancellous surface, and/or Doppler signal) before proceeding to closure.
8. **Confirm final graft position** on orthogonal fluoroscopic views, ensuring the graft spans from the lateral cortical entry through the neck into the subchondral defect without articular penetration.
9. **Close the trap-door window/cortical defect** and the femoral approach in layers; close the donor leg incision in layers as well, with attention to fascial closure to reduce donor-site morbidity.

**B. Rotational osteotomy (Sugioka transtrochanteric)**

1. **Confirm candidacy intraoperatively**: after exposure, directly assess the size and location of the viable (non-necrotic) segment relative to the necrotic segment to confirm the preoperative plan remains appropriate before committing to the osteotomy.
2. **Approach via the lateral transtrochanteric route**: perform a greater trochanteric osteotomy (or a trochanteric-sparing anterior approach variant, per surgeon preference) to gain extensile access to the proximal femur and femoral neck.
3. **Perform the transtrochanteric osteotomy** at the templated level (intertrochanteric, below the femoral head-neck junction), completing the cut circumferentially under direct visualization and fluoroscopic confirmation.
4. **Rotate the femoral head-neck-proximal fragment** anteriorly (anterior rotational osteotomy) or posteriorly (posterior rotational osteotomy), depending on the location of the viable segment, by the preoperatively calculated angle, to bring the intact viable articular cartilage into the primary weight-bearing zone and rotate the necrotic segment out of it.
5. **Confirm femoral head congruency and joint range of motion** through the newly rotated position — take the hip through flexion/extension and rotation to ensure the repositioned viable segment articulates appropriately with the acetabulum without new impingement or instability.
6. **Provisionally fix the rotated fragment** and confirm the correction angle fluoroscopically before definitive fixation.
7. **Achieve definitive fixation** with a fixed-angle blade plate or angled compression device spanning the osteotomy, sized and positioned per preoperative templating, ensuring rigid fixation adequate for early protected mobilization.
8. **Reattach the greater trochanteric osteotomy fragment**, if used for the approach, with wires, cables, or a claw-plate construct.
9. **Close in layers**, with a drain per surgeon preference given the extensive proximal femoral exposure and expected bleeding from cancellous bone surfaces.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a two-team approach for vascularized fibular grafting whenever feasible (one team harvesting the graft while the second prepares the recipient site) — this materially reduces total operative and anesthesia time in a procedure that is already long, without compromising either step.
- Preserve adequate proximal and distal fibular segments (roughly 6–8 cm at each end) during harvest to protect the proximal tibiofibular joint and the distal ankle mortise/syndesmosis — inadequate distal preservation specifically risks postoperative ankle instability, the most functionally significant donor-site complication.
- Pack morselized cancellous autograft around the vascularized strut graft rather than relying on the strut alone to fill the necrotic cavity — the strut provides structural/vascular support, but the surrounding cancellous graft supports the subchondral plate and promotes broader creeping substitution across the defect.
- Confirm graft perfusion by direct inspection of bleeding from the cut cancellous surface immediately after anastomosis, before final positioning and closure — this is the earliest and most reliable check for anastomotic patency, well before a Doppler or imaging study could detect a problem.
- For rotational osteotomy, directly reassess the size/location of the viable segment intraoperatively before finalizing the rotation angle — preoperative imaging estimates are a starting point, but the intraoperative view of the actual necrotic-viable boundary is the definitive guide to how much rotation is truly needed and achievable.
- Take the hip through a full intraoperative range of motion after provisional fixation of the rotational osteotomy, before final fixation — this is the best way to catch new impingement or joint incongruity created by the rotation while it is still easy to adjust.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that both procedures are technically demanding, lower-volume techniques with outcomes that are strongly operator- and center-dependent — referring to (or partnering with) a high-volume hip-preservation center for either procedure, rather than attempting an infrequent case independently, is itself a technical/outcomes decision worth making explicitly.
- Have a clear, preoperatively discussed conversion plan (to THA) in mind for both procedures — since both are performed in younger patients with the specific goal of delaying arthroplasty, the intraoperative and postoperative threshold for "this is not working" should be defined in advance rather than improvised.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Vascularized fibular graft donor-site morbidity: ankle instability, EHL weakness, sensory deficit, and rare compartment syndrome are the recognized complications of the donor leg — meticulous preservation of adequate proximal/distal fibular segments and careful fascial closure mitigate but do not eliminate this risk.
- Nonunion of the graft or technical failure of the microvascular anastomosis are recognized failure modes of vascularized grafting — anastomotic failure in particular can be catastrophic for graft viability if not recognized and addressed promptly (early return to the OR for exploration/revision anastomosis if perfusion is in question).
- Rotational osteotomy is technically demanding and requires an intact viable segment to rotate into the weight-bearing zone — inadequate viable segment size or poor patient selection undermines the procedure's rationale regardless of technical execution quality, reinforcing the importance of rigorous preoperative and intraoperative candidacy confirmation.
- New femoroacetabular impingement or joint incongruity can be created by the rotational correction itself if the rotation angle is misjudged — intraoperative range-of-motion assessment before final fixation is the key safeguard.
- Both procedures convert to THA if unsuccessful; prior osteotomy in particular increases subsequent THA technical complexity due to altered proximal femoral anatomy, retained or removed hardware, and altered version/offset that must be accounted for in stem selection and templating.
- Vascularized grafting is technically demanding overall and has largely been superseded in general practice by core decompression with biologic augmentation given the morbidity/complexity trade-off, but is retained as an option at specialized centers for large lesions where decompression alone would be expected to fail.
- Prolonged operative and anesthesia time (particularly for vascularized grafting, given microsurgical anastomosis) carries the attendant general risks of longer surgery — venous thromboembolism, positioning-related complications, and increased infection risk from prolonged exposure — that should factor into patient selection and perioperative planning.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not detail a distinct phased rehabilitation protocol for these two procedures. Reported survivorship for vascularized fibular grafting in expert hands is 70–80% at 5–10 years in selected series — historically the best reported joint-preservation results for large lesions. General ONFH joint-preservation principles apply: protected (typically toe-touch to partial) weight-bearing during the early healing/decision window — extended for both procedures relative to core decompression alone given the more extensive bone work and, for rotational osteotomy, the need for osteotomy union — and serial surveillance imaging to detect interval collapse, graft incorporation, or osteotomy healing/nonunion. Return to full weight-bearing and higher-demand activity is generally staged over several months and confirmed radiographically (graft incorporation on MRI/CT, osteotomy union on plain radiographs) before progression, rather than by a fixed timeline alone.
