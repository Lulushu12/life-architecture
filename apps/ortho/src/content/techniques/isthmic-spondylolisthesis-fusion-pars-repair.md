## Indications

Persistent radiculopathy or a high-grade (Meyerding III–V) or progressive slip despite conservative care (core stabilization, activity modification, bracing in adolescents). Low-grade (Meyerding I–II) slips that are symptomatic and refractory to conservative care are candidates for decompression plus fusion, or for isolated pars repair in selected young patients.

## Positioning & setup

Posterior approach for decompression, fusion, and pars repair constructs. For high-grade slips, exposure typically extends from L4/L5 to S1, with iliac fixation available for long constructs or poor sacral bone quality.

## Steps

1. **For low-grade (I–II), symptomatic and refractory disease:** perform decompression if radiculopathy arises from foraminal stenosis at the pars defect/L5-S1, combined with instrumented posterolateral or interbody (TLIF/ALIF) fusion.
2. **Consider direct pars repair** (Buck's repair, Scott wiring, or pedicle screw-hook constructs) as an alternative in young patients with an isolated symptomatic pars defect, minimal disc degeneration, and no appreciable slip — this preserves motion at the segment rather than fusing it.
3. **For high-grade (III–V) slips:** perform instrumented fusion, typically spanning L4/L5 to S1, with iliac fixation considered for long constructs or poor sacral bone quality.
4. **Decide between partial reduction and in-situ fusion.** Partial reduction to improve sagittal balance and fusion surface area is favored by many surgeons, but aggressive full reduction carries a recognized risk of L5 nerve root traction injury and should be undertaken with intraoperative neuromonitoring.

## Pitfalls

- L5 nerve root traction injury is a specific risk during aggressive reduction of a high-grade isthmic slip — mitigate with intraoperative neuromonitoring whenever significant reduction is planned.
- The reduction-versus-in-situ-fusion debate persists without a single correct answer; document the sagittal-balance/fusion-surface rationale for whichever strategy is chosen.
- Direct pars repair is appropriate only in a narrow population (young, isolated symptomatic pars defect, minimal disc degeneration, no appreciable slip) — it is not a substitute for fusion once there is meaningful disc degeneration or slip.

## Post-op protocol

Follow the general instrumented fusion protocol: early mobilization with brace per surgeon preference in weeks 0–2, advancing walking/isometric core activation through weeks 2–6, progressive core/paraspinal strengthening from weeks 6–12, and functional strengthening/conditioning from months 3–6, with return to high-demand manual labor or axial-loading sport generally not before 4–6 months guided by clinical exam and radiographic fusion progression. Union rates for instrumented fusion in this population are reported in the 90–97% range in modern series; smoking, diabetes, osteoporosis, and multilevel constructs reduce union rates and should prompt more conservative timelines. For adolescents managed with a low-grade pars stress lesion in a brace rather than surgically, typical bracing duration is 8–12 weeks with serial clinical reassessment, and return to provocative sport only after a pain-free period and, where imaging was used to confirm healing (SPECT/CT or MRI), radiological evidence of lesion consolidation.
