## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** Persistent radiculopathy or a high-grade (Meyerding III–V) or progressive slip despite conservative care (core stabilisation, activity modification, bracing in adolescents). Low-grade (Meyerding I–II) slips that are symptomatic and refractory to conservative care are candidates for decompression plus fusion, or for isolated direct pars repair in selected young patients with a symptomatic pars defect but minimal disc degeneration and no appreciable slip.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications.**
- **For direct pars repair specifically:** any appreciable slip (even low Meyerding I with measurable translation), meaningful disc degeneration at the affected level (Pfirrmann III or worse), bilateral facet arthropathy, or an adult patient beyond the window where the disc is expected to tolerate continued motion — these findings favour fusion over motion-preserving repair, since repair does not address a degenerated disc or an already-mobile segment.
- **For fusion in general:** active systemic or local infection, uncontrolled medical comorbidity precluding a safe anaesthetic, and unrealistic patient expectations not addressed by pre-operative counselling.
- Active smoking is a relative, not absolute, contraindication but should prompt structured cessation counselling before elective fusion given its strong association with pseudarthrosis — document this discussion.
- Poor bone quality (osteoporosis) is a relative contraindication to instrumentation without additional planning — screen with DEXA/FRAX in postmenopausal women and older men, and consider cement augmentation, larger-footprint interbody devices, or pre-operative anti-osteoporotic therapy (teriparatide has the best supporting evidence) when surgery can be safely delayed.
- Aggressive full reduction of a high-grade slip is relatively contraindicated without intraoperative neuromonitoring, given the recognised risk of L5 nerve root traction injury.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** standing lateral radiographs to grade the slip (Meyerding), flexion-extension radiographs to assess dynamic motion at the pars defect/disc level, and MRI to assess disc degeneration, foraminal stenosis, and nerve root compression at the slip level. CT (thin-cut, with reconstructions) is the best study to characterise the pars defect itself (fracture morphology, degree of sclerosis/chronicity) and is essential when direct repair is being considered, since an established pseudarthrosis with sclerotic, rounded margins heals far less reliably than an acute or subacute lesion. SPECT/bone scan or MRI oedema (STIR) can help distinguish an active, potentially "healable" lesion from an inactive chronic non-union in younger patients being considered for repair versus prolonged bracing.
- **Slip percentage and sagittal parameters:** measure Meyerding grade and, for high-grade slips, pelvic incidence, pelvic tilt, and sacral slope — these drive the reduction-versus-in-situ decision and the extent of construct needed for sagittal balance.
- **Implant/graft planning:** decide fusion construct (posterolateral only vs TLIF/ALIF interbody) and level extent (single-level for most low-grade slips; L4/L5 to S1, occasionally with iliac fixation, for high-grade slips) before the day of surgery. For direct pars repair, plan screw trajectory and length off the pre-operative CT — pars screw diameter is typically 4.0–4.5 mm cortical/cancellous, length individualised to pedicle and pars anatomy (commonly 18–24 mm for a Buck-type trans-pars screw).
- **Neuromonitoring:** book intraoperative neuromonitoring (free-run and triggered EMG at minimum; consider SSEP/MEP) whenever meaningful reduction of a high-grade slip is planned, given the specific L5 root traction risk.
- **Iliac fixation:** for long constructs (multilevel, or extending proximal to L4) or poor sacral bone quality, plan iliac screw fixation pre-operatively and have the appropriate iliac screw set available.
- **Equipment/instrumentation:** standard posterior pedicle screw instrumentation set spanning the planned levels, interbody cage trial/inserter set (TLIF or ALIF sizes per approach), pars repair hardware (cortical screws for Buck's technique, cerclage wire/cable for Scott wiring, or a pedicle screw-hook/rod construct) as applicable, high-speed burr, and an operating microscope or loupes/headlight.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anaesthesia:** general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation; arterial line and additional monitoring access are reasonable for longer multilevel or high-grade reduction cases given the potential for greater blood loss and the need for stable haemodynamics during neuromonitoring.
- **Positioning:** prone on a radiolucent spinal frame (Jackson table, Wilson frame, or Andrews frame) with hips extended for the posterior approach when interbody/reduction manoeuvres are planned (extension helps with reduction mechanics at L5-S1) — this differs subtly from the flexed, lordosis-reducing position preferred for a straightforward decompression. Free the abdomen to reduce epidural venous pressure and bleeding. Pad all pressure points; protect eyes, chest, iliac crests, and peroneal nerves at the fibular heads.
- **Table setup:** confirm the table/frame allows unobstructed AP and lateral fluoroscopy across all planned levels, including the sacrum/iliac wings if iliac fixation is planned.
- **Neuromonitoring setup:** place EMG needle electrodes in the relevant myotomes (tibialis anterior, EHL, gastrocnemius, and others per protocol) before draping, and confirm baseline signals with the neuromonitoring team before incision, especially when reduction is planned.
- **Draping and imaging setup:** wide sterile prep from above the planned upper instrumented level to the sacrum/posterior iliac crests if iliac fixation is planned; confirm fluoroscopic (or navigation, if used) landmarks are marked on the skin before incision.
- **Surface landmarks:** mark iliac crests (approximate L4-L5 level), posterior superior iliac spines (S1/iliac screw entry region), and the midline spinous process line before prep, then confirm the operative level(s) radiographically once positioned and draped.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Time-out and level confirmation.** Confirm patient, procedure, and level(s) with a formal time-out and fluoroscopic/spot radiograph localisation before incision.
2. **Exposure.** Midline posterior incision over the planned levels; subperiosteal dissection of the paraspinal musculature off the spinous processes, lamina, facet joints, and transverse processes bilaterally, extending laterally enough to expose pedicle entry points at every instrumented level (and out to the iliac crest if iliac fixation is planned).
3. **For low-grade (Meyerding I–II), symptomatic and refractory disease:**
   a. Place pedicle screws at the planned levels under fluoroscopic (or navigated) guidance, confirming trajectory and depth with AP and lateral imaging before final seating.
   b. Perform decompression if radiculopathy arises from foraminal stenosis at the pars defect/L5-S1 — typically a laminectomy/laminotomy with facetectomy sufficient to decompress the traversing and exiting roots, which in isthmic disease are often compressed by fibrocartilaginous hypertrophy at the pars defect itself rather than by disc material alone.
   c. If an interbody fusion is planned (TLIF or ALIF), prepare the disc space (discectomy, endplate preparation preserving the subchondral bone plate, sizing trials) and place the interbody cage with graft material, then place and secure rods, confirming compression across the interbody space and lordosis restoration.
   d. If posterolateral fusion only, decorticate the transverse processes and lateral facet joints and lay autograft/allograft bone across the prepared bed.
4. **Consider direct pars repair** as an alternative to fusion in young patients with an isolated symptomatic pars defect, minimal disc degeneration, and no appreciable slip — this preserves motion at the segment:
   a. Expose the pars defect directly, debride fibrous/granulation tissue from the defect with a small curette down to bleeding bone at both margins, taking care not to destabilise the defect further.
   b. **Buck's technique:** create a screw entry notch in the caudal lamina roughly 10 mm lateral to the spinous process, angle the trajectory approximately 30° laterally toward the ipsilateral pedicle to cross the pars defect and gain purchase in the pedicle/vertebral body; drill the path with a 3.2 mm bit, then place a 4.5 mm cortical screw (typically 18–24 mm) across the defect without fully tightening.
   c. Pack autogenous bone graft (harvested locally from the adjacent lamina/decompression bone, or iliac crest if more volume is needed) densely into the debrided pars defect before final screw tightening compresses the graft.
   d. **Scott wiring** (alternative technique): pass a cerclage wire or cable around the transverse process and the spinous process/lamina at the same level to compress the pars defect, again after bone grafting the defect.
   e. **Pedicle screw-hook/rod construct** (Morscher-type): place a pedicle screw and a sublaminar or infralaminar hook connected by a short rod, providing compression across the grafted defect — this technique has been reported with the highest fusion rates among direct repair options in comparative series and is favoured when instrumentation is otherwise being used.
   f. Confirm compression across the defect fluoroscopically before closure.
5. **For high-grade (III–V) slips:** plan instrumented fusion typically spanning L4/L5 to S1; place pedicle screws at each level (and iliac screws if constructing a long fusion or if sacral bone quality is poor), decompress the neural elements (often significant foraminal narrowing at the slip level from the kyphotic "step" and fibrocartilaginous mass at the pars defect), and prepare the L5-S1 (or relevant) disc space for interbody support.
6. **Decide between partial reduction and in-situ fusion.**
   a. If in-situ fusion is chosen, accept the existing slip, place instrumentation and interbody support in the current alignment, and focus on achieving a solid fusion bed and neuroforaminal decompression rather than correcting translation.
   b. If reduction is chosen, perform it gradually and incrementally using the pedicle screw-rod construct (sequential rod contouring/cantilever, in-situ benders, or a reduction-specific screw/tower system) under continuous neuromonitoring, favouring **partial** rather than complete reduction — enough to improve sagittal balance, restore disc space height for interbody graft apposition, and increase the fusion surface area, while limiting the traction placed on the L5 nerve root.
   c. Watch neuromonitoring signals (free-run and triggered EMG, SSEP/MEP if used) continuously during reduction; stop and back off the reduction manoeuvre immediately if a significant, sustained signal change occurs, and reassess before proceeding further or abandoning further reduction in favour of in-situ fixation.
7. **Interbody placement (when performed).** Complete discectomy and endplate preparation at the slip level, place a trial to confirm sizing, then insert the interbody cage packed with graft material; confirm position and disc height/lordosis restoration on fluoroscopy.
8. **Final fixation.** Place and secure definitive rods, confirm screw/rod/hook construct integrity, and obtain final AP and lateral fluoroscopic images confirming instrumentation position, slip correction (if performed), and interbody/graft placement across all levels.
9. **Bone grafting of the posterolateral gutters.** Decorticate the transverse processes and lateral facets and pack with autograft (local bone from the decompression, supplemented with allograft/bone graft substitute as needed) or iliac crest autograft for larger constructs.
10. **Closure.** Achieve meticulous haemostasis, consider a subfascial drain for larger multilevel exposures, and close the fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin in layers.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- On CT, look specifically at whether the pars defect margins are sharp/acute or rounded/sclerotic before offering direct repair — a chronic, sclerotic pseudarthrosis heals far less reliably than a recent lesion, and this distinction should shift borderline cases toward fusion.
- When placing a Buck's screw, resist the temptation to fully tighten before graft impaction — tightening first leaves no room to densely pack the defect, which undermines the whole point of the repair.
- For high-grade slip reduction, think of it as incremental and reversible at each step — small sequential corrections with continuous neuromonitoring feedback, rather than a single large reduction manoeuvre, give you the chance to stop before a traction injury becomes established.
- Restoring even a modest amount of disc height and lordosis at L5-S1 through partial reduction meaningfully improves interbody cage apposition and graft-host bone contact, which is part of why many surgeons favour partial reduction over strict in-situ fusion even without full anatomic correction.
- In high-grade slips, the L5 nerve root is often already stretched and thinned by the deformity before you start — have a low threshold to accept a smaller correction if EMG activity appears early, rather than chasing a fully corrected sagittal profile.
- For adolescent/young adult isthmic slips being considered for repair rather than fusion, confirm the disc is genuinely healthy (Pfirrmann I–II) on MRI — even a mildly degenerated disc at the level predicts a less durable result from motion-preserving repair.
- When decompressing at the pars defect, the compressive tissue is frequently a dense fibrocartilaginous mass continuous with the defect itself (not simply disc material) — plan for firm, deliberate resection with a curette or small Kerrison rather than expecting it to come away as easily as ligamentum flavum.
- For iliac fixation, confirm the starting point and trajectory relative to the posterior superior iliac spine on lateral and Ferguson (angled AP) fluoroscopic views before final seating — a malpositioned iliac screw is a recognised source of prominent hardware and later pain requiring removal.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- L5 nerve root traction injury is a specific risk during aggressive reduction of a high-grade isthmic slip — mitigate with intraoperative neuromonitoring whenever significant reduction is planned, and favour partial over complete reduction.
- The reduction-versus-in-situ-fusion debate persists without a single correct answer; document the sagittal-balance/fusion-surface rationale for whichever strategy is chosen, and record neuromonitoring findings throughout any reduction manoeuvre.
- Direct pars repair is appropriate only in a narrow population (young, isolated symptomatic pars defect, minimal disc degeneration, no appreciable slip) — it is not a substitute for fusion once there is meaningful disc degeneration or slip, and attempting it outside this population predictably underperforms.
- Under-tightening or over-tightening a Buck's screw before graft impaction, or drilling a trajectory that does not adequately cross the defect and gain pedicle/vertebral body purchase, compromises construct stability and fusion rate — plan the trajectory off pre-operative CT rather than freehand.
- Surgical site infection and pseudarthrosis risks scale with construct length, number of levels, smoking, diabetes, and osteoporosis, as with any instrumented lumbar fusion — document smoking-cessation counselling and consider bone-health optimisation pre-operatively.
- Instrumentation failure (screw loosening/pullout, rod fracture) is more frequent in osteoporotic bone, long constructs, and at the caudal-most level of long fusions — consider cement augmentation or expandable screws, and pre-operative bone health optimisation, in osteoporotic patients.
- Wrong-level surgery is a specific risk in transitional lumbosacral anatomy (common in this population, since a lumbarised S1 or sacralised L5 can make level counting ambiguous) — confirm the level with intraoperative imaging referenced against the pre-operative studies, not by counting alone.

## Postoperative Protocol

Follow the general instrumented fusion protocol: early mobilisation with brace per surgeon preference in weeks 0–2, advancing walking/isometric core activation through weeks 2–6, progressive core/paraspinal strengthening from weeks 6–12, and functional strengthening/conditioning from months 3–6, with return to high-demand manual labor or axial-loading sport generally not before 4–6 months guided by clinical exam and radiographic fusion progression. Union rates for instrumented fusion in this population are reported in the 90–97% range in modern series; smoking, diabetes, osteoporosis, and multilevel constructs reduce union rates and should prompt more conservative timelines. For adolescents managed with a low-grade pars stress lesion in a brace rather than surgically, typical bracing duration is 8–12 weeks with serial clinical reassessment, and return to provocative sport only after a pain-free period and, where imaging was used to confirm healing (SPECT/CT or MRI), radiological evidence of lesion consolidation. Following direct pars repair, follow a comparable but often slightly more conservative course given the goal of achieving bony union across the defect rather than a broad fusion mass — obtain CT at 6–12 months if union is uncertain before clearing high-demand sport.
