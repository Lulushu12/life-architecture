## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Coughlin-Shurnas grade 1–2 hallux rigidus failing a reasonable trial of shoe modification, NSAIDs, and a dorsiflexion-blocking orthotic.
- Select grade 3 disease with a **preserved pain-free midrange arc** of 1st MTP motion — pain confined to terminal dorsiflexion, not present through the mid-arc.
- Adjunct **Moberg (dorsiflexion) proximal phalanx closing-wedge osteotomy** in borderline grade 2/3 joints where passive dorsiflexion remains functionally limited after adequate bony decompression, or in athletes/dancers who need more sagittal-plane clearance than cheilectomy alone provides.
- A positive, if temporary, response to an intra-articular corticosteroid injection supports the 1st MTP as the pain generator and lends some confidence that decompression will help.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Coughlin-Shurnas grade 3 with pain **throughout** the midrange arc, or grade 4 disease — cheilectomy alone is unreliable here; arthrodesis or a synthetic cartilage implant should be favored.
- Global joint-space loss with plantar or central cartilage involvement rather than a dorsal-predominant pattern — cheilectomy addresses dorsal impingement, not diffuse degeneration.
- Active infection, inadequate soft-tissue envelope, or uncontrolled inflammatory arthropathy driving the joint destruction (address the systemic disease first).
- Unrealistic patient expectations of a "cure" rather than symptom modification and motion preservation — counsel that cheilectomy does not reverse the underlying osteoarthritis and can fail with disease progression, at which point conversion to arthrodesis remains an option.

## Preoperative Planning

- Weight-bearing AP and lateral radiographs of the foot to characterize the dorsal osteophyte size, the pattern of joint-space narrowing (dorsal versus global — global narrowing predicts a worse cheilectomy outcome), and any dorsal phalangeal osteophyte contributing to impingement.
- Document passive 1st MTP dorsiflexion (normal ~65–75°) and explicitly record **whether pain occurs only at end-range or throughout the arc** — this single clinical distinction, more than the radiographic grade, predicts whether cheilectomy will succeed.
- Consider a diagnostic/therapeutic intra-articular corticosteroid injection preoperatively if the pain generator is in doubt; a clear, if temporary, response supports proceeding.
- Instrumentation: standard small-fragment power saw or osteotome/mallet set for the dorsal resection, rongeur, small curettes for any loose osteochondral debris, and a mini C-arm available in the room to confirm the adequacy and symmetry of resection before closure.
- If a Moberg osteotomy is anticipated, have small-fragment screws or staples and a sagittal saw available; template the amount of dorsiflexion closing wedge needed (typically 3–5 mm dorsal-based wedge at the proximal phalanx base) based on residual dorsiflexion deficit.
- Counsel the patient explicitly that cheilectomy is a joint-preserving, not joint-curing, procedure, and that conversion to arthrodesis remains available if symptoms recur or the underlying arthritis progresses.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- Performed under ankle block/popliteal regional block with sedation, spinal, or general anesthesia per patient and surgeon preference; regional block is well suited given the short operative time and benefits of avoiding early postoperative pain.
- Supine position with a bump under the ipsilateral hip if needed to bring the foot into a neutral, non-externally-rotated position for the dorsal approach.
- Thigh or calf pneumatic tourniquet, exsanguinate the limb with an Esmarch bandage before inflation; standard tourniquet time limits apply (generally keep under 1.5–2 hours, though this case is typically well under that).
- Prep and drape the foot and ankle free to allow full passive ROM testing intraoperatively.
- Mini C-arm positioned to allow AP and lateral fluoroscopic views of the 1st MTP without breaking the sterile field — confirm imaging quality before incision.
- Mark the dorsal midline incision (or slightly medial to the extensor hallucis longus) over the 1st MTP joint, and mark the palpable extent of the dorsal osteophyte percutaneously as a resection landmark.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Make a longitudinal dorsal or dorsomedial incision centered over the 1st MTP joint, staying medial to the extensor hallucis longus (EHL) tendon to protect it and the dorsomedial cutaneous nerve.
2. Develop full-thickness flaps and retract the EHL laterally; incise the joint capsule longitudinally in line with the skin incision, preserving a cuff of tissue on each side for closure.
3. Expose the dorsal metatarsal head and identify the dorsal osteophyte/exostosis, along with any impinging dorsal osteophyte on the proximal phalanx base.
4. Perform a synovectomy of hypertrophic, inflamed synovium as needed to improve visualization and reduce postoperative synovitis.
5. Using a sagittal saw or osteotome, resect the dorsal **~25–30% of the metatarsal head**, oriented so the resection plane restores a smooth arc into the remaining articular surface rather than leaving a flat shelf — a flat resection edge itself becomes a new site of impingement.
6. **Preserve the plantar articular cartilage** — this is a decompression of dorsal impingement, not a resurfacing procedure; over-resection destabilizes the joint and risks iatrogenic instability/transfer problems.
7. Resect any impinging dorsal osteophyte on the proximal phalanx base with a rongeur or saw, and debride loose osteochondral fragments from the joint.
8. Take the joint through a full passive range of motion intraoperatively; if impingement persists at end-range dorsiflexion, resect additional dorsal bone incrementally rather than accepting an inadequate result — undersized resection is the single most common technical reason for recurrent impingement.
9. Confirm resection adequacy and symmetry with fluoroscopy (AP and lateral) before proceeding further.
10. If dorsiflexion remains functionally limited in a borderline grade 2/3 joint despite an adequate cheilectomy, proceed to a **Moberg osteotomy**: make a dorsal-based closing-wedge osteotomy at the base of the proximal phalanx (typically 3–5 mm wedge), close the osteotomy to translate the distal phalanx into relative dorsiflexion, and fix with a small screw, staple, or crossed K-wires.
11. Recheck passive ROM and confirm no residual bony impingement with the joint taken through terminal dorsiflexion.
12. Irrigate copiously, repair the capsule with absorbable suture, and close the subcutaneous layer and skin in standard fashion.
13. Apply a light compressive dressing that does not restrict early motion; avoid a rigid postoperative splint that would prevent the early ROM protocol critical to this procedure's success.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Resect *generously but symmetrically* — surgeons more often under-resect than over-resect out of caution, and undersized resection is the leading cause of recurrent impingement and dissatisfaction.
- Contour the resection into a smooth curve rather than a flat cut; a flat dorsal shelf simply recreates a new, sharper impingement point at a slightly more plantar location.
- Take the toe through a full passive ROM arc on the table before closure — this is the best intraoperative check that the resection is adequate; do not rely on visual assessment alone.
- Address the phalangeal-side osteophyte, not just the metatarsal head — impingement is frequently two-sided, and leaving a prominent phalangeal osteophyte will blunt the benefit of an otherwise adequate metatarsal resection.
- Use fluoroscopy liberally intraoperatively; it is inexpensive reassurance that the resection is adequate and symmetric before the patient leaves the room.
- When adding a Moberg osteotomy, resist the temptation to over-correct dorsiflexion — excessive correction transfers load to the IP joint and can create a new source of discomfort with shoe-wear.
- Perform a thorough synovectomy; residual hypertrophic synovium is a common driver of persistent postoperative swelling and stiffness independent of the bony work.
- Start active/active-assisted ROM as early as tolerated postoperatively (within days, not weeks) — this procedure's success depends heavily on preventing the joint from re-stiffening into a new impingement pattern during healing.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Undersized resection** or progression of underlying arthritis causes recurrent dorsal impingement and incomplete relief — the dorsal resection must be adequate, not conservative to a fault.
- Selecting cheilectomy for a joint with pain **throughout** the midrange arc (grade 4, or a grade 3 that has progressed) is unreliable — reassess the Coughlin-Shurnas grade and pain pattern before committing to this procedure over arthrodesis/implant.
- Intra-articular corticosteroid injection preoperatively can help confirm the 1st MTP as the pain generator, but repeated injections lose effect and should not substitute for definitive surgical timing when indicated.
- Failing to address a concurrent phalangeal osteophyte leaves a residual impingement source even after adequate metatarsal-side resection.
- Over-aggressive resection risks destabilizing the joint and accelerating plantar-sided degeneration — the plantar cartilage must be preserved.
- EHL tendon injury or dorsomedial cutaneous nerve injury/neuroma from careless dissection — identify and protect both structures early in the exposure.
- Delayed initiation of postoperative motion allows recurrent stiffness/impingement to develop even after a technically adequate resection — this is a rehabilitation failure as much as a technical one.
- If a Moberg osteotomy is performed, malunion or over/under-correction of the dorsiflexion wedge alters IP joint loading and shoe-wear tolerance similarly to arthrodesis malposition.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bear as tolerated in a postoperative shoe **immediately**.
- **Early active ROM within the first 1–2 weeks** is important to prevent recurrent dorsal impingement/stiffness.
- Return to regular shoes by **4–6 weeks**.
- Return to sport/impact activity typically **6–8 weeks**.
- Reported success (satisfactory pain relief, procedure durability) is roughly **80–90%** in appropriately selected patients at intermediate-to-long-term follow-up.
- General return-to-sport gating applies: near-full pain-free active ROM, pain-free single-leg heel-rise/hop, swelling resolved, graduated activity progression — soft-tissue-only procedures like cheilectomy are typically cleared for contact/pivoting sport by 6–8 weeks, earlier than osteotomy/fusion procedures.
