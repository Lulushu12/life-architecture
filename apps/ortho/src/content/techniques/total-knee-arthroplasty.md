## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Multicompartmental or inflammatory OA, failed non-operative management and/or failed joint-preserving surgery (HTO/UKA), fixed deformity, ligamentous insufficiency incompatible with UKA/HTO, or diffuse KL4 disease. Also the default for inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic, crystal), active/recent joint sepsis or uncontrolled infection risk factors ruling out compartment-sparing surgery, and fixed varus/valgus deformity not passively correctable, flexion contracture >15–20°, or arc of motion <90°.

**Contraindications**:
- Active local or systemic infection (uncontrolled sepsis, active skin/soft-tissue infection over the operative field).
- Extensor mechanism disruption that cannot be reconstructed concurrently.
- Neuropathic (Charcot) joint without stabilization plan.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease precluding safe tourniquet use and wound healing, uncorrected.
- Medical instability precluding anesthesia (relative — optimize rather than absolutely exclude where possible).

**Relative contraindications requiring pre-operative optimization** (see Preoperative Planning and Pitfalls): uncontrolled diabetes (target HbA1c <7.5–8%), active smoking, morbid obesity (soft threshold BMI ≥40 commonly used to trigger a weight-optimization discussion), and recent intra-articular corticosteroid injection (many surgeons enforce a ≥3-month washout given signals of increased early PJI risk).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Full-length hip-to-ankle standing alignment films** are mandatory regardless of alignment philosophy chosen — measure HKA, MPTA, LDFA, and JLCA, and determine the patient's constitutional CPAK phenotype if using kinematic/restricted-kinematic/functional alignment.
- **CT-based 3D bone modeling** increasingly used pre-operatively for robotic-assisted planning — generates a 3D bone model for implant sizing, rotational alignment assessment (transepicondylar axis, posterior condylar axis, Whiteside's line), and simulated gap-balance planning before the incision is made.
- **Alignment philosophy decision made pre-operatively** (see Step-by-Step Technique) — mechanical, kinematic, restricted kinematic, or functional alignment — documented in the plan since it changes both cutting-guide setup and intra-operative balancing strategy.
- **Implant selection**: bearing/constraint philosophy (CR vs PS vs medial-pivot/ultracongruent) and fixation method (cemented, cementless, or hybrid) should be decided pre-operatively based on bone quality, deformity magnitude, and patient age/activity, with the operating surgeon's default augmented by case-specific factors (e.g., cementless favored in younger higher-bone-quality patients; cemented preferred in osteoporotic or markedly deformed proximal tibiae).
- **Instrumentation/equipment**: conventional jig-based instrumentation, or robotic arm/navigation system with registration arrays if robotic-assisted; full range of trial femoral/tibial/insert sizes; augments and stems available for unexpected bone loss; a full range of insert thicknesses for gap-balancing contingencies.
- **Patellar resurfacing decision**: pre-operatively assess patellofemoral disease severity and surgeon/practice pattern (near-universal resurfacing in the US, more selective in parts of Europe) — resurfacing modestly reduces secondary patellar resurfacing/anterior knee pain revision in patients with pre-operative patellofemoral arthritis.
- **Pre-operative medical optimization**: HbA1c, smoking cessation counseling, corticosteroid injection washout interval, and BMI optimization discussion as indicated (see Pitfalls).
- **Staged vs simultaneous bilateral TKA decision**, if applicable, factoring age and cardiac comorbidity into the plan before the day of surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: spinal or general anesthesia, typically combined with a peripheral nerve block (adductor canal block preferred over femoral nerve block to preserve quadriceps strength for same-day mobilization) and/or peri-articular local infiltration analgesia as part of a multimodal, opioid-sparing protocol.
- **Positioning**: supine on the operating table with a well-padded lateral thigh post (or a dedicated leg-positioning device) to allow the knee to be held in flexion for exposure and cutting, and a distal foot support that allows the knee to hang free in ~90° flexion. A tourniquet is applied high on the thigh after limb exsanguination (Esmarch or elevation).
- **Tourniquet strategy**: many contemporary protocols limit tourniquet time or use it selectively (e.g., inflated only for cementing) to reduce quadriceps deconditioning and post-operative pain, though full-case tourniquet use remains common and acceptable.
- **Robotic/navigation setup, if used**: place tracking pins in the femur and tibia at sites that will not interfere with the planned implant or incision, then perform bony registration and a range-of-motion/laxity assessment before final cutting-block placement — this step is what allows real-time gap-balance simulation described under alignment philosophy.
- **Draping**: standard extremity drape from mid-thigh (above the tourniquet) to the foot, with a stockinette and adhesive drape sealing the field; ensure the leg can be freely flexed/extended and stressed in varus/valgus through the drape for intra-operative balancing checks.
- **Surface landmarks marked pre-incision**: midline or slightly medial parapatellar incision line from proximal pole of patella to a point just medial to the tibial tubercle, patellar borders, joint line, and the planned medial parapatellar (or subvastus/midvastus, per surgeon preference) arthrotomy line.
- Fluoroscopy is not routinely required for conventional jig-based TKA but is integral to some navigation/robotic platforms per the specific system's workflow.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and exposure**: make a midline (or slightly medial) skin incision from the superior pole of the patella to just medial to the tibial tubercle. Perform a medial parapatellar arthrotomy (most common; extensile and versatile) or a subvastus/midvastus approach (quadriceps-sparing, potentially faster early quad recovery, technically more limited exposure), then evert or subluxate the patella laterally and flex the knee to expose the joint.
2. **Assess deformity and balancing drivers under direct vision/exam**: confirm flexion contracture and coronal laxity intra-operatively, since these drive balancing strategy more than the static pre-operative radiograph alone; remove osteophytes early (they cause false soft-tissue tightness and post-operative impingement if left).
3. **Perform the initial bone resections per the chosen alignment philosophy**:
   - **Mechanical alignment (MA)**: cut the distal femur and proximal tibia each perpendicular to their respective mechanical axes (using intramedullary femoral and extramedullary or intramedullary tibial guides), targeting a neutral (180° ± 3°) overall HKA regardless of native anatomy; ligament releases are used as needed to balance the resulting gaps.
   - **Kinematic alignment (KA)**: reference pre-arthritic femoral and tibial cut planes (accounting for cartilage wear) to restore the patient's native joint line and rotational axes without a fixed target, aiming to reproduce natural soft-tissue tension and minimize releases.
   - **Restricted kinematic (rKA) / functional alignment (FA)**: restore native joint-line obliquity (rKA) or prioritize real-time balanced flexion/extension gaps (FA, typically robotic-enabled), but cap any single parameter's deviation from neutral (commonly ±5° boundaries on HKA, MPTA, LDFA) to stay within a safe zone — plan and audit using CPAK phenotyping.
4. **Size and rotate the femoral component**: use posterior condylar axis, transepicondylar axis, and Whiteside's (AP) line as cross-checks for femoral rotation regardless of alignment philosophy — discrepancy between these references (common in a valgus or dysplastic knee) should prompt re-evaluation rather than blind reliance on a single landmark.
5. **Complete the tibial cut**, matching slope and resection depth to the pre-operative plan and chosen alignment target; a conservative, minimal resection preserves bone stock and avoids over-resection-driven instability.
6. **Trial components and assess flexion/extension gaps**: insert trial femoral and tibial components with a trial insert, and assess gap symmetry and balance in extension and at 90° flexion using spacer blocks, laminar spreaders, or a tensioning device. Apply the core gap-balancing principles: distal femoral resection/augmentation affects the extension gap only; femoral component sizing (AP dimension) affects the flexion gap only; proximal tibial resection or insert thickness affects both gaps symmetrically. Use these levers, rather than indiscriminate soft-tissue release, to achieve two congruent, appropriately tensioned rectangles in flexion and extension.
7. **Balance soft tissue as needed**: perform sequential, incremental medial or lateral releases (deep MCL, posteromedial corner, posterolateral corner/ITB, as indicated) only as needed to correct residual coronal imbalance after bony-lever adjustments — over-release is harder to correct than under-release, so proceed incrementally and re-check gaps after each step. Watch specifically for mid-flexion instability, which can result from excessive distal femoral resection in a knee with a pre-operative flexion contracture as the posterior capsule becomes lax once the contracture is released.
8. **Select and trial bearing/constraint**: CR requires a competent, well-balanced PCL; PS substitutes a cam-post mechanism and is more forgiving in PCL-deficient or over-released knees; medial-pivot/ultracongruent designs aim to reproduce more natural kinematics and are increasingly paired with kinematic/functional alignment techniques. Confirm full range of motion without cam-post impingement (PS) or posterior instability (CR) with trials in place.
9. **Address the patella**: evert or sublux, resurface if planned (resect to restore native patellar thickness, avoid over- or under-resection, and center the component to avoid lateral overhang), and assess tracking through range of motion — perform a lateral retinacular release only if tracking remains abnormal after confirming correct femoral/tibial component rotation, not as a default step.
10. **Final trial reduction and range-of-motion check**: take the knee through full flexion-extension, assess coronal stability at 0° and 30° with varus/valgus stress, confirm patellar tracking, and reconfirm the overall alignment (fluoroscopically or via navigation/robotic readout) matches the planned target before committing to final components.
11. **Cement or press-fit final components**: for cemented fixation, pulse-lavage and dry the bone surfaces thoroughly, apply cement in a controlled, pressurized technique to all cut surfaces, and remove extruded cement (especially posteriorly) before it cures. For cementless fixation, confirm rigid mechanical press-fit of femoral and tibial components; note that most surgeons still cement the patellar button even in an otherwise cementless (hybrid) construct given a signal of cementless patellar component migration in comparative data.
12. **Final wound closure**: release the tourniquet (if inflated) to check hemostasis before or during closure, achieve meticulous hemostasis, consider a drain per surgeon preference, and close in layers (capsule, subcutaneous, skin) with attention to patellar tracking maintained through closure of the arthrotomy.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Cross-check femoral rotation against at least two independent landmarks (posterior condylar axis, transepicondylar axis, Whiteside's line) rather than trusting one alone — they frequently disagree in valgus or dysplastic knees, and reconciling the discrepancy up front prevents a rotationally malpositioned component that later causes flexion instability or patellar maltracking.
- Remove peripheral osteophytes before assessing any gap — residual osteophytes create false soft-tissue tightness during trialing and are a preventable cause of post-operative impingement pain and stiffness.
- Use the correct bony lever for the specific gap problem: distal femoral resection/augment for extension-only imbalance, femoral component AP sizing for flexion-only imbalance, and tibial resection/insert thickness for a gap that is tight or loose symmetrically in both flexion and extension — reaching for a soft-tissue release when a bony-lever adjustment would fix the problem is a common inefficiency that increases release-related instability risk.
- Release soft tissue incrementally and re-check the gap after each step, rather than performing a large release up front — under-release can be extended, but over-release cannot be easily reversed, and iterative small releases with re-measurement give the most controllable, reproducible endpoint.
- Watch specifically for mid-flexion instability in knees with a significant pre-operative flexion contracture — aggressive distal femoral over-resection to "fully correct" the contracture can leave the posterior capsule lax once the contracture releases, producing a knee that is stable in full extension and 90° flexion trial positions but unstable in between.
- When using robotic or navigated platforms, use the intra-operative gap-balance simulation to compare planned bone cuts against predicted gaps before committing to any cut — this is the main practical advantage robotics offers over conventional instrumentation, since it lets you adjust the plan pre-emptively rather than reactively after cutting.
- Be deliberate, not reflexive, about lateral retinacular release for patellar tracking — first confirm femoral and tibial component rotation are correct, since malrotation is a common root cause of tracking problems that a retinacular release alone will not fix and may worsen (devascularizing the patella).
- In cementless technique, prioritize a genuinely rigid mechanical press-fit on both femoral and tibial sides before proceeding — inadequate initial stability is the technical root cause behind most cementless-specific early failures, and this is a moment to slow down and confirm, not assume.
- Document and report the achieved CPAK phenotype (or final HKA/MPTA/LDFA) post-operatively when using kinematic/restricted-kinematic techniques — this creates a feedback loop for auditing whether your intended alignment strategy was actually reproduced, since current evidence suggests some post-operative CPAK "drift" from the pre-operative plan does not clearly worsen outcomes but should still be tracked.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI)** is the leading cause of early revision, incidence ~1–2% in primary TKA, higher with diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, or prior knee surgery; diagnosis follows MSIS/ICM criteria. Broad treatment-selection logic:

| Scenario | Typical strategy |
|---|---|
| Acute (<4–6 weeks from index surgery or acute hematogenous, stable implant, susceptible organism) | DAIR (debridement, antibiotics, implant retention) with modular component exchange |
| Chronic infection, well-fixed components, favorable host/organism | One-stage revision (increasingly favored in select European centers with strong culture-directed antibiotic protocols) |
| Chronic infection, difficult organism, compromised host, loose components | Two-stage revision (antibiotic spacer, organism-directed IV antibiotics, reimplantation once cleared) — remains the reference standard in most of the US and much of Europe for chronic PJI |

- **Aseptic loosening** — technical root causes include malalignment, imbalanced gaps, and inadequate cement/press-fit technique; minimize by confirming rigid fixation at the time of implantation and adhering to alignment targets.
- **Stiffness/arthrofibrosis** — manipulation under anesthesia typically considered if <90° flexion by 6–12 weeks; early recognition and aggressive PT engagement in the first weeks reduce the need for later intervention.
- **Instability** (flexion or extension gap mismatch) — traced back to an inadequately balanced gap at the index procedure; the gap-balancing sequence in Step-by-Step Technique is the primary prevention.
- **Periprosthetic fracture** — risk factors include osteoporotic bone, notching of the anterior femoral cortex during femoral preparation (avoid by careful cutting-block placement), and stem-tip stress risers in revision-type constructs.
- **Extensor mechanism disruption** (patellar tendon/quadriceps tendon rupture, patellar fracture) — avoid excessive patellar eversion tension, protect the tendon insertions during exposure, and avoid over-resection of the patella.
- **Venous thromboembolism** — prophylaxis per AAOS/ACCP guidance; aspirin is now supported as adequate prophylaxis for standard-risk patients in most contemporary protocols, reserving more potent anticoagulation (LMWH, DOACs) for elevated-risk patients.
- **Persistent unexplained pain** — roughly 10–20% of patients report residual dissatisfaction despite a technically well-functioning implant, underscoring the ongoing alignment-philosophy debate and the importance of managing pre-operative expectations.
- **Pre-operative optimization pitfalls**: uncontrolled diabetes (target HbA1c <7.5–8%), active smoking (cessation recommended ≥4 weeks pre-op), and a recent intra-articular corticosteroid injection (many surgeons enforce a ≥3-month washout given signals of increased early PJI risk) are modifiable risk factors that should be addressed before elective TKA. BMI is a modifiable risk factor for PJI, wound complications, and revision (soft threshold commonly cited ≥40 to trigger a pre-op weight-optimization discussion).
- **Simultaneous bilateral TKA** reduces total hospital/rehab burden but carries higher perioperative cardiopulmonary and mortality risk, particularly in patients >70 or with cardiac comorbidity — most guidance favors staged bilateral TKA in higher-risk patients.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peri-operative | Day 0–1 | Weight-bearing as tolerated same-day/next-day (modern rapid-recovery/ERAS pathways) | Begin CPM or active-assisted ROM per protocol | Multimodal analgesia, early mobilization, DVT prophylaxis initiation |
| Acute rehab | 0–2 weeks | Full WBAT with assistive device weaned as tolerated | Goal ≥90° flexion by 2 weeks, full extension | Gait training, quad sets, staircase training |
| Subacute | 2–6 weeks | Independent ambulation | Progress toward 110–120° flexion | Outpatient PT 2–3x/week, strengthening, edema control |
| Strengthening | 6–12 weeks | — | Functional ROM target 0–120°+ | Progressive resistance, balance/proprioception, low-impact conditioning |
| Return to work | Variable | — | — | Sedentary 3–6 weeks; physical labor 3–4 months |
| Return to activity/sport | 3–6 months | — | — | Low-impact sport (walking, swimming, cycling, golf) 3 months; higher-demand recreational activity by 6 months; guideline consensus discourages high-impact/pivoting sport indefinitely after TKA given polyethylene wear/loosening risk |

VTE prophylaxis: contemporary AAOS/ACCP-aligned protocols support aspirin as adequate prophylaxis for standard-risk patients, reserving more potent anticoagulation (LMWH, DOACs) for patients with elevated VTE risk factors.
