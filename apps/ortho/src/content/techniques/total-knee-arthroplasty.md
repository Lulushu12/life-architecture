## Indications

Multicompartmental or inflammatory OA, failed non-operative management and/or failed joint-preserving surgery (HTO/UKA), fixed deformity, ligamentous insufficiency incompatible with UKA/HTO, or diffuse KL4 disease. Also the default for inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic, crystal), active/recent joint sepsis or uncontrolled infection risk factors ruling out compartment-sparing surgery, and fixed varus/valgus deformity not passively correctable, flexion contracture >15–20°, or arc of motion <90°.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed in the monograph beyond the requirement for full-length hip-to-ankle standing alignment films for pre-operative planning regardless of alignment philosophy chosen, and increasing pre-operative use of CT for robotic-assisted planning (3D bone models, rotational alignment assessment).

## Steps

1. **Confirm indication and assess intra-operative balancing drivers**: flexion contracture and coronal laxity under exam drive intra-operative balancing strategy more than the static radiograph alone.
2. **Select alignment philosophy** (the central controversy in modern primary TKA):
   - **Mechanical alignment (MA)**: cuts both components perpendicular to their respective mechanical axes, targeting neutral (180° ± 3°) HKA regardless of native anatomy. Decades of registry track record and predictable technique, but forces ligament releases in knees whose native anatomy is not neutral (most CPAK types other than V) and is associated with a subset of "well-aligned but unhappy" patients (~15–20% dissatisfaction historically despite good objective alignment).
   - **Kinematic alignment (KA)**: resects bone to restore the patient's pre-arthritic native joint line and rotational axes, without a fixed alignment target, aiming to reproduce natural soft-tissue tension and avoid releases. Early/mid-term RCT and meta-analysis data show KA at least equivalent, and in several trials superior, PROM scores versus MA at 2–5 years; long-term survivorship in extreme (outlier) native alignments is still debated.
   - **Restricted kinematic (rKA) / functional alignment (FA)**: hybrid approaches — rKA restores native joint-line obliquity but caps deviation from neutral (commonly ±5° boundaries on HKA, MPTA, LDFA); FA (typically robotic-enabled) prioritizes balanced flexion/extension gaps and soft-tissue tension intra-operatively, adjusting cuts in real time. CPAK phenotyping is the operational language used to plan and audit these strategies. The field's current center of gravity favors restricted kinematic/functional alignment within a safe boundary over unrestricted native-anatomy KA or rigid classic MA, particularly on robotic/navigated platforms.
3. **Select bearing/constraint**: cruciate-retaining (CR, requires PCL competence and careful balancing) vs posterior-stabilized (PS, cam-post substitutes for PCL function, more forgiving in PCL-deficient or over-released knees) vs medial-pivot/ultracongruent designs (aim to reproduce more natural kinematics — medial ball-in-socket, lateral rollback — and have gained popularity with kinematic/functional alignment techniques). No RCT-level evidence shows a clear, consistent PROM advantage of one bearing philosophy over another; selection is largely surgeon/deformity-driven.
4. **Select fixation**: cementless TKA utilization has risen from roughly 3% (2015) to ~9% (2021), a ~242% relative increase, and to roughly one-fifth of primary TKAs by the mid-2020s. Modern porous/highly-porous metal-backed designs with mechanical interlock plus biologic ongrowth show mid-term survivorship comparable to cemented fixation; most surgeons still cement the patellar button even in an otherwise cementless (hybrid) construct given a signal of cementless patellar component migration in paired RCT data. Cementless fixation is favored in younger, higher-bone-quality patients; cemented fixation remains preferred in osteoporotic bone, markedly deformed proximal tibiae, and revision settings.
5. **Consider robotic assistance (RA-TKA)**: contemporary pooled RCT meta-analyses (2024–2025, >20 RCTs, ~2,700 patients) show RA-TKA achieves significantly fewer mechanical-axis outliers (risk ratio ~0.33) and smaller mean deviation from the planned axis (~1° tighter) versus conventional jig-based TKA, though clinical/functional PROMs and complication rates remain statistically similar through mid-term follow-up. Robotics is the enabling technology that makes functional/restricted-kinematic alignment practically reproducible at scale.
6. **Decide on patellar resurfacing**: remains surgeon/region-dependent (near-universal in the US, more selective in parts of Europe); meta-analyses show no consistent difference in revision-for-any-reason but a modestly lower rate of secondary patellar resurfacing/anterior knee pain revision when resurfaced primarily in patients with pre-operative patellofemoral arthritis.

## Pitfalls

- **Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI)** is the leading cause of early revision, incidence ~1–2% in primary TKA, higher with diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, or prior knee surgery; diagnosis follows MSIS/ICM criteria. Broad treatment-selection logic:

| Scenario | Typical strategy |
|---|---|
| Acute (<4–6 weeks from index surgery or acute hematogenous, stable implant, susceptible organism) | DAIR (debridement, antibiotics, implant retention) with modular component exchange |
| Chronic infection, well-fixed components, favorable host/organism | One-stage revision (increasingly favored in select European centers with strong culture-directed antibiotic protocols) |
| Chronic infection, difficult organism, compromised host, loose components | Two-stage revision (antibiotic spacer, organism-directed IV antibiotics, reimplantation once cleared) — remains the reference standard in most of the US and much of Europe for chronic PJI |

- Other complications: aseptic loosening; stiffness/arthrofibrosis (manipulation under anesthesia typically considered if <90° flexion by 6–12 weeks); instability (flexion or extension gap mismatch); periprosthetic fracture; extensor mechanism disruption (patellar tendon/quadriceps tendon rupture, patellar fracture); venous thromboembolism (prophylaxis per AAOS/ACCP guidance — aspirin now supported as adequate prophylaxis for standard-risk patients in most contemporary protocols); persistent unexplained pain (~10–20% of patients report residual dissatisfaction despite a technically well-functioning implant — underscores the alignment-philosophy debate).
- **Pre-operative optimization pitfalls**: uncontrolled diabetes (target HbA1c <7.5–8%), active smoking (cessation recommended ≥4 weeks pre-op), and a recent intra-articular corticosteroid injection (many surgeons enforce a ≥3-month washout given signals of increased early PJI risk) are modifiable risk factors that should be addressed before elective TKA. BMI is a modifiable risk factor for PJI, wound complications, and revision (soft threshold commonly cited ≥40 to trigger pre-op weight-optimization discussion).
- **Simultaneous bilateral TKA** reduces total hospital/rehab burden but carries higher perioperative cardiopulmonary and mortality risk, particularly in patients >70 or with cardiac comorbidity — most guidance favors staged bilateral TKA in higher-risk patients.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peri-operative | Day 0–1 | Weight-bearing as tolerated same-day/next-day (modern rapid-recovery/ERAS pathways) | Begin CPM or active-assisted ROM per protocol | Multimodal analgesia, early mobilization, DVT prophylaxis initiation |
| Acute rehab | 0–2 weeks | Full WBAT with assistive device weaned as tolerated | Goal ≥90° flexion by 2 weeks, full extension | Gait training, quad sets, staircase training |
| Subacute | 2–6 weeks | Independent ambulation | Progress toward 110–120° flexion | Outpatient PT 2–3x/week, strengthening, edema control |
| Strengthening | 6–12 weeks | — | Functional ROM target 0–120°+ | Progressive resistance, balance/proprioception, low-impact conditioning |
| Return to work | Variable | — | — | Sedentary 3–6 weeks; physical labor 3–4 months |
| Return to activity/sport | 3–6 months | — | — | Low-impact sport (walking, swimming, cycling, golf) 3 months; higher-demand recreational activity by 6 months; guideline consensus discourages high-impact/pivoting sport indefinitely after TKA given polyethylene wear/loosening risk |

VTE prophylaxis: contemporary AAOS/ACCP-aligned protocols support aspirin as adequate prophylaxis for standard-risk patients, reserving more potent anticoagulation (LMWH, DOACs) for patients with elevated VTE risk factors.
