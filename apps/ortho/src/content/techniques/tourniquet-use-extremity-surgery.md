## Indications

Pneumatic tourniquet application for extremity surgery requiring a bloodless or reduced-bleeding surgical field, most commonly total knee arthroplasty and other cemented lower-extremity procedures. Increasingly used selectively — full-case, cementation-only, or omitted entirely — rather than as a default in every case, particularly once IV tranexamic acid (TXA) is part of the standard blood-management protocol.

## Positioning & setup

Cuff pressure is set at the limb occlusion pressure plus a 50–100mmHg safety margin, commonly translating to approximately 250–300mmHg at the thigh (lower for calf-level or pediatric use). The limb is exsanguinated by Esmarch bandage wrap or elevation before cuff inflation.

## Steps

1. **Exsanguinate the limb** by Esmarch bandage or elevation prior to inflation.
2. **Set cuff pressure** to limb occlusion pressure + 50–100mmHg (≈250–300mmHg thigh; lower at the calf or in pediatric cases).
3. **Inflate for a maximum single duration of 90–120 minutes.** If additional tourniquet time is needed, deflate and reperfuse the limb for several minutes before reinflating.
4. **Choose a use pattern based on the clinical scenario:**
   - Full-case tourniquet: maximal field visualization and shorter operative time, favored by lower-volume surgeons or where visualization is otherwise limited.
   - "Cementation-only" short tourniquet: inflate immediately before cement application, deflate after prosthesis seating — captures the visualization benefit during the critical cementing step while minimizing total ischemia time.
   - Tourniquet-less technique with TXA as the primary hemostatic strategy: avoids tourniquet-specific morbidity entirely.
5. **Deflate and reperfuse** at case completion (or between inflation cycles if the 90–120 minute limit is reached intraoperatively).

## Pitfalls

- Tourniquet-related nerve palsy (typically neurapraxia, usually reversible but occasionally prolonged) is the most direct ischemia-time-dependent complication — respect the 90–120 minute maximum single inflation window.
- Skin injury/chemical burns can occur from prep solution pooling under the cuff — ensure the limb is dry before cuff application and prep solution is not allowed to seep beneath it.
- Post-tourniquet syndrome (transient stiffness/weakness/swelling) and, rarely, deep vein thrombosis or compartment syndrome exacerbation are recognized complications of prolonged or high-pressure use.
- Contemporary comparative literature — largely in the context of routine TXA use — shows that avoiding the tourniquet (or using a short "cementation-only" tourniquet) reduces postoperative pain scores, quadriceps inhibition/early strength deficit, thigh bruising, and wound complication rates, with no clinically meaningful difference in total blood loss, transfusion rate, or long-term functional outcome once TXA is the primary hemostatic strategy.
- The tourniquet-less/limited-tourniquet debate remains one of genuine clinical equipoise rather than a settled recommendation: a Ranawat Award-winning RCT found tourniquet use did not impact the trajectory of early TKA recovery either way; a 2023 RCT under a contemporary ERAS protocol found limited tourniquet use had no meaningful benefit; but a 2024 RCT (130 knees) found standard tourniquet use reduced total blood loss without significant added morbidity and advocated its regular use in primary TKA. Do not treat "tourniquet-less is better" as a settled conclusion.

## Post-op protocol

No tourniquet-specific postoperative protocol beyond standard limb monitoring for neurovascular status and compartment syndrome signs in the immediate postoperative period; the choice of tourniquet strategy is folded into the broader ERAS/blood-management pathway (see the ERAS and TXA protocols) rather than followed by a distinct rehabilitation track of its own.
