## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Extremity surgery requiring a bloodless or reduced-bleeding surgical field, most commonly total knee arthroplasty and other cemented lower-extremity procedures where visualization of bony surfaces and the cement-bone interface is important.
- Increasingly used **selectively** — full-case, cementation-only, or omitted entirely — rather than as a default in every case, particularly once IV tranexamic acid (TXA) is part of the standard blood-management protocol.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Severe peripheral vascular disease or calcified/non-compressible vessels — occlusion pressure may be unachievable or may itself injure the vessel; consider a limited or absent tourniquet strategy and discuss with vascular surgery if disease is severe.
- Sickle cell disease/trait — historically taught as an absolute contraindication due to sickling risk under ischemia/hypoxia; contemporary practice more often treats it as a relative contraindication managed with careful oxygenation, avoidance of hypothermia and acidosis, and shorter inflation times rather than an automatic bar, but this should be an explicit, documented, case-specific decision rather than a default.
- Active or recent DVT in the operative limb — tourniquet-induced venous stasis and endothelial changes are a theoretical concern; individualize.
- Severe crush injury or compartment syndrome risk in the limb — a tourniquet can mask or worsen evolving compartment pressures and is generally avoided.
- Lymphedema or prior lymph node dissection in the limb (e.g., ipsilateral axillary dissection for upper-extremity cases) — relative contraindication due to concern for exacerbating lymphedema and infection risk, though evidence is mixed.
- Preexisting nerve injury/neuropathy in the limb — not an absolute contraindication but raises the stakes of any tourniquet-related neurapraxia and should prompt shorter inflation times and careful documentation of baseline exam.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Cuff sizing:** select cuff width appropriately for limb circumference — an undersized cuff requires higher pressure to achieve the same occlusion and increases the risk of tissue injury; a properly sized (wider) cuff occludes at lower pressure.
- **Limb occlusion pressure (LOP) strategy:** decide preoperatively whether pressure will be set by a fixed formula (LOP + safety margin, or a standard empiric thigh pressure) or by direct LOP measurement — LOP is the minimum pressure needed to stop arterial inflow distal to the cuff for that specific patient, and individualized LOP-based dosing (LOP + margin) has been shown in comparative trials to allow lower cuff pressures than empiric standard pressure settings without compromising field quality, translating to less soft-tissue and nerve trauma.
- **Blood management plan integration:** confirm whether IV TXA is part of the case plan, since this materially changes the tourniquet strategy decision (full-case vs. cementation-only vs. tourniquet-less) — see Technical Tips below.
- **Equipment:** confirm a functioning, calibrated pneumatic tourniquet unit and appropriately sized cuff are available; have Esmarch bandage (or an alternative exsanguination method for patients where Esmarch wrapping is relatively contraindicated, e.g., DVT risk, fracture) ready.
- **Patient-specific flags:** review for peripheral vascular disease, sickle cell status, prior DVT, and neuropathy as above so the inflation-time and pressure strategy is decided in advance, not improvised.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia type:** tourniquet use is compatible with general, spinal, or regional anesthesia; be aware that tourniquet inflation itself can be uncomfortable/painful under regional block alone if inflation time is prolonged (tourniquet pain is a distinct, poorly-localized aching sensation that can emerge even through an otherwise effective block after 30–45 minutes) — discuss expected tourniquet duration with anesthesia so supplemental analgesia/sedation can be anticipated.
- **Positioning:** position the limb so the cuff can be applied to the proximal thigh (or upper arm for upper-extremity cases) over adequate soft-tissue padding, proximal to the surgical field, with the limb supported in a way that allows elevation for exsanguination before inflation.
- **Padding and skin protection:** apply cuff padding (cast padding or a purpose-made sleeve) smoothly, without wrinkles, directly beneath the cuff to distribute pressure and protect skin.
- **Draping and prep solution control:** this is a critical, frequently underemphasized step — ensure the limb and padding are completely dry before cuff application, and use prep/draping technique (e.g., an impervious barrier or towel at the cuff's distal edge, careful prep solution application that avoids pooling) that prevents alcohol- or chlorhexidine-based prep solution from wicking under the cuff. Pooled prep solution under an inflated cuff is a well-recognized cause of chemical burns.
- **Fluoroscopy/imaging setup:** not tourniquet-specific; ensure the tourniquet cuff and its tubing do not obstruct planned fluoroscopic views for the case.
- **Landmarks:** mark the intended cuff position proximal to the surgical field before draping; note and document baseline distal pulses/neurovascular exam before inflation.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Apply padding smoothly around the proximal limb**, free of wrinkles, at the planned cuff location.
2. **Apply the pneumatic cuff over the padding**, positioned as proximal as the case allows while remaining outside the planned surgical/prep field.
3. **Confirm the limb and cuff area are completely dry**, and set up drape/barrier technique to prevent prep solution from tracking beneath the cuff during skin preparation.
4. **Determine target cuff pressure.** If using a fixed-margin approach: set pressure at the limb occlusion pressure plus a 50–100mmHg safety margin (commonly translating to approximately 250–300mmHg at the thigh; lower for calf-level, upper-extremity, or pediatric use). If direct LOP measurement is available, individualized LOP-based dosing allows reliably lower pressures for the same occlusion quality.
5. **Exsanguinate the limb** by Esmarch bandage wrap (distal to proximal) or by elevation for several minutes, per the plan established for that patient (elevation preferred over firm Esmarch wrapping in situations with DVT concern, acute fracture, or infected/tumor tissue where compressive wrapping is undesirable).
6. **Inflate the cuff to the target pressure**, and note/record the inflation start time — this timestamp is essential for tracking toward the 90–120 minute maximum.
7. **Proceed with the surgical approach and case.** Communicate elapsed tourniquet time periodically with the team (many ORs use an audible or visual timer) so the 90–120 minute ceiling does not arrive as a surprise mid-critical-step.
8. **Choose (or execute the preplanned) use pattern based on the clinical scenario:**
   - **Full-case tourniquet:** maximal field visualization and shorter operative time, favored by lower-volume surgeons or where visualization is otherwise limited.
   - **"Cementation-only" short tourniquet:** inflate immediately before cement application, deflate after prosthesis seating and cement cure — captures the visualization benefit during the critical cementing step while minimizing total ischemia time.
   - **Tourniquet-less technique with TXA as the primary hemostatic strategy:** avoids tourniquet-specific morbidity entirely; requires meticulous intraoperative hemostasis to compensate for the less pristine field.
9. **If additional tourniquet time is needed beyond 90–120 minutes**, deflate and allow limb reperfusion for a minimum of several minutes (commonly cited as roughly 10–15 minutes, longer if the first inflation approached the upper time limit) before reinflating for the remainder of the case.
10. **Deflate the cuff at case completion** (or at the planned cementation-only endpoint). Reassess the field for bleeding once perfusion returns — this is when missed bleeding points become apparent, so build in time for a final hemostasis check after deflation rather than closing immediately.
11. **Document total tourniquet time and pressure used** in the operative note — this is directly relevant to any postoperative neurovascular complaint.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Use a **cuff width appropriately matched to limb circumference**; a wider cuff occludes at a lower pressure than a narrow one, directly reducing soft-tissue and nerve trauma for the same occlusion quality.
- When available, use **direct LOP measurement rather than a fixed empiric pressure** — LOP-based dosing (LOP + margin) has been shown to permit meaningfully lower cuff pressures than standard fixed settings without compromising bloodless-field quality, and lower pressure is the single biggest lever for reducing nerve/soft-tissue complications.
- **Elevate rather than Esmarch-wrap** for exsanguination when there is any concern about occult DVT, an acute fracture proximal to the cuff, or tumor/infected tissue where compressive milking of the limb is undesirable.
- Keep an **explicit, visible tourniquet timer** running from inflation — the most common reason a case exceeds the safe inflation window is simply losing track of elapsed time during a technically demanding step.
- If a case is trending toward requiring extended tourniquet time, **plan the deflation/reperfusion interval proactively** rather than reactively — build a natural pause point (e.g., before final component impaction) where deflation costs the least momentum.
- For **cementation-only tourniquet use**, have the cuff and pressure pre-set and ready so inflation can happen quickly once canal preparation is complete — minimizing the gap between decision-to-inflate and actual occlusion keeps total ischemia time as short as possible.
- After deflation, **wait for a genuine hemostasis check under reperfused conditions** before closing — bleeding points invisible in a bloodless field are the main source of postoperative hematoma when this step is skipped.
- In patients with **known peripheral vascular disease**, consider a lower target pressure and shorter planned inflation time, and have a lower threshold to check pulses/Doppler signals distally both before and after tourniquet use.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Tourniquet-related nerve palsy** (typically neurapraxia, usually reversible but occasionally prolonged) is the most direct ischemia-time- and pressure-dependent complication — respect the 90–120 minute maximum single inflation window and use the lowest effective pressure (LOP + margin where feasible).
- **Skin injury/chemical burns** can occur from prep solution pooling under the cuff — ensure the limb is dry before cuff application and prep solution is not allowed to seep beneath it; this is an entirely avoidable complication with careful draping technique.
- **Post-tourniquet syndrome** (transient stiffness/weakness/swelling) and, rarely, deep vein thrombosis or compartment syndrome exacerbation are recognized complications of prolonged or high-pressure use.
- **Tourniquet pain** through an otherwise adequate regional/neuraxial block can emerge after roughly 30–45 minutes of inflation and is a distinct phenomenon from surgical pain — anticipate it rather than mistaking it for inadequate block or being caught without a supplemental analgesia/sedation plan.
- Contemporary comparative literature — largely in the context of routine TXA use — shows that avoiding the tourniquet (or using a short "cementation-only" tourniquet) reduces postoperative pain scores, quadriceps inhibition/early strength deficit, thigh bruising, and wound complication rates, with no clinically meaningful difference in total blood loss, transfusion rate, or long-term functional outcome once TXA is the primary hemostatic strategy.
- The tourniquet-less/limited-tourniquet debate remains one of genuine clinical equipoise rather than a settled recommendation: a Ranawat Award-winning RCT found tourniquet use did not impact the trajectory of early TKA recovery either way; a 2023 RCT under a contemporary ERAS protocol found limited tourniquet use had no meaningful benefit; but a 2024 RCT (130 knees) found standard tourniquet use reduced total blood loss without significant added morbidity and advocated its regular use in primary TKA. Do not treat "tourniquet-less is better" as a settled conclusion.
- An **undersized cuff or an empiric "one pressure for everyone" policy** is a common, avoidable source of unnecessarily high tourniquet pressure — individualized sizing and, where feasible, LOP-based dosing reduces this risk without any downside to field quality.
- **Missed postoperative bleeding from an inadequate post-deflation hemostasis check** presents as an expanding thigh/calf hematoma or unexpectedly high drain output in the first postoperative hours — if this occurs, reexamine the intraoperative sequence for whether deflation-phase hemostasis was adequately assessed.

## Postoperative Protocol

No tourniquet-specific postoperative protocol beyond standard limb monitoring for neurovascular status and compartment syndrome signs in the immediate postoperative period, with particular attention to any new numbness, weakness, or pain out of proportion in the distribution proximal to the cuff if tourniquet time approached the upper limit intraoperatively. The choice of tourniquet strategy is folded into the broader ERAS/blood-management pathway (see the ERAS and TXA protocols) rather than followed by a distinct rehabilitation track of its own.
