## Indications

Definitive fixation of unstable pelvic ring injuries — Tile B/C or Young-Burgess APC-II/III, VS, LC-II/III with displaced crescent fracture — and displaced traumatic sacral fractures, performed once damage-control resuscitation is complete and physiology has normalized. Timing is typically 2–10 days post-injury, balancing earlier stabilization (reduced pain, ICU length of stay, pulmonary complications) against physiologic readiness.

## Positioning & setup

Percutaneous, fluoroscopically guided technique for most posterior ring fixation. Sacral dysmorphism (an oblique/narrow upper sacral segment, residual S1 ala slope, mammillary body prominence, acute S1/S2 disc angulation) occurs in roughly 30–40% of the population and materially constrains safe percutaneous S1 screw trajectories — a "normal" S1 corridor may not exist. Always assess pelvic inlet/outlet radiographs and the lateral sacral view (or thin-cut CT with sagittal reconstructions) before planning any iliosacral or transiliac-transsacral screw, since an S2 corridor or alternate trajectory may be required.

## Steps

1. **Anterior ring — symphyseal diastasis**: symphyseal plating with a 6–8 hole plate, minimum 2 screws per side.
2. **Anterior ring — ramus fractures**: percutaneous retrograde or antegrade ramus screws, especially useful in obese patients or as a minimally invasive alternative to plating.
3. **Posterior ring — SI joint disruption or amenable sacral fracture**: percutaneous iliosacral screws (S1 ± S2, 7.0–7.3 mm cannulated) after closed or percutaneous reduction.
4. **Posterior ring — bilateral instability or maximal fixation strength needed**: transiliac-transsacral (TITS) screws for bilateral posterior instability, U/H-type sacral fractures, or osteoporotic bone; requires a confirmed safe corridor (screen for dysmorphism first).
5. **Posterior ring — spinopelvic dissociation or inadequate percutaneous purchase**: lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation — iliac screws plus pedicle screws connected by rods, ± transsacral bar — for highly comminuted sacral fractures or when percutaneous screw purchase is inadequate.
6. **When closed reduction fails**: open reduction of crescent fractures or SI dislocation via an anterior or posterior approach.
7. In severely osteoporotic bone, consider cement-augmented ("screw-in-cement") iliosacral or TITS screws to improve fixation strength and reduce loosening/cutout risk.

## Pitfalls

- Sacral dysmorphism is present in roughly a third of patients and can eliminate a safe standard S1 trajectory entirely — imaging review before screw planning is mandatory, not optional.
- Nonunion/malunion of the pelvic ring is more common with inadequate posterior fixation of vertically unstable (Tile C, APC-III, VS) patterns, presenting as chronic pain, gait asymmetry, or sitting intolerance.
- Symptomatic hardware and screw loosening/cutout are particular risks in osteoporotic bone with standard iliosacral screws — this is the rationale for increasing use of cement-augmented constructs and TITS screws in the fragility population.
- Denis Zone III sacral fractures with neurologic deficit and displacement are a relative indication for open decompression plus lumbopelvic fixation rather than percutaneous screws alone.
- Recent comparative evidence (2024–2025) finds TITS screw fixation achieves comparable or better stabilization with less surgical morbidity than lumbopelvic (triangular) constructs for minimally displaced H-/U-type sacral fragility fractures, supporting a percutaneous-first strategy when the pattern permits.
- In dysmorphic sacral anatomy with Denis II vertical shear patterns, combined iliosacral + transiliac-transsacral screw constructs significantly outperform TTS alone in reducing vertical displacement — worth considering when dysmorphism limits a purely transsacral trajectory.

## Post-op protocol

**Pelvic ring injury (stable fixation construct)**:
- Weeks 0–6: touch-down/toe-touch weight-bearing on the operated side(s) if posterior fixation is percutaneous with good bone quality and anatomic reduction; non-weight-bearing if fixation is tenuous (severe osteoporosis, comminution, lumbopelvic construct). DVT prophylaxis continued; bed mobility, transfer training, seated exercises; wean off opioids.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing as radiographs confirm healing (callus bridging, no progressive displacement), gait training with assistive device, hip/core strengthening avoiding resisted hip flexion/adduction against fixation early on.
- Weeks 12+: full weight-bearing once union confirmed — typically 12 weeks for percutaneous posterior fixation in good bone, 4–6 months for lumbopelvic constructs or severely comminuted patterns. Return to low-impact activity; hardware removal not routine unless symptomatic.
- Return to work: sedentary work 6–12 weeks; manual labor typically 4–6 months, individualized to fracture pattern and fixation stability.

**Traumatic sacral fracture (percutaneous or lumbopelvic fixation)**:
- Weeks 0–6: weight-bearing dictated by construct — touch-down/toe-touch if percutaneous screws in good bone with anatomic reduction; non-weight-bearing on affected side(s) for 6 weeks if lumbopelvic fixation was required; monitor for evolving neurologic deficit at each visit if the initial injury involved Denis Zone II/III.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing guided by radiographic callus; initiate core and hip girdle strengthening; coordinate neurogenic bowel/bladder rehabilitation with urology/neurorehabilitation if a foraminal/canal injury caused deficit.
- Months 3–4: full weight-bearing once union confirmed; return to desk work 6–10 weeks, manual labor/heavy lifting deferred to 4–6 months.
- Return to sport: low-impact activity at 3–4 months once union confirmed; contact/axial-load sport deferred to 6–9 months and contingent on resolution of any neurologic deficit.

**Fragility fractures fixed percutaneously**: weight-bearing as tolerated is generally permitted immediately, since the fixation strategy is chosen specifically to allow immediate loading in this population — a key advantage of surgical fixation over conservative care for FFP II–IV.
