## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- Definitive fixation of unstable pelvic ring injuries — Tile B/C or Young-Burgess APC-II/III, VS, LC-II/III with a displaced crescent fracture.
- Displaced traumatic sacral fractures (Denis I–III), performed once damage-control resuscitation is complete and physiology has normalized.
- FFP III/IV fragility fractures with displacement or a failed conservative trial, and FFP II fractures with refractory pain despite analgesia and mobilization attempts.
- Timing for traumatic patterns is typically 2–10 days post-injury, balancing earlier stabilization (reduced pain, ICU length of stay, pulmonary complications) against physiologic readiness.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- No safe percutaneous corridor on preoperative imaging (severe sacral dysmorphism, prior instrumentation, or unusual anatomy) without a confirmed alternate trajectory — do not proceed on a "standard" trajectory without imaging confirmation in these patients.
- Denis Zone III sacral fracture with a progressive or significant neurologic deficit and canal compromise — favor open decompression (laminectomy) plus lumbopelvic fixation over percutaneous screws alone, since percutaneous screws do not decompress the canal.
- Irreducible displacement by closed/percutaneous means — open reduction via an anterior or posterior approach is required rather than forcing a percutaneous screw across a malreduced joint.
- Active local skin infection or contamination over the planned percutaneous entry site.
- Inadequate imaging capability (fluoroscopy quality, or unavailability of navigation/intraoperative CT where dysmorphism makes standard views unreliable) — do not proceed with a technique whose safety depends on imaging you cannot actually obtain and interpret.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: AP pelvis, inlet, and outlet radiographs plus fine-cut CT with sagittal reconstructions through the sacrum are mandatory before planning any iliosacral or transiliac-transsacral screw. Specifically assess for **sacral dysmorphism** — an oblique or narrow upper sacral segment, residual S1 ala slope, mammillary body prominence, and acute S1/S2 disc angulation — present in roughly 30–40% of the population; a "normal" S1 corridor may not exist in these patients, and an S2 corridor or an alternate trajectory (iliosacral screw at S2, or combined iliosacral + transiliac-transsacral construct) may be required instead.
- **Corridor planning**: trace the intended screw trajectory on inlet, outlet, and (where available) 3D/CT reconstructions before the case. In dysmorphic anatomy, a projection angled roughly 30° cephalad and 30° ventral relative to standard inlet/outlet views (aligned with the upper sacral alar pedicle axis) can improve visualization of the true safe corridor and is worth having available if standard views are equivocal.
- **Implant selection**: 7.0–7.3 mm cannulated screws (fully or partially threaded depending on whether compression across the SI joint/fracture is desired) for iliosacral fixation; longer fully threaded screws for transiliac-transsacral (TITS) constructs spanning both ilia; a 6–8 hole pelvic plate for symphyseal diastasis; cannulated ramus screw systems for anterior ramus fractures. In severely osteoporotic bone, plan for cement-augmented ("screw-in-cement") constructs — fenestrated cannulated screws compatible with cement delivery.
- **Construct choice by pattern**: bilateral posterior instability, U/H-type sacral fractures, or a need for maximal fixation strength (osteoporotic bone) favor TITS screws over unilateral iliosacral screws; spinopelvic dissociation, highly comminuted sacral fractures, or inadequate percutaneous purchase favor lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation — decide this before the case, since intraoperative "upgrading" from a percutaneous plan to an open lumbopelvic construct is a materially different setup.
- **Equipment**: large C-arm capable of inlet, outlet, and true lateral sacral views without repositioning the patient; radiolucent table; cannulated screw sets; a femoral distractor or Schanz pins for closed reduction maneuvers; cement delivery system if augmentation is planned; have an open posterior or anterior reduction set available as a backup if closed reduction fails.
- **Anticoagulation/medical review**: many candidates for TITS/iliosacral screws in the fragility population are on anticoagulants — plan perioperative anticoagulation management given the retroperitoneal location of these constructs.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard for percutaneous posterior fixation to allow reliable positioning, muscle relaxation for closed reduction maneuvers, and breath-holding during fluoroscopy; regional/neuraxial technique is occasionally used in select frail patients for symphyseal or ramus screw work alone.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table for most percutaneous posterior ring and anterior ring work — this allows inlet, outlet, and lateral sacral fluoroscopic views to be obtained by C-arm movement without repositioning the patient. Some surgeons prefer prone positioning for isolated posterior fixation; supine is more versatile when combined anterior and posterior work is planned in the same setting.
- **Reduction aids on the table**: have the ability to apply longitudinal traction (via a femoral traction pin or a bump under the sacrum) and internal/external rotation force through the iliac crest (via a Schanz pin joystick in the ilium) to reduce vertical and rotational displacement before screw placement — plan and set up for this before draping, not after a first pass at closed reduction fails.
- **Draping**: prep and drape circumferentially enough to allow bilateral posterior ring access if a TITS or bilateral iliosacral construct is planned, plus the anterior pelvis if combined anterior ring fixation (symphyseal plate or ramus screws) is planned in the same setting.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: confirm before draping that true inlet, outlet, and lateral sacral views can all be obtained — an acceptable inlet view shows the anterior cortex of the S1 body superimposed on the S2 body; an acceptable outlet view shows the pubic symphysis at the level of the S2 foramen with both S1 and S2 foramina visualized. Poor-quality views (from patient body habitus, overlying bowel gas, or C-arm limitations) are a reason to add navigation/intraoperative CT or convert to an open approach, not a reason to proceed on a suboptimal image.
- **Landmarks**: mark the posterior superior iliac spines, iliac crests, and the greater trochanters bilaterally before draping if closed reduction maneuvers via Schanz pins are planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Anterior ring — symphyseal diastasis**: reduce the symphysis with a pelvic reduction clamp (e.g., a symphyseal or Farabeuf clamp placed through small stab incisions or via the open approach if combined with other anterior work), then apply symphyseal plating with a 6–8 hole plate and a minimum of 2 screws per side across the symphysis.
2. **Anterior ring — ramus fractures**: place percutaneous retrograde (from the pubic tubercle toward the posterior column/ilium) or antegrade (from the supra-acetabular region toward the pubic tubercle) ramus screws under fluoroscopic guidance, confirming the trajectory stays within the ramus corridor on both inlet/outlet and obturator oblique views throughout passage — this is especially useful in obese patients where an open anterior approach is higher risk, or as a minimally invasive alternative to plating.
3. **Posterior ring — closed reduction**: before any screw placement, reduce the SI joint or sacral fracture using longitudinal traction and rotational correction via a Schanz pin joystick in the iliac crest, confirmed on inlet/outlet fluoroscopy; accept only a reduction that restores symmetry on both views before proceeding to screw placement — a screw placed across a malreduced joint fixes the malreduction.
4. **Posterior ring — iliosacral screw placement (SI joint disruption or amenable sacral fracture)**: with the reduction held, plan the S1 (± S2) starting point on the lateral ilium under a true lateral sacral fluoroscopic view, confirming the starting point sits within the safe zone bounded by the sacral ala, the S1 foramen, and the posterior sacral cortex. Advance a guidewire under sequential inlet, outlet, and lateral views — on the outlet view the guidewire should track within the inferior half of the sacral body silhouette, and on the inlet view within the posterior half of the sacral body, to stay clear of the neural foramina and the anterior sacral cortex/presacral vessels. Overdrill and place a 7.0–7.3 mm cannulated screw with a washer; use a partially threaded screw if compression across the joint/fracture is desired, fully threaded if compression is not desired (e.g., a comminuted sacral fracture where compression could over-reduce or displace fragments).
5. **Posterior ring — TITS screws (bilateral instability or maximal fixation strength)**: after confirming a safe transsacral corridor exists (screen for dysmorphism preoperatively — this is not a decision to make for the first time intraoperatively), advance a longer fully threaded screw from one iliac wing across the sacral body (typically at S1, occasionally S2) to engage the contralateral ilium, under the same sequential inlet/outlet/lateral fluoroscopic monitoring as a standard iliosacral screw. TITS constructs are particularly indicated for U/H-type sacral fractures, bilateral posterior instability, and osteoporotic bone where maximal fixation strength is needed.
6. **Posterior ring — lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation** (spinopelvic dissociation, highly comminuted sacral fracture, or inadequate percutaneous purchase): place bilateral iliac screws (typically via a small incision over the posterior superior iliac spine, directed toward the anterior inferior iliac spine) and lumbar pedicle screws (typically L4/L5), then connect with rods spanning the lumbar spine to the pelvis, adding a transsacral bar/screw across the fracture for additional stability where the fracture pattern allows. This construct bypasses reliance on sacral bone quality/corridor and is the fallback when percutaneous screw purchase alone is judged inadequate.
7. **When closed reduction fails**: proceed to open reduction of a crescent fracture or SI joint dislocation via an anterior (for anterior SI joint access) or posterior (for direct posterior SI joint/crescent fragment visualization) approach, then fix with a plate and/or screws under direct vision rather than continuing to attempt percutaneous fixation across an unreduced joint.
8. **Cement augmentation in osteoporotic bone**: if planned, use fenestrated cannulated screws and inject cement under live fluoroscopy through the screw once positioned, monitoring continuously for cement extravasation toward the neural foramina, canal, or presacral vessels — stop injection immediately if extravasation is seen.
9. **Final fluoroscopic confirmation**: obtain inlet, outlet, and lateral sacral views (and Judet/obturator-iliac oblique views if anterior column screws were also placed) confirming final screw position is fully intraosseous, reduction is maintained, and no screw threads cross into the sacral canal or neural foramina.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Screen every patient for sacral dysmorphism before planning the corridor — it is present in roughly 30–40% of the population, and proceeding on an assumed "standard" S1 trajectory in a dysmorphic sacrum is a well-recognized mechanism of iatrogenic neural injury.
- A true lateral sacral view is worth the extra fluoroscopic time even when using primarily inlet/outlet technique — some series report acceptable outcomes with inlet/outlet views alone, but the lateral view remains the most direct check on foraminal and canal violation and is particularly valuable in anatomy that looks even slightly atypical on inlet/outlet views.
- On the outlet view, keep the guidewire/screw trajectory in the inferior half of the sacral body silhouette; on the inlet view, keep it in the posterior half — these two rules of thumb correspond to staying clear of the neural foramina anteriorly-inferiorly and the anterior sacral cortex/presacral vessels anteriorly.
- In dysmorphic anatomy where standard inlet/outlet views are hard to interpret, add a roughly 30° cephalad, 30° ventral oblique view aligned with the upper sacral alar pedicle axis — this can clarify the true safe corridor when standard views are ambiguous.
- Do not chase a difficult reduction with escalating screw compression — if the joint will not reduce with traction and a joystick, stop and convert to open reduction rather than accepting a malreduction fixed in place by a well-placed screw.
- In dysmorphic sacra with a Denis II vertical shear pattern, a combined iliosacral + transiliac-transsacral screw construct significantly outperforms a TTS-alone construct for controlling vertical displacement per recent biomechanical data — consider this combined construct specifically when dysmorphism limits confidence in a purely transsacral trajectory.
- For minimally displaced H-/U-type sacral fragility fractures, recent comparative outcomes data favor TITS screw fixation over lumbopelvic (triangular) constructs, achieving comparable or better stabilization with less surgical morbidity — reserve the more morbid lumbopelvic construct for patterns that truly need it (significant displacement, gross comminution, inadequate percutaneous purchase) rather than defaulting to it.
- Have a low threshold to add cement augmentation in visibly osteoporotic bone at the time of screw placement rather than waiting for postoperative loosening to prompt revision — this is far easier to do primarily than to retrofit.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Sacral dysmorphism** is present in roughly a third of patients and can eliminate a safe standard S1 trajectory entirely — imaging review before screw planning is mandatory, not optional.
- **Neural injury** from a malpositioned guidewire or screw crossing into a foramen or the sacral canal — continuous sequential fluoroscopic monitoring during wire advancement, not just a final check, is what prevents this.
- **Malreduction fixed in place** by forcing screw placement across an inadequately reduced joint — always achieve and confirm reduction on inlet/outlet views before final screw passage.
- **Nonunion/malunion of the pelvic ring** is more common with inadequate posterior fixation of vertically unstable (Tile C, APC-III, VS) patterns, presenting as chronic pain, gait asymmetry, or sitting intolerance.
- **Symptomatic hardware and screw loosening/cutout** are particular risks in osteoporotic bone with standard iliosacral screws — this is the rationale for increasing use of cement-augmented constructs and TITS screws in the fragility population.
- **Cement extravasation** toward the neural foramina, canal, or presacral venous plexus during augmented screw placement — inject under continuous live fluoroscopy and stop immediately at the first sign of extravasation.
- **Denis Zone III sacral fractures with neurologic deficit and displacement** are a relative indication for open decompression plus lumbopelvic fixation rather than percutaneous screws alone, since percutaneous fixation does not decompress the canal.
- **Vascular injury** (presacral venous plexus, or the corona mortis/obturator vessels during anterior ramus screw work) is a recognized, if uncommon, complication — plan trajectories to avoid the anterior sacral cortex on the inlet view for posterior screws, and be mindful of vascular anatomy near the ramus for anterior screws.
- **Under-recognizing an inadequate construct**: TITS screws or a single iliosacral screw may be biomechanically insufficient for spinopelvic dissociation or grossly comminuted patterns — do not force a percutaneous solution onto a pattern that the preoperative plan already identified as needing lumbopelvic fixation.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Pelvic ring injury (stable fixation construct)**:
- Weeks 0–6: touch-down/toe-touch weight-bearing on the operated side(s) if posterior fixation is percutaneous with good bone quality and anatomic reduction; non-weight-bearing if fixation is tenuous (severe osteoporosis, comminution, lumbopelvic construct). DVT prophylaxis continued; bed mobility, transfer training, seated exercises; wean off opioids.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing as radiographs confirm healing (callus bridging, no progressive displacement), gait training with assistive device, hip/core strengthening avoiding resisted hip flexion/adduction against fixation early on.
- Weeks 12+: full weight-bearing once union confirmed — typically 12 weeks for percutaneous posterior fixation in good bone, 4–6 months for lumbopelvic constructs or severely comminuted patterns. Return to low-impact activity; hardware removal not routine unless symptomatic.
- Return to work: sedentary work 6–12 weeks; manual labor typically 4–6 months, individualized to fracture pattern and fixation stability.

**Traumatic sacral fracture (percutaneous or lumbopelvic fixation)**:
- Weeks 0–6: weight-bearing dictated by construct — touch-down/toe-touch if percutaneous screws in good bone with anatomic reduction; non-weight-bearing on affected side(s) for 6 weeks if lumbopelvic fixation was required; monitor for evolving neurologic deficit at each visit if the initial injury involved Denis Zone II/III.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing guided by radiographic callus; initiate core and hip girdle strengthening; coordinate neurogenic bowel/bladder rehabilitation with urology/neurorehabilitation if a foraminal/canal injury caused deficit.
- Months 3–4: full weight-bearing once union confirmed; return to desk work 6–10 weeks, manual labor/heavy lifting deferred to 4–6 months.
- Return to sport: low-impact activity at 3–4 months once union confirmed; contact/axial-load sport deferred to 6–9 months and contingent on resolution of any neurologic deficit.

**Fragility fractures fixed percutaneously**: weight-bearing as tolerated is generally permitted immediately, since the fixation strategy is chosen specifically to allow immediate loading in this population — a key advantage of surgical fixation over conservative care for FFP II–IV.
