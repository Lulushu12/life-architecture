## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Isolated, symptomatic patellofemoral OA (Iwano grade III–IV) with preserved tibiofemoral compartments, typically in patients younger than the usual TKA population, whose maltracking (if any) has already been or can be concurrently addressed.

**Contraindications**:
- Instability/maltracking as the dominant driver of symptoms, uncorrected — this needs realignment (tibial tubercle osteotomy, MPFL reconstruction), not isolated arthroplasty.
- Inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic, crystal disease).
- Any meaningful tibiofemoral chondral disease on either compartment — this progresses to needing early TKA conversion and should be treated as TKA up front rather than PFA.
- Active or recent joint sepsis, uncontrolled diabetes, active local skin/soft-tissue infection.

**Relative contraindications**: severe, uncorrected trochlear dysplasia (Dejour C/D) without a concurrent or staged realignment plan; significant patella baja/alta that would compromise trochlear component tracking; and patients with diffuse, low-grade multi-site pain out of proportion to imaging findings, in whom isolated compartment surgery is less likely to resolve symptoms.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Skyline/Merchant view** to assess patellofemoral joint space, subluxation, and tilt, and to grade disease severity (Iwano classification).
- **Lateral radiograph** for patellar height (Caton-Deschamps or Insall-Salvati ratio) and to screen for any unexpected tibiofemoral joint space narrowing.
- **CT or MRI measurement of TT-TG (tibial tubercle–trochlear groove) distance and trochlear dysplasia grading (Dejour A–D)** whenever instability or maltracking is part of the symptom complex — this determines whether a realignment procedure must accompany or substitute for arthroplasty; isolated wear without instability is managed differently from dysplasia-driven arthrosis.
- **MRI** to confirm the tibiofemoral compartments are genuinely spared before committing to isolated PFA rather than TKA — this is the single most important planning step, since progression/undetected tibiofemoral disease is the leading cause of late PFA revision.
- **Implant family selection**: inlay (trochlear component recessed flush with native cartilage, less dependent on precise rotational alignment) vs onlay (resurfaces and extends proximally onto the anterior femoral cortex, more anatomically constrained trochlear geometry, requires precise rotational positioning analogous to TKA femoral component placement) — select based on surgeon experience and the degree of trochlear dysplasia present (onlay designs generally handle more dysplastic trochlear anatomy better since they replace rather than merely cap the native groove).
- **Plan for concurrent realignment procedures** (tibial tubercle osteotomy, MPFL reconstruction, lateral release) if instability is present but not the dominant driver — these should be planned and consented for pre-operatively, not decided intra-operatively.
- **Instrumentation**: trochlear/patellar sizing trials spanning the templated size ± one size, patellar reaming/resurfacing instruments, and — if a concurrent tibial tubercle osteotomy is planned — the separate osteotomy fixation set.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: spinal or general anesthesia, typically combined with an adductor canal block and/or peri-articular local infiltration analgesia, similar to UKA given the comparably rapid recovery pathway.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard table with a lateral thigh post and a foot support/leg holder allowing knee flexion to at least 90–110° for trochlear exposure and patellar tracking assessment through range of motion. A tourniquet is applied high on the thigh after exsanguination.
- **Fluoroscopy**: not routinely required for standard PFA but may be used to confirm trochlear component position and patellar tracking in more complex or revision cases, or when combined with a tibial tubercle osteotomy requiring fixation confirmation.
- **Draping**: standard extremity drape from mid-thigh to the foot; ensure the leg can be freely flexed and the patella tracked through a full arc of motion for intra-operative assessment.
- **Surface landmarks marked pre-incision**: midline or slightly medial parapatellar incision (similar to a TKA incision but the arthrotomy and exposure can often be more limited/quadriceps-sparing than a full TKA given the isolated compartment target), patellar borders, and tibial tubercle if a concurrent osteotomy is planned.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and exposure**: make a midline or slightly medial skin incision, then a medial parapatellar (or quadriceps-sparing subvastus/midvastus, where exposure allows) arthrotomy. Evert or subluxate the patella laterally to expose the trochlea.
2. **Directly inspect both tibiofemoral compartments** before proceeding — this is the critical intra-operative checkpoint that confirms the pre-operative imaging assessment of "isolated" patellofemoral disease; convert to TKA if unexpected tibiofemoral chondral disease is found.
3. **Assess trochlear dysplasia and patellar tracking dynamically** through range of motion before cutting, to confirm the pre-operative realignment plan (if any) is still appropriate.
4. **Prepare the trochlea**: for an onlay design, resect/ream the distal anterior femur and trochlear groove to accept the prosthetic trochlear component, referencing rotational landmarks (transepicondylar axis, posterior condylar axis) analogous to TKA femoral component rotation — precise rotational positioning here is the main technical determinant of good patellar tracking with onlay designs. For an inlay design, ream a recessed seat so the trochlear component sits flush with the surrounding native cartilage, which is less rotation-sensitive but requires accurate depth control to avoid a proud or recessed component.
5. **Size and position the trochlear component**: err toward proximal/lateral trochlear component position that follows the native Q-angle and avoids overhang, since a malpositioned or oversized trochlear component is a recognized cause of maltracking and anterior knee pain post-operatively.
6. **Prepare and resurface the patella**: resect the patellar articular surface conservatively, matching native patellar thickness with the prosthetic component to avoid overstuffing the patellofemoral joint (a common cause of persistent anterior knee pain and restricted flexion), and center the patellar component to avoid lateral overhang.
7. **Trial components and assess tracking**: with trial trochlear and patellar components in place, take the knee through a full range of motion and confirm smooth, centered patellar tracking without lateral subluxation or component impingement at terminal extension or deep flexion.
8. **Perform any planned concurrent realignment**: if a tibial tubercle osteotomy or MPFL reconstruction is part of the plan, perform and fix it at this stage, then re-check tracking with the arthroplasty trials in place before finalizing either construct.
9. **Assess for residual lateral tightness**: perform a lateral retinacular release only if tracking remains abnormal after confirming correct trochlear component position and rotation — as with TKA, this should not be a reflexive step.
10. **Cement final components**: pulse-lavage and dry the bone surfaces, cement the trochlear and patellar components with attention to complete cement mantle coverage and removal of extruded cement, particularly around the patellar component periphery where retained cement can cause synovitis or loose-body symptoms.
11. **Final range-of-motion and tracking check**: cycle the knee through full flexion-extension with final components in place, confirm centered tracking, and reconfirm both tibiofemoral compartments remain undisturbed by the procedure.
12. **Closure**: close the arthrotomy in layers with attention to maintaining balanced patellar tracking through closure, consider a drain if bleeding is significant, and release the tourniquet before or during closure to check hemostasis.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Treat the intra-operative inspection of both tibiofemoral compartments as a mandatory checkpoint, not a formality — this is the step most responsible for avoiding the most common reason for late PFA revision (unrecognized or progressive tibiofemoral disease), so be prepared to convert to TKA if findings do not match the pre-operative plan.
- For onlay designs, apply the same rigor to femoral component rotation that you would in a TKA — reference the transepicondylar axis and posterior condylar axis, since onlay trochlear geometry is far less forgiving of rotational error than inlay designs.
- Avoid overstuffing the patellofemoral joint — match resection depth to native composite thickness on both the trochlear and patellar sides; overstuffing is a common, avoidable cause of persistent anterior knee pain, the leading cause of early PFA revision.
- Assess patellar tracking dynamically, through a full range of motion, with trial components in place before committing to final components — static assessment in extension alone will miss tracking problems that only appear at mid-flexion angles.
- Do not default to a lateral release — first confirm trochlear component position and rotation are correct, since a release performed to compensate for component malposition treats the wrong problem and can devascularize the patella unnecessarily.
- If instability/maltracking is present but not dominant, plan and perform the realignment procedure (tibial tubercle osteotomy, MPFL reconstruction) concurrently rather than deferring it — addressing maltracking at the index procedure is more reliable than revisiting it later.
- Favor onlay implant designs, or at minimum more careful rotational planning, in patients with higher-grade trochlear dysplasia (Dejour C/D), since these trochleae depart furthest from the geometry an inlay design assumes.
- Counsel patients pre-operatively using contemporary (second-generation implant) survivorship data rather than older first-generation data, since the two implant eras have meaningfully different track records and patient expectations should be set accordingly.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Progression of tibiofemoral compartment OA** requiring conversion to TKA is the most common reason for late revision — strict patient selection (truly isolated PF disease) up front is the main mitigation.
- **Persistent anterior knee pain** is the leading cause of early revision — commonly related to component overstuffing, malposition, or unaddressed maltracking.
- **Patellar maltracking/instability** can occur if the trochlear component is malrotated — reinforces the need for precise rotational positioning, especially with onlay designs.
- **PFA-to-TKA conversion** carries a small but real risk of inferior outcomes versus primary TKA if patellar bone stock or trochlear geometry is compromised — another reason to be strict about isolated-disease selection criteria at the index procedure.
- Modern (second-generation) implants have markedly improved on the poor track record of first-generation designs; counsel patients using contemporary rather than historical survivorship data — international registry data show approximately 90% survival at 5 years and 82% at 10 years, and a 2025 series in isolated Iwano grade IV disease reported 86% survival at 10 years. Most revisions occur early (within ~2.5 years).
- Comparative GRADE-assessed evidence (2025) supports PFA over TKA in appropriately selected isolated PF OA on the basis of better early functional recovery, while acknowledging TKA's superior long-term implant survivorship as a trade-off to discuss with patients.

## Postoperative Protocol

Generally follows a UKA-like accelerated pathway (immediate weight-bearing as tolerated, early ROM, faster functional recovery than TKA) given the absence of tibiofemoral bone resection — return to low-impact activity by 6–8 weeks, full recovery by 3 months in most series. If a concurrent tibial tubercle osteotomy was performed, weight-bearing and ROM progression should follow the more conservative osteotomy-protection protocol until fixation and healing are confirmed, rather than the accelerated PFA-alone pathway.
