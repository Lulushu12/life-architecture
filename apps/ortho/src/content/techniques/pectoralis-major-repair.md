## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Complete pectoralis major rupture (Tietjen type III, tendon avulsion from the humerus — the most common and most amenable to anatomic repair) in young athletic patients or anyone seeking return to heavy pressing/contact sport.
- Given the consistently demonstrated strength/endurance deficits (bench press, adduction, internal rotation) with nonoperative management, surgical repair is generally recommended for active/high-demand patients regardless of exact timing, though acute repair (<3–4 weeks from injury) is strongly preferred over delayed reconstruction given superior outcomes.
- Partial tears with significant functional deficit or progressive symptoms in an active patient.
- Chronic/retracted ruptures in patients who still desire meaningful strength restoration, understanding that reconstruction (not primary repair) will be required and outcomes are somewhat inferior to acute repair.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Low-demand, sedentary patients with partial-thickness (intramuscular) injuries where a residual strength deficit is functionally acceptable — nonoperative management is reasonable here.
- Active local infection.
- Tietjen type I (sternocostal origin/muscle belly) tears — these are typically managed nonoperatively, as they occur within the muscle belly rather than at a tendon-bone or tendon-tendon interface amenable to secure fixation.
- Significant medical comorbidities precluding elective surgery, or inability to comply with the extended brace/immobilization and rehabilitation protocol.
- Ongoing, undisclosed anabolic steroid use without a plan to discontinue — steroid use is associated with compromised tendon quality and is a recognized risk factor for re-rupture; address this directly in preoperative counseling.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Establish time from injury explicitly** — this single data point (</> 3–4 weeks) is the primary determinant of which technique to use (primary repair vs allograft reconstruction) and the strongest predictor of outcome; document it clearly at the first visit.
- **Imaging**: MRI of the shoulder/chest wall is the primary planning study — it confirms the diagnosis, localizes the tear (sternocostal origin, musculotendinous junction, or tendinous insertion — Tietjen I/II/III), quantifies retraction and tendon gap, and distinguishes complete from partial tears; ultrasound can be a useful adjunct/confirmatory tool but MRI is preferred for surgical planning given the need to assess both sternal and clavicular head involvement separately.
- **Classify by both location and completeness**: confirm whether both the sternal and clavicular heads are torn (common) or only one — this changes the repair plan, since each head may need to be identified and repaired independently.
- **Graft planning for chronic/retracted tears**: if presenting >4–6 weeks from injury or with a large tendon gap on imaging, plan for allograft reconstruction (Achilles tendon allograft is the most commonly used) — confirm graft availability and sizing preoperatively rather than discovering the need intraoperatively.
- **Fixation implant selection**: suture anchors, transosseous tunnels, or cortical button constructs for acute repair — cortical button techniques are increasingly popular for their fixation strength, particularly to meet the high failure load demanded in athletes; have the chosen system available along with a backup option.
- **Equipment**: standard open shoulder set, humeral drill/tap system matched to the chosen fixation implant, suture anchors or cortical button system, and allograft preparation instrumentation on standby for chronic cases.
- **Medical optimization**: screen for and address anabolic steroid use, and counsel on its association with tendon quality and re-rupture risk.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically combined with an interscalene or infraclavicular regional block for postoperative analgesia given the significant early postoperative pain associated with pectoralis repair.
- **Position**: supine or a slight beach-chair recline (~30° trunk elevation) with the operative arm free-draped to allow full range of motion intraoperatively (adduction, internal rotation, extension) for tensioning and testing the repair; the ipsilateral chest wall and axilla must be accessible.
- **Table setup**: standard operating table with a shoulder positioner or bump under the ipsilateral scapula to elevate the shoulder slightly; ensure the arm can be brought through a full range of motion without obstruction from table attachments.
- **Draping**: wide exposure from the midline of the chest, across the deltopectoral groove, to the mid-humerus, and encompassing the axilla — the incision typically follows the deltopectoral interval extending toward the axillary fold.
- **No tourniquet** (not applicable to a chest/shoulder girdle approach); **no routine fluoroscopy** required.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: clavicle, coracoid process, deltopectoral groove, axillary fold, and the palpable defect/retracted tendon stump if identifiable preoperatively — mark the planned incision along the deltopectoral interval extending distally toward the pectoralis major insertion on the humerus.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm time from injury and tear pattern** at the start of the case, cross-referencing preoperative MRI with intraoperative findings before committing to primary repair versus allograft reconstruction.
2. **Approach through the deltopectoral interval**, extending the incision distally along the lateral border of the pectoralis major toward its humeral insertion; identify and preserve the cephalic vein within the interval.
3. **Identify the ruptured tendon stump(s)**: mobilize hematoma and scar tissue to expose the sternal and clavicular heads separately — do not assume a single confluent tendon mass represents both heads without confirming their individual continuity.
4. **Débride the tendon stump edges** back to healthy, bleeding tissue and débride the humeral insertion site (native footprint just lateral to the bicipital groove) of residual fibrous tissue to expose bleeding bone.
5. **Acute repair (<3–4 weeks)**: prepare the humeral footprint and place the chosen fixation construct — suture anchors placed along the anatomic pectoralis major footprint, transosseous tunnels drilled through the humeral cortex, or a cortical button construct providing bicortical fixation for maximal pull-out strength.
6. **Pass heavy, non-absorbable sutures through the tendon stump(s)** in a locking (Krackow or similar) fashion to distribute load and resist suture pull-through, tagging the sternal and clavicular heads with separate suture sets if both are torn.
7. **Identify and repair both sternal and clavicular heads separately** when both are torn — pass and fix each head's sutures independently onto the appropriate portion of the anatomic footprint (the sternal head inserts distal/posterior to the clavicular head); repairing only the more visible head under-restores strength and is a specific, avoidable technical error.
8. **Reduce and fix the tendon to the prepared footprint**, tensioning with the arm in adduction and slight internal rotation (approximating a functional resting position) to avoid overtensioning that would restrict extension/external rotation, or undertensioning that would leave a gap.
9. **Test repair security** by taking the arm gently through abduction, extension, and external rotation intraoperatively to confirm the repair does not gap or fail under physiologic excursion before closure.
10. **Chronic/retracted rupture (>4–6 weeks) or a large tendon gap**: if primary tendon-to-bone apposition is not achievable, prepare an Achilles tendon allograft (or equivalent), weave/suture it to the retracted native tendon stump proximally (Pulver-Taft or similar tendon-to-tendon technique), and fix the distal end of the graft to the humeral footprint using the same anchor/tunnel/button options as for acute repair — bridging the gap rather than forcing primary apposition under excessive tension.
11. **Irrigate and close in layers**: reapproximate the deltopectoral interval loosely, close subcutaneous tissue and skin, and apply a sterile dressing.
12. **Apply the postoperative brace/sling** with the arm in a position of adduction and restricted external rotation/extension to protect the repair before the patient leaves the OR.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Actively separate and identify the sternal and clavicular heads early in the exposure — they are frequently retracted to different degrees and can be mistaken for a single confluent structure if not deliberately teased apart.
- Use a locking stitch (Krackow or similar) rather than simple sutures through the tendon stump — pectoralis major tendon under athletic loading conditions demands high suture pull-through resistance.
- Favor cortical button or bicortical transosseous fixation over unicortical suture anchors alone when maximal construct strength is a priority (competitive athletes, heavy manual laborers) — this reflects the high failure loads demanded of this repair.
- Tension the repair with the arm in adduction and slight internal rotation, then dynamically test through abduction/extension/external rotation before closing — this catches an undertensioned or gap-prone repair while it can still be revised.
- For chronic cases, do not force primary tendon-to-tendon apposition under high tension if a gap is present — bridge with allograft instead; excessive tension on a marginal primary repair is a more common cause of early failure than the decision to use a graft.
- Counsel and document anabolic steroid use directly and early — it affects both surgical planning (tissue quality expectations) and postoperative counseling regarding re-rupture risk.
- Emphasize early referral to referring physicians/trainers/team staff when pectoralis major rupture is suspected — the single largest lever on ultimate outcome is getting the patient to acute repair within the 3–4 week window, not a specific technical maneuver.
- When in doubt about tendon gap size intraoperatively, have allograft available even for a case planned as acute primary repair — retraction is sometimes greater than anticipated on preoperative imaging.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Failing to identify and repair both sternal and clavicular heads separately when both are torn is a specific, avoidable pitfall — partial repair of only the more visible head under-restores strength.
- Re-rupture is more common after chronic/allograft reconstruction than after acute primary repair; steroid users and delayed presenters carry elevated risk.
- Time-to-repair is the single most consistent predictor of outcome — early referral for suspected pectoralis major rupture (rather than delayed presentation) is the key practical lever available to improve outcomes, and is a message worth relaying to referring physicians/trainers.
- Chronic/allograft reconstruction outcomes are still favorable overall but generally somewhat inferior to acute primary repair, with higher complication rates (particularly re-rupture).
- Overtensioning the repair during fixation restricts extension/external rotation and risks early failure under normal shoulder motion; undertensioning leaves a mechanically weak gap — dynamic intraoperative testing through a functional range of motion helps catch either error before closure.
- Missing a concomitant partial tear of the uninvolved head (assuming a "complete" rupture is limited to one head when both are partially involved) under-restores strength if not addressed.
- Hematoma formation is common given the vascularity of the region — meticulous hemostasis before closure reduces postoperative swelling, pain, and infection risk.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Sling/abduction brace 4–6 weeks with restricted external rotation and extension to protect the repair.
- Passive ROM early; active ROM from ~6 weeks.
- Resisted internal rotation/adduction strengthening deferred to ~10–12 weeks.
- Return to heavy bench-press/contact sport typically 4–6 months, with some protocols extending to 6–9 months for allograft reconstructions.
