## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Prophylactic fixation for Mirels ≥8 (or the gray-zone Mirels 7, by clinical judgment) metastatic long-bone lesions, or impending/completed pathologic fracture.
- Diaphyseal long-bone lesions, where intramedullary nailing is generally preferred over plate-and-screw constructs because it is load-sharing, allows immediate weight-bearing, and prophylactically protects the entire bone length against subsequent lesions.
- Long-bone pathologic fracture in multiple myeloma, treated with the same biomechanical principles as metastatic disease.

**Contraindications / better served by an alternative construct**

- Endoprosthetic replacement (e.g., proximal femoral replacement) is favored instead of nailing/fixation for extensively destroyed peri-articular bone (especially proximal femur/femoral neck) where fixation constructs would have insufficient purchase or unacceptable failure risk.
- Plate-and-screw constructs with cement augmentation are used instead for peri-articular and upper-extremity lesions not amenable to nailing or replacement.
- Active systemic sepsis, uncorrected severe coagulopathy, or a patient too medically unstable to tolerate anesthesia are contraindications to elective prophylactic fixation — stabilize medically first when time allows, or coordinate with medical/radiation oncology on urgency versus risk.
- Very limited life expectancy (days) with an asymptomatic, low-risk lesion may not warrant surgery — individualize against goals of care.
- Extensive articular/peri-articular destruction where a nail would have inadequate distal or proximal fixation purchase — plan an alternative construct rather than forcing a nail into unsuitable bone.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the Mirels score (site, pain, lesion type, % cortical destruction) and lesion characteristics on plain radiographs plus cross-sectional imaging (CT for cortical detail, MRI for marrow/soft-tissue extent) to select the fixation strategy.
- Assess overall systemic disease status, expected survival, and planned systemic/radiation therapy timing with medical and radiation oncology — this affects both urgency and construct choice.
- Template nail length and diameter to span the entire bone from a proximal to a distal fixation point, accounting for any planned interlocking screw pattern; oversize the canal reamed slightly to accommodate a robust nail given the compromised bone.
- Obtain baseline labs relevant to bleeding/coagulation and, for suspected hypervascular primaries (renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular carcinoma), coordinate preoperative selective arterial embolization (typically within 24–48 hours of surgery) to reduce intraoperative blood loss.
- For acetabular involvement in a patient also needing hip-adjacent fixation, obtain dedicated pelvic imaging and classify by the Harrington classification to plan reconstruction complexity in advance (curettage + cement + standard cup vs cage/triflange/custom reconstruction).
- For suspected or known spinal involvement (myeloma, widespread metastatic disease), assess spinal stability with the SINS score and coordinate with spine surgery if instability or neurologic compromise from epidural disease is present.
- Cross-match blood appropriately — metastatic lesions, especially from renal cell or thyroid primaries, can bleed briskly from reamed canal and lesion curettage.
- Confirm implant availability (nail length/diameter options, interlocking screw sets, cement for augmentation if a hybrid plate-cement construct is anticipated) before the day of surgery.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or regional anesthesia depending on the bone involved and patient comorbidity/medical fitness; consider regional techniques preferentially in frail oncology patients where appropriate to reduce systemic anesthetic burden.
- Positioning follows standard technique for the long bone being nailed: supine on a fracture table or radiolucent table for femoral nailing (antegrade or retrograde per lesion location), supine for tibial nailing, and lateral or beach-chair positioning for humeral nailing depending on approach (antegrade vs retrograde).
- Use a radiolucent table with a fluoroscopy unit positioned to allow unobstructed AP and lateral imaging along the entire length of the bone — confirming nail length and interlock position at both ends is essential.
- Prep and drape the entire limb circumferentially and extensile enough to address the whole bone, not just the lesion segment.
- Mark surface landmarks for the entry portal (piriformis fossa/trochanteric entry for femoral nails, patellar tendon-splitting or medial parapatellar for tibial nails, appropriate entry point for humeral nails) and confirm entry point trajectory with fluoroscopy before making the incision.
- Have blood products available in the room per preoperative cross-match, particularly for known hypervascular metastases.
- If pathologic fracture through a completed break is present, position and drape to allow closed or limited-open reduction under fluoroscopic control before nail passage.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Confirm the indication via Mirels score (≥8, or 7 with supporting clinical factors such as peritrochanteric location or functional pain) or presence of a completed pathologic fracture.
2. Select the construct: intramedullary nail for diaphyseal lesions; endoprosthetic replacement for extensively destroyed peri-articular bone (especially proximal femur/femoral neck); plate-and-screw with cement augmentation for peri-articular or upper-extremity lesions not amenable to the other two options.
3. Position, prep, and drape the entire limb; confirm entry portal location under fluoroscopy before incision.
4. Make the entry portal and establish canal access with a guide wire/awl under fluoroscopic guidance, exercising particular care where the entry site itself is weakened by tumor.
5. Ream the canal progressively, sending curetted/reamed tumor tissue for pathology when the diagnosis is not already established (a pathologic fracture or destructive lesion without a known primary should have tissue sent even during "routine" fixation).
6. Pass a guide wire the full length of the bone and confirm position on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy before reaming to final diameter.
7. Reduce any completed fracture under fluoroscopic control (closed manipulation, traction on a fracture table, or limited-open reduction) before passing the definitive nail.
8. Select and pass a nail long enough to span the entire bone — not just the lesion — from a proximal to a distal fixation point beyond any area of cortical compromise. A short segmental nail that leaves unprotected bone proximal or distal to the lesion is a recognized, avoidable cause of subsequent fracture at the nail end.
9. Place proximal and distal interlocking screws under fluoroscopic guidance; consider additional or angle-stable interlocks in severely compromised bone for extra rotational/axial stability.
10. Consider cement augmentation around the lesion site (injected through the nail's perforations or via a separate cortical window) in select cases where the surrounding bone is severely osteolytic and additional mechanical support is desired, coordinating this with the reaming/nailing sequence so cement does not obstruct nail passage.
11. For acetabular metastatic disease requiring concurrent reconstruction, proceed per the Harrington classification: curettage + cement + standard cup for lesser defects, versus cage/triflange or custom reconstruction for major column deficiency.
12. If preoperative selective arterial embolization was performed for a hypervascular metastasis (renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular carcinoma), confirm it was completed within the planned window and proceed with heightened attention to hemostasis regardless, as embolization reduces but does not eliminate bleeding risk.
13. For multiple myeloma or metastatic disease with spinal instability (SINS score) or neurologic compromise from epidural disease, coordinate decompression and instrumented stabilization as an adjunct (or separate staged procedure), generally combined with radiation and systemic therapy.
14. Confirm final construct alignment, rotation (clinically and fluoroscopically), and interlock position in both planes before closure.
15. Close in layers over a drain if reaming/curettage produced significant intramedullary bleeding; obtain a post-fixation radiograph of the entire bone before leaving the room.
16. Plan post-fixation radiation, typically initiated ~2–3 weeks after surgery once wound healing allows, to reduce local tumor progression and construct failure.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always template and pass the nail to span the entire bone, not just the visible lesion — this is the single highest-yield technical decision in this procedure and directly prevents a well-documented failure mode (fracture at an unprotected nail end).
- Send curetted or reamed material for pathology whenever the primary diagnosis is not firmly established preoperatively — fixation surgery is often the only tissue-sampling opportunity in a patient who will not otherwise be biopsied.
- Consider preoperative embolization for known or suspected hypervascular primaries (renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular) — it measurably reduces intraoperative blood loss and should be scheduled close enough to surgery (within 24–48 hours) to remain effective.
- Use a fracture table or reliable traction setup for femoral shaft lesions to maintain length and rotation during reaming and nail passage, since pathologic bone offers little inherent stability to resist shortening.
- In severely osteolytic bone, consider cement augmentation around the nail at the lesion site for added mechanical support, especially when postoperative radiation (which does not add mechanical strength acutely) is planned.
- Cross the entire bone with interlocking screws placed in solid, non-lesional bone whenever possible, and consider additional interlocks in markedly compromised segments for extra fixation points.
- Coordinate the timing of surgery with chemotherapy cycles and counts (avoid operating during a neutropenic nadir when feasible) and with radiation oncology so post-fixation radiation can start promptly once the wound allows.
- When multiple lesions are present along the same bone or in adjacent segments, plan the single nail to protect all of them simultaneously rather than treating the "worst" lesion in isolation.
- Obtain a full-length post-fixation radiograph before the patient leaves the room to confirm no unprotected segment was missed — this is easy to overlook when working under time pressure with a single fluoroscopic image intensifier.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Nailing only the lesion segment rather than the entire bone is a recognized, avoidable cause of subsequent fracture at the nail end — always protect the full bone length in a load-sharing construct.
- Risk of nonunion is elevated when fixing a completed pathologic fracture compared with a fracture through normal bone, particularly if radiation has been given to the site — this is the central rationale for prophylactic fixation thresholds (Mirels) rather than waiting for fracture.
- Endoprosthetic replacement rather than fixation should be chosen when peri-articular bone destruction is extensive enough that a fixation construct would have insufficient purchase — attempting fixation in this setting risks unacceptable failure.
- Acetabular reconstruction complexity should be assessed formally with the Harrington classification rather than judged intraoperatively without a framework — major column deficiency requires cage/triflange or custom reconstruction, not standard cup fixation.
- Brisk intraoperative bleeding from a hypervascular lesion (renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular carcinoma) can occur on reaming/curettage even after preoperative embolization — have blood products available and be prepared to abbreviate the procedure if hemodynamically necessary.
- Malrotation is easy to miss in pathologic femur fractures fixed on a fracture table, since the usual visual/tactile cues from healthy cortical bone are degraded — check rotation clinically (comparing to the contralateral limb) as well as fluoroscopically before final interlock placement.
- Reaming can push tumor debris systemically (fat/tumor embolization) — be alert for intraoperative hemodynamic or respiratory changes, particularly in patients with extensive medullary tumor burden.
- Failure to coordinate with medical/radiation oncology can result in either unnecessary delay of a needed prophylactic fixation or surgery scheduled during a neutropenic/thrombocytopenic window, raising infection and bleeding risk respectively.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Protection | 0–2 weeks | WB as tolerated (load-sharing construct) | Wound care, extended DVT prophylaxis, pain control, chemo-cycle coordination if applicable |
| 2 — Early mobilization | 2–6 weeks | Continue WB as tolerated | Neutropenia checks before advancing high-fall-risk activity in chemo patients; early gait training |
| 3 — Progressive loading | 6–12 weeks | Full WB | Gait normalization, home exercise program |
| 4 — Strengthening & return to function | 3–6 months | Full WB established | Goals individualized around prognosis and systemic therapy tolerance rather than a fixed timeline |

Post-fixation radiation is typically initiated ~2–3 weeks after surgery once wound healing allows. Metastatic bone disease patients prioritize immediate mobilization and pain control over strict phase adherence, given competing survival and quality-of-life considerations. Return to work: sedentary work typically 6–12 weeks after IM nailing once wound-healed and pain-controlled; physical labor delayed 4–6 months or longer and may never be fully regained after proximal femoral/pelvic endoprosthetic reconstruction. Systemic anticancer therapy plus a bone-modifying agent (denosumab or zoledronic acid) reduces skeletal-related events and should continue alongside surgical recovery; denosumab does not require renal dose adjustment, an advantage in myeloma patients with renal impairment, but carries osteonecrosis-of-the-jaw and rebound-hypercalcemia risk on discontinuation.
