## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Progressive collapsing foot deformity (PCFD) / tibialis posterior dysfunction (TPD) that has failed a structured 3–6 month trial of orthotic/brace management (custom UCBL or semi-rigid orthotic, gastroc-soleus stretching, PT for PTT strengthening) in a compliant patient.
- The specific construct is selected additively based on which components of the PCFD consensus classification are present — hindfoot valgus, talonavicular (TN) uncoverage/peritalar subluxation, forefoot abduction, forefoot varus/medial column instability, ankle valgus/deltoid insufficiency — and whether each is flexible or rigid/arthritic.
- Talonavicular uncoverage >40% is a threshold favoring the addition of lateral column lengthening to a base medializing calcaneal osteotomy (MCO) + FDL transfer construct.
- Presence of arthritis or fixed/rigid deformity in any given joint converts the plan for that joint from joint-preserving (osteotomy/tendon transfer) to arthrodesis.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Inadequate conservative trial (<3 months) in a patient who has not been counseled on or tried orthotic/brace management.
- Active infection or skin compromise over planned incisions.
- Uncontrolled diabetes with neuropathy or significant peripheral vascular disease — evaluate for occult Charcot neuroarthropathy before attributing progressive flatfoot to PCFD/TPD alone, since the two can be difficult to distinguish and require fundamentally different management.
- Severe, diffuse triple-joint arthritis with poor bone stock in a low-demand or medically frail patient may be better served by bracing than a major reconstruction.
- Uncorrected inflammatory arthropathy (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis) as the primary driver of deformity should be co-managed with rheumatology, as ongoing synovitis will compromise soft-tissue reconstruction durability.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: weightbearing AP/lateral foot and ankle radiographs (talonavicular uncoverage percentage, Meary's angle, calcaneal pitch); hindfoot alignment (Saltzman) view for coronal hindfoot valgus; weightbearing CT (WBCT) increasingly used to more accurately classify each PCFD component (hindfoot valgus, TN uncoverage, forefoot abduction/varus, ankle valgus) than plain radiographs alone, and is particularly valuable in borderline or complex cases; MRI to assess spring ligament and deltoid ligament integrity when medial or ankle-level pathology is suspected.
- **Clinical staging**: single-leg heel rise, "too-many-toes" sign, double-leg heel rise for hindfoot flexibility, Silfverskiöld test to distinguish isolated gastrocnemius vs. gastroc-soleus equinus contracture, manual medial column dorsiflexion stress test for medial column hypermobility — perform these systematically and document each PCFD component as flexible or rigid before finalizing the operative plan.
- **Implant/graft planning**: MCO fixation (typically one or two large cannulated screws); FDL transfer fixation (interference screw, suture anchor, or bone tunnel technique into the navicular); lateral column lengthening graft — structural allograft (tricortical iliac crest) remains standard, with porous titanium wedge implants an evolving alternative reporting high osseointegration and maintained correction; Cotton osteotomy graft (structural allograft or autograft wedge) and plate/staple fixation; deltoid reconstruction graft (allograft tendon or FDL-based, per surgeon preference) when indicated; arthrodesis hardware (screws/plates) and bone graft for any joint being fused.
- **Equipment**: full osteotomy and fixation sets for MCO, lateral column lengthening (Evans-type calcaneal osteotomy saw/graft insertion instruments or CC distraction arthrodesis set), Cotton osteotomy, and arthrodesis (as backup even in a primarily joint-preserving plan, since intraoperative cartilage findings can change the plan); fluoroscopy for all osteotomy/fusion steps.
- **Case-specific planning**: sequence the additive construct mentally before incision — MCO is typically performed first to re-establish the hindfoot mechanical axis, followed by FDL transfer, then lateral column lengthening if indicated, then medial column stabilization, then deltoid reconstruction if indicated — since each step changes the alignment the next step is judged against.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: popliteal sciatic nerve block (± saphenous block for medial incisions) combined with general or spinal anesthesia/sedation is standard given the frequently multi-incision, multi-hour nature of these reconstructions.
- **Positioning**: supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip to internally rotate the leg and improve access to the medial and lateral hindfoot; the leg is prepped free from above the knee to the toes to allow full intraoperative manipulation and repeated clinical alignment checks (viewing the heel from behind, checking the "too-many-toes" sign intraoperatively) after each construct component is added.
- **Tourniquet**: thigh tourniquet, exsanguinated before inflation; given the length of these reconstructions, plan for possible deflation/re-inflation and communicate anticipated case length with anesthesia.
- **Fluoroscopy**: mini C-arm positioned to obtain AP, lateral, and calcaneal axial views without repositioning the leg — needed repeatedly throughout the case for MCO position, lateral column lengthening graft sizing/position, Cotton osteotomy wedge size, and final alignment checks.
- **Landmarks**: mark the course of the sural nerve and peroneal tendons for the calcaneal osteotomy incision, the navicular tuberosity and FDL course for the tendon transfer incision, the sinus tarsi/anterior calcaneal body for lateral column lengthening, and the medial cuneiform for Cotton osteotomy, before prepping.
- **Draping**: free-drape the leg circumferentially to allow repeated alignment assessment (standing simulated load is not possible intraoperatively, but manual hindfoot positioning and heel alignment viewing from the foot of the table are essential checks after each step).

## Step-by-Step Technique

Build the construct additively, adding components only as indicated by the deformity components present on preoperative classification and confirmed intraoperatively:

1. **Medializing calcaneal osteotomy (MCO)**: approach through an oblique lateral incision positioned posterior to the peroneal tendons and away from the sural nerve, carried directly to bone. Perform the osteotomy perpendicular to the calcaneal axis (or angled ~45° to the plantar hindfoot per surgeon preference) under fluoroscopic guidance, then translate the tuberosity fragment medially — typically at least 10 mm of displacement is needed to meaningfully shift ankle/hindfoot contact mechanics. Provisionally pin, confirm position on lateral and axial fluoroscopy, and fix with one or two large cannulated screws.
2. **FDL tendon transfer**: expose the FDL through a medial incision extending from behind the medial malleolus to the navicular; identify and harvest the FDL at the master knot of Henry (confirming it is indeed the FDL and not the FHL by the Linburg-Comstock connection/tenodesis check), then route it into a drill hole in the navicular tuberosity (or secure to the residual PTT stump/spring ligament complex), tensioning with the foot held in slight plantarflexion/inversion. Address equinus concurrently with a gastrocnemius recession or Achilles tendon lengthening (per Silfverskiöld findings) through the same or a separate posteromedial/posterior incision.
3. **Spring ligament repair/reconstruction** (when indicated): through the same medial exposure, assess the spring ligament complex for attenuation contributing to plantar-medial talar head sag; repair with direct suture/anchor technique if tissue quality allows, or reconstruct with allograft/autograft augmentation for more attenuated tissue, performed alongside the FDL transfer.
4. **Lateral column lengthening** (when TN uncoverage >40% or forefoot abduction persists after MCO): approach the anterior calcaneus through a small lateral incision anterior to the peroneal tendons and superior to the calcaneocuboid joint (for an Evans-type osteotomy), or expose the calcaneocuboid joint directly if performing a distraction arthrodesis (favored when the CC joint is already arthritic). Perform the osteotomy/joint preparation under fluoroscopic guidance, distract to correct forefoot abduction and TN uncoverage, size and place a structural graft (allograft or porous titanium wedge) into the defect, and fix with a plate or staple.
5. **Medial column stabilization** (when forefoot varus or medial column hypermobility persists after hindfoot correction): for flexible forefoot varus, perform a Cotton osteotomy — a dorsal opening-wedge osteotomy of the medial cuneiform, distracted and filled with a structural graft, fixed with a plate; for medial column instability/hypermobility, perform a first TMT (Lapidus-type) or naviculocuneiform fusion instead, denuding the joint surfaces and fixing with plate/screw constructs.
6. **Deltoid ligament reconstruction** (when ankle valgus is flexible and the deltoid is attenuated but the ankle joint is preserved): expose the deep and superficial deltoid origin/insertion, reconstruct using allograft tendon or an FDL-based technique, tensioning with the hindfoot held in the corrected position achieved by the preceding steps.
7. **Conversion to arthrodesis** (for any joint with fixed/rigid deformity or arthritis identified pre- or intraoperatively): denude the involved joint(s) — isolated talonavicular fusion is often sufficient since TN motion drives most hindfoot motion; double (TN + subtalar) or triple (TN + subtalar + calcaneocuboid) arthrodesis for combined disease; tibiotalocalcaneal (TTC) fusion when ankle arthritis/valgus coexists with rigid hindfoot disease; pantalar fusion for combined ankle + hindfoot + midfoot arthritis (rare, high morbidity, reserved for salvage). Reduce to a corrected, plantigrade position, provisionally pin, confirm alignment clinically and fluoroscopically, then achieve rigid fixation with screws/plates and bone graft any voids.
8. **Final alignment check and closure**: with all indicated components completed, recheck hindfoot alignment from behind, the "too-many-toes" sign, and forefoot-to-hindfoot relationship before closing; close each incision in layers, being mindful of skin bridge width between the medial and lateral incisions to reduce wound complication risk.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Sequence the construct in the order above — MCO first to reset the hindfoot mechanical axis — since each subsequent step (lateral column lengthening, medial stabilization, deltoid reconstruction) is judged against the alignment achieved by the prior step, not the original deformity.
- Achieve at least 10 mm of medial translation with the MCO; smaller displacements are less effective at shifting hindfoot/ankle contact mechanics and risk undercorrection.
- Keep the calcaneal osteotomy incision posterior to the peroneal tendons and mark the sural nerve preoperatively — reported iatrogenic sural nerve symptom rates after open medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy range from roughly 7–25%, making incision placement and careful retraction genuinely consequential, not a formality.
- Confirm the harvested tendon is truly the FDL (not FHL) during the transfer — the two tendons are adjacent at the master knot of Henry and a mistaken harvest compromises both the transfer and great toe flexion.
- Do not skip lateral column lengthening or medial column stabilization when the consensus classification indicates it, even if the MCO alone appears to visually improve the arch — undercorrection of forefoot abduction or medial instability is the most common cause of recurrent deformity after isolated MCO + FDL transfer.
- Address Achilles/gastrocnemius equinus concurrently, not as an afterthought — uncorrected equinus is a well-recognized driver of recurrence regardless of how well the bony correction is executed.
- When performing lateral column lengthening, err toward a conservative graft size and check TN coverage and forefoot alignment on fluoroscopy before final graft impaction — overcorrection here is a recognized cause of lateral column overload and iatrogenic varus.
- Consider a porous titanium wedge implant as an alternative to structural allograft for lateral column lengthening or osteotomy augmentation — evolving evidence shows high rates of osseointegration and maintained radiographic correction, though allograft remains the more established option.
- Plan skin bridges between medial and lateral incisions generously (at least several centimeters where possible) — concurrent multiple incisions materially raise wound complication risk in this patient population.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Undercorrection of forefoot abduction or medial column instability is the most common cause of recurrent deformity after isolated MCO + FDL transfer — do not skip lateral column lengthening or medial stabilization when the consensus classification indicates it.
- Achilles/gastrocnemius equinus must be addressed concurrently or correction will recur.
- Sural nerve injury is a recognized risk with calcaneal osteotomy — reported symptomatic rates of roughly 7–25% after open approaches underscore the importance of careful incision placement.
- Overcorrection to varus is a risk with lateral column lengthening — check alignment on fluoroscopy before final graft/fixation.
- Wound complications are more likely with concurrent multiple incisions — plan skin bridge width and staged/limited incisions where feasible.
- Nonunion at any osteotomy or fusion site is a risk, particularly with structural graft-dependent procedures (lateral column lengthening, Cotton osteotomy) — porous titanium wedge implants are an evolving alternative to structural allograft, with high reported osseointegration and maintained correction.
- Patient-reported outcomes do not always correlate tightly with radiographic correction — systematic review data show imperfect correlation between the two, supporting outcome measurement and counseling beyond radiographs alone.
- Missing an occult Charcot process masquerading as progressive flatfoot (particularly in a diabetic/neuropathic patient) leads to an inappropriate joint-preserving plan in a foot that actually needs superconstruct-level fixation — screen for sensory deficits before committing to a standard PCFD ladder.

## Postoperative Protocol

**Osteotomy/tendon transfer track (MCO + FDL ± lateral column lengthening)**: 0–2 weeks splint, non-weightbearing (NWB), elevation; 2–6 weeks NWB in cast or removable boot, with early passive/active-assisted ankle ROM initiated around 2–4 weeks if soft-tissue healing allows; 6–10/12 weeks progressive weightbearing in a controlled ankle motion (CAM) boot as radiographs show osteotomy consolidation; 10–12 weeks transition to supportive shoewear with orthotic/brace, begin formal PT for gait retraining and PTT/gastroc-soleus strengthening; return to low-impact work/activity by 3–4 months; return to sport/high-demand activity 5–6 months, contingent on radiographic union and functional strength symmetry.

**Arthrodesis track (triple, TTC, pantalar)**: 2 weeks splint NWB; 6 weeks NWB in cast (non-Charcot) — for Charcot/neuropathic patients extend NWB to 8–12+ weeks given impaired bone healing biology; serial radiographs (± CT if union uncertain) before progressing; gradual weightbearing progression over 4–6 weeks once union is confirmed; brace/orthotic support and PT for 3–6 months; return to sedentary work ~2–3 months, physical work/sport typically 6–9 months and only after solid union.

Return-to-work/sport general principles: sedentary work resumes earliest (often 2–6 weeks post-op depending on procedure); standing/walking-intensive work requires solid union/wound healing plus demonstrated weightbearing tolerance; return to impact sport requires radiographic union, full painless ROM, and strength ≥85–90% of the contralateral limb on functional testing.
