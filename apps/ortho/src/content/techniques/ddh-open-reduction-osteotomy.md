## Indications

Open reduction is indicated when closed reduction fails, in children presenting after walking age (roughly >18 months, though thresholds vary by center), or when a narrow safe zone or interposed soft tissue precludes stable closed reduction.

## Positioning & setup

Anterior (Smith-Petersen) approach is most common for isolated open reduction in infants/toddlers. Medial (Ludloff) approach is used by some centers in very young infants to avoid violating the abductor musculature, at the cost of a higher reported AVN risk in some series and a blind view of the acetabulum.

## Steps

1. **Select approach** — anterior (Smith-Petersen) for most infants/toddlers; medial (Ludloff) selectively in very young infants.
2. **Release the iliopsoas at the pelvic brim**, not through the joint, to avoid injuring the medial femoral circumflex vessels supplying the femoral head.
3. **Excise obstructing structures only as needed** to achieve concentric reduction — a redundant/hypertrophied ligamentum teres and inverted labrum, trimmed conservatively; over-aggressive capsulorrhaphy can itself restrict later remodeling.
4. **Add femoral shortening derotation osteotomy** for high dislocations (Tönnis III–IV or IHDI III–IV) or children walking more than ~2 years — it reduces pressure on the reduced head, corrects excess femoral anteversion, and allows reduction without excessive tension.
5. **Select pelvic osteotomy for persistent acetabular dysplasia** based on age and deformity:
   - **Salter innominate osteotomy** — redirectional, single-plane correction; requires a congruent, spherical femoral head and open triradiate cartilage.
   - **Pemberton acetabuloplasty** — incomplete osteotomy hinging on the triradiate cartilage, reshapes rather than redirects the acetabulum; useful for more severe dysplasia.
   - **Dega/San Diego osteotomy** — more versatile multi-planar reshaping; popular for neuromuscular and syndromic dysplasia as well as residual DDH dysplasia.
   - **Triple innominate or Ganz periacetabular osteotomy** — reserved for the older child/adolescent with closed/closing triradiate cartilage and residual dysplasia.
6. **When combining femoral shortening with a high (Tönnis IV/IHDI IV) pelvic osteotomy, accept modest intentional shortening** rather than fully correcting limb length in one stage — this reduces reduction tension and AVN risk; residual discrepancy can be revisited later if clinically significant.

## Pitfalls

- Release the iliopsoas at the pelvic brim, not through the joint, to protect the medial femoral circumflex vessels — injuring them is a direct route to iatrogenic AVN.
- Excise the ligamentum teres/inverted labrum only as needed; over-aggressive capsulorrhaphy can restrict remodeling.
- Do not attempt to fully correct limb length in a single stage when combining femoral shortening with pelvic osteotomy in a high dislocation — accept modest intentional shortening to reduce tension and AVN risk.
- A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found comparable long-term acetabular remodeling outcomes between well-executed closed and open reduction when concentric reduction is achieved — reinforcing that reduction quality, not method alone, drives outcome.

## Post-op protocol

- Weeks 0–6: immobilization (spica cast for combined femoral/pelvic osteotomy), no formal rehabilitation; monitor for cast complications; interval imaging to confirm maintained reduction.
- Weeks 6–12: cast change under anesthesia if planned; total immobilization typically ~10–14 weeks depending on age, procedure, and reduction stability.
- Post-cast (~week 12 onward): transition to a removable abduction orthosis, typically weaned over 2–3 months; gentle active range-of-motion play.
- Long-term: interval ultrasound/radiograph surveillance for acetabular remodeling through at least age 2, and periodic follow-up radiographs through skeletal maturity — a meaningful minority require secondary pelvic osteotomy years later even after successful initial reduction.
- Return to normal activity: after cast/brace removal and confirmed stable reduction (~3–4 months); low-impact activity once orthosis weaned, higher-impact play per surgeon judgment, typically by 6 months.
