## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Failed closed reduction (unstable, non-concentric, or requiring an unsafe degree of abduction).
- Presentation after walking age (roughly >18 months, though thresholds vary by center) — closed reduction success falls substantially and open reduction is often planned from the outset.
- A narrow safe zone of abduction, or interposed soft tissue on arthrogram (inverted labrum, hypertrophied ligamentum teres, pulvinar, constricted capsule) precluding stable closed reduction.
- Teratologic dislocation (arthrogrypotic, myelomeningocele-associated), which is fixed in utero and generally does not respond to harness or closed methods.

**Contraindications / relative considerations**
- Active infection or medically unstable child — defer until optimized.
- Extremely young, otherwise Pavlik- or closed-reduction-eligible hip — open reduction is a step up in morbidity and should not be first-line when closed methods remain reasonably likely to succeed.
- Severely compromised skin/soft-tissue envelope (rare) may alter approach choice or timing.

## Preoperative Planning

- Obtain current AP pelvis radiograph (Tönnis/IHDI grade) and, in older or previously treated children, cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI) to assess femoral head position, acetabular version, and any residual dysplasia in three dimensions.
- Decide the approach (Smith-Petersen vs Ludloff) based on age, prior surgery, and surgeon experience.
- Plan concomitant procedures in advance rather than deciding intraoperatively where possible: anticipate whether femoral shortening derotation osteotomy will be needed (higher Tönnis/IHDI grade, older/walking child) and which pelvic osteotomy (Salter, Pemberton, Dega/San Diego, or later triple/Ganz PAO) best fits the child's age and deformity pattern.
- Template the femoral shortening length (if planned) against the degree of proximal migration on preoperative imaging, and select fixation hardware (pediatric plate/screws sized to the femur).
- Have arthrography/contrast, fluoroscopy, and standard pediatric pelvic osteotomy instrumentation (osteotomes, laminar spreaders/wedge graft if using Salter, curved osteotomes for Pemberton/Dega) available.
- Consent should cover the possibility of adding or modifying the planned pelvic/femoral procedure based on intraoperative findings (e.g., femoral head sphericity, degree of tension after reduction).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia; consider regional/caudal block as an analgesic adjunct.
- Supine on a radiolucent table, pelvis level and secured, entire operative limb and hemipelvis prepped free (including the iliac crest if a pelvic osteotomy is planned) to allow full manipulation and rotation assessment.
- Position the C-arm for AP and, as needed, oblique/lateral pelvic views; obtain baseline images before incision.
- Mark surface landmarks: anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), iliac crest, and the planned bikini-line incision one fingerbreadth below and parallel to the inguinal crease for the Smith-Petersen approach; for the Ludloff approach, mark the adductor longus/gracilis interval in the groin.
- If combining with femoral shortening, mark the lateral femoral incision as well and plan draping to allow access to both the anterior/pelvic field and the lateral femoral field without re-prepping.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Select approach** — anterior (Smith-Petersen, via a bikini-line incision along the iliac crest and down toward the ASIS) for most infants/toddlers and for any case in which a concomitant pelvic osteotomy is planned; medial (Ludloff, via the adductor longus/gracilis interval) selectively in very young infants when avoiding the abductor musculature is prioritized, accepting a more limited/blind view of the acetabulum and a higher reported AVN risk in some series.
2. **Develop the intermuscular interval** — for Smith-Petersen, the interval between sartorius and tensor fascia lata, protecting the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve as it crosses the field; split the iliac apophysis and elevate the abductors subperiosteally from the outer table of the ilium (and the inner table if a pelvic osteotomy is planned).
3. **Release the iliopsoas at the pelvic brim**, not through the joint, to avoid injuring the medial femoral circumflex vessels supplying the femoral head — this both improves exposure and lengthens a contracted psoas tendon that contributes to the deformity.
4. **Open the joint capsule** (typically a T- or Z-shaped capsulotomy) and identify the femoral head, labrum, ligamentum teres, transverse acetabular ligament, and pulvinar (fibrofatty tissue filling the true acetabulum).
5. **Excise obstructing structures only as needed** to achieve concentric reduction — a redundant/hypertrophied ligamentum teres and inverted labrum are trimmed conservatively; clear the pulvinar and release a tight transverse acetabular ligament as needed. Over-aggressive capsulorrhaphy or excision can itself restrict later remodeling.
6. **Reduce the femoral head into the true acetabulum** under direct vision, confirming a snug, concentric fit without excessive tension.
7. **Perform capsulorrhaphy** — imbricate/plicate the redundant capsule (a T-shaped or U-shaped capsular incision facilitates this) with the hip held in flexion and moderate abduction, tightening the capsule to help maintain the reduction; suture-anchor techniques are an option where capsular tissue is thin.
8. **Add femoral shortening derotation osteotomy** for high dislocations (Tönnis III–IV or IHDI III–IV) or children walking more than ~2 years — performed through a separate lateral subtrochanteric incision, it reduces pressure on the reduced head, corrects excess femoral anteversion, and allows reduction without excessive tension; fix with a pediatric plate.
9. **Select pelvic osteotomy for persistent acetabular dysplasia** based on age and deformity:
   - **Salter innominate osteotomy** — a complete osteotomy through the ilium above the acetabulum, hinged anteriorly at the pubic symphysis and redirected/held open with an iliac crest wedge graft; redirectional, single-plane correction; requires a congruent, spherical femoral head and open triradiate cartilage.
   - **Pemberton acetabuloplasty** — an incomplete osteotomy hinging on the triradiate cartilage, reshapes rather than redirects the acetabulum; useful for more severe dysplasia; typically stabilized with the wedge graft alone (no internal fixation needed since it is inherently stable at the hinge).
   - **Dega/San Diego osteotomy** — a more versatile, incomplete multi-planar reshaping osteotomy; popular for neuromuscular and syndromic dysplasia as well as residual DDH dysplasia because it can correct coverage deficits in multiple directions.
   - **Triple innominate or Ganz periacetabular osteotomy** — reserved for the older child/adolescent with closed/closing triradiate cartilage and residual dysplasia, where a hinge on the triradiate cartilage is no longer available/reliable.
10. **When combining femoral shortening with a high (Tönnis IV/IHDI IV) pelvic osteotomy, accept modest intentional shortening** rather than fully correcting limb length in one stage — this reduces reduction tension and AVN risk; residual discrepancy can be revisited later if clinically significant.
11. **Confirm final reduction and coverage on fluoroscopy** (and, if concern remains, intraoperative arthrogram) before closure — assess femoral head containment through a range of motion, particularly in the position the hip will be casted in.
12. **Close in layers** — capsule, reattach/repair the iliac apophysis and abductor origin, fascia, subcutaneous tissue, and skin; apply a spica cast (or combined hip/knee immobilization per the procedures performed) in a safe, moderately flexed and abducted position.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Release the iliopsoas at the pelvic brim, working from lateral to medial along the tendon, rather than hunting for it inside the joint — this keeps the dissection away from the medial femoral circumflex vessels.
- Protect the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve early in the Smith-Petersen approach; it crosses the sartorius-TFL interval superficially and is easily stretched or divided if not specifically identified.
- Clear the pulvinar and any redundant ligamentum teres before attempting reduction — a full true acetabulum is a common, correctable reason a head will not seat concentrically.
- Use a T- or U-shaped capsular incision for capsulorrhaphy; it is technically easier to imbricate symmetrically than a simple linear capsulotomy and gives a more reliably snug closure.
- When adding a Salter osteotomy, size the iliac crest wedge graft generously rather than under-filling the osteotomy gap — an undersized graft risks loss of the achieved redirection as the osteotomy settles.
- In a high (Tönnis IV/IHDI IV) dislocation, plan for staged or intentional partial correction of limb length via femoral shortening rather than forcing a fully anatomic length in one sitting — this is the single most effective way to keep reduction tension (and therefore AVN risk) low.
- Confirm concentric reduction dynamically through a range of motion intraoperatively (not just in the position of maximal stability) before deciding the cast position — a hip that only stays reduced in extreme positions is not a durable reduction.
- If using the Ludloff (medial) approach in a very young infant, accept its more limited visualization as the trade-off for avoiding the abductors, but have a low threshold to convert to an anterior approach if concentric reduction cannot be confirmed.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Release the iliopsoas at the pelvic brim, not through the joint, to protect the medial femoral circumflex vessels — injuring them is a direct route to iatrogenic AVN.
- Excise the ligamentum teres/inverted labrum only as needed; over-aggressive capsulorrhaphy can restrict remodeling.
- Do not attempt to fully correct limb length in a single stage when combining femoral shortening with pelvic osteotomy in a high dislocation — accept modest intentional shortening to reduce tension and AVN risk.
- The Ludloff (medial) approach carries a higher reported AVN risk in some series and offers a blind view of the acetabulum compared with the anterior approach — reserve it for carefully selected very young infants.
- Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (numbness/dysesthesia over the anterolateral thigh) is a recognized complication of the Smith-Petersen approach if the nerve is not specifically protected.
- Redislocation or residual subluxation after open reduction can occur from an inadequately cleared soft-tissue block, insufficient capsulorrhaphy, or excessive postoperative motion in a poorly molded cast — confirm concentric, stable reduction intraoperatively before closing.
- A 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found comparable long-term acetabular remodeling outcomes between well-executed closed and open reduction when concentric reduction is achieved — reinforcing that reduction quality, not method alone, drives outcome.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: immobilization (spica cast for combined femoral/pelvic osteotomy), no formal rehabilitation; monitor for cast complications; interval imaging to confirm maintained reduction.
- **Weeks 6–12**: cast change under anesthesia if planned; total immobilization typically ~10–14 weeks depending on age, procedure, and reduction stability.
- **Post-cast (~week 12 onward)**: transition to a removable abduction orthosis, typically weaned over 2–3 months; gentle active range-of-motion play.
- **Long-term**: interval ultrasound/radiograph surveillance for acetabular remodeling through at least age 2, and periodic follow-up radiographs through skeletal maturity — a meaningful minority require secondary pelvic osteotomy years later even after successful initial reduction.
- **Return to normal activity**: after cast/brace removal and confirmed stable reduction (~3–4 months); low-impact activity once orthosis weaned, higher-impact play per surgeon judgment, typically by 6 months.
