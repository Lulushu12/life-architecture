## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications.** In situ or reduced percutaneous fixation is used for Garden I–II (non-displaced/valgus-impacted) femoral neck fractures, and for displaced (Garden III–IV) fractures in young/biologically young patients (generally <60, extending case-by-case into the 60s–70s in physiologically young patients) where head preservation and avoidance of arthroplasty is the priority despite AVN/nonunion risk (nonunion 10–30%, AVN 10–40% depending on displacement and reduction quality). Two constructs are used: three cannulated screws in an inverted-triangle configuration, or the Femoral Neck System (FNS) — a fixed-angle sliding blade-and-screw construct.

**Contraindications/relative contraindications:**

- Displaced (Garden III–IV) fractures in low-demand patients over roughly 60–65 — arthroplasty is standard of care in this group given unacceptable fixation reoperation rates (see the arthroplasty technique guide).
- Fractures that cannot be reduced to an acceptable Garden alignment index (155–180° on both AP and lateral) by closed means and where limited open reduction is not feasible or desired — consider arthroplasty instead, particularly in older patients.
- Active local or systemic infection — treat the septic source first.
- Pathologic fracture through tumor — requires oncologic workup/planning rather than standard trauma fixation, unless urgent stabilization is required.
- Significant comminution of the femoral neck/head precluding stable fixation — favor arthroplasty.
- Pre-existing advanced ipsilateral hip osteoarthritis — arthroplasty is likely to be needed eventually regardless, so primary arthroplasty may be preferable to fixation followed by later conversion.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** AP pelvis and cross-table lateral hip; an internal-rotation AP view improves fracture-line visualization when pain allows. Measure the Pauwels angle on the AP view to anticipate shear demand on the construct, recognizing that this measurement is reduction-dependent and can be favorably changed by achieving an anatomic reduction.
- **Implant selection:** decide cannulated screws vs. FNS pre-operatively when possible, based on Pauwels angle, bone quality, and implant availability/cost — FNS is particularly favored for higher-shear (Pauwels III, >50°) patterns and for younger patients where a fixed-angle mechanism better resists varus collapse; cannulated screws remain a valid, cheaper, widely available default.
- **Equipment:** fracture table (or a radiolucent table with a well-leg holder) with a C-arm capable of unobstructed true AP and true (cross-table) lateral imaging of the femoral neck; cannulated screw set (typically 6.5, 7.0, or 7.3 mm) or the FNS instrument set as selected; guidewires, a washer if used, and a reduction clamp/joystick set available for cases requiring limited open or closed-assisted reduction.
- **Timing:** plan for urgent scheduling in displaced fractures in young patients — most surgeons target reduction within 6–12 h, though evidence for a hard cutoff is weaker than commonly taught; this population should not be deprioritized within the standard 24–48 h surgical-timing pathway regardless.
- **Blood management:** tranexamic acid dosing plan with anesthesia; crossmatch is rarely needed for isolated percutaneous fixation given typically low blood loss, but confirm per patient comorbidity.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** spinal or general anesthesia per patient factors; regional block (fascia iliaca/femoral nerve) as an analgesic adjunct is standard, especially valuable given this is often an awake/lightly-sedated-tolerant procedure in appropriate patients.
- **Positioning:** supine on a fracture table with the affected limb secured in a foot holder to allow controlled traction, internal rotation, and abduction/adduction for closed reduction maneuvers; the contralateral leg is positioned out of the fluoroscopic field (well-leg holder or scissored position) to allow an unobstructed cross-table lateral view.
- **C-arm setup:** position the C-arm to sweep between true AP (image intensifier over the hip, beam vertical) and cross-table lateral (beam horizontal, image intensifier lateral to the hip, beam passing beneath the contralateral thigh) without repositioning the base — confirm this arc is clear before draping.
- **Reduction check before prepping:** obtain provisional traction/internal rotation views confirming an acceptable or correctable Garden alignment index before proceeding; if closed reduction is unsatisfactory, plan for a limited open (mini-open, anterior capsulotomy) reduction rather than accepting a malreduction.
- **Draping and landmarks:** mark the greater trochanter and the anticipated percutaneous screw entry sites (typically along the lateral femoral shaft, 2–4 cm apart, starting roughly at or just distal to the level of the lesser trochanter) before incision; drape widely enough to allow conversion to a limited open approach if needed.
- **Tourniquet:** not used.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Reduce the fracture.** Attempt closed reduction first using longitudinal traction, internal rotation (typically 15–20°), and slight abduction under fluoroscopy; target a Garden alignment index of 155–180° on both AP and lateral views. Reduction quality drives outcome more than implant choice — an anatomically reduced fracture converts an unfavorable native Pauwels III (high-shear) pattern into a mechanically favorable construct.
2. **If closed reduction is unsatisfactory**, proceed to a limited open reduction through a small anterior (Smith-Petersen-type) capsulotomy, using a bone hook or joystick on the femoral head/neck fragment to achieve reduction under direct vision, then confirm on fluoroscopy before proceeding to fixation.
3. **Choose and set up the construct:**
   - **Cannulated screws** — plan three guidewires in an inverted-triangle configuration (two proximal, one distal, apex toward the calcar), positioned adjacent to the inferior (calcar), posterosuperior, and anterosuperior cortices of the femoral neck to maximize spread and pull-out strength while avoiding femoral head penetration. For Pauwels II (30–50°) patterns, use meticulous screw positioning including a posteroinferior screw specifically to resist shear across the fracture.
   - **Femoral Neck System (FNS)** — a fixed-angle sliding blade-and-screw construct; favored for higher-shear (Pauwels III, >50°) patterns, particularly in younger patients, where a fixed-angle mechanism better resists the tendency of the fracture to slide into varus that a parallel-screw construct cannot fully control.
4. **Make small stab incisions** over the lateral femoral shaft at the templated entry points, spaced to achieve maximal triangular spread within the femoral neck rather than clustering guidewires together.
5. **Place guidewires under simultaneous AP and lateral fluoroscopy**, starting distal/lateral on the shaft and directing each wire to its planned cortical landing zone in the femoral head — confirm each wire is central-to-slightly-inferior within the head on both views (never superior), analogous to the tip-apex-distance principle used in cephalomedullary nailing, and advance to within a few millimeters of subchondral bone without penetrating the joint.
6. **Check wire spread and divergence** on the AP and lateral views before drilling/reaming — the inverted-triangle configuration should show maximal, evenly distributed spacing within the neck, since tightly clustered screws provide inferior rotational and bending resistance.
7. **Measure depth**, then drill/tap over each guidewire as required by the implant system (many cannulated screw systems are self-tapping and do not require a separate tap step in cancellous bone).
8. **Place the cannulated screws (or FNS blade-and-screw) over the guidewires** to the templated depth, achieving interfragmentary compression for the cannulated-screw construct (partially threaded screws) while avoiding over-compression that could shorten/telescope a comminuted neck excessively.
9. **Confirm final implant position on both AP and lateral fluoroscopy** — central-to-slightly-inferior head position, adequate divergence/spread, no joint penetration, and no loss of reduction from the pre-fixation images.
10. **Assess construct stability intraoperatively** (gentle passive range of motion, if applicable to the construct/approach) before closing.
11. **Irrigate and close** the small stab incisions with subcutaneous and skin sutures; if a limited open reduction was performed, close the capsulotomy and layered incision in standard fashion.
12. **Obtain final intraoperative or immediate postoperative imaging** confirming implant position and reduction before the patient leaves the table.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Achieve the best reduction you can before worrying about screw configuration — an anatomically reduced Pauwels III fracture behaves better mechanically than a poorly reduced lower-angle fracture, so invest disproportionate effort in reduction quality.
- Space the three guidewires for maximal triangular spread within the femoral neck rather than clustering them centrally — spread, not just number, drives rotational and bending stability of the construct.
- Place the inferior (calcar) guidewire first as a stable reference, then triangulate the posterosuperior and anterosuperior wires around it — this sequencing reduces the chance of overlapping/crossing wires during placement.
- For Pauwels II–III patterns, prioritize a posteroinferior screw position specifically — this position most directly resists the shear vector across a steeply angled fracture line.
- Use fluoroscopy to confirm central-to-slightly-inferior (never superior) head position for every screw/blade, not just the first one placed — later wires can be inadvertently biased superiorly as the surgeon compensates for earlier wire positions.
- Do not over-compress a comminuted or osteoporotic femoral neck with lag-screw technique — excessive compression can shorten the neck and worsen post-fixation limb-length/offset outcomes without improving union.
- If closed reduction repeatedly fails, move to a limited open (mini-anterior capsulotomy) reduction early rather than accepting a marginal closed reduction — reduction quality matters more to outcome than avoiding a small additional incision.
- Consider FNS over cannulated screws pre-operatively (not intraoperatively) when the Pauwels angle is high and patient bone quality is borderline — the decision is best made with full pre-operative templating rather than as an intraoperative pivot.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Reduction quality is the dominant determinant of outcome — a mechanically unfavorable Pauwels III pattern that is anatomically reduced behaves better than a poorly reduced lower-Pauwels-angle fracture.
- Standard screw constructs have the highest fixation-failure/nonunion rate in Pauwels III patterns; consider a fixed-angle device (FNS or femoral neck locking plate) in this group.
- Screw cutout or failure of fixation is a recognized mode of failure requiring conversion to arthroplasty.
- Femoral neck shortening after fixation is associated with inferior hip function (FAITH trial cohort).
- AVN typically presents 1–3 years post-injury and requires long-term radiographic surveillance regardless of initial fixation success.
- Structures at risk: the lateral femoral circumflex artery and its ascending branches with overly proximal/medial percutaneous incisions on the anterior thigh — keep stab incisions along the lateral shaft as planned rather than drifting anteriorly.
- Clustered (poorly triangulated) screw placement provides inferior mechanical stability even when all three screws are technically within the femoral head/neck — spread matters as much as position.
- A superiorly placed guidewire/screw, even at an acceptable overall tip-apex-type distance, is associated with a higher cutout risk than a central or slightly inferior position — do not accept a superior trajectory on the theory that "it's still in the head."

## Postoperative Protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is increasingly favored even for fixation in the elderly, as fixed-angle devices tolerate it.
- Touch-down/partial weight-bearing for 6 weeks is still used by many surgeons for Pauwels III or comminuted patterns, advancing to full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks with radiographic union.
- Union is typically confirmed by trabecular consolidation by 12–16 weeks; obtain a delayed-union workup (labs, CT) if no radiographic progression by 4–6 months.
- AP/lateral films at 2, 6, and 12 weeks.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks once pain-controlled and independent with transfers/short-distance ambulation; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing.
- High-demand recreational activity/sport (relevant to younger patients) is generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.
