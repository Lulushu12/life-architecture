## Indications

In situ or reduced percutaneous fixation is used for Garden I–II (non-displaced/valgus-impacted) femoral neck fractures, and for displaced (Garden III–IV) fractures in young/biologically young patients (generally <60, extending case-by-case into the 60s–70s in physiologically young patients) where head preservation and avoidance of arthroplasty is the priority despite AVN/nonunion risk (nonunion 10–30%, AVN 10–40% depending on displacement and reduction quality). Two constructs are used: three cannulated screws in an inverted-triangle configuration, or the Femoral Neck System (FNS) — a fixed-angle sliding blade-and-screw construct.

## Positioning & setup

Fracture table or radiolucent table with fluoroscopy for AP and cross-table lateral imaging of the femoral neck. Reduction (closed or, if needed, limited open) must be achieved before screw/blade insertion — the implant does not itself correct malalignment.

## Steps

1. **Reduce the fracture** — closed reduction is attempted first; target a Garden alignment index of 155–180° on both AP and lateral views. Reduction quality is reduction-dependent for outcome even more than implant choice: an anatomically reduced fracture converts an unfavorable native Pauwels III (high-shear) pattern into a mechanically favorable construct.
2. **Achieve reduction urgently** in displaced fractures in young patients — most surgeons target reduction within 6–12 h, though evidence for a hard cutoff is weaker than commonly taught; regardless, this population should not be deprioritized within the standard 24–48 h surgical-timing pathway.
3. **Choose the construct:**
   - **Cannulated screws** — three screws placed in an inverted-triangle configuration; for Pauwels II (30–50°) patterns use meticulous screw positioning including a posteroinferior screw to resist shear.
   - **Femoral Neck System (FNS)** — a fixed-angle sliding blade-and-screw construct; favored for higher-shear (Pauwels III, >50°) patterns, particularly in younger patients, where a fixed-angle mechanism better resists the tendency of the fracture to slide into varus.
4. **Confirm central-to-slightly-inferior implant position** within the femoral head on fluoroscopy, analogous to the tip-apex distance principle used for cephalomedullary nailing.
5. **Assess construct choice against cost/availability** — cannulated screws remain a valid, cheaper, widely available default, particularly relevant in resource-variable practice settings; FNS offers comparable or modestly better functional scores and time-to-union with a shorter learning-curve-independent mechanism, at the cost of a single larger implant and higher hardware cost.

## Pitfalls

- Reduction quality is the dominant determinant of outcome — a mechanically unfavorable Pauwels III pattern that is anatomically reduced behaves better than a poorly reduced lower-Pauwels-angle fracture.
- Standard screw constructs have the highest fixation-failure/nonunion rate in Pauwels III patterns; consider a fixed-angle device (FNS or femoral neck locking plate) in this group.
- Screw cutout or failure of fixation is a recognized mode of failure requiring conversion to arthroplasty.
- Femoral neck shortening after fixation is associated with inferior hip function (FAITH trial cohort).
- AVN typically presents 1–3 years post-injury and requires long-term radiographic surveillance regardless of initial fixation success.

## Post-op protocol

- Weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is increasingly favored even for fixation in the elderly, as fixed-angle devices tolerate it.
- Touch-down/partial weight-bearing for 6 weeks is still used by many surgeons for Pauwels III or comminuted patterns, advancing to full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks with radiographic union.
- Union is typically confirmed by trabecular consolidation by 12–16 weeks; obtain a delayed-union workup (labs, CT) if no radiographic progression by 4–6 months.
- AP/lateral films at 2, 6, and 12 weeks.
- Return to sedentary work typically 4–8 weeks once pain-controlled and independent with transfers/short-distance ambulation; physically demanding work 3–6 months contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing.
- High-demand recreational activity/sport (relevant to younger patients) is generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.
