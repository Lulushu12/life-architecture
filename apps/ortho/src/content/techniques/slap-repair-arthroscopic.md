## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Snyder type II SLAP lesions (superior labrum + biceps anchor detached) in young/high-demand patients, especially overhead/throwing athletes, with a clear peel-back mechanism confirmed intraoperatively and failed structured physiotherapy.
- Type IV lesions may also be repaired depending on extent of biceps tendon involvement and patient age/demand.
- Type I lesions are debrided only (no repair); type III lesions have the bucket-handle fragment excised without repairing the (stable) anchor.
- Genuine structured PT trial (scapular kinetics, posterior capsule stretching, kinetic chain work) has failed, given the meaningful proportion of patients who improve without surgery.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Patient age >35–40 years, revision setting, or concomitant biceps pathology — biceps tenodesis is an increasingly favored alternative to repair in these situations, given comparable-or-better return-to-play and lower reoperation rates.
- A labral finding that does not demonstrate a true peel-back mechanism intraoperatively — many labral findings that appear SLAP-like on MRI are normal sublabral recesses, and repairing a non-pathologic recess is a preventable error, not a genuine indication.
- Untreated significant scapular dyskinesis or GIRD (>20° side-to-side internal rotation deficit) that has not been addressed with a structured kinetic-chain rehabilitation program — address these first, since untreated deficits predict recurrence/failure of isolated labral treatment.
- Glenohumeral instability requiring a separate Bankart or capsular procedure that has not been planned for concomitantly — an isolated SLAP repair will not address a coexisting anteroinferior instability pattern.
- Active infection, or inability to comply with the sling and delayed-loading rehabilitation protocol required for labral healing.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MR arthrography is the diagnostic study of choice for suspected SLAP pathology — it improves sensitivity/specificity for labral tears over conventional MRI and helps characterize the SLAP subtype and any concomitant pathology (subscapularis, biceps pulley, posterior labrum/Bankart).
- **Exam correlation**: no single clinical test is definitive for SLAP; use a cluster of provocative tests (Speed's, O'Brien's active compression, biceps load II, dynamic labral shear) alongside imaging rather than relying on any one finding, since individual test accuracy is modest.
- **Assess and document GIRD and scapular dyskinesis preoperatively** (internal rotation at 90° abduction bilaterally; scapular assistance/retraction tests) — these should be addressed with a structured PT program before and after surgery, and their presence/severity factors into counseling on expected outcomes.
- **Decision point to finalize before the OR**: confirm with the patient whether SLAP repair or biceps tenodesis is the primary plan if intraoperative findings support either, particularly in patients near the 35–40 age threshold, so intraoperative decision-making is not the first time this is discussed.
- **Equipment**: standard shoulder arthroscopy tower, suture anchors (typically 1–3 depending on lesion extent) sized for glenoid rim placement, suture-passing devices, and biceps tenodesis implants on standby in case intraoperative findings favor conversion to tenodesis.
- **Athlete-specific planning**: for competitive throwers, discuss realistic return-to-sport timelines and the position-specific data (baseball pitchers have worse return-to-sport rates than other overhead athletes regardless of technique chosen) as part of informed consent.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia combined with an interscalene regional block, as for other arthroscopic shoulder procedures.
- **Position**: lateral decubitus position is generally favored for SLAP repair, since it allows balanced arm traction that improves glenohumeral joint distraction and visualization of the superior labrum/biceps anchor, and facilitates dynamic intraoperative testing of the peel-back mechanism with the arm positioned in abduction–external rotation; beach-chair is an acceptable alternative per surgeon preference, particularly if concomitant subacromial/cuff work favors that position.
- **Table setup**: axillary roll under the down side if lateral decubitus, arm in balanced skin/skeletal traction (typically 5–10 lb) with the ability to reposition the arm intraoperatively into abduction–external rotation for peel-back testing without releasing traction entirely.
- **Draping**: standard wide shoulder arthroscopy drape from the base of the neck to the mid-humerus.
- **No tourniquet or fluoroscopy required** for standard arthroscopic SLAP repair.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: posterior "soft spot," acromion borders, coracoid, and planned anterior/anterosuperior portal sites — mark before prep to preserve orientation.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Establish the posterior viewing portal** and perform diagnostic glenohumeral arthroscopy, systematically inspecting the superior labrum, biceps anchor, articular cuff surface, subscapularis, and posteroinferior labrum for concomitant pathology.
2. **Confirm a true peel-back mechanism intraoperatively** with the arm brought into abduction–external rotation before placing any anchors — watch for the superior labrum and biceps anchor rotating medially over the glenoid rim; many labral findings that appear SLAP-like on MRI are normal sublabral recesses, and repairing a non-pathologic recess is a preventable error.
3. **Establish the anterior (and, if needed, anterosuperior) working portal** using outside-in spinal-needle localization to achieve an appropriate angle of approach to the superior glenoid rim for anchor placement.
4. **Debride the detached labral edge and the glenoid rim** at the SLAP lesion site lightly with a shaver or rasp to create a bleeding bone bed for healing, without excessively removing labral tissue needed for repair.
5. **Place suture anchors** at the superior glenoid rim (typically 1–2 for a type II lesion, positioned posterior and/or anterior to the biceps anchor depending on the peel-back pattern observed) to re-approximate the detached labrum/biceps anchor to the glenoid rim.
6. **Pass sutures through the labral tissue** using a suture-passing device, capturing adequate labral substance to prevent suture cut-through, and shuttle/tie (or use knotless fixation) to secure the labrum back down to the glenoid rim in its anatomic position.
7. **For type IV lesions**, extend anchor fixation or biceps tenodesis to the biceps-tendon-involved portion of the bucket-handle tear depending on the extent of tendon involvement and patient age/demand — a purely labral repair is insufficient if a meaningful portion of the biceps tendon itself is torn.
8. **For type III lesions**, excise the unstable bucket-handle fragment with a shaver or basket punch; the anchor is stable in this pattern, so anchor repair is not needed — confirm anchor stability by probing before deciding not to repair.
9. **Avoid over-tightening the posterosuperior labrum** during fixation — after final anchor placement, take the arm through abduction–external rotation again to confirm normal excursion has not been restricted; over-tightening can precipitate internal impingement symptoms postoperatively.
10. **Consider biceps tenodesis instead of repair intraoperatively** when the patient is >35–40 years old, this is a revision setting, or concomitant biceps pathology is present and was discussed preoperatively — convert to tenodesis technique at this point if findings support it rather than proceeding with a labral repair likely to underperform in this population.
11. **Address concomitant pathology** (subscapularis tear, posterior/Bankart labral extension, cuff pathology) at the same setting per the combined-pathology repair principle if present.
12. **Final inspection**: probe the repair circumferentially to confirm secure fixation without gapping, remove instruments under direct visualization, close portal incisions, and apply a sterile dressing and sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Test the peel-back mechanism with the arm actively brought into abduction–external rotation before placing a single anchor — this single step is the most effective way to avoid repairing a normal anatomic variant.
- Position anchors just posterior and/or anterior to the biceps anchor based on where the peel-back and detachment are actually observed, rather than a fixed default position — lesion extent varies and anchor placement should match the pathology.
- Capture generous labral tissue when passing sutures — thin bites are the most common cause of suture cut-through and early fixation failure in soft labral tissue.
- Recheck excursion in abduction–external rotation after final fixation, not just before — this catches an over-tightened repair while it can still be revised, rather than discovering restricted internal rotation postoperatively.
- Have biceps tenodesis instruments open and available for every SLAP case in a patient near or over the 35–40 age threshold — this avoids delay if intraoperative findings favor conversion.
- Address concomitant kinetic-chain deficits (GIRD, scapular dyskinesis) with a structured PT program both before and after surgery — surgery alone does not correct a kinetic-chain problem, and untreated deficits predict recurrence.
- In throwers specifically, counsel realistic expectations about return-to-sport before surgery, independent of whether repair or tenodesis is chosen — this is a patient-population effect, not a technique failure, and is best addressed at the consent conversation rather than after a slower-than-expected recovery.
- Use lateral decubitus traction to your advantage for dynamic peel-back testing — the ability to reposition the arm into abduction–external rotation under traction is a specific advantage of this position for SLAP assessment.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Over-tightening the posterosuperior labrum restricts internal rotation and can precipitate internal impingement postoperatively.
- Repairing a normal sublabral recess mistaken for a SLAP lesion is avoidable by confirming a true peel-back mechanism intraoperatively rather than repairing on MRI findings alone.
- Baseball pitchers have worse return-to-sport rates than other overhead athletes regardless of whether repair or tenodesis is chosen — set expectations accordingly rather than attributing a poor outcome to technique selection alone.
- Untreated kinetic-chain deficits (GIRD >20° side-to-side, scapular dyskinesis) predict recurrence/failure of isolated labral treatment in throwers — address these with physiotherapy before and after surgery, not just the labral lesion in isolation.
- A meaningful proportion of patients improve without surgery; ensure a genuine structured PT trial (scapular kinetics, posterior capsule stretching, kinetic chain) has failed before proceeding, given SLAP repair's nontrivial reoperation/failure rate in throwers.
- Thin labral suture bites risk cut-through and early construct failure, particularly in degenerative or attenuated labral tissue — take generous bites even if it requires additional portal access.
- Failing to address concomitant pathology (subscapularis tear, posterior labral extension) at the index procedure leaves a residual pain/instability generator that an isolated SLAP repair will not resolve.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Sling 3–4 weeks.
- Avoid active elbow flexion/supination against resistance and biceps loading for ~6 weeks.
- Passive ROM early; active ROM from ~6 weeks.
- Strengthening from 8–12 weeks.
- Throwing progression not started before 4–5 months.
- Full return to competitive overhead sport typically 6–9 months (throwers on the longer end).
