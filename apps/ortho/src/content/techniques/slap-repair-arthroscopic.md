## Indications

Snyder type II SLAP lesions (superior labrum + biceps anchor detached) in young/high-demand patients, especially overhead/throwing athletes, with a clear peel-back mechanism confirmed intraoperatively and failed structured physiotherapy. Type IV lesions may also be repaired depending on extent of biceps tendon involvement and patient age/demand. Type I lesions are debrided only (no repair), and type III lesions have the bucket-handle fragment excised without repairing the (stable) anchor. Biceps tenodesis is an increasingly favored alternative to repair, particularly beyond age 35–40, in revision settings, or with concomitant biceps pathology.

## Positioning & setup

Not detailed beyond arthroscopic access with the arm positioned in abduction–external rotation to intraoperatively test for a peel-back mechanism before committing to repair.

## Steps

1. **Confirm a true peel-back mechanism intraoperatively** with the arm in abduction–external rotation before placing any anchors — many labral findings that appear SLAP-like on MRI are normal sublabral recesses, and repairing a non-pathologic recess is a preventable error.
2. **Place suture anchors** at the biceps anchor/superior labrum to re-approximate the detached labrum to the glenoid rim (type II) or the biceps-tendon-involved portion of a bucket-handle tear (type IV, depending on extent).
3. **For type III lesions**, excise the unstable bucket-handle fragment; the anchor is stable in this pattern, so anchor repair is not needed.
4. **Avoid over-tightening the posterosuperior labrum** during fixation — over-tightening can restrict internal rotation and precipitate internal impingement symptoms postoperatively.
5. **Consider biceps tenodesis instead of repair** when the patient is >35–40 years old, this is a revision setting, or concomitant biceps pathology is present — systematic reviews show comparable-or-better return-to-play and lower reoperation rates with tenodesis in these situations.

## Pitfalls

- Over-tightening the posterosuperior labrum restricts internal rotation and can precipitate internal impingement postoperatively.
- Repairing a normal sublabral recess mistaken for a SLAP lesion is avoidable by confirming a true peel-back mechanism intraoperatively rather than repairing on MRI findings alone.
- Baseball pitchers have worse return-to-sport rates than other overhead athletes regardless of whether repair or tenodesis is chosen — set expectations accordingly rather than attributing a poor outcome to technique selection alone.
- Untreated kinetic-chain deficits (GIRD >20° side-to-side, scapular dyskinesis) predict recurrence/failure of isolated labral treatment in throwers — address these with physiotherapy before and after surgery, not just the labral lesion in isolation.
- A meaningful proportion of patients improve without surgery; ensure a genuine structured PT trial (scapular kinetics, posterior capsule stretching, kinetic chain) has failed before proceeding, given SLAP repair's nontrivial reoperation/failure rate in throwers.

## Post-op protocol

- Sling 3–4 weeks.
- Avoid active elbow flexion/supination against resistance and biceps loading for ~6 weeks.
- Passive ROM early; active ROM from ~6 weeks.
- Strengthening from 8–12 weeks.
- Throwing progression not started before 4–5 months.
- Full return to competitive overhead sport typically 6–9 months (throwers on the longer end).
