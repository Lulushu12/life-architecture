## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Stage-based salvage for scapholunate advanced collapse (SLAC) or scaphoid nonunion advanced collapse (SNAC) arthritis, per Watson staging.
- Reserved for patients who have failed activity modification, splinting, and NSAIDs, or who present with established arthrosis beyond the point where ligament reconstruction is viable.

**Contraindications**
- **Absolute**: active wrist infection; inadequate bone stock in the proximal capitate/hamate/triquetrum for 4-corner fusion or lunate fossa; radiocarpal arthrosis for PRC specifically (see below).
- **Relative**: PRC is contraindicated with capitolunate (midcarpal) arthritis (Stage III) — poor cartilage at the capitate head/lunate fossa predicts failure; low-demand elderly patients may be poor candidates for the more durable but stiffer 4-corner fusion when PRC would preserve more motion with acceptable durability; total wrist arthroplasty is relatively contraindicated in high-demand/heavy laborers given higher revision/complication rates than fusion; inflammatory arthropathy (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis) affecting multiple carpal rows may favor total wrist fusion over limited procedures given progressive disease.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging/staging**: PA/lateral/oblique wrist radiographs staged per Watson classification (I: radial styloid/distal scaphoid; II: entire radioscaphoid joint; III: capitolunate joint involved, radiolunate typically spared; IV/extended: pancarpal). CT is useful to confirm cartilage status at the capitate head and lunate fossa specifically, since this determines PRC candidacy and is not always reliably assessed on plain films.
- **Implant/graft selection**: for 4-corner fusion, select a dorsal circular locking plate or a headless-screw construct (surgeon preference; both are widely used) and plan bone graft (local from the excised scaphoid/distal radius, or iliac crest if needed) to pack the fusion site. For total wrist arthroplasty, template implant sizing on preoperative radiographs per the specific system's protocol and confirm adequate radial and third-metacarpal bone stock. For total wrist fusion, plan a dorsal plate spanning the radius to the third metacarpal.
- **Equipment**: standard hand/wrist set, oscillating saw, dorsal circular fusion plate/headless screw system, bone graft harvesting instruments, total wrist arthroplasty or fusion implant system if that pathway is selected, mini C-arm.
- **Case-specific planning**: confirm Watson stage and specifically capitolunate cartilage status before committing to PRC vs 4-corner fusion — this decision is best made with direct intraoperative joint inspection as the final check even when imaging suggested a stage.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

General anesthesia or regional block (supraclavicular/axillary); supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table, tourniquet inflated after exsanguination. Drape circumferentially to allow full dorsal wrist exposure. Position the mini C-arm for AP and lateral wrist imaging before final draping. Mark the dorsal longitudinal incision landmarks (Lister's tubercle, third metacarpal base to distal radius) before incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Expose the wrist dorsally.** Longitudinal incision centered over Lister's tubercle/third extensor compartment, transpose EPL radially, elevate a capsular flap to expose the radiocarpal and midcarpal joints, and directly inspect the capitate head and lunate fossa cartilage — this intraoperative inspection is the final determinant of PRC candidacy regardless of preoperative staging.

2. **Stage I — radial styloidectomy.** Resect the radial styloid (typically the distal 3–5 mm, staying ulnar to the radioscaphocapitate ligament origin to avoid destabilizing the wrist) to decompress the radioscaphoid articulation; combine with SL reconstruction or scaphoid excision as appropriate to the underlying diagnosis (SLAC vs SNAC).

3. **Stage II — choose between 4-corner fusion and PRC based on direct cartilage inspection from step 1.**
   a. **Scaphoidectomy + four-corner fusion (4CF)**: excise the scaphoid completely, decorticate the capitate-lunate, lunate-triquetrum, triquetrum-hamate, and capitate-hamate articular surfaces, correct any residual DISI by holding the lunate in neutral/slight flexion, pack the decorticated surfaces with bone graft, and fix with a dorsal circular locking plate or crossed/parallel headless compression screws across the four bones.
   b. **Proximal row carpectomy (PRC)**: excise the scaphoid, lunate, and triquetrum en bloc, leaving the capitate head to articulate directly with the radius' lunate fossa; smooth any irregular capitate head cartilage margins and confirm a congruent, stable articulation through a full range of motion before closure. PRC requires healthy capitate head and lunate fossa cartilage to succeed — confirmed at step 1.
   c. Decision factors: cartilage status (mandatory for PRC), patient age/demand (PRC favored in lower-demand/older patients, 4CF favored for higher-demand patients given more reliable durability under load), and surgeon preference/experience.

4. **Stage III — capitolunate arthritis present.** PRC is contraindicated; proceed with scaphoidectomy + 4CF as in step 3a.

5. **Stage IV/pancarpal — total wrist fusion or total wrist arthroplasty.**
   a. **Total wrist fusion**: decorticate the radiocarpal and remaining intercarpal joints, pack with bone graft, and span a dorsal plate from the radius to the third metacarpal, contouring it to position the wrist in slight extension (~10–15°) and neutral deviation.
   b. **Total wrist arthroplasty**: prepare the distal radius and carpal/metacarpal bed per the selected implant system's protocol, trial and confirm soft-tissue balance and implant stability through range of motion, then implant final components — reserved for lower-demand patients prioritizing motion preservation, given higher revision/complication rates than fusion.

6. **Adjunct at any stage — wrist denervation.** Perform partial denervation (typically posterior interosseous nerve neurectomy via the floor of the fourth extensor compartment, ± anterior interosseous nerve neurectomy via a separate volar incision) either as a standalone procedure in lower-demand patients with predominantly pain-driven symptoms, or as an adjunct to any of the above salvage stages for residual pain.

7. **Emerging adjunct — capitate resurfacing/pyrocarbon implant.** For selected Stage II/III wrists, a pyrocarbon capitate resurfacing implant may be used at the capitolunate/radiocapitate articulation as a motion-preserving alternative to fusion; this remains an emerging technique with early-to-midterm outcome series only and is not yet established first-line.

8. **Closure.** Repair the capsule, close extensor retinaculum loosely to prevent bowstringing while avoiding tendon entrapment, close skin in layers, and apply a well-padded splint/cast incorporating the wrist in the position appropriate to the procedure performed (neutral for PRC/4CF, slight extension for total wrist fusion).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Make the PRC-vs-4CF decision only after direct intraoperative inspection of the capitate head and lunate fossa cartilage — preoperative staging is a guide, not a substitute for what you see on the table.
- When performing 4-corner fusion, correct any residual lunate DISI (hold it in neutral/slight flexion) before final fixation — fusing the lunate in a persistently extended position perpetuates dorsal impingement and limits extension postoperatively.
- Stay ulnar to the radioscaphocapitate ligament origin when performing a radial styloidectomy — over-resection destabilizes the wrist by removing the origin of this key volar stabilizer.
- For PRC, smooth any irregular capitate head cartilage margins even in an otherwise "acceptable" joint — small osteophytes or cartilage flaps left in place can catch against the lunate fossa and cause early symptomatic wear.
- Position the wrist in slight extension (roughly 10–15°) when fusing for total wrist fusion — this optimizes functional grip and typing/keyboard positioning postoperatively better than a neutral or flexed position.
- Consider combining wrist denervation with any salvage procedure in patients with a strong pain (versus purely mechanical) component to their presentation — it adds relatively little morbidity and can meaningfully improve postoperative comfort.
- Use local bone graft from the excised scaphoid (in 4CF) before reaching for iliac crest — it is usually sufficient volume and avoids a second donor site.
- Counsel patients preoperatively about the PRC-vs-4CF trade-off in terms understandable to them: PRC preserves more motion but is less durable under high load; 4CF sacrifices some motion for more reliable long-term durability, particularly in higher-demand patients.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- PRC requires healthy capitate head/lunate fossa cartilage — performing it in the presence of capitolunate arthritis (Stage III) leads to poor outcomes; verify cartilage status before committing to PRC over 4CF.
- Failed PRC can leave persistent radiocapitate/radiolunate arthritis; failed 4CF can result in nonunion or adjacent-joint arthritis — either may ultimately require conversion to total wrist fusion.
- Recent meta-analyses (2024) trend toward PRC showing comparable-or-superior motion and equivalent-to-superior functional outcomes versus 4CF in appropriately selected Stage II wrists, but 4CF still retains the advantage where midcarpal cartilage is already compromised — the choice is not purely a matter of surgeon preference.
- Total wrist arthroplasty carries higher revision/complication rates than fusion and should be reserved for lower-demand patients where motion preservation is prioritized over durability.
- Over-resection of the radial styloid beyond the radioscaphocapitate ligament origin destabilizes the wrist — a common technical error when styloidectomy is performed more aggressively than needed.
- Residual DISI left uncorrected at 4-corner fusion perpetuates dorsal impingement and limits postoperative wrist extension.
- Fusing total wrist fusion in a non-functional position (excessive flexion or extension) compromises postoperative hand function even with a solid fusion mass.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not provide a stage-specific rehabilitation table for salvage procedures. General principles for this diagnostic cluster apply: certified hand therapy input from the earliest safe point (edema control, scar management, desensitization); a graded return-to-work/return-to-sport protocol (light duty at union/fusion onset → progressive loading → unrestricted at confirmed radiographic union/fusion and functional strength ≥90% of contralateral side) is preferred over a fixed calendar date. As a general benchmark from related fusion/reconstruction procedures in this cluster, protected immobilization phases run 6–12+ weeks (longer for fusion procedures until trabecular bridging is confirmed on CT), with return to heavy labor typically at 3–4 months and full unrestricted use later depending on the specific salvage procedure performed.
