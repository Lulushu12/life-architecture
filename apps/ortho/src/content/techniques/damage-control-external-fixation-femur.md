## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**

- Physiologically unstable or "borderline" polytrauma patients in whom definitive prolonged femoral nailing (with its reaming, embolic load, and operative time/blood loss) risks acting as a "second hit," worsening systemic inflammatory response, coagulopathy, and ARDS/multi-organ failure risk.
- Any femoral shaft or distal femur fracture that must be provisionally stabilized while a higher-priority procedure (laparotomy, thoracotomy, neurosurgical decompression) proceeds, or while resuscitation is ongoing.
- Severely contaminated open fractures (Gustilo IIIB/C) where definitive internal fixation is deferred pending debridement and soft-tissue coverage planning.
- Vascular injury requiring temporary shunting — rapid skeletal stabilization protects the vascular repair and must precede formal reconstruction.
- Bilateral femur fractures in the unstable/borderline patient, as a bridge to staged sequential or simultaneous conversion nailing once physiology allows.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**

- Stable, resuscitated patients with a straightforward isolated femoral shaft fracture — definitive nailing within 24 h (early total care) is preferred and does not require a damage-control interim step.
- Planned pin sites overlying a large open wound, planned flap territory, or planned definitive plate/nail incision should be modified in the frame's design to avoid pin-tract contamination of the definitive surgical field.
- Severe local soft-tissue loss or bone loss precluding safe percutaneous pin placement at the standard corridor — consider an alternative fixator configuration or site.
- Known allergy/reaction to fixator pin material (rare) — plan alternative pin composition.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: portable AP/lateral of the femur (full length, hip to knee) is generally sufficient to plan pin trajectories and frame configuration; detailed CT/articular imaging can be deferred until after damage control unless a concurrent decision (e.g., vascular repair planning) requires it urgently.
- **Frame selection**: choose a spanning (knee-bridging, hip-to-tibia or femur-to-tibia) configuration if the fracture is very distal, comminuted, or associated with knee ligamentous instability; a non-spanning (femur-only) frame is preferable when the fracture pattern and pin purchase allow, since it preserves knee motion during the damage-control interval. Have both large-fragment (5-6 mm) Schanz pins and the corresponding clamps/carbon-fiber or radiolucent bars available, sized for rapid modular assembly.
- **Equipment**: pre-assembled or a modular external fixation set, a hand or power driver with the ability to pre-drill (particularly in dense cortical bone to avoid thermal necrosis), fluoroscopy for pin-site and reduction confirmation, and clamps allowing intraoperative adjustment of length/rotation/alignment after pin placement.
- **Physiologic staging**: confirm which category the patient falls into before entering the room — stable (early total care reasonable), borderline (multiple long-bone + thoracic/abdominal injury, hypotension responsive to resuscitation, coagulopathy, hypothermia, lactate/base-deficit not yet normalized — favor DCO), or unstable/in extremis (ongoing hemodynamic instability, severe coagulopathy, severe hypothermia <32-35°C, persistently elevated lactate — DCO mandatory). This classification, not injury severity score alone, should drive the decision.
- **Case-specific planning**: for bilateral femur fractures, plan pin sites and frame configuration for both limbs simultaneously so that a single OR setup and prep can address both without repositioning; coordinate with the general/trauma surgery team on OR sequencing if a concurrent laparotomy or other procedure is planned in the same setting.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard, coordinated with the trauma/anesthesia team as part of an overall damage-control sequence; the procedure is deliberately brief and should not by itself dictate anesthetic technique beyond standard trauma resuscitation anesthesia.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent (flat) table — a fracture table with traction is not needed and adds unnecessary setup time in a damage-control setting; gross length and alignment are restored manually and through the fixator construct itself, not via traction-table mechanics.
- **Draping**: wide prep of the entire limb circumferentially from iliac crest to below the knee (or ankle if a spanning knee-to-tibia frame is planned), keeping the wound (if open) accessible for simultaneous or sequential debridement.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: position the C-arm for full femoral length access before draping; confirm the image intensifier can reach both the proposed proximal and distal pin sites and the fracture site itself without repositioning mid-case.
- **Landmarks**: mark the anticipated proximal and distal pin corridors before incision. For the distal femur, commonly cited safe zones are approximately 90 mm ± proximal to the proximal pole of the patella and 3 mm medial for one sagittal pin, and approximately 55 mm ± proximal to the patella and 2 mm lateral for a second, with coronal-plane pins roughly 30 mm and 100 mm proximal to the lateral epicondyle — use these as a starting reference, always confirmed under direct fluoroscopic and, where feasible, open visualization to avoid the neurovascular bundle and joint capsule/synovium.
- **Tourniquet**: not routinely used; pin sites are typically remote from the fracture and definitive hemostasis is achieved locally at each pin site rather than via proximal tourniquet control.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the physiologic staging decision** (stable/borderline/unstable) with the trauma team immediately before proceeding — this determines whether damage-control external fixation, rather than definitive nailing, is the correct operation today.
2. **Position the patient supine** on a flat radiolucent table; prep and drape the entire limb widely.
3. **Manually restore gross length, alignment, and rotation** under fluoroscopic guidance before or during pin placement — an assistant applies longitudinal traction and corrects obvious angulation/rotation while pins are placed.
4. **Place the proximal pin cluster** (typically two pins in the proximal femur, or across the pelvis/proximal femur if a hip-spanning configuration is chosen) using a stab incision, blunt dissection to bone to protect soft tissue, and a pin sleeve/trocar to guide safe, centered placement; pre-drill in dense cortical bone to avoid thermal necrosis, then insert the Schanz pin to bicortical purchase confirmed on fluoroscopy.
5. **Place the distal pin cluster** in the distal femoral metaphysis (or across the proximal tibia if a knee-spanning frame is chosen), respecting the safe corridors described above and staying well clear of the joint capsule, extensor mechanism, and the neurovascular bundle at the adductor hiatus/popliteal fossa.
6. **Attach connecting bars/clamps** to the pin clusters, then apply longitudinal traction and correct residual angulation/rotation through the frame itself before final clamp tightening — the fixator becomes the reduction tool at this stage, not just a stabilizer.
7. **Confirm final alignment** on AP and lateral fluoroscopy (or portable radiographs) — acceptable damage-control alignment is grossly restored length/rotation/angulation; anatomic reduction is not the goal and should not be pursued at the expense of speed.
8. **Tighten all clamps** to final construct rigidity, then re-check alignment has not drifted during tightening.
9. **Dress pin sites** and any associated open wound per institutional open-fracture protocol; document neurovascular status distally before leaving the OR.
10. **Total target time typically <30-45 minutes** — this is the operational definition of "damage control" and should guide pace throughout; do not allow perfectionistic reduction attempts to extend operative time in a physiologically unstable patient.
11. **Plan conversion.** Document a target window for conversion to definitive fixation (see below) and communicate it to the ICU/ward team so serial physiologic markers are tracked with that goal in mind.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Pre-drill before inserting large-fragment Schanz pins in dense femoral cortical bone — self-tapping insertion without pre-drilling risks thermal necrosis and subsequent pin loosening/infection.
- Use blunt dissection down to bone and a protective sleeve/trocar for every pin — femoral pin placement carries real risk to the femoral artery, vein, and profunda branches proximally, and to the popliteal neurovascular bundle distally; do not place pins by skin-to-bone power drilling alone.
- Place pins in a plane that will not interfere with the planned definitive fixation incision or implant trajectory (e.g., avoid the lateral distal femur if a locked plate is anticipated at conversion, or avoid the piriformis/trochanteric region if an antegrade nail is planned) wherever the injury pattern allows this foresight.
- When bilateral fixation is needed, set up and pin both limbs before final frame assembly on either side — this avoids re-draping or repositioning and keeps total anesthesia time minimal.
- Use the fixator bars/clamps themselves as the reduction tool: apply distraction and correct angulation through the frame after pin placement rather than trying to achieve perfect alignment purely with manual traction beforehand.
- A well-padded, briefly-used perineal post (if a fracture table is used for any reason) or, preferably, avoiding the fracture table altogether in favor of a flat table and manual/frame-assisted reduction reduces pudendal nerve and soft-tissue risk in an already physiologically stressed patient.
- Document distal pulses and a clear neurovascular exam immediately before and after frame application — this baseline is essential for later comparison, particularly if compartment syndrome or a missed vascular injury is subsequently suspected.
- Involve vascular surgery early and simultaneously (not sequentially) when a hard sign of vascular injury is present — do not let external fixation "finish" before shunting/vascular repair begins.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Pin-tract infection** risk rises appreciably with external fixator dwell time beyond 2-3 weeks — do not delay conversion to definitive fixation once physiology allows.
- **Neurovascular injury from pin placement** — case reports describe vascular injury following femoral external fixation pin placement; adhere to safe corridors, use blunt dissection with a protective sleeve, and perform a thorough vascular exam both intraoperatively and before the patient leaves the OR so any abnormality can be addressed in a controlled setting rather than discovered later.
- **The DCO-versus-ETC debate** remains unresolved by high-quality randomized evidence; current literature (including a 2024 porcine polytrauma model) suggests DCO induces less trauma-induced coagulopathy than ETC, and a 2025 registry propensity-matched analysis supports a physiology-adapted (not protocol-rigid) approach.
- **Over-reliance on a fixed calendar-day protocol** — the operative decision should be individualized to the patient's real-time physiologic status rather than applied as a blanket rule based on injury severity score alone.
- **Delaying definitive fixation when the patient could tolerate it** — early definitive stabilization (<24 h) when tolerated is associated with a significantly lower risk of fat embolism syndrome (adjusted OR ≈0.42 in bilateral fractures); do not default to a fixed calendar-day DCO protocol when the patient could tolerate earlier definitive fixation.
- **Missed or inadequately treated vascular injury** — hard signs of vascular injury (absent pulse, active hemorrhage, expanding hematoma, thrill/bruit, cold pulseless limb) require temporary intraluminal shunting followed by rapid skeletal stabilization (external fixator, not a lengthy definitive procedure) to protect the vascular repair — warm ischemia beyond ~6 hours substantially increases amputation risk, so revascularization speed takes priority over anatomic perfection of the initial fixation.
- **Frame instability/loss of reduction** during the damage-control interval from inadequate clamp tightening or an under-braced construct — check and, if needed, reinforce the frame before transfer out of the OR and again if the patient requires prolonged ICU dwell time before conversion.
- **Pin-site placement conflicting with the definitive fixation incision or implant path** — plan pin trajectories with the anticipated conversion procedure in mind whenever the clinical situation allows that foresight.

## Postoperative Protocol

1. **Classify patient physiologic status continuously**: stable → early total care (ETC, definitive nailing within 24 h reasonable); borderline → favor DCO with close reassessment; unstable/in extremis → DCO mandatory until resuscitation targets are met.
2. **Monitor physiologic parameters serially** (lactate, base deficit, core temperature, coagulation panel) to determine readiness for conversion.
3. **Convert to definitive reamed IM nailing** once physiologic parameters normalize (lactate <2-2.5 mmol/L, base deficit corrected, core temperature normal, coagulopathy resolved) — most literature and practice favor conversion within roughly day 2-14, with many centers targeting under 2 weeks to minimize pin-tract infection risk.
4. **For bilateral femur fractures**: in a resuscitated, hemodynamically stable patient, same-session bilateral reamed antegrade nailing is safe and preferred (avoids a second anesthetic, allows symmetric early mobilization); in the unstable/borderline patient, bilateral damage-control external fixation followed by staged sequential (or simultaneous, if physiology allows) conversion nailing is appropriate.
5. Systemic complications of prolonged immobility/damage-control staging — pin-tract infection, pneumonia, pressure injury, deconditioning — all argue for converting to definitive fixation as soon as physiology allows rather than defaulting to a fixed calendar-day protocol.
6. After conversion to definitive reamed IM nailing, follow the standard femoral shaft nailing rehabilitation protocol (early mobilization, weight bearing as tolerated per construct stability, progressive ROM and strengthening).
7. Bilateral femur fracture patients require closer monitoring for fat embolism syndrome, ARDS, and VTE in the first 48-72 hours, and typically need bilateral simultaneous mobilization planning (two physiotherapists or a hoist, wheeled frame/platform walker) given the absence of a "good leg" to pivot on.
