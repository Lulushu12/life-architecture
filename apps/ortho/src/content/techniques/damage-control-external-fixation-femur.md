## Indications

Physiologically unstable or "borderline" polytrauma patients in whom definitive prolonged femoral nailing (with its reaming, embolic load, and operative time/blood loss) risks acting as a "second hit," worsening systemic inflammatory response, coagulopathy, and ARDS/multi-organ failure risk. Damage-control orthopedics (DCO) substitutes rapid, low-physiologic-cost temporary external fixation for definitive fixation, deferring the latter until the patient is resuscitated.

## Positioning & setup

Spanning or non-spanning external fixator, applied rapidly (typically <30-45 minutes) to restore gross length/alignment and allow ICU resuscitation, imaging, and further surgery (laparotomy, thoracotomy, neurosurgery) to proceed without the femur competing for OR time or physiologic reserve.

## Steps

1. Classify patient physiologic status: stable → early total care (ETC, definitive nailing within 24 h reasonable); borderline (multiple long-bone + thoracic/abdominal injury, hypotension responsive to resuscitation, coagulopathy, hypothermia, lactate/base-deficit not yet normalized) → favor DCO; unstable/in extremis (ongoing hemodynamic instability, severe coagulopathy, severe hypothermia <32-35°C, persistently elevated lactate) → DCO mandatory.
2. Apply a spanning or non-spanning external fixator rapidly (<30-45 minutes), restoring gross length and alignment.
3. Allow ICU resuscitation and treatment of other injuries to proceed without the femur competing for OR time.
4. Monitor physiologic parameters serially (lactate, base deficit, core temperature, coagulation panel) to determine readiness for conversion.
5. Convert to definitive reamed IM nailing once physiologic parameters normalize (lactate <2-2.5 mmol/L, base deficit corrected, core temperature normal, coagulopathy resolved) — most literature and practice favor conversion within roughly day 2-14, with many centers targeting under 2 weeks to minimize pin-tract infection risk.
6. For bilateral femur fractures specifically: in a resuscitated, hemodynamically stable patient, same-session bilateral reamed antegrade nailing is safe and preferred (avoids a second anesthetic, allows symmetric early mobilization); in the unstable/borderline patient, bilateral damage-control external fixation followed by staged sequential (or simultaneous, if physiology allows) conversion nailing is appropriate.

## Pitfalls

- Pin-tract infection risk rises appreciably with external fixator dwell time beyond 2-3 weeks — do not delay conversion to definitive fixation once physiology allows.
- The DCO-versus-ETC debate remains unresolved by high-quality randomized evidence; current literature (including a 2024 porcine polytrauma model) suggests DCO induces less trauma-induced coagulopathy than ETC, and a 2025 registry propensity-matched analysis supports a physiology-adapted (not protocol-rigid) approach.
- The operative decision should be individualized to the patient's real-time physiologic status rather than applied as a blanket rule based on injury severity score alone.
- Early definitive stabilization (<24 h) when tolerated is associated with a significantly lower risk of fat embolism syndrome (adjusted OR ≈0.42 in bilateral fractures) — do not default to a fixed calendar-day DCO protocol when the patient could tolerate earlier definitive fixation.
- Hard signs of vascular injury (absent pulse, active hemorrhage, expanding hematoma, thrill/bruit, cold pulseless limb) require temporary intraluminal shunting followed by rapid skeletal stabilization (external fixator, not a lengthy definitive procedure) to protect the vascular repair — warm ischemia beyond ~6 hours substantially increases amputation risk, so revascularization speed takes priority over anatomic perfection of the initial fixation.

## Post-op protocol

- Systemic complications of prolonged immobility/damage-control staging — pin-tract infection, pneumonia, pressure injury, deconditioning — all argue for converting to definitive fixation as soon as physiology allows rather than defaulting to a fixed calendar-day protocol.
- After conversion to definitive reamed IM nailing, follow the standard femoral shaft nailing rehabilitation protocol (early mobilization, weight bearing as tolerated per construct stability, progressive ROM and strengthening).
- Bilateral femur fracture patients require closer monitoring for fat embolism syndrome, ARDS, and VTE in the first 48-72 hours, and typically need bilateral simultaneous mobilization planning (two physiotherapists or a hoist, wheeled frame/platform walker) given the absence of a "good leg" to pivot on.
