## Indications

Reserved for severe (generally >50°), functionally significant, persistent excess femoral anteversion beyond the age when spontaneous correction would be expected — typically not considered before age 8–10, and often deferred to adolescence to allow maximal natural remodeling first. Observation remains first-line for the overwhelming majority of children, since anteversion decreases progressively through childhood and in-toeing resolves in the large majority by age 8–10 without surgery.

## Positioning & setup

Level of osteotomy is chosen by associated pathology: subtrochanteric for isolated torsional correction; distal femoral when combined with patellofemoral instability correction (e.g. concurrent MPFL reconstruction).

## Steps

1. **Confirm the deformity is severe and persistent** by clinical rotational profile (Craig's/trochanteric prominence angle test) ± CT/MRI rotational profile for surgical planning.
2. **Select osteotomy level** — subtrochanteric for isolated torsional correction; distal femoral if combined with patellofemoral instability correction.
3. **Perform the derotation** to correct the measured excess anteversion.
4. **Select fixation** — intramedullary nail versus plate; a 2026 randomized controlled trial found comparable union and complication rates between the two, giving surgeons flexibility to select based on local expertise and cost rather than a demonstrated superiority of one implant.
5. **Combine with MPFL reconstruction** at the distal femoral level when concurrent patellofemoral instability is present.

## Pitfalls

- Under-correction, assessed intraoperatively and postoperatively by clinical rotational profile, is the more common technical pitfall than over-correction.
- Nonunion or delayed union is uncommon with modern rigid fixation but a recognized risk at the subtrochanteric level given relatively lower vascularity.
- Implant-related symptoms requiring removal are more common with plate fixation than with buried intramedullary constructs — a factor in the ongoing nail-vs-plate discussion.
- A 2026 comparative series of surgical techniques for idiopathic excessive anteversion found several viable technique variants without one clearly superior in outcome, reinforcing that technique selection remains surgeon- and case-dependent.

## Post-op protocol

- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing (partial, per fixation construct — plate constructs are often more restrictive initially than locked intramedullary nail constructs), active-assisted ROM, avoidance of torsional/twisting loads across the osteotomy site.
- Weeks 6–12: progressive weight-bearing to full as radiographic union is confirmed; physiotherapy for hip and thigh strengthening, gait retraining to normalize foot progression angle now that the underlying torsional deformity is corrected.
- Months 3–4: return to full unrestricted activity/sport once solid radiographic union is confirmed and strength/ROM are symmetric with the contralateral limb; return-to-sport timing is broadly similar regardless of nail versus plate fixation.
- Return to school: 2–3 weeks (partial weight-bearing, crutch-assisted).
