## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Severe (generally >50°) excess femoral anteversion, functionally significant, and persistent beyond the age when spontaneous correction would be expected — typically not considered before age 8–10, and often deferred to adolescence to allow maximal natural remodeling first.
- Functional impairment attributable to the torsional deformity — recurrent tripping, cosmetically/socially significant in-toeing gait, or torsional contribution to patellofemoral instability/pain.

**Relative indications**
- Concurrent patellofemoral instability where increased femoral anteversion is a contributing rotational malalignment factor (addressed at the distal femoral level, often combined with MPFL reconstruction).

**Contraindications / first-line alternative**
- Observation is first-line and appropriate for the overwhelming majority — natural remodeling with growth resolves the in-toeing gait in most children by age 8–10. Surgery should not be offered before this natural-history window has been allowed to play out except in unusual, severe, functionally disabling cases.
- Active infection, medical instability, or a family/child unable to comply with the postoperative weight-bearing/rehab protocol are relative contraindications to elective correction.
- Mild-to-moderate anteversion without functional limitation, regardless of age, does not warrant surgery — gait training/physiotherapy and reassurance are appropriate instead.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm the deformity clinically with a rotational profile examination (Craig's/trochanteric prominence angle test, prone, knees flexed 90°) documenting increased internal rotation (often >70°) with reduced external rotation.
- Obtain CT or MRI rotational profile (femoral and tibial torsion, both sides) for precise preoperative measurement and surgical planning — clinical exam alone is not sufficiently precise for planning the exact degree of correction.
- Decide osteotomy level based on associated pathology: subtrochanteric for isolated torsional correction; distal femoral (supracondylar) when combined with patellofemoral instability correction (e.g. concurrent MPFL reconstruction), since distal correction directly addresses the rotational component of the extensor mechanism malalignment.
- Select fixation in advance: intramedullary nail versus plate, based on surgeon/institutional experience, femoral canal size (nailing may be technically limited in a very young/small child), and cost — a 2026 randomized controlled trial found comparable union and complication rates between the two, so this choice can reasonably be driven by local expertise.
- Template the degree of derotation against the measured excess anteversion and the desired clinical target (generally correcting into the normal adult range, ~10–15°, rather than over-rotating into relative retroversion).
- If combining with MPFL reconstruction, plan graft choice/sizing and tunnel positions concurrently.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General anesthesia, with regional block (e.g. femoral/adductor canal or fascia iliaca) as an analgesic adjunct.
- Supine on a radiolucent table; for subtrochanteric osteotomy, position similar to other proximal femoral osteotomies with a small ipsilateral buttock bump and the whole limb prepped free for intraoperative rotational assessment.
- For distal femoral osteotomy, ensure the knee can be flexed intraoperatively for fixation and rotational testing, with a bump or leg holder as needed.
- Mark the femoral shaft axis, the entry portal (subtrochanteric or distal, depending on level and fixation choice), and, if combined with MPFL reconstruction, the medial knee incision and femoral tunnel landmarks.
- Position fluoroscopy to obtain unobstructed AP and lateral views of the chosen osteotomy level, and confirm baseline rotational landmarks (e.g. lesser trochanter profile, femoral neck axis) before making the cut.
- Have a sterile rotational reference (marked pin in the distal fragment referenced to a fixed proximal landmark, or a goniometer taped to the limb) set up before the osteotomy is completed, since rotation must be controlled and measured the moment the femur becomes mobile.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the deformity is severe and persistent** by clinical rotational profile (Craig's/trochanteric prominence angle test) and CT/MRI rotational profile obtained for surgical planning.
2. **Select osteotomy level** — subtrochanteric for isolated torsional correction; distal femoral if combined with patellofemoral instability correction.
3. **Expose the femur** at the chosen level through a lateral approach, splitting/elevating the vastus lateralis and exposing the bone subperiosteally at the planned osteotomy site.
4. **Establish rotational reference marks** before cutting the bone — e.g., place a mark or pin proximal and distal to the planned osteotomy, aligned in the sagittal plane, so the exact degree of correction can be read directly off the marks once the osteotomy is mobile.
5. **Perform the osteotomy** — a transverse or short oblique cut at the chosen level, using an oscillating saw or osteotomes, taking care to complete it cleanly to avoid an uncontrolled fracture line that could compromise fixation.
6. **Perform the derotation** — rotate the distal fragment relative to the proximal fragment by the preoperatively planned amount (correcting the measured excess anteversion, generally targeting the normal adult range rather than over-correcting into retroversion), confirming the correction against the rotational reference marks.
7. **Provisionally fix the fragments** in the corrected rotational position (guide pins, clamp, or the nail/plate itself depending on system) and confirm limb rotation clinically (foot progression angle, hip internal/external rotation arc) and on fluoroscopy before final fixation.
8. **Complete definitive fixation**:
   - **Intramedullary nail**: ream/prepare the canal as needed for the child's size, pass the nail across the osteotomy maintaining the corrected rotation, and lock proximally and distally.
   - **Plate**: apply the plate to the lateral (or as indicated) femoral surface spanning the osteotomy, achieve compression/neutralization as appropriate, and place screws bicortically.
9. **Reconfirm rotation and alignment on fluoroscopy** in both AP and lateral planes after definitive fixation, since fixation hardware can subtly shift the achieved correction.
10. **If combined with MPFL reconstruction**, proceed with graft harvest/preparation, femoral and patellar tunnel placement, and graft fixation at this stage, tensioning the graft with the knee at the surgeon's preferred flexion angle.
11. **Assess stability and range of motion** of the hip/knee before closure.
12. **Close in layers** — vastus lateralis/fascia over the fixation, subcutaneous tissue, skin.
13. **Obtain final intraoperative or immediate postoperative imaging** to document fixation position and achieved correction.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Set up a reliable rotational reference (pins or marks proximal and distal to the osteotomy) before completing the cut — once the femur is fully osteotomized, there is no anatomic landmark left to judge rotation by eye alone.
- Cross-check the corrected rotation both clinically (foot progression angle, hip rotational arc with the knee flexed 90° off the end of the table) and against the reference marks before final fixation — relying on either method alone risks a missed rotational error.
- Target correction into the normal adult anteversion range rather than aggressively over-rotating; under-correction is the more common technical pitfall than over-correction, but a deliberately modest, accurate correction is preferable to an aggressive one that overshoots into relative retroversion.
- When performing a distal femoral osteotomy combined with MPFL reconstruction, sequence the derotation and fixation before final graft tensioning, since limb rotation affects patellar tracking and therefore graft isometry.
- Choose nail versus plate based on the child's canal size and local expertise rather than a presumed superiority of one implant — outcome data show comparable union and complication rates between the two.
- If using a plate, anticipate that implant-related irritation is more likely to prompt hardware removal than with a buried intramedullary nail — counsel families about this possibility at the time of surgery.
- Complete the osteotomy cleanly with a controlled saw cut rather than osteotomes alone at the subtrochanteric level, where an uncontrolled crack can propagate into the surrounding metaphyseal bone and complicate fixation.
- Recheck rotation on fluoroscopy after the fixation device is fully seated — plates and nails can subtly torque the fragment during final tightening/locking, silently eroding the correction achieved during provisional fixation.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Under-correction**, assessed intraoperatively and postoperatively by clinical rotational profile, is the more common technical pitfall than over-correction.
- **Nonunion or delayed union** is uncommon with modern rigid fixation but a recognized risk at the subtrochanteric level given relatively lower vascularity.
- **Implant-related symptoms requiring removal** are more common with plate fixation than with buried intramedullary constructs — a factor in the ongoing nail-vs-plate discussion.
- **Loss of correction during final fixation tightening** — reconfirm rotation on fluoroscopy after the implant is fully seated, not only during provisional fixation.
- **Neurovascular injury** is uncommon at either osteotomy level with careful subperiosteal exposure but should be considered when placing distal locking screws (proximity to the popliteal neurovascular bundle at the distal femoral level) or working near the profunda femoris perforators proximally.
- A 2026 comparative series of surgical techniques for idiopathic excessive anteversion found several viable technique variants without one clearly superior in outcome, reinforcing that technique selection remains surgeon- and case-dependent.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6**: protected weight-bearing (partial, per fixation construct — plate constructs are often more restrictive initially than locked intramedullary nail constructs), active-assisted ROM, avoidance of torsional/twisting loads across the osteotomy site.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive weight-bearing to full as radiographic union is confirmed; physiotherapy for hip and thigh strengthening, gait retraining to normalize foot progression angle now that the underlying torsional deformity is corrected.
- **Months 3–4**: return to full unrestricted activity/sport once solid radiographic union is confirmed and strength/ROM are symmetric with the contralateral limb; return-to-sport timing is broadly similar regardless of nail versus plate fixation.
- **Return to school**: 2–3 weeks (partial weight-bearing, crutch-assisted).
