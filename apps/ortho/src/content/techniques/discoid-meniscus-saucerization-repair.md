## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:

- Symptomatic complete or incomplete discoid lateral meniscus (torn, or presenting with a painless or painful snapping/clunking knee).
- Wrisberg-variant (unstable) discoid meniscus, regardless of tear presence, once symptomatic — the unanchored posterior horn requires stabilization, not observation.
- An associated peripheral tear discovered after saucerization of a discoid meniscus.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:

- Asymptomatic discoid meniscus is not an indication for surgery — observation only; resection of an asymptomatic discoid meniscus is not indicated and exposes the patient to unnecessary surgical risk without a symptom to treat.
- Severely degenerated, irreparable discoid tissue — saucerization with an attempted repair of unsalvageable tissue is not appropriate; subtotal/total meniscectomy is reserved for this scenario only.
- Active joint infection or an unrelated acute injury requiring treatment first (e.g., a concurrent unaddressed physeal injury in a very young child) may warrant staging.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI is the primary diagnostic and planning study — look for the "bow-tie sign" on ≥3 consecutive sagittal slices (versus the normal 1–2 slices in a normal semilunar meniscus) and increased meniscal width on coronal images. Carefully assess the posterior horn and meniscocapsular junction on MRI for any sign of instability or an occult peripheral tear, though instability itself is ultimately a clinical/arthroscopic diagnosis.
- **Classification to plan around**: shape (complete — covers the entire tibial plateau — versus incomplete — partial coverage) and, most importantly for surgical planning, stability of the posterior peripheral attachment (Wrisberg variant lacks the normal posterior meniscotibial/coronary attachment and relies only on the meniscofemoral ligament).
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: standard knee arthroscopy set with a full-radius shaver and a meniscal biter/basket punch set for graduated saucerization; have inside-out or all-inside meniscal repair instrumentation (cannulas, zone-specific curved needles or an all-inside device system) opened and available in every case, since the decision to repair is often made intraoperatively after saucerization reveals instability.
- **Age-specific planning**: in very young children (<12 years), confirm the family understands the surgical goal (preserve as much stable, functional meniscal tissue as possible) and the expectation of excellent outcomes with repair-when-indicated, since the reflex in younger patients has historically been more aggressive resection.
- **Anticipate a posteromedial or posterolateral accessory incision** if inside-out repair instrumentation will be needed for the posterior horn — mark and prep accordingly even if the plan is primarily all-inside.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard, particularly in the pediatric/adolescent population; a regional block (femoral or adductor canal) may be added for postoperative analgesia in adolescents and adults.
- **Positioning**: supine with a lateral post or leg holder positioned to allow the knee to move freely from full extension to at least 120–130° of flexion, since posterior horn assessment and repair require deep flexion and a figure-4 (externally rotated, flexed) position for lateral compartment opening.
- **Tourniquet**: a high-thigh tourniquet is typically applied and available, inflated selectively if visualization is compromised by bleeding rather than used routinely for the entire case.
- **Setup**: standard arthroscopic fluid inflow/outflow system; have an accessory posterolateral portal and instrumentation available, since lateral compartment access for a discoid meniscus (particularly a Wrisberg variant with an unstable posterior horn) can be tighter than a normal knee.
- **Landmarks**: mark standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals; mark a posterolateral accessory portal site as a contingency for posterior horn instrumentation or inside-out repair suture retrieval.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Perform diagnostic arthroscopy through standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals; confirm the discoid morphology, its extent (complete vs. incomplete), and identify any tear pattern.
2. Correlate arthroscopic findings with the preoperative bow-tie sign and coronal width measurements; document symptom correlation before proceeding — do not resect an incidentally discovered, asymptomatic contralateral discoid meniscus found at the time of surgery for an unrelated procedure.
3. Begin central saucerization with a meniscal biter or basket punch, working from the free edge outward in a graduated, circumferential fashion to progressively re-establish a normal semilunar shape.
4. Use a full-radius shaver to smooth and contour the resected edge, converting the flat central resection into a tapered, stable rim.
5. Target a final peripheral rim width of approximately 6–8 mm, matching the contour of a normal lateral meniscus — err toward leaving slightly more tissue and re-assessing than over-resecting, since tissue removed cannot be replaced.
6. Once the saucerization is complete, systematically probe the entire posterior peripheral attachment (posterior horn to the meniscocapsular/meniscotibial junction) with a hook probe in a figure-4 position to assess stability.
7. Determine whether the posterior attachment is a Wrisberg variant (no meniscotibial attachment, hypermobile posterior horn) or has a discrete peripheral tear — both require repair.
8. If unstable or torn, repair the posterior peripheral attachment using inside-out sutures (via a posterolateral accessory incision, protecting the peroneal nerve and lateral geniculate vessels) or an all-inside device, reducing the posterior horn to its anatomic position on the tibial rim before securing sutures.
9. Re-probe after repair to confirm restoration of stability — the meniscus should no longer sublux or "clunk" with dynamic flexion-extension testing.
10. If the discoid tissue is found to be severely degenerated, fragmented, or otherwise irreparable at any point in this sequence, convert to subtotal/total meniscectomy, preserving as much peripheral rim as feasible rather than performing a complete meniscectomy by default.
11. Perform a final dynamic range-of-motion check through full flexion-extension and rotation to confirm no residual mechanical snapping, impingement, or instability.
12. Close portal incisions and any accessory incision in standard layered fashion.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Perform saucerization progressively and re-probe frequently — it is far easier to remove a bit more tissue than to have removed too much; work in small increments, especially near the periphery.
- Always test for instability after — not only before — saucerization; removing the central tissue can unmask a subtle posterior horn instability that was not apparent when the disc was intact.
- Use the figure-4 position (hip flexed/externally rotated, knee flexed) to open the lateral compartment and improve access to the posterior horn for both probing and repair.
- In the Wrisberg variant, remember the meniscofemoral ligament (Humphry or Wrisberg) is the only remaining posterior fixation — preserve it and use it as an anatomic landmark when re-securing the posterior horn.
- Keep the shaver blade oriented away from the peripheral rim during central debridement to avoid inadvertently thinning or destabilizing the intended stable remnant.
- Have both inside-out and all-inside repair options available; the posterolateral corner's proximity to the peroneal nerve and popliteus tendon makes meticulous, protected instrument passage essential regardless of which technique is chosen.
- In younger children, be conservative with resection volume relative to the joint size — a proportionally larger residual rim reduces recurrent snapping risk while still achieving a functional semilunar shape.
- Document the final rim width and repair construct in the operative note, since these details meaningfully affect postoperative protocol selection (saucerization-alone vs. saucerization-plus-repair).

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Recurrent instability/snapping if an unstable posterior attachment is not repaired at the time of saucerization.
- Progressive degenerative change can occur over time even after well-executed saucerization + repair.
- Recent comparative data (2024) favor saucerization + repair over subtotal meniscectomy for complex discoid tears on 2-year outcomes and OCD-development risk, reinforcing a repair-preserving strategy even for complex patterns.
- Saucerization ± repair, performed appropriately, produces excellent outcomes even in children under 12 — repair-when-indicated is favored over blanket resection in the youngest symptomatic patients.
- Over-resection during saucerization leaves an unstable, thin remnant rim that behaves biomechanically like a partial meniscectomy and is prone to further tearing.
- Missing a subtle Wrisberg-type instability because the posterior horn was not specifically probed after saucerization — instability can be easy to overlook once the bulk of the disc is removed and the joint "looks" more normal.
- Injury to the peroneal nerve or popliteus tendon during posterolateral portal placement or inside-out suture retrieval — maintain careful, protected technique and know the posterolateral safe zone.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph does not specify a discoid-specific phased weight-bearing/ROM protocol distinct from the generic rehabilitation framework (Phase I–V, criteria-based progression). Follow the general post-arthroscopy framework, individualized to whether saucerization alone or saucerization + peripheral repair was performed (the latter warranting a more protected course analogous to peripheral meniscus repair given the repair construct) — in practice this means a period of restricted flexion and protected weight-bearing (comparable to peripheral meniscus repair protocols) when a peripheral repair was added, versus an earlier, less-restricted progression when saucerization alone was sufficient.
