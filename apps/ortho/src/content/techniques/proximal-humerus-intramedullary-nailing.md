## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**:
- Surgical-neck-predominant 2-part proximal humerus fractures (Neer 2-part surgical neck, AO/OTA 11-A), particularly in osteoporotic bone, where a straight antegrade nail with multiplanar proximal locking provides load-sharing fixation through a smaller incision than plating.
- Selected 3-part fractures with a surgical-neck-predominant pattern and manageable tuberosity displacement, in centers/systems experienced with nail-based tuberosity reduction and fixation techniques.
- Patients in whom minimizing soft-tissue stripping is a priority (osteoporotic bone, compromised soft-tissue envelope, or a desire to preserve the deltopectoral interval for a future arthroplasty if fixation fails).

**Contraindications**:
- Head-splitting fractures — not amenable to nail fixation; ORIF or arthroplasty is preferred.
- Complex 4-part fractures with significant tuberosity comminution and displacement, where indirect nail-based tuberosity control is unreliable — favor locking-plate ORIF or arthroplasty depending on age/bone quality.
- Fracture-dislocations with unreconstructable head fragments.
- Pre-existing rotator cuff pathology (large full-thickness supraspinatus tear) that would be worsened by entry-portal violation of the cuff — relative contraindication; consider a rotator-cuff-sparing entry technique or an alternative implant.
- Narrow proximal medullary canal or head anatomy unsuitable for the available nail diameter/proximal locking geometry.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: true AP (Grashey), scapular-Y, and axillary lateral views (mandatory to exclude glenohumeral dislocation and assess head-splitting/impaction); CT with 2D/3D reconstruction for surgical planning in displaced 2-/3-part patterns to confirm tuberosity displacement is manageable through an indirect/percutaneous technique.
- **Implant templating**: select nail diameter and length against the contralateral humerus or injury films; confirm the chosen system offers multiplanar proximal locking (including calcar/inferomedial locking screw options), which is increasingly the deciding factor for osteoporotic bone.
- **Entry-portal planning**: review the preoperative axillary/AP imaging for the relationship between the humeral head apex and the supraspinatus footprint — anatomic studies suggest a meaningful minority of proximal humeri have "critical" anatomy where some violation of the supraspinatus footprint by a standard entry portal is difficult to avoid; this informs the consent discussion about expected postoperative shoulder discomfort.
- **Reduction aids**: have percutaneous reduction instruments (joysticks/Schanz pins, bone hooks, percutaneous clamps) available, since this is largely an indirect/minimally invasive reduction technique.
- **Equipment**: full or mini C-arm fluoroscopy capable of true AP, axillary, and scapular-Y equivalent views without patient repositioning; cannulated proximal targeting jig for the chosen nail system.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, commonly combined with an interscalene block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Position**: modified beach-chair position, torso elevated 30–60°, head secured, operative shoulder brought to the edge of the table for full range of arm motion during entry-point access and fluoroscopy.
- **Table setup**: a radiolucent table or table extension allowing the C-arm to obtain true AP, axillary, and scapular-Y equivalent views of the shoulder without moving the patient; an arm positioner or assistant support to hold the arm in slight adduction/neutral rotation during entry-point localization.
- **Draping**: drape to allow full shoulder access from the base of the neck to mid-humerus and free arm movement for fluoroscopic imaging and rotational reduction maneuvers.
- **Landmarks**: mark the anterolateral acromial edge, the planned superior portal (roughly in line with the humeral shaft axis, just posterior to the bicipital groove), and confirm the fracture level with fluoroscopy before incision.
- **Fluoroscopy sequencing**: obtain a baseline true AP and axillary-equivalent image after positioning and before incision to confirm reduction plan and entry trajectory.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision.** Make a small longitudinal incision from the anterolateral acromial edge extending proximally/laterally, centered over the planned entry portal.
2. **Split the deltoid.** Split the deltoid in line with its fibers, staying within roughly 5 cm of the acromion to protect the axillary nerve, which crosses the deltoid's deep surface at that distance.
3. **Expose the rotator cuff interval.** Identify the supraspinatus tendon and the long head of the biceps tendon as landmarks.
4. **Split (do not detach) the supraspinatus.** Incise the supraspinatus tendon longitudinally, in line with its fibers, for roughly 2–3 cm, centered just posterior to the long head of the biceps tendon over the foreseen entry point; place stay sutures in the tendon edges before spreading, both to control the split and to allow a secure repair at closure.
5. **Localize the entry point.** The ideal entry point is at or near the apex (zenith) of the humeral head, slightly medial to the greater tuberosity's articular margin — approximately at a distance from the articular margin that corresponds to the width of the supraspinatus footprint, and centered anteroposteriorly. Confirm with a guidewire and biplanar fluoroscopy (true AP and axillary/precipice-equivalent views) before committing to reaming — the entry point sits in line with the humeral shaft's anatomic axis, not simply "the highest point visible."
6. **Reduce the fracture.** For a surgical-neck 2-part pattern, use percutaneous joysticks (Schanz pins in the head and shaft fragments), a bone hook through the rotator cuff interval, or gentle manual traction/manipulation under fluoroscopic control to restore head-shaft alignment before or immediately after guidewire passage. For 3-part patterns, address displaced tuberosity fragments first with heavy suture passed through the tendon-bone junction to allow indirect reduction as the nail and locking screws are placed.
7. **Pass the guidewire and ream (if indicated).** Advance the guidewire down the medullary canal across the reduced fracture under fluoroscopic guidance; ream only as much as the specific nail system requires, avoiding overreaming in osteoporotic metaphyseal bone.
8. **Insert the nail.** Advance the nail over the guidewire to the appropriate depth (typically countersunk a few millimeters below the articular cartilage to avoid subacromial impingement), confirming depth and rotation fluoroscopically.
9. **Proximal locking.** Place multiplanar proximal locking screws via the targeting jig, including calcar/inferomedial locking screw options where the system allows — these support the head fragment against varus collapse in a fashion analogous to calcar screws in plating.
10. **Secure tuberosity fragments.** If tuberosity fragments were mobilized for reduction, tension the previously placed heavy sutures through the proximal locking screws or dedicated suture holes in the nail/screws to augment tuberosity fixation, particularly for the greater tuberosity (supraspinatus/infraspinatus insertion).
11. **Distal locking.** Place distal interlocking screws per the system's jig, generally 1–2 screws depending on fracture stability requirements.
12. **Confirm fixation fluoroscopically.** Obtain final AP and axillary views confirming reduction, nail depth (avoiding proud proximal nail prominence against the acromion or cuff), and no screw penetration into the joint — dynamic rotation views are valuable here just as in plate fixation.
13. **Repair the supraspinatus split.** Repair the tendon split side-to-side using the previously placed stay sutures — a meticulous repair here measurably affects postoperative shoulder pain and function.
14. **Layered closure.** Repair the deltoid split, subcutaneous tissue, and skin.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Place stay sutures in the supraspinatus tendon edges before splitting it — this keeps the split controlled, prevents inadvertent tendon tearing during retraction, and sets up an efficient, secure repair at closure.
- Confirm the entry point with biplanar fluoroscopy before reaming; the correct trajectory is in line with the humeral shaft's anatomic axis and centered anteroposteriorly at the head's apex just posterior to the biceps tendon — "eyeballing" the highest visible point of the head is a common source of a mismatched, off-axis entry that later shows up as varus or valgus nail malposition.
- A guidewire placed and checked on both true AP and axillary views before reaming catches a malpositioned entry while it is still easy to correct.
- Use a bone hook through the rotator cuff interval or percutaneous Schanz-pin joysticks to control the head fragment during reduction — this avoids the more extensive soft-tissue stripping a formal open reduction would require and preserves the minimally invasive advantage of nailing.
- For 3-part fractures with a displaced tuberosity, place heavy non-absorbable sutures through the tendon-bone junction before final nail seating — tensioning these sutures against the nail/proximal screws after the nail is placed is far easier than trying to reduce a tuberosity around an already-seated implant.
- Seat the nail a few millimeters below the articular cartilage surface — proud nail prominence against the acromion or under the cuff is a recognized cause of postoperative impingement pain and is easy to avoid with careful depth confirmation.
- Do not overream in osteoporotic proximal metaphyseal bone — the metaphyseal cancellous bone is often soft enough that reaming beyond what is needed for nail passage weakens fixation purchase rather than helping it.
- Keep the deltoid split within about 5 cm of the acromion, as with any deltoid-splitting exposure in this region — beyond that distance the axillary nerve is at meaningfully higher risk from retraction or inadvertent extension of the split.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Entry point precision is critical — an imprecise entry point risks iatrogenic rotator cuff injury; anatomic studies suggest a meaningful proportion of proximal humeri have anatomy in which some violation of the supraspinatus footprint by the entry portal is difficult to avoid even with careful technique, which is why postoperative shoulder discomfort is a relatively common (though usually self-limited) patient complaint after antegrade proximal humerus nailing.
- Not appropriate for 3-/4-part or head-splitting patterns with unreconstructable tuberosity comminution, where ORIF or arthroplasty is preferred.
- Overreaming or forcing the nail through resistance in osteoporotic metaphyseal bone can strip the proximal fixation purchase rather than improving it.
- A proud (non-countersunk) nail tip against the acromion or rotator cuff causes mechanical impingement pain and may require later nail removal/exchange.
- As with all proximal humerus fixation, tuberosity healing and rotator cuff integrity remain major drivers of functional outcome independent of nail-specific technique — a technically excellent nail insertion does not guarantee a good functional result if tuberosity fixation fails.
- Screw penetration into the glenohumeral joint is possible with proximal locking screws in a head that has settled/collapsed slightly after nail insertion — always confirm with dynamic rotation fluoroscopy, not static AP/axillary views alone.
- Axillary nerve injury is possible from deltoid split extension or aggressive retraction, even though the approach is designed to stay within a "safe" 5 cm zone from the acromion.

## Postoperative Protocol

No nail-specific rehabilitation timeline is described beyond the general proximal humerus pathway: early passive/active-assisted ROM, resisted strengthening deferred until radiographic union is evident (typically by 6–12 wk), and full unrestricted activity by 3–6 months once strength and ROM are near-symmetric with no residual instability.
