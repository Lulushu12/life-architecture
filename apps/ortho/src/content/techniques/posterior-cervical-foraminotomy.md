## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Pure foraminal soft disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte causing unilateral single-level radiculopathy, without central stenosis or kyphosis.
- Failed conservative care (activity modification, NSAIDs, structured PT, ± ESI) for at least 6–12 weeks, or a progressive motor deficit sooner, with a concordant, foraminal/posterolateral lesion on MRI.
- Patients in whom avoiding anterior approach morbidity (dysphagia, RLN injury) and preserving motion without an implant is a priority — best suited to posterolateral, not central, pathology.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- Central or bilateral pathology, or a predominantly axial-plane central disc herniation — the posterior approach cannot safely retract the cord/thecal sac to reach central pathology the way an anterior approach can directly address it.
- Myelopathy or cord compression — this is a radiculopathy-only procedure.
- Significant kyphosis — while foraminotomy does not itself rely on lordosis for its mechanism the way laminoplasty does, significant kyphosis often signals broader degenerative disease better addressed by a fusion-based strategy.
- Significant pre-existing segmental instability at the level (foraminotomy removes some facet bone, which can destabilize an already-marginal segment).
- Predominantly axial neck pain without a clear radicular component — foraminotomy addresses nerve root compression, not facet-mediated or discogenic axial pain.

## Preoperative Planning

- MRI to confirm a posterolateral/foraminal soft disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte as the compressive lesion, and to exclude central stenosis or a predominantly central disc fragment.
- CT is useful when foraminal osteophyte/uncovertebral hypertrophy (bony, rather than soft disc) is the suspected compressive element, to plan the extent of bony removal needed.
- Flexion-extension radiographs to confirm the absence of instability at the target level and to document baseline alignment.
- Confirm laterality and level meticulously against the clinical exam (dermatomal/myotomal pattern) — this is a unilateral, single-level procedure, and a wrong-side or wrong-level error is entirely preventable with careful preoperative cross-checking.
- Decide on technique: open microscopic keyhole foraminotomy versus a minimally invasive tubular/endoscopic approach, based on surgeon experience and equipment availability — both achieve the same decompressive goal via a smaller bony window in the MIS variant.
- No implant is planned, so there is no graft/hardware sizing step — equipment needs are a high-speed burr, Kerrison rongeurs of varying footprint, and microscope or loupe magnification.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General endotracheal anesthesia; standard precaution against excessive neck extension during intubation/positioning applies whenever coexisting stenosis is present, though this is less critical than in myelopathic/high-grade stenosis cases given the more limited, unilateral pathology typically treated with this procedure.
- Prone position on a radiolucent frame or horseshoe headrest (some surgeons use a semi-sitting or lateral "park bench" position, particularly for MIS/tubular techniques, per surgeon preference), neck in slight flexion to open the interlaminar space at the target level.
- Arms tucked, pressure points padded, eyes protected, abdomen free-hanging to minimize venous engorgement and intraoperative bleeding.
- Mark and confirm the correct side and level with lateral (and if needed AP) fluoroscopy before incision — given this is a unilateral procedure at a single level, this confirmation step deserves particular discipline.
- Prep and drape a limited field centered over the marked level and side; for MIS/tubular technique, plan the skin incision slightly off midline on the operative side to allow a direct trajectory down to the target lamina/facet junction.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the pathology is posterolateral and single-level**: unilateral foraminal soft disc herniation or foraminal osteophyte causing radiculopathy, without central stenosis or kyphosis — reconfirm laterality against the clinical exam one final time before incision.
2. **Incision and exposure.** For an open approach, make a midline or slightly paramedian incision centered over the target level; for MIS/tubular technique, make a small incision off midline on the operative side and dock a tubular or expandable retractor down to the lamina/facet junction under fluoroscopic guidance.
3. **Confirm the level** with intraoperative fluoroscopy before proceeding with bony work.
4. **Expose the lamina and medial facet** at the target level on the symptomatic side only, subperiosteally, preserving the contralateral musculature and facet capsule entirely (this is what makes the procedure motion-preserving and lower-morbidity).
5. **Create the keyhole.** Using a high-speed burr, remove a small window of bone spanning the inferomedial aspect of the lamina above and the superomedial aspect of the lamina below, extending laterally to include the medial portion of the facet joint overlying the foramen — the "keyhole" — while preserving enough of the facet joint (generally no more than 50% of the facet) to avoid iatrogenic instability.
6. **Identify the nerve root.** Using a fine nerve hook and Kerrison rongeurs, carefully open the ligamentum flavum and identify the exiting nerve root at the lateral extent of the exposure — work from a known, less-compressed area toward the compressed segment to avoid probing blindly against a taut, compressed root.
7. **Decompress the foramen.** Undercut the facet and pedicle superiorly and inferiorly with Kerrison rongeurs and a fine curette, following the nerve root laterally until it is visibly mobile and a nerve hook passes freely along its undersurface and dorsal surface.
8. **Address the compressive lesion directly.** For a foraminal soft disc fragment, gently mobilize the nerve root (typically retracting it superiorly with a fine nerve hook or dedicated root retractor) and remove the disc fragment from beneath/lateral to the root with a small pituitary rongeur or curette, working with the root under direct vision at all times. For a foraminal osteophyte, continue burring/undercutting the bony foramen until adequate decompression is confirmed.
9. **Confirm adequate decompression.** The nerve root should be visibly decompressed and mobile through its full accessible course within the foramen; a nerve hook or fine probe should pass along the root without tethering.
10. **Preserve segmental motion.** No implant is placed — confirm the facet joint retains adequate bony integrity (generally >50% preserved) before closing, since over-resection risks iatrogenic instability.
11. **Hemostasis and closure.** Achieve meticulous hemostasis, particularly from epidural veins adjacent to the root; irrigate; close the fascia, subcutaneous layer, and skin (or remove the tubular retractor and close the small MIS incision) in layers.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Work from a normal or less-compressed zone toward the compressed segment when first identifying the nerve root — this avoids introducing an instrument into a taut, compressed root space blindly, which risks injury.
- Limit facet resection to roughly the medial third to half of the joint — this is generally enough to fully decompress the foramen while preserving enough facet integrity to avoid iatrogenic segmental instability, which is the main long-term risk trade-off of this procedure.
- Retract the nerve root gently and only as much as needed (typically superiorly) when removing a foraminal disc fragment — excessive retraction of an already-compressed, edematous root risks a transient or even persistent postoperative dysesthesia.
- Use copious irrigation and small, incremental Kerrison bites near the root rather than large bites, to maintain a clear, bloodless field and tactile control near neural structures.
- For MIS/tubular technique, confirm docking position with fluoroscopy before beginning bony work — an off-target dock forces awkward, higher-risk maneuvering to reach the correct foraminal anatomy.
- If the compressive lesion turns out to be more central or more extensive than expected on preoperative imaging, do not force a posterior-only solution — be prepared to stage or convert to an anterior approach rather than compromising decompression adequacy.
- Confirm root mobility as the definitive intraoperative endpoint, not just the amount of bone removed — a root that is still tethered despite what looks like adequate bony work usually has a missed component (residual disc fragment, unaddressed osteophyte) that needs further exploration.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that this procedure treats the nerve root only — coexisting axial neck pain from facet or discogenic sources will not be addressed and should be set as a separate expectation.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Not suited to central or bilateral pathology, myelopathy, or significant kyphosis — the procedure only addresses posterolateral, single-level compression and does not correct alignment.
- Because there is no anterior exposure, it avoids the dysphagia and recurrent laryngeal nerve risks specific to ACDF, but it also cannot address anterior-predominant or multilevel compressive pathology.
- **Iatrogenic instability from over-resection of the facet joint**: limiting resection to roughly the medial third to half of the joint is the primary preventive measure; a segment that already has borderline instability preoperatively is a poor candidate regardless of technique.
- **Incomplete decompression**: results from under-resection or from misidentifying the level/side — confirm with fluoroscopy and confirm root mobility directly rather than relying on the amount of bone removed as a proxy.
- **Nerve root injury/postoperative dysesthesia**: from excessive retraction or aggressive instrumentation near an already-compressed, edematous root — usually transient but can be persistent; gentle, incremental technique is the main preventive measure.
- **Epidural venous bleeding**: common near the root and foramen; usually controlled with bipolar cautery and hemostatic agents, but can obscure the field if not managed proactively with small, controlled steps rather than large bites.
- **Wrong-level or wrong-side surgery**: preventable with disciplined preoperative marking and intraoperative fluoroscopic confirmation, particularly important given this is a unilateral, single-level procedure where the consequence of an error is a completely missed target.
- **Dural tear/CSF leak**: uncommon but possible during ligamentum flavum removal or facet undercutting close to the thecal sac margin; manage with primary repair if accessible or a fat/muscle patch and sealant, with postoperative positioning precautions as needed.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Return to sedentary work: 1–2 weeks. Return to manual labor: 4–6 weeks.
- Return to contact/collision sport: individualized — this is the lowest-morbidity option among the operative choices, since there is no fusion to protect.
- No collar is typically required given the absence of an implant or fusion construct; early active range of motion is encouraged as tolerated by wound comfort.
- Monitor early for any new or worsening motor deficit, which should prompt urgent postoperative imaging to rule out hematoma or missed compressive pathology, given the small but real risk of incomplete decompression or epidural hematoma near the root.
