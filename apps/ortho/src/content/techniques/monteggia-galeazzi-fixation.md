## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications:**
- **Monteggia injury** (proximal ulna fracture + radiocapitellar dislocation), all Bado types, in adults — non-operative management is not appropriate as definitive treatment; anatomic ulna fixation is indicated regardless of Bado type.
- **Galeazzi injury** (distal-third radius shaft fracture + DRUJ disruption) in adults — anatomic radius fixation is indicated for essentially all displaced Galeazzi fractures.
- Monteggia "variants"/equivalents (Bado II pattern with associated coronoid and/or radial head fracture) — treated as a complex elbow fracture-dislocation using terrible-triad principles in addition to ulna fixation.
- Chronic/missed Monteggia (radial head dislocation not recognized acutely) — reconstructive options (ulnar osteotomy, annular ligament reconstruction) are a distinct, more complex undertaking outside the scope of the acute algorithm below.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications:**
- Active local infection or grossly contaminated open wound not yet debrided — provisional external fixation/staged approach until soft tissues allow definitive plating.
- Severe comminution with segmental bone loss precluding primary plate fixation — consider staged fixation (temporizing external fixation, then definitive plating/grafting).
- Medically unstable polytrauma patient — temporize with splinting/external fixation until damage-control principles allow definitive surgery.
- Isolated, truly non-displaced ulna or radius shaft fracture with a confirmed stable, concentrically reduced joint — vanishingly rare in adults but would not mandate surgery if genuinely stable and non-displaced on serial imaging.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** image the joint above and below any forearm fracture as standard practice. For Monteggia, obtain a true lateral elbow view specifically to judge concentric radiocapitellar reduction and trace the radiocapitellar line (a line through the radial shaft/head should intersect the capitellum on every view, in every degree of elbow flexion). For Galeazzi, obtain a true AP wrist view to assess DRUJ widening, ulnar variance, and any ulnar styloid base fracture. CT is useful for comminuted/segmental patterns or when intra-articular extension into the elbow or wrist is suspected.
- **Templating:** measure fracture length/obliquity on the ulna (Monteggia) or radius (Galeazzi) and plan plate length — typically a 6- to 8-hole 3.5mm plate, longer for comminuted/segmental patterns, aiming for a minimum of 6-8 cortices of fixation on each side of the fracture.
- **Implant selection:** 3.5mm dynamic compression plate (DCP) or locking compression plate (LCP) for the fractured bone; have a small K-wire set (0.062in) available for provisional DRUJ pinning in Galeazzi, and a small-fragment/headless screw or mini-plate set available in case an associated radial head or coronoid fracture is found (Monteggia variant).
- **Case-specific planning:** for Monteggia, review whether the radial head/neck is fractured on preoperative imaging (changes the plan from "ulna fixation alone should reduce the joint" to "combined ulna + radial head/coronoid fixation" per terrible-triad principles); for Galeazzi, plan for possible temporary DRUJ pinning or forearm immobilization in supination if intraoperative stress testing shows instability after radius fixation, and consent the patient for this possibility.
- **Equipment checklist:** small fragment set, pointed/serrated reduction clamps, radiolucent hand table, mini-fluoroscopy (C-arm), pneumatic tourniquet, 0.062in K-wires for DRUJ pinning, TFCC repair instruments on standby for Galeazzi.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** general anesthesia or regional (supraclavicular/axillary brachial plexus block) — a regional block is well suited to this extremity-only procedure and provides useful postoperative analgesia.
- **Position:** supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table; for Monteggia, forearm pronated for the subcutaneous ulnar approach; for Galeazzi, forearm positioned as needed for the volar (Henry) approach to the distal radius, with free rotation available for intraoperative DRUJ stress testing.
- **Tourniquet:** pneumatic tourniquet on the upper arm, exsanguinate, inflate to standard limb pressure (250-300 mmHg, or ~100 mmHg above systolic); document tourniquet time and deflate before final DRUJ stability testing if time allows, since normal soft-tissue tension gives a more reliable stress exam.
- **Fluoroscopy:** mini C-arm positioned across the table, draped into the field — essential intraoperatively for confirming concentric radiocapitellar reduction (Monteggia) on a true lateral, and for DRUJ stress testing in full pronation/supination (Galeazzi) after fixation.
- **Draping:** standard extremity prep from above the tourniquet to the fingertips, with a circumferential drape allowing free forearm rotation and, for Monteggia, access to the elbow for fluoroscopic confirmation.
- **Landmarks marked:** subcutaneous border of the ulna (Monteggia incision); FCR interval and radial styloid-to-antecubital line (Galeazzi/Henry approach); Lister's tubercle and dorsal wrist landmarks if DRUJ pinning is anticipated.

## Step-by-Step Technique

**Monteggia:**

1. Make a longitudinal incision directly over the subcutaneous border of the ulna, centered on the fracture.
2. Elevate soft tissue subperiosteally off the ulna only as much as needed to expose the fracture, minimizing periosteal stripping to protect vascularity.
3. Reduce the fracture anatomically using pointed reduction clamps; restore length, alignment, and rotation precisely — anatomic reduction of the ulna is the primary maneuver that reduces the radial head, so accept nothing less than a fully anatomic reduction of the ulna before assessing the radiocapitellar joint.
4. Apply a 3.5mm DCP or LCP along the subcutaneous border, using compression technique (lag screw + neutralization plate, or a dynamic compression plate) for simple transverse/short oblique patterns, and bridging/locking technique for comminuted patterns; achieve a minimum of 6-8 cortices of fixation on each side.
5. **Confirm concentric radiocapitellar reduction on true lateral fluoroscopy before closing** — the radiocapitellar line should pass through the capitellum in every view.
6. If the radial head is **not** concentrically reduced after solid anatomic ulna fixation, systematically check for: (a) an interposed annular ligament or joint capsule blocking reduction, (b) a subtly malreduced ulna (recheck length/rotation/angulation), or (c) an unaddressed radial head/neck fracture or coronoid fracture (Bado II/"Monteggia variant") — address the specific cause rather than closing on an unreduced joint. If interposed soft tissue is found, a small direct exposure of the radiocapitellar joint (extending the approach or a separate lateral incision) to extract the interposed tissue and confirm reduction is warranted.
7. If a Monteggia variant (associated coronoid and/or radial head fracture) is present, fix these per terrible-triad sequencing principles (coronoid, then radial head, then assess LCL) after the ulna is fixed.
8. Irrigate, close in layers, and splint with the elbow at 90° and forearm in neutral rotation.

**Galeazzi:**

1. Approach the radius through a volar (Henry) incision along a line from the biceps tendon to the radial styloid, developing the interval between brachioradialis and FCR/pronator teres.
2. Reduce the fracture anatomically with pointed clamps, restoring length and rotation; apply a 3.5mm compression plate (DCP or LCP) along the flat volar surface, achieving a minimum of 6-8 cortices of fixation on each side.
3. **Stress-test the DRUJ under fluoroscopy** in full pronation and full supination after radius fixation — compare the ulnar head position to the sigmoid notch; a well-reduced radius typically restores DRUJ stability, but this must be confirmed, not assumed.
4. If the DRUJ is stable through a full rotational arc, no further fixation is needed — immobilize in a well-padded splint with the forearm in a comfortable neutral position.
5. If the DRUJ is unstable but reduces with the forearm in supination, immobilize the forearm in supination in a long-arm cast/splint for 4–6 weeks.
6. If the DRUJ is grossly unstable (does not reduce and hold in any position, or a large ulnar styloid base fragment blocks reduction), place a temporary DRUJ pin (0.062in K-wire, radius to ulna, proximal to the sigmoid notch, with the forearm held in neutral-to-slight supination) for 4–6 weeks.
7. Reserve open TFCC repair for cases with a large ulnar styloid base fracture/fragment blocking reduction, or persistent instability after pinning — approach through a small dorsal or ulnar-sided incision and repair via suture anchor or transosseous technique.
8. Irrigate, close in layers, and splint/cast with the forearm in the position determined by the stability assessment above.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Trace the radiocapitellar line on every single elbow and forearm image, in every plane, before and after fixation — a line through the radial shaft and head should intersect the capitellum in every view; this is the fastest way to avoid a missed or unrecognized Monteggia.
- In Monteggia, achieve anatomic ulna reduction before troubleshooting the radiocapitellar joint — a subtly malreduced ulna (a few degrees of residual angulation or a millimeter of shortening) is a common, correctable cause of persistent radial head subluxation that is easy to overlook once attention shifts to the "joint problem."
- Palpate the entire elbow in every forearm fracture, and the entire forearm/wrist in every elbow fracture, before draping — this simple exam step catches combined injuries that imaging alone can miss if the wrong views are ordered.
- In Galeazzi, always stress-test the DRUJ under fluoroscopy after radius fixation rather than assuming reduction — the "rule of thumb" (>5mm radial shortening, >5° angulation, or ulnar styloid base fracture on the initial injury films) should raise suspicion for instability even when the joint looks reduced on static post-fixation films.
- When placing a DRUJ pin, direct it from radius to ulna proximal to the sigmoid notch, and confirm forearm position (neutral-to-slight supination) under fluoroscopy before final tightening — pinning in a rotated position can lock in a subtle malreduction.
- If a Monteggia variant is discovered intraoperatively (coronoid or radial head fracture not seen on preoperative imaging), do not improvise the sequence — pause and apply terrible-triad principles (coronoid before radial head before LCL assessment) for the added injury.
- Use separate incisions and minimize periosteal stripping in comminuted patterns for both injury types — the same soft-tissue-preservation principles that protect against forearm nonunion and radioulnar synostosis apply here.
- Counsel patients preoperatively that DRUJ instability found intraoperatively may change the postoperative immobilization plan (supination casting or pin placement) — this avoids a difficult conversation in recovery about an "extra" period of immobilization.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- A "missed Monteggia" (chronic radial head dislocation after an ulna fracture treated as isolated) is a recognized and largely preventable diagnostic failure — always trace the radiocapitellar line on every elbow and forearm view, and palpate the entire elbow in every forearm fracture.
- The Galeazzi "rule of thumb": any distal-third radius shaft fracture with >5mm radial shortening, >5° angulation, or a fracture of the ulnar styloid base should raise suspicion for DRUJ instability even if the joint appears reduced on initial films.
- Always image the wrist in a suspected Monteggia and the elbow in a suspected Galeazzi — missed DRUJ injury in Galeazzi is a recognized red flag.
- If the radial head does not reduce concentrically after solid anatomic ulna fixation in Monteggia, do not close without re-addressing the ulna reduction or an unrecognized radial head/coronoid injury.
- Interposed annular ligament or capsule blocking radiocapitellar reduction is easy to miss if the surgeon assumes a persistently subluxated joint simply reflects inadequate ulna reduction — if the ulna is confirmed anatomic and the joint still will not reduce, direct exploration of the radiocapitellar joint is warranted.
- Radioulnar synostosis risk is increased by high-energy/comminuted injury patterns and shared traumatic hematoma between the two bones — minimize unnecessary soft-tissue disruption between the radius and ulna during exposure.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **0–2 weeks:** splint/sling for comfort; begin gentle elbow and wrist AROM early (adjacent joints), guarded forearm rotation.
- **2–6 weeks:** progressive AROM of forearm rotation and adjacent joints; avoid axial loading (no weight-bearing through the arm) until union is progressing.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive strengthening once radiographic union (typically 8–12 weeks for diaphyseal forearm fractures with rigid plate fixation); light functional use.
- **DRUJ-pinned Galeazzi:** pin removal at 4–6 weeks in clinic, then begin forearm rotation AROM progressively.
- **Return to work/sport:** light duty ~6 weeks; full manual labor/contact sport 3–4 months, contingent on union and rotational strength.
