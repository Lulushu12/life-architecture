## Indications

Anterior odontoid screw fixation is used for operative Type II (Anderson-D'Alonzo) odontoid fractures with a favorable fracture line — anteroinferior-to-posterosuperior obliquity, i.e. Type IIA/horizontal-oblique patterns — that are reducible without a large fragment gap, in patients with a body habitus/short neck allowing an adequate screw trajectory. It preserves C1-C2 rotation, which is its principal advantage over posterior C1-C2 fusion.

## Positioning & setup

Requires a trajectory that permits a cannulated screw to be passed from the anteroinferior C2 body into the dens apex; barrel chest and severe kyphosis are relative contraindications because they compromise this trajectory.

## Steps

1. **Confirm fracture-line suitability** — anteroinferior-to-posterosuperior obliquity (horizontal-oblique) fracture lines are favorable; reverse-oblique lines are not suitable for this technique.
2. **Confirm the fracture is reducible** without a large residual fragment gap and confirm intact posterior fragment/dens continuity — comminuted (Type IIA) fractures are not appropriate for this technique.
3. **Plan trajectory** through the anteroinferior C2 body into the dens apex, accounting for body habitus/neck length/kyphosis.
4. **Place a single or double cannulated screw** across the fracture into the dens apex under fluoroscopic guidance.

## Pitfalls

- Not suitable for comminuted (Type IIA) fractures or reverse-oblique fracture lines — these require posterior C1-C2 fusion instead.
- Barrel chest and severe kyphosis are relative contraindications because they compromise the ability to achieve an adequate anterior-to-dens trajectory.
- Requires intact posterior fragment/dens continuity; without it, screw purchase and construct stability are unreliable.

## Post-op protocol

Post-op management follows the general odontoid fracture rehabilitation pathway: bracing/orthosis or wound care and pain control in the acute phase (0–2 weeks); progressive out-of-bed tolerance with brace-protected activity through the subacute phase (2–12 weeks), with interval imaging (flexion-extension radiographs or CT) at 6–12 weeks to confirm healing before brace weaning; progressive loading and brace weaning once radiographic union/stability is confirmed (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable injuries; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months with formal functional capacity evaluation. Return-to-sport/high-demand clearance, particularly for contact/collision sports, is individualized and typically requires solid radiographic fusion (≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, and normalized strength.
