## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Operative Type II (Anderson-D'Alonzo) odontoid fracture with a favorable fracture line — anteroinferior-to-posterosuperior obliquity (horizontal-oblique pattern) — that is reducible without a large residual fragment gap.
- Fracture line perpendicular or near-perpendicular to the axis of screw insertion (maximizes interfragmentary compression).
- Patient body habitus/neck length allowing an adequate anterior C2-body-to-dens-apex trajectory.
- Younger, active patients in whom preservation of C1-C2 rotation (~50% of total cervical rotation) is a priority.
- Acute presentation — biomechanical and biological fixation is most reliable within the first days to ~2–3 weeks of injury before fracture-line resorption/rounding occurs.

**Contraindications (absolute or strongly relative)**
- **Reverse-oblique fracture line** (posteroinferior-to-anterosuperior) — axial screw compression would distract rather than compress the fracture; this is an absolute contraindication to the technique.
- **Comminuted (Type IIA) fracture** — inadequate bone stock for compression across a comminuted base risks distraction nonunion.
- **Barrel chest / severe thoracic kyphosis / fixed cervical flexion deformity** — precludes the caudal-to-cephalad trajectory and adequate neck extension needed for guide-wire and screw passage.
- **Chronic fracture (>6 months) or established nonunion** — fibrous/sclerotic margins do not compress or heal reliably with a single screw.
- **Irreducible fracture or fixed dens displacement** not correctable with traction/positioning.
- **Associated transverse atlantal ligament (TAL) disruption** — an intact TAL is required for atlantoaxial stability even after successful dens union; combined instability is better served by posterior C1-C2 fusion.
- **Osteoporotic or pathologic bone with poor screw purchase expected**, morbid obesity limiting trajectory, or inability to tolerate supine neck extension (severe pulmonary disease, tracheostomy in the field of trajectory).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Thin-cut CT (sagittal/coronal reconstructions)** through C1-C2 is mandatory to define fracture obliquity, comminution, displacement, and angulation, and to simulate the planned screw trajectory preoperatively.
- **MRI** if TAL integrity or cord status is in question, or if ligamentous injury is suspected on CT (e.g., significant lateral mass overhang, prevertebral hematoma).
- **Dynamic/traction trial under fluoroscopy** (or intraoperative reduction) to confirm the fracture is reducible before committing to the anterior approach.
- **Trajectory simulation**: on the sagittal CT, draw a line from the anteroinferior corner of the C2 body through the dens apex — this predicts whether the sternum/chest wall will block the drill/screwdriver at the skin entry point given the patient's habitus and expected neck extension.
- **Implant selection**: cannulated, partially-threaded lag screws (typically 3.5–4.5mm diameter, titanium); have both 1 and 2 screw systems on the table. Single vs double screw is a fracture- and dens-diameter-dependent intraoperative decision — a single screw provides compression but not rotational control; two screws (when dens diameter allows, generally ≥9mm) add rotational stability but require a technically more demanding parallel trajectory.
- **Equipment**: biplanar fluoroscopy (or a single high-quality lateral C-arm plus an AP/open-mouth view) is essential — this procedure cannot be done safely without excellent intraoperative imaging; radiolucent operating table with capacity for a Mayfield or horseshoe headrest and cervical traction attachment; cannulated screw system with guide wires, cannulated drill, tap, and depth gauge; a bolster to allow shoulder retraction.
- **Anticipate a bail-out plan**: consent and prep should cover conversion to posterior C1-C2 fusion if intraoperative fluoroscopy reveals an unworkable trajectory, since the decision to abandon is sometimes only made after positioning and initial dissection.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia with awake fiberoptic intubation** is preferred in patients with an unstable dens or any neurologic concern, to avoid neck manipulation during induction; maintain manual in-line stabilization if intubating after induction.
- **Supine positioning** on a radiolucent table with the head secured in a horseshoe headrest, Mayfield tongs, or with the patient's own halo ring incorporated into the setup if one is already in place.
- **Neck extension is the single most important positioning maneuver** — the neck must be extended enough to align the anteroinferior C2 body with the dens apex along the planned trajectory, confirmed with a true lateral fluoroscopic shot before draping. Insufficient extension is the most common reason a technically sound trajectory becomes unachievable.
- Gentle longitudinal traction (via tongs, halter, or manual reduction under fluoroscopy) is applied to reduce displacement/angulation before final positioning is locked in; reassess reduction on both AP (open-mouth) and lateral views.
- **Shoulder retraction** with wide tape pulled caudally is often needed in short-necked or barrel-chested patients to clear the C-arm and instrument trajectory — plan and test this before draping, since it is far harder to add later.
- **Fluoroscopy setup**: two C-arms (true AP/open-mouth odontoid view and true lateral) positioned orthogonally around the head/neck, or a single C-arm that can be swept between the two planes — confirm both views are obtainable with the head/table in final position before prepping, because this determines whether the case is safely doable.
- **Prep and drape** a standard low anterior cervical (Smith-Robinson-type) field, typically right-sided (surgeon-dependent), extending from the chin to the sternal notch, with enough exposure for a transverse or oblique incision at approximately the C5-C6 level (the low entry point needed to achieve the caudal-to-cephalad trajectory).
- Mark the intended skin incision under fluoroscopic guidance using a spinal needle or radiopaque marker to confirm it overlies the correct trajectory before incising.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm fracture-line suitability and reducibility one final time** under live fluoroscopy after final positioning — reassess obliquity, displacement, and reduction on both AP and lateral views before making the incision.
2. **Make a low transverse (or oblique) anterior cervical incision**, typically centered around C5-C6, on the side of surgeon preference (right-handed surgeons commonly approach from the right).
3. **Develop the standard Smith-Robinson interval** between the sternocleidomastoid/carotid sheath laterally and the trachea/esophagus medially, retracting the strap muscles and dissecting bluntly down to the anterior vertebral bodies.
4. **Extend dissection cephalad** along the anterior surface of the cervical spine to expose the C2-C3 disc space and the anteroinferior border of the C2 body — this is the entry zone for the guide wire. The longus colli may need partial elevation; take care to stay subperiosteal/midline to avoid the sympathetic chain laterally.
5. **Confirm entry point under biplanar fluoroscopy**: the ideal entry point is at or just anterior to the anteroinferior lip of the C2 body, in the midline (or paramedian if placing two screws), low enough that the resulting trajectory can reach the dens apex without violating the C2-C3 disc.
6. **Pass the guide wire(s) under continuous or intermittent biplanar fluoroscopy**, aiming for the posterosuperior tip of the dens, ideally engaging the posterior cortex just at or below the dens apex (bicortical purchase is a key predictor of construct stability). Advance slowly with frequent lateral and AP checks — this is the technically decisive step of the procedure.
7. **Do not accept a suboptimal first pass** — if the initial K-wire trajectory is off-target, do not simply redirect from the same starting hole; a first pass that is not correctable is a bail-out trigger, since a second wire will preferentially track into the first (already-drilled) channel rather than take a new line.
8. **Once trajectory is confirmed on both views**, measure depth over the guide wire, then use the cannulated drill to prepare the screw path, followed by tapping if using a fully-threaded screw system (many partially-threaded lag-screw systems allow self-tapping).
9. **Place the cannulated lag screw(s) over the guide wire(s)**, advancing under lateral fluoroscopy, achieving purchase in the posterior/apical dens cortex and compressing the fracture as the screw seats — watch the fracture line close under fluoroscopy as compression is achieved.
10. **If placing two screws**, place and advance them in a stepwise, alternating fashion (partially advance the first, place the second guide wire, then complete both) to avoid the first screw blocking the trajectory for the second.
11. **Remove guide wires**, confirm final screw position and interfragmentary compression on AP, lateral, and (if available) oblique fluoroscopic views.
12. **Assess construct stability manually** under fluoroscopy with gentle flexion-extension of the head, if the surgeon judges this safe, to confirm no residual gross instability.
13. **Irrigate, achieve hemostasis, and close in layers** — platysma, subcutaneous tissue, and a subcuticular skin closure; a small drain may be used if oozing is significant, particularly with more extensive longus colli dissection.
14. **Postoperative imaging** (CT) before discharge or at first follow-up to document screw position and fracture alignment as a baseline.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Verify the trajectory is achievable on fluoroscopy **before** you commit to the incision — if a true lateral and true AP/open-mouth view cannot both be obtained with the neck maximally (but safely) extended, abandon the anterior approach at this stage rather than after dissection.
- Adequate C2 extension is what aligns the C2-body-to-dens-apex trajectory — small increments of extension can be the difference between an achievable and unachievable case; re-check the lateral fluoroscopic image after every positioning adjustment.
- Use a radiopaque marker or spinal needle on the skin under fluoroscopy to plan the incision level and trajectory before cutting — this avoids a mismatched incision level that then forces an awkward, angled instrument path.
- When placing two screws, a slightly divergent (rather than perfectly parallel) trajectory in the axial plane can make room for both guide wires without one impinging on the other's path in a narrow dens.
- Bicortical purchase at the dens apex (engaging, but not perforating far beyond, the posterior cortex) gives measurably better pull-out strength and compression than unicortical fixation — confirm wire tip position at or just through the posterior cortex on the lateral view.
- Keep the guide wire under fluoroscopic visualization continuously while drilling/tapping over it — advancing the drill without confirming wire position risks advancing the wire itself into the canal or cervicomedullary junction.
- If the fracture reduces well with traction/extension but re-displaces when traction is released, consider whether the trajectory and screw compression alone will be sufficient, or whether a more rigid posterior construct is needed instead.
- A slightly caudal starting point (rather than directly at the anteroinferior C2 lip) gives a shallower, more favorable trajectory angle in short-necked patients — but going too low risks crossing the C2-C3 disc.
- Have low threshold to abandon and convert to posterior C1-C2 fusion if the first guide-wire pass is unsatisfactory — a compromised second attempt through the same channel is a well-recognized cause of screw malposition and fixation failure.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Reverse-oblique fracture lines and Type IIA comminution are not suitable for this technique** — attempting anterior screw fixation in these patterns risks fracture distraction rather than compression and construct failure; use posterior C1-C2 fusion instead.
- **Barrel chest and severe kyphosis are relative contraindications** because they compromise the ability to achieve an adequate anterior-to-dens trajectory — assess this on preoperative imaging and confirm with intraoperative fluoroscopy before committing.
- **Intact posterior fragment/dens continuity is required**; without it, screw purchase and construct stability are unreliable.
- **Suboptimal first guide-wire trajectory**: once an initial K-wire hole is drilled off-target, a second attempt tends to be drawn back into the original channel — plan the first pass carefully and be willing to convert to a posterior approach rather than force a salvage anterior trajectory.
- **Esophageal or tracheal injury** from retraction — use padded, appropriately sized retractors and periodically release retraction during longer cases to reduce risk of postoperative dysphagia and rare esophageal perforation.
- **Vertebral artery injury** is a risk if the trajectory strays laterally off the midline dens axis — stay on true AP and lateral fluoroscopic confirmation throughout wire and screw passage.
- **Screw perforation into the spinal canal** if the trajectory is too steep/posterior or if the wire is advanced without fluoroscopic confirmation — can cause cord injury; always confirm wire tip position on lateral fluoroscopy before drilling over it.
- **Malunion or nonunion** if compression across the fracture is inadequate (e.g., screw threads crossing the fracture line rather than being purely distal to it in a lag-screw construct, or loss of reduction during screw passage).
- **Postoperative dysphagia** is common after anterior cervical exposure at any level, usually transient (days to a few weeks), related to retraction and prevertebral soft-tissue swelling.
- **Symptomatic hardware or screw backout** can occur, particularly with a single-screw construct in an active patient — some late series report an increased nonunion/hardware-related revision rate with single- versus double-screw constructs when dens diameter allows two screws.

## Postoperative Protocol

Post-op management follows the general odontoid fracture rehabilitation pathway: bracing/orthosis or wound care and pain control in the acute phase (0–2 weeks); progressive out-of-bed tolerance with brace-protected activity through the subacute phase (2–12 weeks), with interval imaging (flexion-extension radiographs or CT) at 6–12 weeks to confirm healing before brace weaning; progressive loading and brace weaning once radiographic union/stability is confirmed (3–6 months). Return to sedentary work is often possible by 6–8 weeks for stable injuries; heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months with formal functional capacity evaluation. Return-to-sport/high-demand clearance, particularly for contact/collision sports, is individualized and typically requires solid radiographic fusion (≥4–6 months), full pain-free ROM, and normalized strength.
