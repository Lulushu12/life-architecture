## Indications

Physiologically unstable or "borderline" polytrauma patients (per Pape/Hannover staging: stable, borderline, unstable, in extremis) in whom prolonged definitive fixation risks acting as a systemic "second hit" — precipitating ARDS, MODS, or death. Also indicated for multiple long-bone + pelvic + visceral injuries, significant chest injury (high risk of ARDS with reamed nailing), and significant TBI where a long anesthetic/hypotensive episode is poorly tolerated.

## Positioning & setup

ATLS primary survey and hemorrhage control take priority over detailed fracture assessment. Pelvic ring injuries receive a mechanical binder/sheet at the greater trochanters as an immediate temporizing measure before any definitive orthopedic intervention.

## Steps

1. Stage the patient physiologically: ATLS primary survey, then lactate, base deficit, coagulopathy, core temperature, and hemodynamic stability. ISS ≥ 16 activates the major trauma/polytrauma pathway. A Hannover/Pape "borderline" patient favors damage control orthopaedics (DCO) unless resuscitation trends clearly favor early definitive fixation within the first 24 h.
2. For unstable/borderline patients: apply temporary external fixation of long-bone and pelvic fractures (damage control orthopaedics), pursue hemorrhage control (pelvic binder, REBOA, or angioembolization as indicated), and begin ICU resuscitation to correct the "lethal triad" of hypothermia, coagulopathy, and acidosis before committing to definitive surgery.
3. Run damage control resuscitation in parallel: permissive hypotension (in the absence of TBI) until hemorrhage control, balanced 1:1:1 transfusion ratio (packed red cells:FFP:platelets) for massive transfusion, tranexamic acid within 3 h of injury, and correction of hypothermia/acidosis before any elective conversion to definitive fixation.
4. Manage the pelvic ring specifically: mechanical binder/sheet at the greater trochanters immediately, followed by exam under anesthesia and definitive fixation (percutaneous iliosacral screws, anterior external fixation, or open reduction) once hemodynamically controlled; angioembolization or pre-peritoneal packing addresses ongoing pelvic hemorrhage refractory to mechanical stabilization.
5. Reassess physiologic parameters serially (not just once on admission) — lactate clearance, base deficit, temperature, coagulation status, and hemodynamic trend, not a fixed "golden window," should dictate conversion timing from external fixation to definitive fixation, typically feasible around day 2–10 once the patient has normalized.
6. Use reaming cautiously in the multiply injured patient with significant chest injury — favor unreamed or minimally reamed nailing, or staged conversion from external fixation, when pulmonary reserve is compromised.
7. For physiologically stable or borderline-but-trending-favorably patients, Early Total Care (ETC) — definitive fixation of major long-bone and pelvic/acetabular fractures within the first 24–36 hours — reduces pulmonary complications, ICU stay, and cost when appropriately selected.

## Pitfalls

- No single validated algorithm is proven superior in RCTs; decision-making is physiology-driven, not purely time-driven — do not apply a blanket rule based on injury severity score alone.
- A polytrauma patient with rising lactate, falling temperature, or worsening coagulopathy should prompt abandoning ETC and reverting to DCO, regardless of how far along a planned definitive procedure is.
- Bilateral femoral fixation and prolonged reamed IM nailing are the classic "second hit" procedures most likely to precipitate ARDS/MODS in a physiologically unprepared patient — this is the core rationale for DCO.
- Orthopedic staging decisions cannot be made in isolation from the resuscitation trajectory (transfusion ratio, TXA timing, correction of the lethal triad).

## Post-op protocol

**Polytrauma (general framework)**
- ICU/acute phase: chest physiotherapy, early mobilization as permitted by injury complex and hemodynamic status (reduces ICU-acquired weakness and pulmonary complications), DVT prophylaxis.
- Subacute (weeks 2–8): fracture-specific weight-bearing/ROM protocols run in parallel across injured segments, sequenced by the limiting injury; interdisciplinary rehabilitation (PT/OT, sometimes rehabilitation medicine) is standard for multi-limb injury.
- Nutritional optimization (adequate protein intake, vitamin D repletion, correction of anemia) should be addressed early — catabolic stress from major trauma directly impairs fracture-healing biology and is a modifiable contributor to delayed union/nonunion risk across every fracture in a multi-injury patient.
- Return to work/sport is driven by the most functionally limiting individual injury; formal functional capacity evaluation is useful before clearing manual-labor occupations after major polytrauma.
