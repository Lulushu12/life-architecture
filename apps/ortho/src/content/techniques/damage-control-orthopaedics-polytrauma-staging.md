## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Physiologically unstable or "borderline" polytrauma patients (per Pape/Hannover staging: stable, borderline, unstable, in extremis) in whom prolonged definitive fixation risks acting as a systemic "second hit" — precipitating ARDS, MODS, or death.
- Multiple long-bone + pelvic + visceral injuries, where sequential definitive fixation of every fracture at once would prolong anesthesia and blood loss beyond what the patient's physiology can tolerate.
- Significant chest injury (pulmonary contusion) — high risk of ARDS with reamed nailing, particularly bilateral femoral nailing.
- Significant TBI where a long anesthetic/hypotensive episode, or a physiologic insult from major fixation surgery, is poorly tolerated by an already-injured brain.
- Ongoing hemorrhage or hemodynamic instability requiring damage-control resuscitation in parallel — pelvic ring disruption with mechanical instability, open long-bone fractures with major blood loss.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- The physiologically stable patient with an isolated or limited long-bone injury — Early Total Care (ETC) is preferred here, since DCO's temporary fixation adds an unnecessary second procedure, prolongs hospital stay, and increases pin-site infection risk without a physiologic benefit.
- A "borderline" patient whose resuscitation trend is clearly favorable (normalizing lactate, resolving coagulopathy, rewarming) within the first 24 hours may reasonably proceed to definitive fixation rather than routine damage-control staging — the decision is dynamic, not fixed at admission.
- External fixation is not a substitute for adequate resuscitation — do not use provisional fixation as a reason to defer aggressive correction of the lethal triad (hypothermia, coagulopathy, acidosis); the resuscitation must proceed in parallel, not be deferred to "after the frame is on."
- Do not apply external fixation across a zone of critical contamination or planned flap territory without coordinating with the soft-tissue/orthoplastic plan — poor pin placement can compromise later definitive fixation or flap options.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Physiologic staging tools**: have lactate, base deficit, core temperature, coagulation panel (INR, fibrinogen, platelets), and hemodynamic trend available and trended, not just a single admission value — the Pape/Hannover classification (stable/borderline/unstable/in extremis) and the decision to pursue DCO vs ETC should be reassessed at intervals through the resuscitation, not decided once.
- **Imaging**: whole-body CT ("pan-scan") in the hemodynamically stable-enough patient to identify the full injury burden before sequencing decisions are finalized; orthogonal plain radiographs of each long-bone/pelvic injury for fixator pin-corridor and frame-configuration planning; pelvic imaging (inlet/outlet views, CT) to characterize ring injury pattern before binder/frame application.
- **Equipment/instrumentation list**: external fixation sets appropriate to each anatomic region (femoral, tibial, pelvic supra-acetabular or iliac-crest pin sets), a pelvic binder or sheet for immediate temporizing use, C-arm fluoroscopy, and — if REBOA or angioembolization is anticipated — coordination with interventional radiology/vascular surgery in advance so equipment and personnel are mobilized without delay.
- **Multidisciplinary coordination**: DCO decisions are made jointly with trauma surgery/intensive care, not unilaterally by orthopedics — establish, before the patient reaches the OR, who is tracking the physiologic trend data and what the trigger points are for escalating or de-escalating the fixation strategy.
- **Sequencing plan**: for multiple long-bone injuries, plan the order of external fixator application to minimize total anesthetic time (typically most life- or limb-threatening first — pelvic hemorrhage control, then the injury most likely to cause ongoing blood loss or neurovascular compromise) and to allow simultaneous access for two surgical teams working on different limbs where feasible.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard for damage-control external fixation in the polytrauma patient; anesthesia and trauma/critical-care teams must be in direct communication throughout, since the surgical plan (which fractures get fixed, in what order, how quickly) is dictated by real-time hemodynamic and coagulation status, not a fixed preoperative plan.
- **Positioning**: supine for most long-bone and pelvic external fixation; positioning must accommodate simultaneous access for resuscitation lines, a possible concurrent laparotomy/thoracotomy, and multiple limbs if bilateral or multi-limb fixation is planned — coordinate table setup with general/trauma surgery before draping if a combined procedure is anticipated.
- **Table/imaging setup**: fluoroscopy (C-arm) available and positioned for pelvic and long-bone pin placement; a radiolucent table or table extension is required for pelvic external fixator application and any planned percutaneous iliosacral screw work.
- **Draping**: wide, generous prep and drape fields anticipating that the surgical plan may expand intraoperatively (e.g., an unstable pelvis found to need more extensive stabilization than planned) — prep the abdomen into the field if a concurrent laparotomy is a realistic possibility.
- **Landmarks/temporizing measures before the patient reaches theatre**: apply a mechanical pelvic binder or sheet at the level of the greater trochanters (not the iliac crests) as an immediate temporizing measure for any suspected pelvic ring injury, ideally in the emergency department before transfer to theatre; mark planned external fixator pin corridors and confirm they do not cross a planned laparotomy incision or a zone likely to be needed for later definitive fixation or flap coverage.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Stage the patient physiologically before committing to a fixation strategy**: complete the ATLS primary survey, then obtain lactate, base deficit, coagulation status, core temperature, and hemodynamic trend. An Injury Severity Score ≥ 16 activates the major trauma/polytrauma pathway; a Hannover/Pape "borderline" classification favors damage control orthopaedics (DCO) unless resuscitation trends clearly favor early definitive fixation within the first 24 hours.
2. **Apply mechanical pelvic binder/sheet immediately** at the greater trochanters for any suspected pelvic ring injury, before or on arrival to theatre, as the first temporizing step.
3. **Control hemorrhage in parallel with any orthopedic intervention**: pelvic binder, REBOA, or angioembolization as indicated by the bleeding source and hemodynamic response; do not proceed to definitive orthopedic fixation while active, uncontrolled hemorrhage is present.
4. **For unstable/borderline patients, apply temporary external fixation** of long-bone and pelvic fractures rather than definitive fixation: standard pin placement (safe corridors for the femur, tibia, and pelvis), spanning the fracture or joint as needed, with the goal of restoring gross length/alignment and controlling fracture-site bleeding and soft-tissue further injury — not anatomic reduction.
5. **Run damage control resuscitation concurrently, not sequentially**: permissive hypotension (in the absence of TBI) until hemorrhage control is achieved, balanced 1:1:1 transfusion ratio (packed red cells:FFP:platelets) for massive transfusion, tranexamic acid within 3 hours of injury, and active correction of hypothermia and acidosis — these resuscitation endpoints, not a fixed clock, determine readiness for any further surgery.
6. **Manage the pelvic ring specifically** once the patient is stable enough: exam under anesthesia to characterize residual instability, then definitive fixation (percutaneous iliosacral screws, anterior external fixation, or open reduction depending on pattern) once hemodynamically controlled; angioembolization or pre-peritoneal packing addresses ongoing pelvic hemorrhage refractory to mechanical stabilization (binder/external fixation) alone.
7. **Reassess physiologic parameters serially**, not just once at admission — lactate clearance, trending base deficit, core temperature, coagulation status, and hemodynamic trend, not a fixed "golden window," should dictate the timing of conversion from external fixation to definitive fixation. This is typically feasible around day 2–10 once the patient has normalized, but should be individualized to the trend data rather than a calendar date.
8. **Use reaming cautiously** in the multiply injured patient with significant chest injury when eventually proceeding to IM nailing — favor unreamed or minimally reamed nailing, or staged conversion from external fixation to definitive nailing/plating once pulmonary reserve has recovered, rather than proceeding to reamed nailing while pulmonary status is still marginal.
9. **For patients who are physiologically stable, or borderline but trending favorably**, proceed to Early Total Care (ETC): definitive fixation of major long-bone and pelvic/acetabular fractures within the first 24–36 hours reduces pulmonary complications, ICU stay, and cost when the patient is appropriately selected for this pathway.
10. **At the time of conversion from external fixation to definitive fixation**, remove pin sites from the planned surgical field with adequate lead time where feasible (or plan an intervening pin-site-free interval) to reduce the risk of converting a colonized external fixator pin tract into a deep infection at the definitive implant site.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Treat DCO-vs-ETC as a continuously re-evaluated decision, not a one-time triage call made in the trauma bay — build in explicit reassessment checkpoints (e.g., at 6, 12, and 24 hours) where the physiologic trend, not the initial classification, drives the next step.
- Sequence external fixator application across multiple injured limbs to minimize total anesthetic and tourniquet time — apply the fixator most critical for hemorrhage or neurovascular control first, and use two simultaneous surgical teams on separate limbs when staffing allows.
- Place external fixator pins outside the zone of any anticipated definitive-fixation incision or planned flap territory whenever the fracture pattern allows a choice of corridor — this avoids having to work around (or through) a colonized pin tract at the definitive procedure.
- Keep the external fixator construct simple and fast to apply in the acute damage-control setting (goal: restore length/alignment/rotation and control bleeding, not achieve anatomic reduction) — anatomic precision is deferred to the definitive procedure once the patient is physiologically ready.
- Coordinate pelvic binder removal timing carefully — a binder left on too long risks skin breakdown over the greater trochanters and sacrum; plan definitive pelvic stabilization (or a switch to an anterior external fixator) as soon as the patient's physiology allows, rather than leaving a binder on indefinitely as a default.
- Maintain a running, shared physiologic dashboard (lactate, base deficit, temperature, coagulation, hemodynamics) visible to both the orthopedic and trauma/ICU teams so the decision to convert to definitive fixation is made jointly and on current data, not on orthopedics' schedule alone.
- When damage-control external fixation must span a joint, plan the frame configuration to allow the earliest possible joint mobilization once definitive fixation is completed, rather than leaving unnecessary joint-spanning bars that will contribute to later stiffness.
- Document ischemia time, pin-tract condition, and neurovascular status explicitly at every dressing change/ward round during the DCO interval — these are the data points most likely to be needed if a complication develops and are easy to lose track of in a busy polytrauma admission.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **No single validated algorithm is proven superior in RCTs**; decision-making is physiology-driven, not purely time-driven — do not apply a blanket rule based on injury severity score alone.
- **A polytrauma patient with rising lactate, falling temperature, or worsening coagulopathy** should prompt abandoning ETC and reverting to DCO, regardless of how far along a planned definitive procedure is.
- **Bilateral femoral fixation and prolonged reamed IM nailing** are the classic "second hit" procedures most likely to precipitate ARDS/MODS in a physiologically unprepared patient — this is the core rationale for DCO.
- **Orthopedic staging decisions cannot be made in isolation from the resuscitation trajectory** (transfusion ratio, TXA timing, correction of the lethal triad) — a fixation decision made without current resuscitation data is a common and avoidable error.
- **ARDS/MODS** remains the feared systemic complication of an inappropriately timed "second hit" major fixation surgery — recognized by worsening oxygenation, new bilateral infiltrates, and rising inflammatory markers in the days following a major procedure; management reverts to supportive ICU care and avoidance of further major surgical insult until the patient recovers.
- **Pin-site infection during the DCO interval** can compromise the definitive fixation site if pins are not thoughtfully placed or if the interval between fixator removal and definitive surgery is too short for a colonized tract to settle — track pin sites explicitly and consider culture/antibiotic coverage if there is any sign of infection before converting to definitive fixation.
- **Prolonged, unindicated external fixation** in a patient who has actually stabilized physiologically is itself a complication risk (infection, malalignment, patient discomfort, delayed rehabilitation) — reassess for conversion readiness proactively rather than defaulting to a fixed interval.
- **Delayed union/nonunion and malunion** can result from polytrauma damage-control staging itself (prolonged external fixation, delayed conversion to definitive fixation) — these are recognized downstream complications of the staging strategy, not just of the initial injury.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **ICU/acute phase**: chest physiotherapy, early mobilization as permitted by injury complex and hemodynamic status (reduces ICU-acquired weakness and pulmonary complications), DVT prophylaxis.
- **Subacute (weeks 2–8)**: fracture-specific weight-bearing/ROM protocols run in parallel across injured segments, sequenced by the limiting injury; interdisciplinary rehabilitation (PT/OT, sometimes rehabilitation medicine) is standard for multi-limb injury.
- **Nutritional optimization** (adequate protein intake, vitamin D repletion, correction of anemia) should be addressed early — catabolic stress from major trauma directly impairs fracture-healing biology and is a modifiable contributor to delayed union/nonunion risk across every fracture in a multi-injury patient.
- **Return to work/sport** is driven by the most functionally limiting individual injury; formal functional capacity evaluation is useful before clearing manual-labor occupations after major polytrauma.
