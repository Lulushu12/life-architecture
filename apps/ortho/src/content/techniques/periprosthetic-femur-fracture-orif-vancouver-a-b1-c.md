## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Vancouver A fractures with displacement threatening abductor function or a large fragment.
- Vancouver B1 fractures around/just distal to the stem with the **stem confirmed well-fixed** on imaging and/or intraoperative stress testing.
- Vancouver C fractures well distal to the stem tip, implant uninvolved.
- Most Vancouver A fractures are treated non-operatively and do not require this technique.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- A stem found to be loose on intraoperative stress testing, regardless of preoperative Vancouver classification — this converts the case to a revision arthroplasty (see the companion B2/B3 technique) and ORIF alone will reliably fail.
- Active periprosthetic joint infection — ORIF into an infected bed is unlikely to achieve union and risks establishing chronic infection; work up infection first if there is any clinical suspicion.
- Severely comminuted or segmental fracture patterns with inadequate bone stock to achieve stable fixation even with locking technology — consider revision or a combined plate-revision construct instead.
- Medically unfit patients in whom even a shorter ORIF procedure carries prohibitive risk — coordinate with orthogeriatric/medical teams on timing and, in truly non-ambulatory or end-of-life patients, discuss goals of care and non-operative palliation as an alternative.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** obtain orthopedic-quality full-length femur radiographs (not just the hip/joint) to visualize the entire implant and plan plate length/screw overlap; compare against the earliest available post-index-arthroplasty films to detect any interval subsidence, new circumferential radiolucent line, or cement mantle fracture that would reclassify an apparent B1 as B2.
- CT is useful for fracture comminution/extension planning when plain films are equivocal, and helps confirm implant/canal geometry.
- **Implant selection:** choose a locking compression plate long enough to bypass the stem tip substantially and capture adequate bicortical fixation distally; have both unicortical locking screws (for the segment where the stem occupies the canal) and cerclage cable/wire passers available for the proximal segment. Have cortical strut allograft on standby for cases needing added rotational stability or where screw purchase around the stem is marginal.
- **Equipment list:** full periprosthetic/trauma locking plate set with variable-angle locking screw options, cerclage cable and passer set, cortical strut allograft, a large pointed reduction clamp and bone hook for provisional reduction, and — critically — a backup revision stem system and extraction instruments available in the room in case the stem is found loose intraoperatively and the plan must convert.
- **Case-specific planning:** confirm from operative records or implant stickers the exact stem type/length so the plate can be planned to avoid conflicting with existing proximal fixation (e.g., a collared or proximally porous-coated stem changes where unicortical screws can be placed).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- General or combined general/regional (spinal + catheter) anesthesia is typical; regional anesthesia alone is often inadequate given the length and positioning demands of a combined hip-and-trauma exposure.
- Lateral decubitus position on a radiolucent table is standard for most approaches to allow full-length fluoroscopic imaging of the femur; a bump or peg-board positioner secures the pelvis.
- Prep and drape the entire limb circumferentially from the iliac crest to the knee (occasionally to the ankle) to allow full-length intraoperative imaging and, if needed, extension of the approach or conversion to a combined ETO/revision exposure.
- Set up a large fluoroscopy unit (C-arm) draped and positioned to image the full length of the femur without repositioning — periprosthetic fracture fixation is fluoroscopy-intensive and repeated draping breaks are costly.
- Mark the prior hip incision(s) preoperatively and plan the new incision to incorporate or extend from it where possible; mark the fracture level and planned plate span on the skin using the preoperative full-length films as a guide.
- A radiolucent traction table is not routinely required for ORIF (as opposed to closed nailing), but a bump/positioner that allows longitudinal traction and derotation through an assistant or a femoral distractor is useful for reduction.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm stem stability before committing to ORIF.** Compare current films to the earliest available post-index-arthroplasty series for interval subsidence, a new circumferential radiolucent line, or cement mantle fracture. Even with reassuring imaging, reconfirm intraoperatively by manual stress testing of the stem after exposure (direct axial and rotational load applied to the exposed proximal stem/calcar) — be prepared to convert immediately to revision arthroplasty if the stem is found loose.
2. **Approach and exposure.** Use a lateral or anterolateral approach centered over the fracture, extending proximally to expose the greater trochanter/proximal femur if a Vancouver A component is being addressed concurrently, and distally to expose the full extent of planned plate fixation. Elevate the vastus lateralis off the lateral intermuscular septum, ligating or cauterizing perforating vessels as they are encountered — uncontrolled perforator bleeding is a common source of unexpected blood loss in this exposure.
3. **Fracture reduction.** Reduce the fracture provisionally with a combination of manual traction/derotation, a bone hook, and pointed reduction clamps; use indirect reduction technique (minimizing periosteal stripping at the fracture site) wherever comminution allows, to preserve fracture-site biology. Confirm rotational alignment clinically (comparing to the contralateral limb) and fluoroscopically before committing to fixation.
4. **Vancouver A fixation (when indicated):** for a greater trochanter fragment threatening abductor function, use a claw plate or cable-grip system to restore the abductor lever arm; for a lesser trochanter fragment with iliopsoas avulsion concern, a tension band/cerclage construct with a lag screw into the calcar (when bone quality allows) restores fixation.
5. **Vancouver B1 fixation:** apply a locking compression plate spanning the fracture along the lateral femur. Proximally, place locking screws unicortically around the retained stem where the canal is occupied, or use cerclage cables/wires looped around the plate and femur where screw fixation is not possible due to the implant. Distally, achieve solid bicortical locking (and/or non-locking, compression) fixation well beyond the fracture. Augment with additional cerclage cables and/or a cortical strut allograft laid along the plate for added rotational stability, particularly in osteoporotic bone or where proximal fixation is marginal.
6. **Vancouver C fixation:** treat the fracture as a native periprosthetic diaphyseal injury distal to the implant — apply locked plating (open or via a minimally invasive submuscular technique to preserve fracture-site biology) spanning well proximal and distal to the fracture. Retrograde or antegrade IM nailing can be considered only if implant geometry allows adequate proximal overlap without interfering with the existing stem — confirm this from imaging before committing.
7. **Construct optimization.** Use cable-plate constructs (cables passed through or around dedicated plate cable-grip slots) rather than cerclage cables alone around the retained stem — this materially improves rotational control compared to cables alone, which can loosen and migrate over time without a plate to resist rotation.
8. **Provisional and final fixation check.** Before final tightening, confirm reduction and alignment fluoroscopically in two planes (AP and lateral), check limb length and rotation clinically, and cycle the hip/knee through a gentle range of motion to confirm no hardware impingement.
9. **Final construct assessment.** After final screw/cable tightening, obtain full-length fluoroscopic or radiographic images confirming acceptable alignment, adequate screw/cable purchase, and no missed intraoperative fracture propagation (a real risk with cerclage passage or drilling near thin cortical bone around a retained stem).
10. **Closure.** Close the vastus lateralis fascia over the plate, followed by standard layered closure; place a drain if extensive dissection or perforator ligation occurred. Confirm distal neurovascular status is unchanged from the preoperative exam before leaving the room.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always have a revision stem and extraction tray in the room, even for a "confirmed" B1 case — intraoperative stem stability testing occasionally overturns the preoperative plan, and being unprepared to convert is a foreseeable and avoidable delay.
- Use indirect reduction and minimize periosteal stripping at the fracture site wherever fracture pattern allows — biology matters as much as mechanics for periprosthetic fracture union, especially in older, osteoporotic bone.
- Pass cerclage cables (not wires) where possible around a retained stem — cables have shown less proximal migration than wires in comparative biomechanical work, and typically 3–4 cables/cables-plus-screws are used to secure a construct spanning a retained implant.
- When bypassing a stem tip with a plate, plan screw/cable spacing to avoid clustering fixation points immediately at the stem tip — this recreates the same stress-riser problem the plate is meant to solve.
- Use unicortical locking screws where the stem occupies the canal and reserve bicortical fixation for segments of bone free of implant — attempting bicortical purchase through an occupied canal risks impinging on or damaging the retained stem.
- Confirm rotational alignment by comparing to the contralateral, uninjured limb intraoperatively (with both legs prepped into the field or using pre-marked reference points) — rotational malreduction is easy to miss on fluoroscopy alone.
- For Vancouver C fractures, extend the plate generously proximal and distal to the fracture (aim for at least 2–3 times the fracture length of plate overlap on each side) to distribute load and avoid a new stress riser at the plate ends.
- Add cortical strut allograft along the plate in osteoporotic or marginal-fixation cases — it adds both mechanical support and a biologic scaffold that can incorporate over time.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Misjudging stem stability preoperatively is the central pitfall — always be prepared to convert to revision intraoperatively if the stem is found loose on manual testing.
- Failing to obtain full-length femur imaging leaves implant overlap/bypass planning incomplete.
- Nonunion/malunion risk is higher when fixation does not achieve adequate mechanical stability across the fracture, and when performed around a loose or osteoporotic construct — reassess stem stability if healing stalls.
- Hardware failure and recurrent periprosthetic fracture can occur at the proximal end of the plate — a new stress riser created by the construct itself.
- Perforator vessel injury during vastus lateralis elevation is a frequent, underappreciated source of unplanned blood loss — identify and control perforators proactively rather than reactively.
- Cerclage cable/wire passage around thin, osteoporotic, or previously perforated cortex can itself propagate a fracture — pass cables carefully under direct vision or with a dedicated passer, and check fluoroscopically for new fracture lines after each pass.
- A critical population-level point: **elevated 1-year mortality**, with the PIPPAS multicenter prospective study (2025) specifically characterizing risk factors and mortality outcomes in Vancouver type C fractures. Periprosthetic hip fractures in the elderly carry hip-fracture-equivalent mortality risk and should be treated with the same medical optimization urgency (same-admission surgery where feasible, orthogeriatric co-management, VTE prophylaxis, osteoporosis treatment initiation).

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Weeks 0–6:** protected weight-bearing (toe-touch to 50% partial) pending radiographic union evidence; hip precautions per approach used; formal physiotherapy for gait training, isometric strengthening, and edema control begins immediately.
- **Weeks 6–12:** progression to full weight-bearing by **10–12 weeks** if callus/bridging present on serial films; active strengthening of abductors/core and gait normalization.
- **Weeks 12–16:** union typically expected; delayed union warrants endocrine/metabolic work-up (vitamin D, calcium, parathyroid function) and consideration of bone-stimulation adjuncts beyond 4–6 months if progress stalls.
- Every displaced periprosthetic hip fracture in a patient over 65 should trigger the same orthogeriatric co-management pathway used for native hip fractures (same-admission surgery target, delirium prevention, VTE prophylaxis, bone-health assessment and osteoporosis treatment initiation) given comparable mortality burden — including in implant-uninvolved Vancouver C fractures.
