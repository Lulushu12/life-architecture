## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Symptomatic structural FAI syndrome refractory to conservative care, per the Warwick Agreement framework: symptoms + positive clinical signs (e.g., FADIR) + imaging morphology (cam and/or pincer), failing a minimum of 8–12 weeks, often 3–6 months, of structured physiotherapy.
- Best outcomes are seen in Tönnis grade 0–1, joint space ≥2 mm, and age <40, though good results are reported into the 50s in appropriately selected patients with preserved joint space.
- Mechanical locking/catching from an unstable labral flap or a large loose body may justify earlier surgical consideration even before completing a full conservative trial.

**Contraindications**
- Tönnis grade ≥2 with advanced joint-space narrowing — arthroscopic correction does not reliably improve outcomes and carries a high conversion-to-THA rate; consider arthroplasty counseling instead.
- Significant acetabular dysplasia (LCEA <20°) as an isolated finding — this is a relative-to-absolute contraindication to isolated arthroscopic rim trimming, since further bony resection in an already undercovered hip risks iatrogenic instability; combined or staged osteotomy should be considered instead (see Borderline dysplasia below).
- Active hip joint infection.
- Advanced osteoarthritis or bone-on-bone joint space loss.
- Relative: severe obesity, which increases traction-related complication risk and technical difficulty of achieving adequate distraction.
- Relative: workers' compensation/unresolved secondary-gain litigation status, associated in the literature with inferior patient-reported outcomes independent of surgical technique.
- Relative: unaddressed extra-articular pain generators (lumbar spine, adductor-related groin pain, iliopsoas pathology) that have not been evaluated separately — operating on FAI morphology will not resolve a coexisting extra-articular source of pain.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Radiographs**: AP pelvis (LCEA, Tönnis angle/grade, crossover sign for acetabular retroversion, Shenton's line), frog-leg or Dunn lateral (alpha angle), and false-profile view (anterior center-edge angle) to fully characterize cam, pincer, and coverage parameters before the OR.
- **MRI or MR arthrography**: characterize labral tear pattern/size, chondral status (delamination, particularly anterosuperior and the posteroinferior contrecoup lesion in pincer-type impingement), and confirm absence of significant dysplasia; MR arthrography increases sensitivity for labral/chondral lesions to roughly 90%+ versus 70–80% for non-arthrographic MRI.
- **CT with 3D reconstruction** in cases of suspected combined or atypical version abnormality (femoral or acetabular retroversion/anteversion), or when planning combined osteotomy — provides more precise version measurement than plain radiographs/MRI alone.
- **Diagnostic intra-articular injection** (fluoroscopic- or ultrasound-guided local anesthetic ± corticosteroid) preoperatively to confirm an intra-articular pain generator; a strongly positive response (>50–75% temporary relief) is one of the best available predictors of a favorable surgical outcome and should be documented before committing to surgery, particularly when extra-articular pathology is also plausible.
- **Template the bony resection**: measure the alpha angle at multiple clock-face positions on preoperative imaging to anticipate the extent of cam resection needed to restore a normal head-neck offset (target <50°), and assess acetabular overcoverage (LCEA, crossover sign) to plan rim trimming depth if pincer morphology is present.
- **Equipment**: arthroscopic tower, fluid pump with pressure control, a hip distraction system (traction table with a well-padded perineal post, or a postless distraction system if available), long arthroscopic instruments (70° and 30° arthroscopes), radiofrequency ablation device, motorized burr for osteochondroplasty/acetabuloplasty, suture anchor system for labral repair, and fluoroscopy for intraoperative confirmation of resection adequacy.
- **Consider a postless distraction technique** if available and within the surgeon's experience — growing evidence (see Pitfalls) associates postless traction with a lower rate of perineal/pudendal complications compared with a traditional perineal post.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia is standard, often combined with a regional block (e.g., fascia iliaca or periarticular local infiltration) for postoperative analgesia; muscle relaxation facilitates distraction.
- **Position**: supine on a traction table (most common) or in the lateral decubitus position, depending on surgeon preference and equipment; supine positioning is more common in North American practice.
- **Perineal post**: if used, a well-padded, oversized post (>8–10 cm diameter) positioned laterally against the upper thigh of the operative leg, lateralizing the proximal thigh to avoid direct central perineal pressure — this specifically reduces pudendal nerve compression risk. Postless distraction systems avoid the post-related risk profile entirely and are an increasingly used alternative.
- **Limb positioning for distraction**: the operative hip is positioned in neutral flexion-extension, slight abduction to neutral, and often 15–20° of internal rotation to offset native femoral anteversion and open the joint more symmetrically; some protocols use 10–20° of flexion.
- **Traction parameters**: apply gentle traction to achieve approximately 8–10 mm (roughly 1 cm) of joint distraction, confirmed fluoroscopically before starting; keep applied traction force as low as achieves adequate distraction. Reported safe thresholds associated with no postoperative sensory/motor dysfunction are below approximately 120 lb peak force, 82 lb mean force, and 61 minutes of total traction time — traction time should be minimized and released intermittently during long cases (e.g., between the central- and peripheral-compartment portions of the procedure) whenever feasible.
- **Fluoroscopy**: positioned to confirm distraction distance and portal trajectory before capsulotomy, and used again at the end of the case to confirm adequacy of osteochondroplasty/rim resection and restoration of a normal alpha angle and head-neck offset.
- **Draping**: circumferential drape of the operative leg from the iliac crest to below the knee, with the perineal post (if used) draped separately and accessible for intraoperative padding checks.
- **Surface landmarks marked**: anterior superior iliac spine, greater trochanter, and the planned anterolateral, mid-anterior, and posterolateral portal sites, marked with the hip in the distracted position under fluoroscopic guidance before the first stab incision.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Apply traction and confirm distraction** fluoroscopically (target approximately 8–10 mm of joint space widening) before making any portal, minimizing total traction time from this point forward.
2. **Establish the anterolateral portal** first under fluoroscopic guidance, typically the initial viewing portal, followed by a mid-anterior (or anterior) portal established under direct arthroscopic visualization to maintain a safe triangulation and avoid injury to the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, which crosses near the anterior portal trajectory. A posterolateral or distal accessory portal may be added as needed for instrumentation access.
3. **Perform an interportal capsulotomy** connecting the anterolateral and mid-anterior portals to gain working access to the central compartment, sized to what is needed for instrumentation while preserving as much capsular tissue as reasonable for later repair.
4. **Inspect the central compartment**: assess the labrum circumferentially, the articular cartilage of the acetabulum and femoral head, and the ligamentum teres; correlate findings with preoperative imaging and grade chondral lesions.
5. **Treat the labrum**:
 - *Repair* (preferred when tissue quality and length are adequate): debride the frayed labral edge minimally, prepare the acetabular rim bleeding bed, place suture anchors along the rim at the base of the tear, and pass sutures in a circumferential or mattress fashion to re-establish the labral seal against the femoral head — restoring the suction seal is the biomechanical goal, not simply approximating tissue.
 - *Selective debridement*: reserved for degenerative, non-reparable, or very short/frayed tissue segments; resect only the unstable/torn portion, preserving as much of the labral seal as possible.
 - *Reconstruction* (autograft — iliotibial band or capsule — or allograft): reserved for irreparable tears, prior failed repair, or hypoplastic/absent labral tissue; the segment is excised and a graft strip is anchored circumferentially to recreate labral height and the sealing function.
6. **Release traction** and reposition the hip in flexion to move into the peripheral compartment for cam correction, once central-compartment work is complete — this both reduces total traction time and provides the exposure needed for femoral neck work.
7. **Perform a peripheral capsulotomy** (T-type or extended interportal, per surgeon preference) to expose the femoral head-neck junction circumferentially.
8. **Perform femoral osteochondroplasty for cam morphology**: use a motorized burr to resect the excess anterosuperior (and, as needed, more circumferential) bone at the head-neck junction, working through a dynamic range of motion (flexion, internal rotation) to confirm resection of the exact impinging segment rather than an arbitrary predetermined area. Check resection depth and contour against fluoroscopy and dynamic impingement testing repeatedly during the resection — under-resection is far more common, and far more consequential for the reoperation rate, than over-resection.
9. **Perform acetabular rim trimming/acetabuloplasty for pincer morphology**, if present: after taking down the labrum from the rim (or working around a repaired labrum with careful protection), resect the overhanging acetabular bone with a burr to normalize coverage (target LCEA and crossover sign correction), then re-fix the labrum to the freshly prepared rim if it was taken down. Resect conservatively and re-check coverage parameters frequently — this step carries the highest risk of iatrogenic instability if overdone.
10. **Address chondral lesions** encountered (microfracture/marrow stimulation for focal full-thickness defects, chondroplasty for unstable flap lesions) as indicated by lesion grade and location.
11. **Manage the capsule**: perform capsular closure or plication of the interportal and/or peripheral capsulotomy, which is increasingly favored over leaving an unrepaired capsulotomy, based on biomechanical and clinical data associating unrepaired capsulotomy with postoperative microinstability, particularly in hips with borderline coverage or high-demand/hypermobile patients.
12. **Final dynamic and fluoroscopic check**: take the hip through a full range of motion (including deep flexion-internal rotation and extension-external rotation) to confirm no residual impingement and no iatrogenic instability, and obtain final fluoroscopic images confirming restored alpha angle/head-neck offset and appropriate acetabular coverage.
13. **Close portals** in standard fashion with subcuticular sutures, and infiltrate the portal sites and capsule with local anesthetic for early postoperative analgesia.
14. **Consider combined periacetabular osteotomy** in borderline dysplasia (LCEA 18–25°) with FAI-type symptoms when instability features (positive apprehension, acetabular version abnormality) dominate the clinical picture — either as a combined same-day "FAO" (femoral-acetabular osteotomy) procedure or staged, versus isolated arthroscopy when impingement features dominate without coverage-based instability. For complex or combined cam/pincer deformity not amenable to arthroscopic correction, extensive posterior/global overcoverage, or deformity requiring simultaneous femoral and acetabular correction with direct visualization, an open surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach) should be considered instead of forcing an arthroscopic solution.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Minimize total traction time by sequencing the case to complete central-compartment work first, then releasing traction before moving to the peripheral compartment for cam resection — traction-related nerve injury risk rises with cumulative time and force, not either variable alone.
- Use an oversized, well-padded perineal post positioned laterally against the thigh (not centrally against the perineum) if a post is used at all; consider a postless distraction technique where available, which several comparative series associate with a lower rate of pudendal/perineal complications.
- Confirm portal trajectory under direct visualization for the second (mid-anterior) portal rather than by external landmarks alone — the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve's course is variable enough that landmark-only portal placement carries meaningful injury risk.
- Perform cam resection dynamically: resect a segment, then move the hip through flexion/internal rotation to reassess impingement, rather than resecting a fixed area based on static preoperative imaging alone — the functional impingement zone is best confirmed intraoperatively.
- Check the alpha angle and head-neck offset fluoroscopically at multiple points during the osteochondroplasty, not just at the end — this catches under-resection while it is still easy to correct, rather than after closure.
- When repairing the labrum, prioritize restoring the chondrolabral suction seal over simply reapproximating torn edges — anchor placement close to the articular margin and circumferential suture technique better restore the seal's fluid-sealing/load-distributing function.
- Re-check acetabular coverage parameters (visually and, if needed, fluoroscopically) at intervals during rim trimming rather than resecting the full planned amount in one pass — this is the best safeguard against the single most consequential preventable complication in this procedure (iatrogenic instability from over-resection).
- Repair or plicate the capsule as a default in most cases rather than leaving it open, reserving an unrepaired capsulotomy for specific circumstances (e.g., very stiff hips where increased postoperative motion is prioritized) — routine closure reduces the incidence of postoperative microinstability symptoms.
- Have a diagnostic injection response documented preoperatively and revisit it if intraoperative findings are less than expected — a patient with a strongly positive injection response but underwhelming visualized pathology warrants a careful second look for a missed lesion (chondral flap, loose body, subtle labral tear) before closing.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Iatrogenic instability from over-resection of the acetabular rim, especially in undiagnosed borderline dysplasia, is one of the most consequential and preventable complications — underscores the importance of preoperative LCEA assessment and conservative, incremental rim trimming.
- Under-resection of the cam lesion is the most common reason for revision arthroscopy or persistent symptoms — dynamic intraoperative reassessment through range of motion is the best safeguard.
- Aggressive osteochondroplasty can weaken the femoral neck enough to cause a femoral neck fracture (rare, catastrophic) — avoid excessive resection depth/width, particularly circumferentially, and consider protected weight-bearing postoperatively after extensive resections.
- Traction-related complications: pudendal nerve injury is reported in roughly 1.8% of cases, typically transient (resolving within about 3 months), and is associated with perineal post use and longer traction times; sciatic and femoral neurapraxia are less common and also generally transient. Minimizing traction force/time and using adequate post padding (or a postless technique) are the primary mitigation strategies.
- Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury/neurapraxia can result from portal placement, particularly the anterior/mid-anterior portal — establish accessory portals under direct visualization rather than by external landmarks alone.
- Heterotopic ossification and capsular adhesions/scarring occur in a minority; NSAID prophylaxis is used selectively in higher-risk patients (extensive bony resection, prior heterotopic ossification).
- Progression to osteoarthritis/THA conversion occurs in a meaningful minority over 10+ years even after successful arthroscopy, particularly with pre-existing joint-space narrowing or advanced chondral damage at index surgery — conversion rates up to approximately 24% at 11–12 years have been reported in cohorts with baseline OA, informing preoperative counseling about arthroscopy as a symptom-delaying rather than universally disease-modifying procedure in this subgroup.
- Fluid extravasation (intra-abdominal or extra-articular soft-tissue fluid extravasation) is a recognized, occasionally serious complication of prolonged high-pressure fluid pump use, particularly with capsulotomy left widely open; monitor case duration and fluid pressure, and have a low threshold to check the abdomen intraoperatively/postoperatively in prolonged cases.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing / ROM | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Protection | Weeks 0–2 | 20 lb flat-foot/partial weight-bearing with crutches (labral repair generally more restrictive than isolated debridement); hip flexion limited to <90°, avoid extremes of external rotation to protect capsular repair | Circumduction/passive ROM, quadriceps/gluteal activation, cryotherapy, CPM or stationary bike (no resistance) in many protocols |
| II — Early strengthening | Weeks 2–6 | Progress to full weight-bearing off crutches by ~2–4 weeks (per surgeon protocol and intraoperative findings — microfracture or extensive osteoplasty may extend protection) | Progressive ROM to full, closed-chain strengthening (bridges, mini-squats), gait normalization, aquatic therapy |
| III — Strength/neuromuscular | Weeks 6–12 | Full weight-bearing, full ROM | Hip/core/pelvic strengthening progression, single-leg stability work, stationary bike/elliptical, begin light jogging around 8–12 weeks if strength and gait criteria met |
| IV — Return to sport | 3–6 months | — | Sport-specific drills, plyometrics, cutting/pivoting progression; return-to-sport criteria: symmetric strength (≥90% of contralateral limb), full pain-free ROM, successful sport-specific functional testing, typically 4–6 months for pivoting/contact sport, earlier (~3–4 months) for running-based sport |

Return-to-work timing: sedentary work 1–2 weeks, physically demanding/manual labor 8–12 weeks depending on procedure extent (isolated labral repair faster than combined extensive osteochondroplasty).
