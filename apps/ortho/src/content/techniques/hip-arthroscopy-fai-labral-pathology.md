## Indications

Symptomatic structural FAI syndrome refractory to conservative care (per the Warwick Agreement framework: symptoms + clinical signs + imaging morphology, failing a minimum 8–12 weeks, often 3–6 months, of structured physiotherapy). Best outcomes are seen in Tönnis grade 0–1, joint space ≥2 mm, and age <40 (though good results are reported into the 50s in appropriately selected patients). Absence of significant dysplasia is important — LCEA <20° is a relative contraindication to isolated arthroscopic rim trimming, since further bony resection in an already undercovered hip risks iatrogenic instability.

## Positioning & setup

Patient selection is typically confirmed preoperatively with a diagnostic intra-articular injection (fluoroscopic- or ultrasound-guided local anesthetic ± corticosteroid) to confirm an intra-articular pain generator, which is a strong predictor of a positive surgical response.

## Steps

1. Confirm patient-selection criteria intraoperatively/preoperatively (Tönnis grade, joint space, LCEA, age).
2. Correct cam and/or pincer morphology: femoral osteochondroplasty for cam lesions, acetabular rim trimming for pincer/overcoverage.
3. Treat the labrum: repair is favored over debridement when tissue quality and length are adequate; selective debridement remains an option when repair is not feasible. Labral reconstruction (autograft — iliotibial band or capsule; or allograft) is reserved for irreparable tears, prior failed repair, or hypoplastic/absent labral tissue.
4. Manage the capsule: closure/plication is increasingly favored over an unrepaired capsulotomy, based on biomechanical and clinical data on postoperative microinstability.
5. In borderline dysplasia (LCEA 18–25°) with FAI-type symptoms, consider combined periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) with arthroscopy ("FAO") when instability features (positive apprehension, acetabular version abnormality) dominate; isolated arthroscopy is favored when impingement features dominate without coverage-based instability.
6. For complex or combined cam/pincer deformity not amenable to arthroscopic correction, extensive posterior/global overcoverage, or combined deformity requiring simultaneous femoral and acetabular correction, consider open surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach) for direct visualization instead.

## Pitfalls

- Iatrogenic instability from over-resection of the acetabular rim, especially in undiagnosed borderline dysplasia, is one of the most consequential and preventable complications — underscores the importance of preoperative LCEA assessment.
- Under-resection of the cam lesion is the most common reason for revision arthroscopy or persistent symptoms.
- Aggressive osteochondroplasty can weaken the femoral neck enough to cause a femoral neck fracture (rare, catastrophic).
- Transient traction-related neurapraxia (pudendal, sciatic, femoral) usually resolves; heterotopic ossification and capsular adhesions/scarring occur in a minority.
- Progression to osteoarthritis/THA conversion occurs in a meaningful minority over 10+ years even after successful arthroscopy, particularly with pre-existing joint-space narrowing or advanced chondral damage at index surgery — conversion rates up to approximately 24% at 11–12 years have been reported in cohorts with baseline OA.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing / ROM | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Protection | Weeks 0–2 | 20 lb flat-foot/partial weight-bearing with crutches (labral repair generally more restrictive than isolated debridement); hip flexion limited to <90°, avoid extremes of external rotation to protect capsular repair | Circumduction/passive ROM, quadriceps/gluteal activation, cryotherapy, CPM or stationary bike (no resistance) in many protocols |
| II — Early strengthening | Weeks 2–6 | Progress to full weight-bearing off crutches by ~2–4 weeks (per surgeon protocol and intraoperative findings — microfracture or extensive osteoplasty may extend protection) | Progressive ROM to full, closed-chain strengthening (bridges, mini-squats), gait normalization, aquatic therapy |
| III — Strength/neuromuscular | Weeks 6–12 | Full weight-bearing, full ROM | Hip/core/pelvic strengthening progression, single-leg stability work, stationary bike/elliptical, begin light jogging around 8–12 weeks if strength and gait criteria met |
| IV — Return to sport | 3–6 months | — | Sport-specific drills, plyometrics, cutting/pivoting progression; return-to-sport criteria: symmetric strength (≥90% of contralateral limb), full pain-free ROM, successful sport-specific functional testing, typically 4–6 months for pivoting/contact sport, earlier (~3–4 months) for running-based sport |

Return-to-work timing: sedentary work 1–2 weeks, physically demanding/manual labor 8–12 weeks depending on procedure extent (isolated labral repair faster than combined extensive osteochondroplasty).
