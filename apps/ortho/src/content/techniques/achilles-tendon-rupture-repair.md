## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Acute Achilles tendon rupture in patients who elect (or whose functional demands favor) operative repair over functional non-operative rehabilitation — typically athletes/high-demand patients seeking the lowest re-rupture risk and faster return to work, after shared decision-making that weighs surgical complication risk against the ~1.6 percentage-point re-rupture reduction (op ~2.3% vs non-op ~3.9%) and ~19-day-faster return to work.
- Gap >5 mm on dynamic ultrasound in resting equinus/plantarflexion (relative indication — poor tendon-end apposition non-operatively).
- Open injury, or rupture with concomitant injury requiring surgical exposure (e.g., associated fracture).
- Chronic/neglected rupture (>4-6 weeks), re-rupture after prior treatment, or a large reconstructible gap — these require open repair with augmentation (V-Y advancement, FHL transfer, turndown flap, or allograft).

**Contraindications**
- Active local skin compromise/infection over the planned incision — delay until resolved or stage treatment.
- Severe peripheral vascular disease threatening posterior heel wound healing without vascular optimization first.
- Poor surgical candidacy from a medical standpoint (significant comorbidity burden) where non-operative functional rehabilitation is likely to achieve comparable outcomes with lower risk — this remains a valid choice for many patients, not a fallback of last resort.
- Uncontrolled diabetes or active tobacco use without a modification plan — counsel that these substantially raise wound complication risk, particularly with open technique.
- Relative: very low-demand, sedentary patients for whom the marginal re-rupture-risk reduction from surgery does not outweigh surgical risk — non-operative functional rehabilitation is an equally reasonable choice here.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging:** dynamic ultrasound in resting equinus/plantarflexion to confirm tendon-end apposition and measure the gap — the key data point for surgical decision-making and for choosing percutaneous/mini-open versus open technique (a well-apposed gap favors minimally invasive repair; a large gap suggests the need for open technique or augmentation). MRI is reserved for equivocal cases, suspected chronic/partial rupture, or pre-operative planning of a chronic reconstruction.
- **Implant/equipment selection:** for acute repair, decide in advance between a jig-assisted percutaneous/mini-open system (e.g., Achillon, PARS) and open Krackow/Bunnell core-suture technique based on gap characteristics, surgeon experience, and patient risk factors for wound complications (favor minimally invasive in smokers/diabetics when technically feasible). For chronic/neglected cases or large gaps (>5-6 cm), plan and consent for FHL transfer (harvest set, interference screw or bone tunnel/button) or turndown flap technique in advance — this is not a decision to make for the first time intraoperatively.
- **Suture selection:** heavy, high-tensile non-absorbable or long-acting absorbable core suture (e.g., #2 or heavier) for the Krackow/Bunnell core stitch, plus a finer epitendinous circumferential suture to smooth the repair and add a small amount of additional strength/apposition.
- **Timing:** ideally within 1-2 weeks of injury for optimal tendon-end quality and ease of primary apposition; document exam findings (Thompson test, gap, Matles test) clearly since up to 20-25% of ruptures are missed at first presentation and late-presenting patients need to be triaged toward chronic-reconstruction planning.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia:** popliteal sciatic nerve block (± saphenous block) with sedation is well suited to this procedure and provides substantial post-operative analgesia; general anesthesia is used when regional block is contraindicated or per patient/anesthesia preference.
- **Positioning:** prone with the foot at the end of the table (or on a bump) is standard for posterior Achilles exposure, giving free access for jig placement (Achillon/PARS) or open Krackow/Bunnell suturing and allowing the ankle to be dynamically plantarflexed to assess tendon apposition and set final tensioning.
- **Tourniquet:** thigh tourniquet is typically used, though some surgeons perform percutaneous repair without one given the short operative time and the value of assessing tendon perfusion/plantarflexion cascade; discuss preference before draping.
- **Landmarks to mark:** palpable tendon defect/gap, the medial and lateral tendon borders, and the expected course of the sural nerve — for a posterolateral working portal, the nerve crosses the lateral border of the tendon roughly 2.7-4.5 cm proximal to the fibular tip in most anatomic studies, with individual variation; mark a wide margin and plan to look for it directly rather than relying on landmarks alone.
- **Imaging:** fluoroscopy is not routinely needed for acute repair; consider it for chronic reconstruction with bone tunnel/anchor placement (FHL transfer, turndown) to confirm tunnel trajectory and depth in the calcaneus.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. Prep and drape the leg free from above the knee to the toes with the patient prone, allowing intraoperative assessment of the resting cascade (compare to the contralateral, uninjured leg draped free if a side-to-side tensioning comparison is desired).
2. Palpate and mark the tendon gap; confirm the diagnosis with a Thompson squeeze test under anesthesia before incision.
3. **Percutaneous/mini-open repair (Achillon/PARS or similar jig-assisted system):** make a small transverse or longitudinal incision centered over or just proximal to the rupture site; carefully develop the plane between the tendon and paratenon before introducing the jig, keeping dissection immediately adjacent to the tendon to avoid straying laterally toward the sural nerve. Advance the jig arms proximally within the paratenon sheath (medial and lateral to the tendon), pass sutures through the jig's guide holes at multiple levels, then withdraw the jig, leaving a criss-crossing/box-stitch suture construct within the proximal and distal stumps.
4. Tension the sutures with the ankle held in appropriate resting equinus (typically matched to the uninjured side's resting cascade or ~20-30° plantarflexion) and tie/secure them, approximating the tendon ends; confirm apposition by direct palpation through the small incision and by passive plantar/dorsiflexion testing (a competent repair should transmit motion smoothly without a palpable gap).
5. **Open repair (Krackow or Bunnell core suture):** make a longitudinal posteromedial incision (medial reduces sural nerve risk relative to posterolateral) directly over the defect, incise the paratenon longitudinally and preserve it for later repair, then debride the ends of the ruptured tendon minimally — just enough to freshen frayed, non-viable fibers without shortening the tendon meaningfully.
6. Place a Krackow (locking running) or Bunnell (crisscross) core suture in each tendon stump using heavy non-absorbable or long-acting absorbable suture, then tension and tie the core sutures together with the ankle in the target resting equinus position, re-approximating the tendon ends under direct vision.
7. Reinforce the core-suture repair with a fine circumferential epitendinous suture to smooth the repair surface and add supplemental strength; this also helps close small residual gaps between the core-suture bites.
8. **Chronic/neglected rupture or large gap (>4-6 weeks, or gap not amenable to primary apposition):** proceed to reconstruction rather than forcing primary repair under excessive tension.
   - For moderate gaps: V-Y gastrocnemius-soleus advancement lengthens the proximal muscle-tendon unit to allow tension-free apposition.
   - For gaps >5-6 cm or in athletes/high-demand patients: FHL tendon transfer (harvested through a posteromedial window, whipstitched, and fixed into a calcaneal bone tunnel or with an interference screw) both bridges the gap and adds active plantarflexion power.
   - For extensive scarring/tissue loss: turndown flap (Bosworth/Silfverskiöld-type, using a strip of proximal gastrocnemius aponeurosis folded distally to bridge the defect) or allograft reconstruction for massive defects.
9. Repair the paratenon as a separate layer over the completed tendon repair/reconstruction — this restores the gliding envelope and helps protect the underlying repair.
10. Close skin in layers; apply a well-padded posterior splint or removable boot with the ankle held in the resting equinus position established at the time of tensioning, to be gradually brought toward neutral over the following weeks per protocol.
11. Before leaving the operating room, recheck the resting cascade with the leg relaxed — the operated foot should sit in a slightly more plantarflexed position than neutral, mirroring (not exceeding) the contralateral side, confirming neither over- nor under-tensioning of the repair.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Confirm the diagnosis intraoperatively with a Thompson test under anesthesia before making the incision — this is your last checkpoint before committing to a surgical plan.
- Set final tendon tension by directly comparing the operative ankle's resting cascade in a relaxed prone position to the contralateral, uninjured ankle rather than relying on a fixed numeric angle alone — patients' native resting equinus varies.
- When using a jig-assisted percutaneous system, advance the jig arms within the paratenon sheath hugging the tendon surface, and take a moment to visualize/palpate for the sural nerve before deploying transverse sutures, particularly at the more proximal capture levels where the nerve lies closer to the tendon.
- For open repair, use a medial rather than posterolateral longitudinal incision when the case allows — it keeps the incision away from the sural nerve's course and from shoe-counter pressure during recovery.
- Minimize debridement of the ruptured tendon ends — over-trimming "ragged" fibers can inadvertently shorten the tendon and produce an over-lengthened repair (weak push-off) even when the suture technique itself is technically sound.
- A Krackow stitch generally provides more secure purchase in degenerated or friable tendon tissue than a Bunnell stitch — favor it in delayed presentations or lower-quality tissue.
- When harvesting FHL for augmentation of a large-gap or chronic repair, harvest as distally as the incision allows to maximize tendon length and functional excursion.
- Always repair the paratenon as its own distinct layer at closure — this is not a cosmetic step; paratenon integrity meaningfully affects tendon vascularity, gliding, and adhesion formation during healing.
- Minimally invasive repair is supported as the preferred surgical technique when operative treatment is chosen, given its markedly lower wound infection rate versus open repair, per 2025 pooled RCT meta-analysis — default to it when gap characteristics and surgeon comfort allow.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Sural nerve injury is a real risk with purely percutaneous technique — mini-open/jig-assisted approaches under direct visualization reduce this; intraoperative ultrasound guidance has also been described to further visualize the nerve and tendon in real time during jig passage.
- Wound complication risk is substantially higher with open repair, particularly in smokers, diabetics, and open injuries — favor minimally invasive technique when feasible in these patients.
- Up to 20-25% of ruptures are missed on initial exam — confirm the diagnosis with active resisted testing (Thompson/Simmonds squeeze test, Matles test), not passive tone alone, before committing to a surgical plan.
- Sudden repeat "pop" after repair at 6-12 weeks postoperatively should be treated as re-rupture until proven otherwise.
- For chronic/neglected cases, gap size (>5-6 cm) should drive the choice toward FHL transfer or allograft reconstruction rather than attempted primary repair or V-Y advancement alone.
- Over-tensioning (excessive equinus) shortens the tendon and can limit ultimate dorsiflexion; under-tensioning produces a lengthened, weak repair with diminished push-off power — set tension by direct comparison to the contralateral resting cascade, not by a fixed number alone.
- Excessive debridement of frayed tendon ends before repair is a subtle cause of construct over-lengthening — trim conservatively.
- Deep vein thrombosis is a recognized risk of both operative and non-operative management given the period of relative immobilization — maintain a low threshold for chemoprophylaxis per institutional protocol in higher-risk patients.

## Postoperative Protocol

Representative accelerated/functional pathway (applies to operative and non-operative functional rehabilitation alike):

- **Weeks 0-2:** boot immobilization in equinus (~20-30° plantarflexion), non-weight-bearing or touch-down WB per surgeon protocol; wound/soft-tissue management.
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive weight-bearing in boot as tolerated, wedges removed gradually (~1 wedge/1-2 wks) to bring foot toward neutral by ~6 weeks; begin gentle active ROM out of boot per protocol (~4 wks), avoid resisted dorsiflexion/stretching.
- **Weeks 6-8:** transition out of boot to normal footwear, begin gait retraining, gentle stationary bike, pool therapy; introduce closed-chain calf loading (double-leg heel raises) as strength allows.
- **Weeks 8-12:** progressive strengthening (double- then single-leg heel raises), proprioception, gentle jogging trial around 12 weeks if strength/symmetry criteria met (heel-raise endurance/height ≥~70-80% of contralateral).
- **3-6 months:** sport-specific and plyometric progression, calf circumference and heel-raise symmetry, hop testing.
- **Return to sport:** typically 6-9 months (elite/cutting sports may take up to 9-12 months); criteria-based on limb symmetry index (heel-raise height/repetitions ≥90%, hop tests ≥90%), not time alone. Return to sedentary work: 2-6 weeks; manual labor: 3-4 months depending on demands.

Complications: wound infection (open ~6% vs percutaneous/mini-open ~0.4%), sural nerve injury (higher with purely percutaneous technique), re-rupture (~2-3% operative, up to ~4% non-operative with modern functional rehab), deep vein thrombosis, adhesions/stiffness, sural neuroma.
