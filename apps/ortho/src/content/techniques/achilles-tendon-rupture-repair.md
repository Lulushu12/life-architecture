## Indications

Acute Achilles tendon rupture in patients who elect (or whose functional demands favor) operative repair over functional non-operative rehabilitation — typically athletes/high-demand patients seeking the lowest re-rupture risk and faster return to work, after shared decision-making that weighs surgical complication risk against the ~1.6 percentage-point re-rupture reduction (op ~2.3% vs non-op ~3.9%) and ~19-day-faster return to work. Open repair with augmentation, V-Y advancement, FHL transfer, turndown flap, or allograft reconstruction is reserved for chronic/neglected rupture (>4-6 weeks), re-rupture, or large tendon gaps.

## Positioning & setup

Prone positioning with the foot at the end of the table is standard for posterior Achilles exposure, allowing access for percutaneous jig-assisted systems (e.g., Achillon, PARS) or open Krackow/Bunnell core-suture repair. Dynamic ultrasound in resting equinus/plantarflexion is performed pre-operatively to confirm tendon-end apposition and gap size where relevant to decision-making.

## Steps

1. **Percutaneous/mini-open repair** (e.g., Achillon, PARS, or similar jig-assisted systems): significantly lower wound infection risk (~0.4%) than open repair (~6%), shorter operative time. Mini-open/jig-assisted approaches under direct visualization mitigate the small but real risk of sural nerve injury seen with purely percutaneous techniques.
2. **Open repair** (Krackow or Bunnell core suture ± epitendinous augmentation): preferred for delayed presentation, re-rupture, or when direct tendon-end apposition/augmentation is needed; carries higher wound complication risk, particularly in smokers, diabetics, and open injuries.
3. **Chronic/neglected rupture (>4-6 weeks) or large gap:** reconstructive options include V-Y advancement, FHL tendon transfer (adds active plantarflexion power, favored for gaps >5-6 cm or in athletes/high-demand patients), turndown flap (Bosworth/Silfverskiöld-type), or allograft reconstruction for massive defects.
4. Minimally invasive repair is supported as the preferred surgical technique when operative treatment is chosen, given its markedly lower wound infection rate versus open repair, per 2025 pooled RCT meta-analysis.

## Pitfalls

- Sural nerve injury is a real risk with purely percutaneous technique — mini-open/jig-assisted approaches under direct visualization reduce this.
- Wound complication risk is substantially higher with open repair, particularly in smokers, diabetics, and open injuries — favor minimally invasive technique when feasible in these patients.
- Up to 20-25% of ruptures are missed on initial exam — confirm the diagnosis with active resisted testing (Thompson/Simmonds squeeze test, Matles test), not passive tone alone, before committing to a surgical plan.
- Sudden repeat "pop" after repair at 6-12 weeks postoperatively should be treated as re-rupture until proven otherwise.
- For chronic/neglected cases, gap size (>5-6 cm) should drive the choice toward FHL transfer or allograft reconstruction rather than attempted primary repair or V-Y advancement alone.

## Post-op protocol

Representative accelerated/functional pathway (applies to operative and non-operative functional rehabilitation alike):

- **Weeks 0-2:** boot immobilization in equinus (~20-30° plantarflexion), non-weight-bearing or touch-down WB per surgeon protocol; wound/soft-tissue management.
- **Weeks 2-6:** progressive weight-bearing in boot as tolerated, wedges removed gradually (~1 wedge/1-2 wks) to bring foot toward neutral by ~6 weeks; begin gentle active ROM out of boot per protocol (~4 wks), avoid resisted dorsiflexion/stretching.
- **Weeks 6-8:** transition out of boot to normal footwear, begin gait retraining, gentle stationary bike, pool therapy; introduce closed-chain calf loading (double-leg heel raises) as strength allows.
- **Weeks 8-12:** progressive strengthening (double- then single-leg heel raises), proprioception, gentle jogging trial around 12 weeks if strength/symmetry criteria met (heel-raise endurance/height ≥~70-80% of contralateral).
- **3-6 months:** sport-specific and plyometric progression, calf circumference and heel-raise symmetry, hop testing.
- **Return to sport:** typically 6-9 months (elite/cutting sports may take up to 9-12 months); criteria-based on limb symmetry index (heel-raise height/repetitions ≥90%, hop tests ≥90%), not time alone. Return to sedentary work: 2-6 weeks; manual labor: 3-4 months depending on demands.
- Complications: wound infection (open ~6% vs percutaneous/mini-open ~0.4%), sural nerve injury (higher with purely percutaneous technique), re-rupture (~2-3% operative, up to ~4% non-operative with modern functional rehab), deep vein thrombosis, adhesions/stiffness, sural neuroma.
