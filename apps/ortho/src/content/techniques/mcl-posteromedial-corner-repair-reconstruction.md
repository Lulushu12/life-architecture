## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Grade III instability persisting after a structured 6-week brace trial (isolated cases); combined ACL/MCL injury with gross valgus instability or Stener-like proximal MCL displacement (the torn end trapped superficial to the pes anserinus/sartorial fascia, precluding apposition and healing); chronic medial instability with functional symptoms; and bony avulsion of the MCL origin/insertion with significant displacement.

**Contraindications**:
- Grade I–II isolated MCL injury — these heal reliably with bracing given the ligament's excellent intrinsic vascularity and extra-articular location; surgery is not indicated.
- Grade III isolated injury that has not yet completed a structured 6-week non-operative trial — the MCL's strong healing potential means most isolated Grade III tears should be given the full trial before committing to surgery.
- Active infection or compromised skin envelope over the medial knee.
- Uncorrected significant genu valgum malalignment contributing to chronic medial-sided overload — consider staged or combined osteotomy in chronic/recurrent cases, since isolated ligament reconstruction into a valgus-malaligned limb is prone to recurrent stretch/failure.
- Poor tissue quality precluding durable repair in a delayed/chronic presentation — favor reconstruction over repair in this setting rather than forcing a repair.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Imaging**: MRI to characterize tear location (femoral origin, mid-substance, tibial insertion), tissue quality, and to identify Stener-like displacement of the proximal MCL superficial to the pes anserinus tendons; assess concurrent ACL/PCL/meniscal injury, since combined injury is common and changes the entire surgical plan and sequencing. Plain radiographs (including a valgus stress view if available) to assess for bony avulsion and to quantify medial joint-space gapping.
- **Graft/technique selection**: acute (within ~2–3 weeks), good-quality avulsion-type tears are generally amenable to primary repair with suture anchors ± internal brace/suture-tape augmentation; chronic, attenuated, or mid-substance tears with poor repair tissue are better served by reconstruction using autograft (semitendinosus/gracilis) or allograft.
- **Equipment/instrumentation**: suture anchor set for femoral/tibial fixation points, heavy non-absorbable suture, an internal-brace/suture-tape augmentation kit if used, and — for reconstruction — a graft harvest set, guide pins, cannulated reamers, and interference screws or button fixation sized to the reconstruction technique chosen (e.g., a docking or double-bundle technique reproducing the superficial MCL femoral origin and tibial insertion ~6 cm distal to the joint line).
- **Case-specific planning**: if combined with ACL reconstruction, plan tunnel trajectories to avoid convergence between MCL femoral fixation points and the ACL femoral tunnel, and plan the sequence of procedures (commonly the cruciate reconstruction tunnels are drilled before the MCL is definitively fixed, allowing the MCL repair/reconstruction to be tensioned last with the knee reduced).

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general or spinal/regional anesthesia; a femoral or adductor canal nerve block is commonly added for postoperative analgesia given the medial-sided dissection.
- **Positioning**: supine on a standard table with a leg-holder or lateral post allowing the knee to be flexed and a valgus-opening stress to be applied intraoperatively; the contralateral leg is padded in a well-leg holder if concurrent arthroscopy is planned.
- **Tourniquet**: high-thigh pneumatic tourniquet, padded, limb exsanguinated; inflate as needed for the open medial dissection, deflate for final hemostasis check before closure.
- **Fluoroscopy**: not routinely required unless bony avulsion fixation is planned, in which case a mini C-arm is set up on the medial side of the table.
- **Exam under anesthesia**: valgus stress at 0° (tests secondary restraints — PCL, posteromedial capsule, ACL — in addition to the MCL) and at 30° (isolates the superficial MCL), with side-to-side comparison to the contralateral knee, performed before prepping.
- **Draping**: circumferential prep from mid-thigh to toes with the leg free to allow intraoperative valgus stress testing through the case.
- **Landmarks marked before incision**: medial epicondyle, adductor tubercle, pes anserinus insertion (~6 cm distal to the joint line), joint line itself, and the planned longitudinal medial incision extending from just proximal to the epicondyle to just distal to the pes anserinus.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Diagnostic arthroscopy first** if a concomitant intra-articular injury (ACL/meniscus) is present, to confirm and address those findings before opening the medial side — arthroscopic fluid extravasation into the medial soft tissues is minimized by doing the open MCL work after the arthroscopic portion, or by carefully controlling pump pressure if sequencing is reversed.
2. **Make a longitudinal medial incision** centered over the palpable defect/injury, extending from proximal to the medial epicondyle to distal to the pes anserinus, staying just posterior to the medial border of the patella and patellar tendon.
3. **Identify and protect the saphenous nerve** (infrapatellar branch), which crosses obliquely in the subcutaneous tissue between the sartorius and gracilis tendons — develop the interval carefully and retract the branch rather than dividing it; an injured infrapatellar branch produces a painful neuroma or numbness over the anteromedial proximal tibia.
4. **Expose the superficial MCL** by incising the sartorial fascia longitudinally, identifying the plane between the superficial MCL and the pes anserinus tendons (semitendinosus, gracilis) which lie deep to it.
5. **Characterize the tear pattern**: femoral origin avulsion, mid-substance rupture, or tibial insertion avulsion; note any Stener-like entrapment of the proximal stump superficial to the pes tendons, which prevents spontaneous healing and mandates surgical reduction.
6. **For acute repair**: mobilize the torn ends, reduce any Stener-like displacement, and repair with suture anchors placed at the anatomic femoral origin (proximal/posterior to the medial epicondyle) and/or tibial insertion (~6 cm distal to the joint line, beneath the pes anserinus), using heavy non-absorbable suture in a locking/whipstitch configuration. Augment the repair with a suture-tape internal brace spanning femoral to tibial fixation points when tissue quality is borderline, to protect the repair during early rehabilitation.
7. **For reconstruction** (chronic/attenuated tissue): harvest autograft (commonly semitendinosus, leaving the native insertion or as a free graft) or prepare an allograft; drill femoral and tibial tunnels or use suture-anchor "docking" points at the native superficial MCL origin and insertion; pass the graft and provisionally fix the femoral side first.
8. **Address the deep MCL/posteromedial capsule and posterior oblique ligament** if injured — these structures contribute to combined valgus and rotational stability and are frequently repaired or imbricated concurrently through the same exposure, particularly in posteromedial corner injuries with anteromedial rotatory instability.
9. **Reduce the knee and tension the repair/graft.** With the knee at approximately 20–30° of flexion (avoiding full extension, which risks overconstraining the joint) and a slight varus-corrective/neutral force applied to close the medial gap, fix the tibial side (for reconstruction) or set final anchor tension (for repair).
10. **Provisional fixation check**: cycle the knee through a range of motion and re-check valgus stability at 0° and 30° before final fixation is locked, confirming symmetric endpoint compared to the contralateral limb and no overconstraint limiting terminal extension.
11. **If combined with cruciate reconstruction**, sequence final MCL/PMC fixation after cruciate graft tunnels are drilled and grafts passed, but generally tension and fix the MCL last with the knee reduced, so the cruciate graft tensioning is not fighting a fully constrained medial side.
12. **Closure.** Repair the sartorial fascia over the reconstruction/repair, close subcutaneous layers and skin, and apply a hinged knee brace set per protocol (commonly locked or range-limited in early extension-favoring positions per surgeon preference).

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Always perform bilateral valgus stress exam under anesthesia before incision — this confirms the diagnosis, grades severity accurately without patient guarding, and screens for missed combined cruciate injury.
- Identify and protect the saphenous nerve's infrapatellar branch early in the dissection — it is easy to injure with retractor pressure alone, not just direct transection.
- Specifically look for Stener-like proximal MCL entrapment superficial to the pes anserinus tendons — this pattern will not heal with bracing regardless of grade, and recognizing it preoperatively (on MRI) or intraoperatively changes the plan from "consider bracing" to "operative reduction indicated."
- Augment marginal-quality primary repairs with suture-tape internal bracing rather than relying on suture alone — this protects the repair during the early-motion phase of rehab without requiring prolonged immobilization.
- Tension the medial-sided repair/graft at 20–30° of flexion, not full extension — fixing in extension risks overconstraining the knee and blocking terminal extension postoperatively.
- In combined ACL/MCL cases, fix the MCL side last (after cruciate graft passage) so the medial repair is tensioned against a knee already reduced by the cruciate reconstruction, rather than fighting it.
- Address the deep MCL and posterior oblique ligament, not just the superficial MCL, in posteromedial corner injuries with a rotatory component — treating the superficial MCL alone in this pattern leaves residual anteromedial rotatory instability.
- Avoid excessive dissection/stripping of the medial soft-tissue envelope beyond what is needed for exposure — the MCL's healing advantage comes partly from its vascularity, and unnecessary devascularization works against that advantage even in the reconstructed setting.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Over-immobilization risks stiffness/arthrofibrosis, since the MCL's strong intrinsic healing means prolonged immobilization is unnecessary and counterproductive.
- Under-treated Grade III injury (i.e., missing persistent valgus instability at reassessment) risks chronic valgus laxity.
- Saphenous nerve injury (infrapatellar branch) is a recognized risk with surgical exposure of the medial knee.
- Missing a Stener-like proximal MCL displacement preoperatively leads to an inappropriate non-operative trial in a lesion that cannot heal without reduction.
- Overtensioning the repair/graft in full extension can block terminal extension and predispose to stiffness requiring later manipulation or lysis of adhesions.
- Failing to address concomitant deep MCL/posteromedial corner injury leaves residual anteromedial rotatory instability despite an apparently adequate superficial MCL repair.

## Postoperative Protocol

- Brace-protected ROM 0–90° for the first 2–4 weeks.
- Progressive weight-bearing (protected in hinged brace initially, advancing to full weight-bearing by around 6 weeks).
- Strengthening beginning at 6 weeks.
- Return to sport typically 4–6 months, longer if combined with cruciate reconstruction.
