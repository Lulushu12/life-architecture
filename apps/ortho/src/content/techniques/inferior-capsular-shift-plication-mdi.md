## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**
- Reserved for multidirectional instability (MDI) that has failed a genuine, adequately trialed structured rehabilitation program (per Stanmore/consensus philosophy) — surgery-first approaches to MDI have historically underperformed.
- MDI is atraumatic or minimally traumatic, symptomatic instability in ≥2 directions driven by capsular redundancy/generalized ligamentous laxity rather than a discrete labral tear, often bilateral and associated with connective tissue hyperlaxity.
- A structural (Stanmore Type I or II) component confirmed on exam/imaging (rather than pure Type III muscle-patterning instability) that persists despite documented rehab compliance is the strongest indication for proceeding to surgery.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**
- **Inadequate or unconfirmed rehabilitation trial** — the most important contraindication; do not operate on an MDI patient who has not completed a genuine, compliant structured program (typically a minimum of 3–6 months, sometimes extended to 6–12 months).
- **Voluntary or psychogenic instability**, or unaddressed Stanmore Type III muscle-patterning instability without a structural lesion — surgical outcomes in these patients are poor, and the muscle-patterning problem should be treated (or excluded) with physiotherapy first.
- Unaddressed psychological/behavioral factors contributing to the instability pattern — screen for these before committing to surgery.
- Active connective tissue disease flare or uncontrolled systemic hyperlaxity syndrome where tissue quality may compromise durable plication — counsel accordingly and consider whether reinforcement (e.g., rotator interval closure augmentation) or an open shift is more appropriate than isolated arthroscopic plication.

## Preoperative Planning

- Confirm and **document** the rehabilitation trial (duration, compliance, specific exercises/phases completed) before scheduling surgery — this should be explicit in the chart, not assumed.
- Exam findings to reconfirm and document preoperatively: sulcus sign (graded 1–3) **including whether it reduces with external rotation** (a sulcus that persists with external rotation localizes laxity to the rotator interval and mandates interval closure as part of the plan), load-and-shift in multiple directions, and Beighton hyperlaxity score.
- MR arthrogram to exclude a missed structural lesion (labral tear, HAGL) that would change the operative plan, and to assess capsular volume/redundancy.
- **Implant planning**: multiple suture anchors (typically 4–8, small-diameter 2.4–3.0 mm biocomposite or all-suture) for circumferential plication if proceeding arthroscopically; have both anchor-based and anchorless (pure capsular plication suture) technique options available, as surgeons vary in preferred method for the non-anchor segments of the plication.
- Decide preoperatively between **arthroscopic multi-anchor plication** (favored in modern practice for comparable outcomes with lower morbidity in experienced hands) and **open inferior capsular shift** (reserved for revision surgery or severe global laxity) — base this on laxity severity, revision status, and surgeon experience.
- Plan the plication pattern (typically anteroinferior, inferior, and posteroinferior pleats) based on the dominant direction(s) of instability on exam, not a uniform circumferential tightening applied without regard to the clinical pattern.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: general anesthesia, typically with an interscalene regional block for postoperative analgesia.
- **Positioning**: **lateral decubitus with balanced traction** is generally preferred for arthroscopic circumferential plication, as it provides better and more consistent access to the entire capsular circumference (anterior, inferior, and posterior) than beach chair, which is important given the need to work circumferentially rather than in a single quadrant. Beach chair is used for open inferior capsular shift or by surgeons who prefer it for arthroscopic work.
- Pad all bony prominences (peroneal nerve at the fibular head, axillary roll) meticulously given the typically longer operative time of circumferential plication.
- Prep and drape to allow full circumferential arthroscopic access; if open shift is planned, prepare and drape for a standard deltopectoral approach.
- Mark standard posterior, anterosuperolateral, and anteroinferior portal sites, plus an accessory posterolateral (7 o'clock) portal if posterior/posteroinferior plication is anticipated.
- Perform a bilateral exam under anesthesia (sulcus sign with and without external rotation, load-and-shift in all directions) — document findings on both shoulders since MDI is frequently bilateral and the contralateral exam informs the degree of native laxity to expect.
- Fluoroscopy is not required.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm the indication on the table.** Before committing to surgery, reconfirm (via chart review and repeat conversation if needed) that a genuine, adequately trialed structured rehabilitation program has failed — this is a final checkpoint, not merely a preoperative box to check.
2. **Diagnostic arthroscopy.** Establish portals and perform a full circumferential survey. Assess capsular volume and redundancy directly (a patulous, redundant capsule with a large accessible joint volume is characteristic of MDI, versus the focal labral disruption seen in traumatic instability). Exclude a missed structural lesion (labral tear, HAGL) that would need separate/additional repair.
3. **Assess the rotator interval directly.** With the arm at the side, visualize the rotator interval (superior glenohumeral ligament/coracohumeral ligament complex) between the biceps and the superior subscapularis border; correlate with the preoperative sulcus-with-external-rotation finding.
4. **Choose and confirm technique** (arthroscopic plication vs open shift) based on direct visualization of capsular quality and volume, not solely on the preoperative plan — occasionally, capsular tissue quality found intraoperatively (very thin, attenuated tissue) argues for converting to an open approach for a more robust shift.
5. **Perform circumferential capsular plication.** Working through anterior and posterior portals as needed, place plication stitches (with or without anchors, per technique) in a systematic sequence — typically starting inferiorly (5–7 o'clock) and working anteriorly and posteriorly, taking generous bites of redundant capsule and imbricating it onto itself or onto the labrum to reduce capsular volume and re-tension the IGHL complex.
6. **Tension progressively and symmetrically.** Tie plication sutures sequentially, reassessing joint volume and glenohumeral translation with a probe after each stitch — avoid completing all sutures on one side before checking overall tension, since asymmetric tightening can create iatrogenic loss of motion in one direction while leaving another direction under-corrected.
7. **Close the rotator interval** if a sulcus sign that does not reduce with external rotation was confirmed preoperatively and/or the interval appears patulous on direct visualization — place interval closure sutures capturing the superior glenohumeral ligament/coracohumeral ligament tissue and the anterosuperior capsule/subscapularis border, then tie with the arm in slight external rotation to avoid overtightening.
8. **Reassess stability arthroscopically and by exam** after all plication and interval closure sutures are tied — confirm reduced capsular volume/redundancy visually and a reduction or resolution of the sulcus sign clinically before finishing.
9. **Open inferior capsular shift (if chosen instead).** Through a deltopectoral approach with subscapularis takedown (tenotomy or peel), create a T-shaped capsulotomy, then shift the inferior capsular flap superiorly (and the superior flap inferiorly, overlapping the two) to reduce capsular volume in a durable, tensioned repair; repair the subscapularis anatomically at closure.
10. **Final inspection and closure.** Confirm hemostasis, evacuate the joint (arthroscopic) or irrigate the wound (open), and close in layers/portals. Place the arm in a sling.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Treat the "confirm rehab failure" step as a genuine checkpoint on the day of surgery, not a formality — this single decision point disproportionately determines outcome quality in MDI surgery.
- Choose lateral decubitus with traction for arthroscopic plication when circumferential access is anticipated — it materially improves consistent access to inferior and posterior capsule compared to beach chair.
- Tension plication sutures progressively and re-check global translation after each one rather than tying all sutures before any reassessment — this avoids ending up with an asymmetrically tight repair.
- Always assess the rotator interval directly with the arm at the side and correlate with the preoperative sulcus-with-external-rotation exam — do not close the interval reflexively in every case, but do not skip it when the sign is present.
- Tie interval closure sutures with the arm in slight external rotation to calibrate tension and avoid an iatrogenic loss of external rotation.
- If capsular tissue quality is poor/attenuated on direct visualization, have a low threshold to convert to an open inferior capsular shift, which can achieve a more robust, load-sharing repair in thin or redundant tissue than isolated arthroscopic plication.
- Document bilateral exam findings preoperatively — MDI is frequently bilateral, and this informs both patient counseling (contralateral risk) and the surgeon's calibration of what "normal" laxity looks like for that specific patient.
- Address a missed structural lesion (labral tear, HAGL) found on diagnostic arthroscopy in the same setting rather than proceeding with plication alone and leaving a structural cause of instability untreated.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- Do not proceed to surgery without confirming a genuine structured rehabilitation trial — poor response to rehab should first prompt re-evaluation for a missed structural (Stanmore Type I/II) component or psychological/voluntary instability, not an automatic move to surgery.
- Isolated anteroinferior plication without addressing rotator interval laxity risks residual multidirectional symptoms.
- Overtightening during plication risks loss of external rotation/function — reported in 10–20% of cases when surgery is undertaken without an adequate preceding rehab trial.
- Asymmetric tensioning (tying all sutures on one side before reassessing globally) is a specific technical pitfall that can leave one direction under-corrected while another is overtightened.
- Proceeding with arthroscopic plication on poor-quality, attenuated capsular tissue without considering conversion to open shift risks an early-failing, non-durable repair.
- Failing to exclude voluntary/psychogenic instability or unaddressed Type III muscle-patterning before surgery predicts poor surgical outcomes regardless of technical execution.

## Postoperative Protocol

The monograph presents MDI's structured rehabilitation as the primary non-operative pathway (Phase 1, 0–6 weeks: pain control and isometric activation avoiding provocative positions; Phase 2, 6–12 weeks: progressive dynamic strengthening of scapular stabilizers and rotator cuff with closed-chain proprioceptive drills; Phase 3, 3–6 months: sport-specific/functional progression) rather than a dedicated post-surgical protocol for capsular shift/plication specifically. General return-to-sport criteria apply across procedures and are not purely time-based: full painless ROM within 5–10° of the contralateral side, ≥90% strength symmetry, negative apprehension/relocation testing, and successful sport-specific functional progression before clearance.
