## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Malignant primary spine tumors — chordoma (the most common primary malignant spine tumor in adults, notochordal remnant, sacrum/clivus predominant) and chondrosarcoma — are indicated for en bloc resection with wide margins per Enneking/WBB staging, since both are relatively resistant to conventional photon radiotherapy and margin adequacy is the dominant determinant of local control.

**Contraindications / relative contraindications**:
- Metastatic disease at presentation — relative; local en bloc resection of the primary may still be justified in select cases for local control and quality of life, but the goals of surgery shift and should be discussed explicitly at tumor board.
- Medical unfitness for a major, often combined anterior-posterior procedure with substantial blood loss and prolonged anesthesia time.
- Tumor extent that precludes any negative margin at an acceptable functional cost — an individualized, tumor-board-level decision, not a fixed rule.
- A prior biopsy performed outside the treating center with a tract that cannot be safely incorporated into the resection specimen — this may force re-planning of the resection corridor or, in the worst case, compromise achievable margins.
- Inability to reconstruct lumbopelvic stability adequately after the planned resection level, in a patient who cannot tolerate the resulting functional loss.

## Preoperative Planning

- **Staging**: multidisciplinary tumor board review; staging CT of chest/abdomen/pelvis; MRI for local soft tissue and neural extent; Enneking staging and Weinstein-Boriani-Biagini (WBB) zone/layer mapping to define exactly which zones and layers must be sacrificed to achieve a wide margin.
- **Biopsy**: plan and perform the diagnostic biopsy at (or in direct coordination with) the treating/resecting center, with the tract oriented so it can be excised en bloc at definitive surgery — never as an unplanned outside referral, which risks tumor seeding that compromises later margins.
- **Vascular planning**: preoperative embolization for hypervascular lesions (renal cell metastasis, aggressive hemangioma) to reduce intraoperative blood loss; vascular surgery consultation if great vessel involvement is suspected.
- **Multidisciplinary team**: colorectal surgery consultation for anticipated rectal mobilization in sacral cases; urology consultation for possible ureteral stenting; plastic surgery consultation for flap closure planning of the anticipated soft tissue defect.
- **Reconstruction planning**: template the resection level and the resulting lumbopelvic instability, and plan spinopelvic fixation hardware (iliac bolt/rod-screw constructs) accordingly.
- **Blood management**: activate a massive transfusion protocol plan with blood bank given the potential for major blood loss.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **General anesthesia**, arterial line, central line, large-bore IV access, urinary catheter; consider preoperative ureteral stent placement by urology to aid intraoperative identification and protection.
- Combined anteroposterior approaches are typically staged: an **anterior stage** (supine or lithotomy, for vessel and rectal mobilization) followed by a **posterior stage** (prone) — either in the same anesthetic or as a planned two-stage procedure, depending on tumor level and institutional practice. Combined approaches are generally preferred for total and high sacral resections; a posterior-only approach is more often used for middle/low and lateral resections.
- **Prone positioning on a radiolucent (Jackson) frame** for the posterior stage, with careful padding and the abdomen free, following standard prone-spine positioning principles.
- **Neuromonitoring** (EMG) of lumbosacral nerve roots when nerve preservation is planned, to help distinguish roots that can be spared from those planned for sacrifice.
- **Cell salvage/autotransfusion is generally avoided** in malignant tumor resections due to the theoretical risk of reinfusing tumor cells, unless a leukocyte-depletion filter is used.
- Mark planned incisions, including the prior biopsy tract, which must be encompassed within the resection specimen.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Confirm resectability and the margin plan** at multidisciplinary tumor board, using the WBB-mapped corridor.
2. **Anterior stage** (for proximal/total sacrectomy or combined-approach cases): laparotomy; mobilize the rectum and great vessels off the anterior sacrum; ligate the median sacral vessels and internal iliac vessels as needed; place markers or spacers to protect anterior structures during the subsequent posterior osteotomy. The anterior stage allows safer separation of the tumor from the mesorectal fascia than a posterior-only dissection, particularly for tumors extending proximally to S3.
3. **Ligate and divide the relevant nerve roots anteriorly** if planned, per the margin/function decision made and documented with the patient preoperatively.
4. **Proceed to the posterior stage** (same session or staged), positioning prone.
5. **Wide posterior exposure**, including the planned skin ellipse encompassing the biopsy tract.
6. **Place proximal spinopelvic instrumentation** (pedicle screws plus iliac/S2-alar-iliac screws, or an iliac bolt-rod construct) *before* the osteotomy, so the pelvis remains stable once the sacrum is mobilized.
7. **Perform the osteotomy** at the planned WBB level (for example, through the S1-S2 disc/body) using osteotomes and/or a saw, working under direct palpation of the previously mobilized anterior plane to avoid rectal or vascular injury.
8. **Complete circumferential release and remove the specimen en bloc**, ensuring the biopsy tract remains within the specimen.
9. **Send the specimen for margin assessment** — frozen section where feasible, with definitive margin status from permanent pathology.
10. **Reconstruct lumbopelvic stability**, connecting the proximal instrumentation to the iliac fixation in a triangulated spinopelvic construct (e.g., iliac screws with cross-connectors), since the posterior sacral tension band has been removed.
11. **Achieve hemostasis** — presacral venous plexus bleeding can be sudden and severe; have hemostatic agents, bone wax, and packing readily available.
12. **Coordinate soft tissue closure** — large defects often require a rotational flap (gluteal or vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous [VRAM]); plastic surgery closes in the same setting whenever possible.
13. **Obtain postoperative imaging** to confirm hardware position and correlate with the planned margin planes.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Never allow the initial diagnostic biopsy to be performed outside the treating/resecting center — an improperly placed tract cannot always be excised en bloc and can compromise an otherwise resectable tumor.
- Mobilize and protect the rectum and great vessels from an anterior stage before the posterior osteotomy — operating "blind" from a posterior-only approach on a high or total sacrectomy risks rectal or vascular injury.
- Place spinopelvic instrumentation *before* completing the sacral osteotomy so the pelvis does not become unstable mid-resection.
- Combined anteroposterior approaches are generally preferred for total and high sacrectomies; a posterior-only approach is usually sufficient — and less morbid — for middle, low, and lateral resections, so match the approach to the resection level rather than defaulting to the most extensive approach.
- Counsel patients explicitly and document the margin-vs-function trade-off for nerve root sacrifice before surgery: preserving at least one S3 nerve root bilaterally often maintains meaningful bowel/bladder continence, whereas bilateral S2–S5 sacrifice (high sacral/total sacrectomy) predictably causes complete loss of bowel, bladder, and sexual function.
- Involve plastic surgery early for flap closure planning (gluteal or VRAM flap) — primary closure of large sacral defects has a high dehiscence/infection rate.
- Have hemostatic adjuncts (bone wax, thrombin-soaked gelfoam, packing) readily staged for presacral venous plexus bleeding, which can be sudden and severe.
- Consider carbon fiber-reinforced (CFR-PEEK) instrumentation for reconstruction — comparable clinical efficacy to titanium with reduced imaging artifact, which meaningfully aids postoperative surveillance for local recurrence and radiotherapy planning.
- Generally avoid standard cell salvage/autotransfusion in malignant resections without a leukocyte-depletion filter, given the theoretical risk of reinfusing tumor cells.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Intralesional surgery carries a high local recurrence rate** and should be avoided when en bloc resection is feasible — historically a major problem for chordoma specifically.
- **Unplanned biopsy outside the treating center** risks tumor seeding along the needle tract, compromising the ability to achieve a wide margin at definitive surgery.
- **Functional deficits from planned nerve root sacrifice** in sacrectomy are an expected trade-off, not a complication of technical error — must be counseled explicitly preoperatively.
- **Presacral venous plexus hemorrhage** — can be sudden and severe; anticipate and prepare hemostatic adjuncts before it occurs rather than reacting to it.
- **Rectal or great vessel injury** from posterior-only dissection attempted on a high/total sacrectomy without adequate anterior mobilization.
- **Wound complications** are common after large resections, particularly without early plastic surgery flap planning.
- **Late metastasis** is a recognized risk, notably for chordoma, warranting continued long-term oncologic surveillance beyond local control.
- **Lumbopelvic instability/hardware failure** if spinopelvic reconstruction is inadequate relative to the resection level.
- Carbon fiber-reinforced (CFR-PEEK) instrumentation has shown at least equivalent clinical efficacy to titanium while improving postoperative imaging quality (reduced artifact), aiding surveillance for local recurrence — a reasonable consideration for reconstruction after resection.

## Postoperative Protocol

- **Immediate postoperative (0–48 hours)**: ICU or high-dependency monitoring given the magnitude of blood loss and fluid shifts typical of combined anteroposterior resections; monitor flap perfusion closely if a rotational flap was used for closure.
- **0–2 weeks**: mobilization progressed as spinopelvic stability allows (often more cautious/delayed than routine posterior fusion given the loss of the sacral tension band); wound and flap surveillance; DVT prophylaxis given the prolonged immobility and malignancy-associated VTE risk; bowel/bladder rehabilitation program initiated promptly if nerve roots were sacrificed (self-catheterization training, bowel regimen).
- **2–6 weeks**: progressive physical therapy for transfers and ambulation; continued wound/flap monitoring; pain management transitioned toward oral multimodal regimens.
- **6 weeks–6 months**: staged increase in weight-bearing and activity per the specific reconstruction's mechanical tolerance; ongoing functional rehabilitation for any bowel/bladder/sexual dysfunction from nerve sacrifice, coordinated with urology/colorectal specialists.
- **Long-term**: oncologic surveillance imaging (MRI and/or CT) at scheduled intervals to detect local recurrence, continuing indefinitely given the risk of late recurrence and late metastasis, particularly for chordoma; adjuvant proton/carbon-ion radiotherapy coordinated separately for close/positive margins.
