## Indications

Symptomatic isolated medial compartment OA (KL2-3, Ahlbäck I–III preferred) in a relatively young (typically <60 years), active/high-demand patient with a correctable varus deformity, an intact ACL (or reconstructable concurrently/staged), flexion arc >120°, flexion contracture <10–15°, and preserved lateral and patellofemoral compartments. Also indicated to correct varus malalignment concurrently with meniscal transplant or cartilage restoration procedures, or as a joint-preserving strategy to delay arthroplasty in a young patient who understands eventual TKA is likely.

Favor HTO over UKA when the patient is younger (<55–60), has high physical/occupational demand, wants to return to impact sport, wants to preserve bone stock/avoid a prosthesis, and accepts the possibility of a second surgery (HTO-to-TKA conversion) later.

## Positioning & setup

The monograph does not detail room/positioning specifics beyond standard supine positioning with intra-operative fluoroscopic control used to confirm the planned correction against the Fujisawa weight-bearing line.

## Steps

1. **Confirm candidacy and plan correction** on full-length hip-to-ankle standing radiographs, measuring MPTA, LDFA, HKA, and JLCA; use stress views if a large JLCA suggests soft-tissue laxity contributing to the deformity (affects planned correction magnitude).
2. **Choose osteotomy technique:**
   - **Medial opening-wedge HTO** (current dominant technique): single osteotomy cut proximal to the tibial tubercle; wedge opened to the pre-planned correction; fixed with an angle-stable locking plate (e.g., TomoFix-type construct); gap filled with allograft/synthetic bone substitute or left to heal via guided bone regeneration for smaller corrections. Advantages: single-cut simplicity, easier concurrent ligament/meniscal procedures, avoids fibular osteotomy and peroneal nerve risk. Disadvantage: tends to increase posterior tibial slope if the opening is not carefully biplanar-controlled, and slightly lengthens the limb.
   - **Lateral closing-wedge HTO** (classic technique): more biomechanically stable construct with faster bone healing at the closing surface, but requires a fibular osteotomy/proximal tibiofibular joint disruption (peroneal nerve risk) and shortens the limb.
3. **Correct to the Fujisawa point**: target the weight-bearing line at **62–66% of tibial plateau width from the medial edge**, corresponding to a mechanical axis of roughly **3–6° valgus overcorrection** — deliberate mild overcorrection unloads the medial compartment durably.
4. **Control posterior slope intentionally** — each millimeter of anterior/posterior wedge asymmetry changes slope; avoid inadvertently altering slope beyond plan, since this affects subsequent ACL reconstruction or later TKA conversion biomechanics.
5. **Fix and, if opening-wedge, graft the gap** as needed based on correction magnitude (larger corrections >10° or gaps >10 mm more often warrant bone graft substitute).

## Pitfalls

- **Lateral hinge fracture** is the most common intra-operative complication of opening-wedge HTO — avoid with a controlled, slow osteotomy and hinge protection; consider a hinge screw.
- **Overcorrection >5° valgus** accelerates lateral compartment degeneration; **undercorrection** leaves recurrent medial symptoms — hit the Fujisawa point deliberately.
- **Peroneal nerve injury** risk is specific to the lateral closing-wedge technique (fibular osteotomy/proximal tibiofibular joint disruption).
- **Delayed union/nonunion** is more likely with larger corrections (>10°) or gaps >10 mm without grafting; reported union rates are >90–95% with modern locking plates.
- Uncontrolled posterior slope change can complicate a future ACL reconstruction or TKA conversion.
- Rare but recognized: compartment syndrome.

## Post-op protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protection | 0–2 weeks | Toe-touch/partial (per plate stability and gap-fill graft use) | Gentle passive ROM 0–90°, avoid forced extension against the osteotomy | Pain/swelling control, quad activation (straight-leg raise once achievable), DVT prophylaxis |
| Progressive loading | 2–6 weeks | Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated by 6 weeks if radiographs show healing progression | Advance to full flexion as tolerated | Closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining off crutches |
| Union consolidation | 6–12 weeks | Full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks once radiographic union criteria met | Full ROM goal | Progressive resistance training, proprioception/balance work |
| Return to activity | 3–6 months | — | — | Return to low-impact sport ~3–4 months; return to impact/pivoting sport typically 6–9 months, contingent on confirmed radiographic union and functional testing |
| Return to work | Variable | — | — | Sedentary work 2–4 weeks; physical/manual labor 3–4 months |
