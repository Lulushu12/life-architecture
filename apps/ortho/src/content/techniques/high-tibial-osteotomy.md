## Indications & Contraindications

**Indications**: Symptomatic isolated medial compartment OA (KL2-3, Ahlbäck I–III preferred) in a relatively young (typically <60 years), active/high-demand patient with a correctable varus deformity, an intact ACL (or reconstructable concurrently/staged), flexion arc >120°, flexion contracture <10–15°, and preserved lateral and patellofemoral compartments. Also indicated to correct varus malalignment concurrently with meniscal transplant or cartilage restoration procedures, or as a joint-preserving strategy to delay arthroplasty in a young patient who understands eventual TKA is likely.

Favor HTO over UKA when the patient is younger (<55–60), has high physical/occupational demand, wants to return to impact sport, wants to preserve bone stock/avoid a prosthesis, and accepts the possibility of a second surgery (HTO-to-TKA conversion) later.

**Contraindications**:
- Inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic, crystal disease).
- Lateral or patellofemoral compartment full-thickness cartilage loss on MRI/arthroscopy.
- Fixed (non-passively-correctable) varus deformity, flexion contracture >15–20°, or flexion arc <90–120°.
- Symptomatic lateral compartment disease, or Ahlbäck IV–V bone attrition/subluxation (favors TKA instead).
- Active or recent joint sepsis, uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c >8%), active local skin/soft-tissue infection.

**Relative contraindications**: BMI >30–32 (not absolute, but correlates with slower union and higher loss-of-correction risk), heavy smoking (impairs osteotomy healing more than it impairs arthroplasty wound healing — counsel on cessation), and a varus thrust on gait that does not correct with valgus stress (suggests a lateral ligamentous laxity component that plate fixation alone will not control).

## Preoperative Planning

- **Full-length hip-to-ankle standing radiograph** (weight-bearing, patella forward) is mandatory. Measure MPTA, LDFA, HKA angle, and JLCA to localize the deformity to the tibia (vs a combined or femoral-based deformity, which would instead need a distal femoral osteotomy).
- **Rosenberg (45° flexion weight-bearing PA) view** to confirm the lateral and patellofemoral compartments are truly spared — this is more sensitive for early joint-space narrowing than the standing extension AP and is a common reason plans change intra-operatively if skipped.
- **Skyline/lateral views** to screen the patellofemoral compartment and assess baseline posterior tibial slope, which the osteotomy must reproduce (or intentionally change, in a controlled way).
- **Stress radiographs (valgus stress in extension)** when JLCA is large relative to the bony deformity — a large ligamentous laxity component means less of the correction needs to come from bone, and overcorrecting bone on top of laxity risks lateral compartment overload.
- **Preoperative templating/calculation of correction magnitude**: use a validated method (e.g., Miniaci method or dedicated planning software) on the long-leg film to calculate the opening-wedge gap in millimeters needed to move the weight-bearing line to the Fujisawa point. As a rule of thumb, roughly 1° of correction is achieved per ~1 mm of medial opening at the osteotomy, but this varies with tibial width and osteotomy level — always confirm with a formal template, not the rule of thumb alone.
- **Implant selection**: angle-stable locking plate system (TomoFix-type) sized to the patient's tibial width and planned wedge height; have a range of wedge sizes (typically 5–15 mm in 2.5 mm increments) opened and available, since the intra-operative fluoroscopic correction check frequently differs from the paper plan by a few millimeters.
- **Bone graft/substitute**: have allograft wedge, synthetic bone substitute (e.g., tricalcium phosphate or bioactive glass wedges), or autograft available for gaps generally >10 mm; smaller gaps can be left to fill by guided bone regeneration if plate stability is adequate.
- **Concomitant procedure planning**: if performing simultaneous ACL reconstruction, meniscal allograft transplant, or cartilage restoration, sequence and graft-tunnel planning must account for the osteotomy plate footprint to avoid tunnel/hardware conflict.
- Have **intra-operative fluoroscopy** and a long alignment rod (or cautery cord) available to reconfirm the mechanical axis after provisional fixation.

## Anesthesia & Positioning

- **Anesthesia**: spinal or general anesthesia, often combined with an adductor canal block or femoral nerve block for post-operative analgesia; regional blocks that spare quadriceps strength (adductor canal over femoral) are preferred to allow earlier mobilization.
- **Positioning**: supine on a radiolucent table with a tourniquet high on the operative thigh. A lateral post or bump at the level of the distal thigh helps hold the leg in a reproducible position for valgus stress and fluoroscopy; a foot support/leg holder allows the assistant to control rotation during the osteotomy and wedge-opening steps.
- **Fluoroscopy**: C-arm brought in from the contralateral side (opposite the operative leg) positioned to obtain true AP and lateral views of the proximal tibia without moving the leg significantly between shots; confirm a true AP (patella facing straight up, both femoral condyles superimposed) before any correction measurement, since rotational malpositioning is the most common source of intra-operative alignment measurement error.
- **Alignment rod/cautery cord technique**: after exposure and before the final cut, mark the femoral head center (fluoroscopically or with a hip locator device) and the ankle center (mortise midpoint) on the drapes/skin so the mechanical axis can be re-checked intra-operatively with a cautery cord or long alignment rod stretched from hip center to ankle center over the knee.
- **Draping**: standard extremity drape with the tourniquet isolated, leaving the hip visible or marked externally for the alignment-rod technique, and the foot free to allow rotational control.
- **Surface landmarks marked pre-incision**: tibial tubercle, joint line, fibular head (landmark for the lateral hinge safe zone), and the planned medial incision (typically 4–6 cm, midway between the posteromedial border of the tibia and the tibial crest, starting ~2 cm distal to the joint line).
- Tourniquet inflated after exsanguination, typically released before final wound closure to allow hemostasis and confirm perfusion, though many surgeons close over a released tourniquet if the case has run long.

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and exposure**: make a longitudinal or slightly oblique incision over the anteromedial proximal tibia, centered roughly 2 cm distal to the joint line. Identify and protect the pes anserinus tendons (retract distally or partially release and repair), and elevate the superficial MCL fibers subperiodosteally off the posteromedial tibia to expose the osteotomy site while protecting the deep MCL.
2. **Define the osteotomy plane fluoroscopically**: place two guide pins from the anteromedial tibia toward the fibular head, angled obliquely upward (typically 10–15° from horizontal, aiming just below the lateral tibial plateau, proximal to the tibial tubercle) under fluoroscopic guidance in both AP and lateral planes. The pins define both the osteotomy trajectory and the safe distance from the joint line and posterior neurovascular structures.
3. **Protect the posterior neurovascular bundle**: place a blunt Hohmann or dedicated posterior retractor along the posterior tibial cortex, staying subperiosteal, before making the posterior cut — the popliteal vessels lie close to the posterior tibial cortex at this level and are the structure most at risk.
4. **Perform the osteotomy**: using an oscillating saw and/or osteotomes along the guide pins, cut the anterior and posterior cortices, leaving a lateral hinge of bone intact (typically 8–10 mm from the lateral cortex, corresponding to Nakamura's "zone WL" safe hinge position just distal to the proximal tibiofibular joint and below the lateral plateau). Complete the osteotomy of both the anterior and posterior cortices fully to the level of the hinge — an incompletely cut posterior cortex is a common cause of an uncontrolled, unpredictable hinge fracture during opening.
5. **Open the osteotomy gradually**: insert calibrated opening wedge chisels/spreaders sequentially, opening slowly in small increments (a few millimeters at a time) to allow the lateral cortex to plastically deform rather than fracture — the elasticity of the lateral hinge is time- and rate-dependent, so rushing this step is a direct cause of hinge fracture. Confirm the opening gap against the pre-operative template, generally opening a few millimeters more anteriorly than posteriorly if slope preservation (rather than slope increase) is the goal, since a purely parallel opening tends to increase posterior slope.
6. **Reconfirm correction fluoroscopically**: with the wedge held open (or provisional plate/spacer in place), lay the alignment rod/cautery cord from the femoral head center to the ankle center and confirm it passes through the Fujisawa point — the weight-bearing line at 62–66% of tibial plateau width from the medial edge, corresponding to roughly 3–6° of mechanical valgus overcorrection. Adjust wedge height as needed before committing to fixation.
7. **Apply the locking plate**: position the angle-stable plate (e.g., TomoFix-type) on the anteromedial tibia spanning the osteotomy, and fix with locking screws proximally and distally per the manufacturer's sequence. Insert screws under fluoroscopic control to avoid joint penetration proximally.
8. **Consider a hinge protection screw**: in wider corrections (generally >10°) or when the hinge appears thin/at-risk on fluoroscopy, place an independent lag or positioning screw across the hinge fragment before or in addition to plate fixation to neutralize shear/fracture propagation forces at the hinge.
9. **Graft the gap**: for gaps generally >10 mm, pack the opening with allograft wedge, synthetic bone substitute, or autograft to support the plate and promote union; smaller gaps may be left ungrafted if the plate construct is rigid and the patient's bone quality is good.
10. **Final fluoroscopic and clinical check**: reconfirm the mechanical axis with the alignment rod/cautery cord, check the lateral view for the final posterior slope compared to the pre-operative slope, and take the knee through a full range of motion to confirm no impingement, hardware prominence, or loss of correction with cyclic loading.
11. **Address concomitant procedures** (ACL reconstruction, meniscal transplant, cartilage restoration) at this stage per the pre-operative plan, sequencing tunnels/grafts to avoid the plate footprint.
12. **Closure**: repair the pes anserinus/superficial MCL sleeve back over the plate, close in layers, and consider a drain if bleeding is brisk. Release the tourniquet before or during closure to check hemostasis.

## Technical Tips & Pearls

- Take your time opening the wedge — open slowly in small, controlled increments rather than levering it open in one or two big movements; the lateral cortex needs time to deform plastically, and rushing this step is the single most correctable cause of lateral hinge fracture.
- Aim the osteotomy hinge for Nakamura's safe zone (roughly the region just below the lateral plateau and proximal to the fibular head, "zone WL"), and preserve at least 8–10 mm of intact lateral cortex from the osteotomy plane to the true lateral edge.
- Use a biplanar (ascending) osteotomy cut rather than a single flat cut when technique allows — the added bony surface area improves both fracture resistance and healing potential at the hinge.
- Complete both the anterior and posterior cortical cuts fully before attempting to open the wedge; an incompletely released posterior cortex concentrates stress unpredictably and is a frequent, avoidable cause of hinge propagation into the joint.
- Deliberately control anteroposterior wedge symmetry: open the anterior and posterior aspects of the wedge by different amounts (rather than a single uniform opening) to hold the pre-operative posterior slope constant, since a symmetric opening tends to increase slope.
- Keep a Hohmann or dedicated posterior retractor subperiosteal along the back of the tibia at all times during cutting and opening — this is your protection for the popliteal neurovascular bundle and should never be removed "just for this cut."
- Use the alignment rod/cautery cord technique intra-operatively, not just fluoroscopic angle measurement alone — a physical line from femoral head center to ankle center crossing the tibial plateau is a fast, reliable, low-radiation confirmation that you have hit the Fujisawa point.
- In corrections >10° or gaps >10 mm, plan up front for both bone graft and a supplemental hinge screw rather than deciding intra-operatively — this avoids being caught without graft on hand when the gap turns out larger than templated.
- Repair the pes anserinus/superficial MCL sleeve carefully over the plate at closure; this both reduces plate prominence symptoms and helps preserve valgus stability during early healing.

## Pitfalls & Complications

- **Lateral hinge fracture** is the most common intra-operative complication of opening-wedge HTO — avoid with a controlled, slow osteotomy and hinge protection; consider a hinge screw. Risk rises with medial opening gaps >11 mm; hinge position within Nakamura's zone WL, biplanar cuts, and controlled opening rate are the main modifiable factors described in the literature.
- **Overcorrection >5° valgus** accelerates lateral compartment degeneration; **undercorrection** leaves recurrent medial symptoms — hit the Fujisawa point deliberately, confirmed intra-operatively rather than by template alone.
- **Peroneal nerve injury** risk is specific to the lateral closing-wedge technique (fibular osteotomy/proximal tibiofibular joint disruption).
- **Delayed union/nonunion** is more likely with larger corrections (>10°) or gaps >10 mm without grafting; reported union rates are >90–95% with modern locking plates.
- **Uncontrolled posterior slope change** can complicate a future ACL reconstruction or TKA conversion — an unplanned slope increase from symmetric wedge opening is a common, avoidable technical error.
- **Popliteal neurovascular injury** — rare but catastrophic; mitigated by maintaining a subperiosteal posterior retractor and confirming pin/saw trajectory fluoroscopically before cutting.
- **Compartment syndrome** — rare but recognized; maintain a high index of suspicion for disproportionate post-operative pain.
- **Hardware prominence/irritation** requiring later plate removal is a recognized, generally minor, late complication.

## Postoperative Protocol

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Milestones/PT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protection | 0–2 weeks | Toe-touch/partial (per plate stability and gap-fill graft use) | Gentle passive ROM 0–90°, avoid forced extension against the osteotomy | Pain/swelling control, quad activation (straight-leg raise once achievable), DVT prophylaxis |
| Progressive loading | 2–6 weeks | Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated by 6 weeks if radiographs show healing progression | Advance to full flexion as tolerated | Closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining off crutches |
| Union consolidation | 6–12 weeks | Full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks once radiographic union criteria met | Full ROM goal | Progressive resistance training, proprioception/balance work |
| Return to activity | 3–6 months | — | — | Return to low-impact sport ~3–4 months; return to impact/pivoting sport typically 6–9 months, contingent on confirmed radiographic union and functional testing |
| Return to work | Variable | — | — | Sedentary work 2–4 weeks; physical/manual labor 3–4 months |
