## Quick Reference

**Clubfoot (Ponseti) treatment ladder:** weekly manipulation/casting (5–6 casts avg) → percutaneous Achilles tenotomy (~85–90% of idiopathic feet) → Foot Abduction Brace (FAB) 23h/day ×3mo then naps+nights to age 4–5y → relapse: repeat casting ± re-tenotomy; dynamic supination relapse → tibialis anterior tendon transfer (TATT, age >2.5–3y); rigid/bony relapse in older child → osteotomy/rarely triple arthrodesis.

**Pediatric osteomyelitis treatment ladder:** empiric IV antistaphylococcal agent (± MRSA coverage per local antibiogram) → image-guided or open drainage if abscess/failure to improve at 48–72h → IV→oral step-down once afebrile + clinically improving + CRP trending down (often day 3–5) → total 3–4 weeks uncomplicated, 4–6+ weeks if MRSA/abscess/delayed presentation/immunocompromised.

**Septic arthritis (esp. hip):** surgical emergency — urgent aspiration/arthrotomy or arthroscopic washout, do not wait on imaging if Kocher probability high.

| Kocher predictors present | Approx. probability of septic arthritis (hip) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | <0.2% | Observe, treat as transient synovitis |
| 1 | ~3% | Close follow-up; US if effusion suspected |
| 2 | ~40% | US-guided aspiration |
| 3 | ~93% | Urgent surgical drainage |
| 4 (or 5 with CRP) | ~99% | Emergent surgical drainage, do not delay |

**Osgood-Schlatter / apophysitides:** activity modification + ice + short NSAID course + eccentric quad/hamstring stretching → self-limited, resolves with apophyseal fusion; surgery (ossicle excision) only for symptomatic non-union in skeletally mature patient.

**Juvenile OCD knee:** stable lesion + open physis → non-operative (activity restriction 3–6 months, brace/cast in younger/symptomatic) — healing rates ~50–70%+; unstable/loose body/failed non-op → drilling (stable, intact cartilage) → fixation (salvageable unstable fragment) → debridement/OATS/particulated cartilage (unsalvageable/full-thickness defect).

**Limb length discrepancy — treatment by predicted maturity LLD:**

| Predicted LLD at maturity | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| <2 cm | Observation, shoe lift if symptomatic | — | Rarely indicated |
| 2–5–6 cm | Guided growth (temporary hemiepiphysiodesis, 8-plate) on long side | — | Permanent epiphysiodesis (Phemister/PETS) timed via Paley multiplier |
| >5–6 cm (up to 20+ cm) | — | — | Limb lengthening (external fixator or magnetic internal nail) ± contralateral epiphysiodesis, staged for very large discrepancies |

**Physiologic angular deformity red flags** (→ imaging/metabolic workup, refer): progression beyond age 2–3y, asymmetry, short stature, lateral thrust on gait, family history of skeletal dysplasia/rickets, focal single-bone deformity, MDA (metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle) >11° on AP tibia.

**Blount disease treatment ladder:** Langenskiöld I–II, age <3y → observe ± KAFO brace trial 6–12 months → progressive/Stage II–III <age 4 → lateral proximal tibial guided growth (8-plate) or valgus osteotomy → Stage IV–VI or failed guided growth → osteotomy ± elevation of depressed plateau ± bar resection (if focal, adequate growth remaining).

**Congenital muscular torticollis:** early PT stretching program (ideally start <3–6mo) → >90–95% success if started early/mild → surgical release (unipolar/bipolar/Z-plasty) if persistent restriction (>15–30°) after adequate trial by age 12–18mo–3y or late presentation with fixed contracture.

**Tarsal coalition:** activity modification/NSAIDs → short-leg cast/boot immobilization 4–6 weeks for flare → orthotic (UCBL) → coalition resection + interposition (calcaneonavicular, or talocalcaneal <50% joint surface, minimal degenerative change) → subtalar/triple arthrodesis (extensive coalition, degenerative change, failed resection).

**Flexible flatfoot:** asymptomatic → reassurance only, no treatment alters natural history. Symptomatic → orthotic + gastroc-soleus stretching → surgery (lateral column lengthening ± medial calcaneal osteotomy, gastroc recession) only if refractory, age >8–10y.

**CP hip surveillance frequency by GMFCS:**

| GMFCS | Surveillance |
|---|---|
| I | Clinical exam only; XR if clinical concern |
| II | AP pelvis XR ~age 2 and 6, then as indicated |
| III–V | AP pelvis XR every 6–12 months from ~12–18mo until maturity/hip stable; more frequent if MP rising or >30% |

**Key numbers to remember:** Pirani ≥5 predicts more casts/higher tenotomy rate · Dimeglio ≥15 = severe/grade IV · Migration percentage (MP) >30% = surgical referral threshold in CP · MP >60% = subluxation, 100% = dislocation · Levine-Drennan MDA >11° suggests pathologic infantile Blount vs physiologic bowing · CRP normalizes ~7–10 days with adequate osteomyelitis treatment (more reliable trend marker than ESR).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Clubfoot (congenital talipes equinovarus, CTEV)
A three-dimensional deformity, not a simple hindfoot malposition: **cavus** (forefoot pronation relative to hindfoot), **adductus** (forefoot/midfoot medial deviation at the tarsometatarsal and midtarsal joints), **varus** (hindfoot inversion at the subtalar joint), and **equinus** (ankle plantarflexion) — mnemonic **CAVE**. The talus is the key dysplastic bone: its neck is medially and plantarward deviated, the navicular is subluxated medially on the talar head (can nearly articulate with the medial malleolus), and the calcaneus is adducted and inverted beneath the talus. Medial and posterior soft tissue/ligamentous and musculotendinous structures (posterior tibial tendon, FHL, FDL, deltoid, spring ligament, posterior capsule, Achilles) are contracted. Idiopathic clubfoot occurs in ~1/1000 live births, bilateral in ~50%; distinguish from **positional** (fully passively correctable, no true bony deformity), **syndromic/teratologic** (arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele, distal arthrogryposis) which respond less predictably to Ponseti casting, and **complex/atypical** clubfoot (short, plump, transverse plantar crease, deep posterior crease, hyperextensible first ray) requiring a modified Ponseti technique.

### Pediatric osteomyelitis
Predominantly **hematogenous**, seeding the richly vascularized, sluggish-flow metaphyseal sinusoidal loops adjacent to the growth plate — femur and tibia most common. In infants <18 months, transphyseal vessels persist, so metaphyseal infection readily crosses into the epiphysis/joint (concurrent septic arthritis more common in neonates/young infants). *S. aureus* is the dominant pathogen; community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA, typically PVL-toxin producing) causes more aggressive disease with higher rates of abscess, DVT, and multifocal involvement. *Kingella kingae* is the leading cause in children <4 years and is frequently culture-negative — PCR of joint/bone fluid or throat swab significantly improves yield. Group B strep and gram-negatives predominate in neonates; *Salmonella* and *S. aureus* both occur in sickle cell disease.

### Septic arthritis (child)
Bacterial invasion of the synovium triggers a host inflammatory cascade (proteolytic enzymes, cytokines) that degrades articular cartilage within days. The **hip** (and to a lesser extent shoulder) is uniquely vulnerable because the femoral head blood supply runs via retinacular vessels along the femoral neck within the joint capsule — a tense effusion can tamponade this supply and cause **avascular necrosis**, making hip septic arthritis a true surgical emergency. Most commonly hematogenous (same organisms as osteomyelitis); can be contiguous with adjacent metaphyseal osteomyelitis, especially in joints where the metaphysis is intracapsular (hip, shoulder, elbow, ankle).

### Osgood-Schlatter disease & apophysitides
Traction apophysitis: repetitive tensile overload at a tendon-apophysis junction during the adolescent growth spurt, when the secondary ossification center is mechanically weaker than the tendon it anchors, produces microavulsion, fragmentation, and reactive bone formation. Osgood-Schlatter affects the **tibial tubercle** (patellar tendon insertion); analogous sites include the **inferior patellar pole** (Sinding-Larsen-Johansson), **calcaneal apophysis** (Sever disease — most common cause of heel pain age 8–12), iliac crest, ischial tuberosity (hamstring origin — can progress to true avulsion fracture with forceful eccentric contraction in older adolescents), and medial humeral epicondyle (Little League elbow).

### Juvenile osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) of the knee
Focal subchondral bone injury (likely a combination of repetitive microtrauma and localized vascular insufficiency) leading to separation of a segment of subchondral bone ± overlying articular cartilage from the surrounding healthy bone. Classic location is the **posterolateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle** (non-weight-bearing notch area); also occurs on the lateral condyle, trochlea, and patella. "Juvenile" (open distal femoral physis) OCD has a fundamentally better prognosis than adult OCD because of ongoing bone remodeling capacity and typically smaller, more peripheral lesions.

### Limb length discrepancy (LLD)
Congenital (proximal focal femoral deficiency, fibular/tibial hemimelia, hemihypertrophy/hemihypotrophy) or acquired (growth plate injury/Salter-Harris arrest, infection, tumor, neuromuscular asymmetric growth, Blount, Legg-Calvé-Perthes with head collapse/shortening). Growth velocity of each physis (distal femur ~9–10 mm/yr, proximal tibia ~6 mm/yr contribute most to lower-limb length) determines both the natural evolution of an LLD over time and the timing window for growth-modulating surgery.

### Rotational & angular deformities (physiologic)
Lower-limb alignment and torsional profile follow a predictable developmental trajectory driven by in-utero positioning (physiologic varus/internal rotation at birth) followed by progressive derotation and a swing through valgus in the toddler/preschool years before settling into adult alignment by mid-childhood. Most "deformities" referred in children under age 7–8 are variations of normal development, not pathology.

### Blount disease (tibia vara)
A growth disturbance of the **posteromedial proximal tibial physis** causing progressive varus, internal tibial torsion, and (in later stages) a medial metaphyseal-epiphyseal depression/beak. Pathogenesis is multifactorial: abnormal mechanical compressive overload of the medial physis (associated with obesity, early walking age, and African ancestry) disturbs endochondral ossification, creating a vicious cycle of worsening varus → more medial overload. Infantile (<4y, most common, often bilateral), juvenile (4–10y), and adolescent (>10y, often obesity-associated, unilateral) forms are distinguished by age of onset, symmetry, and typical Langenskiöld stage at presentation.

### Congenital muscular torticollis (CMT)
Fibrosis of the **sternocleidomastoid (SCM)** muscle, believed related to intrauterine positioning/compartment syndrome of the muscle (breech presentation, multiple gestation, first pregnancy) with possible venous congestion causing a fibrotic "pseudotumor" in a subset of infants. The contracted SCM tilts the head toward the affected side and rotates the chin away from it. Associated with **hip dysplasia** (shared intrauterine positional mechanism — screening hip ultrasound recommended) and positional plagiocephaly.

### Tarsal coalition
Failure of segmentation of the primitive mesenchyme between two tarsal bones, producing a fibrous, cartilaginous, or osseous bridge — most commonly **calcaneonavicular** (anterior middle facet region) or **talocalcaneal** (middle facet). The coalition restricts subtalar/midtarsal motion; symptoms characteristically emerge as the coalition ossifies (calcaneonavicular ~age 8–12; talocalcaneal ~age 12–16), producing a rigid, painful, "peroneal spastic" flatfoot.

### Flexible flatfoot
A normal developmental stage in most toddlers: the medial longitudinal arch is masked by a fat pad and ligamentous laxity, and develops progressively through early childhood. True pathologic flexible flatfoot (rare in isolation) reflects generalized ligamentous laxity, accessory navicular, or (when painful/progressive) a spectrum overlapping with early posterior tibial tendon dysfunction in adolescents.

### Cerebral palsy — orthopedic overview
CP is a static brain injury with a non-static musculoskeletal phenotype: spasticity and impaired selective motor control cause progressive muscle-tendon contracture, and asymmetric/excessive muscular forces across growing bone remodel the proximal femur (coxa valga, excessive anteversion) and acetabulum, driving progressive **hip displacement** — the dominant treatable cause of pain and loss of function in non-ambulatory CP. Torsional and angular long-bone deformities (femoral anteversion, tibial torsion), foot deformities (equinus, equinovarus, planovalgus), and neuromuscular scoliosis complete the typical musculoskeletal burden, all of which worsen with growth if untreated.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

- **Clubfoot:** clinical diagnosis at birth; assess reducibility of each component, hindfoot equinus, presence of deep posterior/medial creases, muscle bulk (calf atrophy is typical and does not by itself indicate a worse prognosis). Radiographs rarely needed for idiopathic cases; screen for associated conditions (spine, hips) in atypical/syndromic presentations.
- **Osteomyelitis/septic arthritis:** history (fever, refusal to bear weight/pseudoparalysis in infants, focal bone/joint pain), CRP (rises within 6h, peaks ~36–50h, most sensitive single marker and best for following response — normalizes ~7–10 days with adequate treatment), ESR (slower to rise and slower to fall, can paradoxically increase early in treatment — do not use alone to judge response), WBC (often normal, poor sensitivity), blood cultures (positive in only ~30–60%, still worth obtaining before antibiotics), plain radiographs (often normal for the first 7–10 days; look for soft-tissue swelling, joint space widening/effusion for septic arthritis). **MRI is the imaging gold standard** for osteomyelitis (sensitivity ~90–97%) — defines marrow edema, subperiosteal/intraosseous abscess, and distinguishes osteomyelitis from malignancy (Ewing sarcoma, leukemia) and Langerhans cell histiocytosis, which can mimic it. Ultrasound is useful and rapid for detecting a hip effusion (guides aspiration) but cannot reliably distinguish septic from sterile effusion. Joint aspiration remains the diagnostic gold standard for septic arthritis (cell count typically >50,000/mm³ with >90% PMNs, though this threshold has poor individual sensitivity/specificity and must be interpreted with the full clinical picture; Gram stain and culture, plus PCR for *Kingella* in children <4y).
- **Osgood-Schlatter/apophysitis:** clinical diagnosis — focal tenderness and prominence over the tibial tubercle, pain reproduced with resisted knee extension; radiographs only to exclude other pathology (tumor, avulsion fracture, infection) or if atypical presentation.
- **Juvenile OCD:** AP, lateral, and **tunnel/notch view** radiographs (best for classic posterolateral medial condyle lesion). MRI for staging stability: high-signal (T2) rim between fragment and parent bone, cyst formation beneath the fragment, and a breach in the overlying articular cartilage are the classic MRI instability criteria. Arthroscopic probing remains the most reliable stability assessment when surgery is undertaken.
- **LLD:** clinical block test (blocks under short limb until pelvis level), full-length standing EOS/scanogram radiograph (gold standard for measurement — obtain serial studies to project growth), and CT scanogram as an alternative. Rotational profile and growth-plate status (open/closing) documented at each visit.
- **Rotational/angular deformities:** clinical rotational profile (foot progression angle, hip internal/external rotation in extension, thigh-foot angle, transmalleolar axis) plotted against age norms; standing long-leg alignment radiographs only if pathologic bowing suspected (asymmetry, progression beyond expected age, focal single-bone involvement) — measure the **mechanical axis** and the **Levine-Drennan metaphyseal-diaphyseal angle (MDA)** on a single AP tibia in suspected early Blount.
- **Blount disease:** standing AP long-leg radiograph — MDA >11° in a child <2–3y is suspicious for pathologic (Blount) rather than physiologic bowing before Langenskiöld changes become visually obvious; serial films to stage by Langenskiöld.
- **CMT:** clinical exam (head tilt, chin rotation, passive ROM deficit, palpable SCM mass/band); cervical spine radiographs/MRI reserved for atypical presentation (painful onset, older child, no palpable SCM contracture, associated neurologic findings) to exclude atlantoaxial rotatory subluxation, Klippel-Feil, or posterior fossa/spinal cord tumor. Screening **hip ultrasound** recommended given the DDH association.
- **Tarsal coalition:** foot radiographs (AP/lateral/oblique — calcaneonavicular coalition visible on the 45° oblique as the classic "anteater sign"; talocalcaneal coalition suggested indirectly by the "C-sign" on lateral view and talar beaking, but plain films frequently miss it). **CT is the gold standard** for characterizing extent, precise anatomy, and surgical planning (especially talocalcaneal, middle facet). MRI adds value for coalitions that are still fibrous/cartilaginous (not yet visible on CT/XR) and for assessing secondary cartilage/joint changes.
- **Flexible flatfoot:** single-leg heel-rise and Jack's (great toe) dorsiflexion test — arch reconstitution confirms flexibility/normal windlass mechanism; assess gastrocnemius-soleus tightness (Silfverskiöld test) as it commonly co-exists and drives symptoms.
- **CP hip surveillance:** standardized supine AP pelvis radiograph measuring **migration percentage (Reimers index)** — the percentage of the femoral head lateral to Perkin's line, uncovered by the acetabulum — is the core surveillance metric, obtained at intervals dictated by GMFCS level (see Classification/Quick Reference).

## Classification

### Pirani score (clubfoot severity/monitoring)
Six binary/half-point signs (0 = normal, 0.5 = mild, 1 = severe), scored weekly during Ponseti casting.

| Component | Sign |
|---|---|
| **Hindfoot contracture score (HFCS)** | Posterior crease |
| | Empty heel (non-palpable calcaneal tuberosity) |
| | Rigidity of equinus |
| **Midfoot contracture score (MFCS)** | Curvature of lateral border of foot |
| | Severity of medial crease |
| | Lateral head of talus coverage (palpability) |

**Total score 0–6** (HFCS 0–3 + MFCS 0–3). **Management implication:** used serially to track correction progress with each cast; a higher **initial** total score (particularly >4–5) predicts a greater number of casts required and a higher likelihood of needing percutaneous Achilles tenotomy. A plateauing or worsening score despite technically correct casting should prompt reassessment of technique or suspicion of an atypical/complex foot.

### Dimeglio classification (clubfoot severity)
Four reducibility parameters, each graded 0–4 (0 = fully correctable/hypercorrectable, 4 = fixed/non-reducible): **equinus** (sagittal plane), **varus** (frontal plane), **derotation around the talar head** (horizontal plane, i.e., how much the hindfoot can be externally rotated relative to the ankle mortise), and **adduction of the forefoot** (horizontal plane) — subtotal 0–16. Four additional 0–1 points are added for: posterior crease, medial crease, cavus, and poor muscle condition — total **0–20**.

| Grade | Score | Descriptor | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | <5 | Soft, benign (postural-leaning) foot | Fewer casts, rapid correction, high likelihood of correction without tenotomy |
| II | 5–9 | Soft foot, moderate | Standard Ponseti course, tenotomy commonly still required |
| III | 10–14 | Stiff foot, severe | More casts, tenotomy almost always required, closer relapse surveillance |
| IV | 15–20 | Very stiff, severe (often syndromic/teratologic) | Longest casting course, highest relapse/re-intervention rate, consider modified technique |

### Kocher criteria (differentiating septic arthritis from transient synovitis, hip)
Four independent predictors: (1) **non-weight-bearing** on the affected limb, (2) **ESR >40 mm/hr**, (3) **fever >38.5°C**, (4) **serum WBC >12,000/mm³**. A later modification (Caird et al.) added **CRP >20 mg/L** as a fifth predictor, improving sensitivity.

| Predictors positive | Probability of septic arthritis | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | <0.2% | Observation, supportive care (presumed transient synovitis) |
| 1 | ~3% | Close outpatient follow-up |
| 2 | ~40% | Ultrasound-guided hip aspiration |
| 3 | ~93% | Urgent surgical drainage |
| 4 (or ≥4 of 5 with CRP added) | ~99% | Emergent surgical drainage without delay |

The original cohort's high pretest probability limits generalizability to lower-prevalence populations — external validation studies show substantially lower predictive accuracy elsewhere; use as an adjunct to, never a replacement for, clinical judgment and a low threshold for aspiration in a toxic-appearing or rapidly worsening child.

### Langenskiöld classification (infantile Blount disease)
Six radiographic stages of the proximal medial tibial metaphyseal-epiphyseal complex.

| Stage | Radiographic findings | Typical age/reversibility | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Irregular medial metaphyseal beaking, mild metaphyseal-diaphyseal angulation | Often <2y, frequently reversible | Observe or brace (KAFO) trial; many resolve spontaneously if diagnosed very early |
| II | Sharper medial metaphyseal depression, physis widened medially | ~2–3y | Brace trial; guided growth if progressive |
| III | Deepening of the metaphyseal defect with a medial bony "step" | ~2.5–4y | Guided growth (lateral hemiepiphysiodesis) or valgus osteotomy if brace fails/age >3–4y |
| IV | Epiphysis becomes wedge-shaped, encroaching into the metaphyseal depression | ~4–6y | Osteotomy ± guided growth; higher relapse risk |
| V | Epiphysis cleft into separate medial and lateral fragments | ~5–10y | Osteotomy with elevation of the depressed medial plateau; consider physeal bar assessment |
| VI | Bony bridge (physeal bar) forms across the medial physis | ~9–13y | Osteotomy + bar resection if focal and adequate growth remaining; otherwise combined osteotomy/epiphysiodesis |

Higher stage at presentation correlates with higher recurrence risk after correction and a greater likelihood that guided growth alone will fail, favoring primary osteotomy.

### Salenius-Vankka curve (physiologic tibiofemoral angle by age)
Normative reference curve of the tibiofemoral (knee) angle from birth to skeletal maturity, used to judge whether a child's angular alignment falls within the expected physiologic range (±2 SD) for age.

| Age | Expected alignment |
|---|---|
| Birth | ~15° varus |
| ~18–24 months | Neutral (transition point) |
| ~3–4 years | Peak physiologic valgus, ~10–12° |
| ~6–7 years | Settles toward adult alignment, ~5–7° valgus |
| Skeletal maturity | Adult alignment, ~5–7° valgus |

**Management implication:** a child whose varus persists or worsens past ~24 months (i.e., fails to enter the expected valgus swing) — especially with a Levine-Drennan MDA >11° — should be evaluated for pathologic Blount disease or another cause of pathologic bowing rather than reassured as physiologic. Conversely, valgus within the expected 3–4-year-old peak, or varus in a child under ~18–24 months without red-flag features, warrants observation only.

### GMFCS — Gross Motor Function Classification System (cerebral palsy)
Five levels based on self-initiated movement, with emphasis on sitting, walking, and use of wheeled mobility (age-banded descriptors below reflect the 6–12-year band).

| Level | Functional description | Hip displacement risk (untreated) | Hip surveillance implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Walks without limitations | Low (~1–5%) | Clinical exam only; XR if concern |
| II | Walks with limitations (long distances, uneven terrain, crowds) | Low-moderate (~10–15%) | AP pelvis XR at ~age 2 and 6, then as indicated |
| III | Walks using a hand-held mobility device; may self-propel a wheelchair | Moderate-high (~40–50%) | AP pelvis XR every 6–12 months from ~12–18mo |
| IV | Self-mobility with limitations; may use powered mobility, transported in a manual wheelchair | High (~70%) | AP pelvis XR every 6–12 months from ~12–18mo, closer if MP rising |
| V | Transported in a manual wheelchair; severely limited self-mobility | Very high (up to ~90%) | AP pelvis XR every 6–12 months from ~12mo until maturity/stable |

GMFCS level also guides selection among spasticity treatments (SDR favored for ambulatory GMFCS II–III with good selective motor control; intrathecal baclofen favored for generalized spasticity in GMFCS IV–V) and predicts likelihood of ambulation and need for SEMLS versus salvage hip/spine surgery.

**Regional practice note:** for surgeons practicing in Romania/Europe alongside US-trained colleagues, hip surveillance protocol structure is broadly convergent — the Swedish **CPUP** national registry protocol, the **Australian/AACPDM-aligned consensus guideline**, and North American POSNA-endorsed practice all use GMFCS-stratified radiographic intervals anchored on migration percentage, so a single GMFCS-based surveillance schedule (as tabulated above) is defensible and guideline-concordant regardless of which national program a given center formally follows; the main cross-program variation is in the precise interval (6 vs 12 months) at intermediate GMFCS levels rather than in the underlying MP-driven treatment thresholds.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Clubfoot — Ponseti method (first-line for essentially all idiopathic clubfeet):** weekly gentle manipulation and long-leg casting (knee flexed ~90° to control rotation). Correction sequence: (1) elevate the first ray to correct cavus and align the forefoot with the hindfoot (never pronate the forefoot to correct cavus — this worsens the deformity), (2) simultaneously abduct the foot beneath the talar head with counter-pressure on the lateral talar head (never push on the calcaneus/heel) while equinus and hindfoot varus correct passively as the foot abducts to ~60–70° relative to the tibial axis. Average 5–6 casts. Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy is required in ~85–90% of idiopathic feet once abduction is achieved but ankle dorsiflexion remains <10–15°; performed under local anesthesia in clinic, followed by a final cast for ~3 weeks in maximum abduction/dorsiflexion. **Bracing (Foot Abduction Brace/Denis-Browne bar)** at 70° external rotation on the corrected side (and ~40–45° on the unaffected side if unilateral) is mandatory: full-time wear (~23h/day) for 3 months, then naps + nighttime (~12–14h/day) until approximately age 4–5 years. **Brace non-adherence is the single strongest predictor of relapse** — published relapse rates run roughly 80%+ with poor adherence versus <10–20% with consistent bracing.

**Osteomyelitis/septic arthritis:** antibiotics are the mainstay alongside any indicated drainage (see Operative). Empiric selection is guided by local MRSA prevalence and clindamycin resistance rates: where MRSA risk is low, an antistaphylococcal penicillin (oxacillin/nafcillin) or first-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) suffices; where MRSA risk is significant, clindamycin (if local resistance <10–15%) or vancomycin (severe/ICU-level illness, high local clindamycin resistance, or definitive MRSA) is used. Add gram-negative coverage in neonates; consider a second/third-generation cephalosporin when *Kingella kingae* is likely (age <4y, subacute presentation, low inflammatory markers). **Duration:** modern practice favors shorter total courses than classic teaching. IV therapy continues until clear clinical improvement, afebrile 24–48–72h, and a downtrending CRP (typically day 3–5), after which oral step-down is used for the remainder; total duration ~3–4 weeks for uncomplicated hematogenous osteomyelitis with a brisk response, versus 4–6+ weeks for MRSA, abscess formation, delayed presentation, or immunocompromised hosts. This mirrors the UK **BOAST (British Orthopaedic Association Standards for Trauma) guideline on the management of bone and joint infection in children**, which similarly endorses early IV-to-oral switch once clinical/biochemical response is confirmed rather than a fixed 4–6-week IV course, and **POSNA** position statements supporting shorter, response-guided therapy over historical fixed-duration dogma.

**Osgood-Schlatter/apophysitides:** activity modification (not strict cessation) proportional to symptoms, ice after activity, a short NSAID course for flares, and a structured quadriceps/hamstring stretching and gradual eccentric strengthening program. An infrapatellar strap can reduce symptomatic traction during sport. Reassure that resolution occurs with apophyseal fusion (typically within 12–24 months).

**Juvenile OCD (stable lesions, open physis):** first-line non-operative management — activity/impact restriction for 3–6 months (crutches/protected weight-bearing or brief immobilization in younger or more symptomatic patients), with repeat radiographs/MRI at ~3-month intervals to confirm healing before graduated return to sport. This is the arena where juvenile lesions clearly outperform adult OCD.

**LLD:** observation for small/non-progressive discrepancies; shoe lift for symptomatic discrepancies not yet meeting a surgical threshold.

**Rotational/angular deformities (physiologic):** observation only — no brace, shoe modification, or orthotic has been shown to alter the natural history of physiologic bowing, intoeing, or torsional variants; this includes historically popular Denis-Browne bars and "corrective" shoes for isolated torsional variants. Reassess at intervals through the expected resolution window (varus to age ~2; femoral anteversion/intoeing typically improves through age 8–10).

**Blount disease:** a **brace trial (KAFO)** is reasonable in Langenskiöld I–II, especially in a child under ~3 years with mild-moderate deformity, given for 6–12 months with radiographic monitoring; failure to improve or progression to Stage III mandates surgical guided growth or osteotomy.

**CMT:** structured **physical therapy** — passive SCM stretching, active repositioning/tummy time, and parent-taught home exercise program — is first-line and highly effective when begun early (ideally before 3–6 months of age); success rates exceed 90–95% in mild-moderate cases started early. Kinesio-taping and positioning aids are reasonable adjuncts.

**Tarsal coalition:** activity modification and NSAIDs for flares; a period of **short-leg cast or CAM boot immobilization (4–6 weeks)** for an acute painful episode, followed by a semi-rigid or UCBL orthotic, is a reasonable and often effective first-line trial before considering resection.

**Flexible flatfoot:** reassurance for asymptomatic children (the overwhelming majority) — this is a normal developmental variant, not a disease requiring treatment. For symptomatic feet, arch-support orthotics and a gastrocnemius-soleus stretching program (particularly when Silfverskiöld testing shows isolated gastrocnemius tightness) are first-line; evidence that orthotics change long-term arch development is weak, but symptomatic benefit is real for many.

**CP — spasticity/contracture:** oral antispasmodics (baclofen, diazepam, tizanidine) for generalized mild spasticity; serial casting and bracing (AFOs) for early dynamic equinus; stretching programs throughout, understanding they modestly slow but do not prevent contracture progression in moderate-severe spasticity.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Botulinum toxin A** injections for focal spastic muscles in CP (most commonly gastrocnemius-soleus, hip adductors, hamstrings) — reduces dynamic tone for roughly 3–6 months, used to facilitate bracing/casting, delay (not replace) surgery, and improve comfort/hygiene in non-ambulatory patients; dosed by muscle and body weight with attention to total-dose ceilings.
- **Ultrasound-guided hip aspiration** in suspected septic arthritis with intermediate Kocher probability — both diagnostic (cell count, Gram stain, culture, *Kingella* PCR) and therapeutic in reducing intracapsular pressure while definitive management is arranged.
- **Serial casting** functions as an adjunctive/non-surgical procedure in both CMT (rarely used) and early dynamic equinus in CP, and is the core technique of the Ponseti method for clubfoot.
- **Intrathecal baclofen pump** — an implanted, titratable adjunct for severe generalized spasticity (typically GMFCS IV–V) when oral agents are insufficient or cause unacceptable sedation; reversible (pump can be removed/turned off) unlike ablative procedures.

### Operative

**Clubfoot relapse surgery:** dynamic (but passively correctable) supination during swing phase from an overactive/unopposed tibialis anterior, typically presenting after age 2.5–3 years, is treated with **tibialis anterior tendon transfer (TATT)** to the lateral cuneiform. Rigid/recurrent deformity is first managed with repeat Ponseti casting ± repeat percutaneous tenotomy, which remains effective even in the walking-age child. True fixed relapse or neglected/atypical clubfoot failing repeat conservative treatment may require soft-tissue releases (much less common in the Ponseti era) or, in the older child approaching skeletal maturity, bony correction (lateral column shortening/calcaneocuboid decancellation, midfoot/hindfoot osteotomies) with triple arthrodesis reserved as a last resort for severe, rigid, degenerative deformity.

**Osteomyelitis/septic arthritis:** surgical drainage is indicated for a **subperiosteal or intraosseous abscess** on imaging, frank purulence, **failure to improve clinically within 48–72 hours** of appropriate antibiotics, or any associated septic joint. **Hip (and shoulder) septic arthritis is a surgical emergency** given the tamponade risk to the femoral head blood supply — emergent open arthrotomy or arthroscopic washout is recommended, generally within 24 hours of diagnosis; other joints (knee, ankle, elbow) can sometimes be managed with serial percutaneous aspiration if rapidly improving, though many centers favor early arthroscopic washout across all major joints given superior visualization and debridement. Chronic/recurrent osteomyelitis or a sequestrum requires formal surgical debridement.

**Juvenile OCD:** unstable lesion, loose body, or failure of an adequate (3–6 month) non-operative trial are the core indications. Technique selection follows lesion characteristics: **transarticular or retroarticular (extra-articular) drilling** for a stable lesion with intact overlying cartilage that has failed non-operative care (stimulates a healing response without disturbing the articular surface); **internal fixation** (bioabsorbable pins or headless compression screws) for an unstable but salvageable fragment with viable subchondral bone; **debridement with microfracture, or osteochondral autograft/allograft transfer (OATS) / particulated juvenile cartilage** for an unsalvageable fragment or a full-thickness cartilage defect after fragment excision. Retroarticular drilling is favored when preserving the articular surface is paramount (e.g., very young patient, small stable lesion).

**LLD — epiphysiodesis and lengthening:** **guided growth (temporary hemiepiphysiodesis with a tension-band/8-plate)** is reversible and preferred when there is meaningful growth remaining and precision timing is less critical (or when combined angular correction is also needed) — growth resumes after implant removal, but correction can rebound if the plate is removed too early relative to the deformity achieved. **Permanent epiphysiodesis** (percutaneous transphyseal screw — PETS — or open Phemister technique) is timed using the **Paley multiplier method**: the current LLD (or the segment length) is multiplied by an age- and sex-specific multiplier to predict the discrepancy at skeletal maturity, and the epiphysiodesis is scheduled so that arresting growth of the longer limb's physis (or physes) allows the shorter limb to "catch up" to equalize by maturity — the multiplier simplifies the older Anderson-Green and Moseley straight-line-graph methods and is the most widely used modern planning tool. **Limb lengthening** (external fixator — Ilizarov ring or monolateral frame — or an internal magnetic motorized nail such as PRECICE) is reserved for discrepancies beyond the practical range of epiphysiodesis (generally >5–6 cm), often combined with contralateral epiphysiodesis, and staged over multiple lengthening episodes for very large (>10–15 cm) discrepancies such as PFFD or severe congenital deficiencies.

**Rotational deformities:** derotational osteotomy (femoral and/or tibial) is reserved for severe, functionally limiting deformity persisting beyond the expected age of spontaneous resolution (generally not considered before age 8–10), with a clear functional indication — frequent tripping, significant cosmetic distress, or patellofemoral symptoms correlating with excessive femoral anteversion on CT rotational profile — rather than for radiographic/clinical angle alone.

**Blount disease:** **guided growth** (lateral proximal tibial and, if needed, proximal fibular hemiepiphysiodesis with an 8-plate/tension-band construct) is favored in younger children (roughly <4–5 years, Langenskiöld I–III) with adequate growth remaining and mild-moderate deformity — it is lower-morbidity than osteotomy but requires reliable follow-up and works only while growth remains. **Proximal tibial valgus osteotomy** (acute correction, or gradual correction with an external fixator for larger deformities to avoid acute neurovascular injury/compartment syndrome) is used for more severe deformity, older children, or guided-growth failure; **elevation of the depressed medial tibial plateau** is added for Langenskiöld IV–V; **physeal bar resection** (with fat or other interposition material) is considered for a focal Stage VI bar with substantial growth remaining, sometimes combined with contralateral epiphysiodesis or guided growth if the bar is extensive.

**CMT surgical release:** indicated after failure of an adequate physical therapy trial — persistent head tilt/rotational deficit (>15–30°) beyond roughly age 12–18 months to 3 years, or late presentation (>1 year of age) with a fixed contracture. **Unipolar (distal) release** at the clavicular/sternal origin, **bipolar release** (both origin and mastoid insertion), or **Z-plasty/endoscopic release** for improved cosmesis are all described; choice depends on severity and surgeon preference. Post-operative stretching/bracing protocol is essential to prevent recurrent contracture.

**Tarsal coalition:** **coalition resection with interposition** (fat graft, extensor digitorum brevis muscle belly, or bone wax/cement) is first-line surgery for a symptomatic calcaneonavicular coalition, and for a talocalcaneal (middle facet) coalition involving **less than ~50% of the joint surface area** with minimal secondary degenerative change or talar beaking on imaging, in an appropriately selected older child/adolescent. **Subtalar or triple arthrodesis** is reserved for extensive coalition, significant degenerative change, failed prior resection, or associated fixed hindfoot deformity — growth-sparing alternatives are exhausted first given the fusion's permanence.

**Flexible flatfoot (refractory, symptomatic, age >8–10y):** **lateral column lengthening (Evans calcaneal osteotomy)** ± medial displacement calcaneal osteotomy corrects hindfoot valgus and midfoot abduction; **gastrocnemius recession or Achilles lengthening** addresses concurrent contracture (a very common driver of symptoms); **subtalar arthroereisis** (an implant limiting excessive subtalar pronation) is a more controversial, reversible option with lower-quality evidence; medial soft-tissue procedures (spring ligament repair, FDL transfer) are reserved for the uncommon adolescent presentation resembling early PTTD, or symptomatic accessory navicular (**Kidner procedure**).

**CP hip:** management follows the surveillance migration percentage (MP) and age. **Soft-tissue release** (adductor longus ± iliopsoas lengthening) is used for MP 30–40% in a younger child with a reasonably spherical femoral head — the goal is to halt progression, not necessarily fully reduce the hip. **Combined proximal femoral varus derotation osteotomy (VDRO) ± pelvic osteotomy** (Dega, San Diego, Pemberton, or Salter, chosen by age and acetabular deficiency pattern) is indicated for MP >40–50% or established/recurrent subluxation. **Salvage procedures** (Chiari osteotomy, proximal femoral resection/valgus osteotomy [McHale procedure], shelf arthroplasty) are reserved for painful, chronic dislocation in an older or lower-functioning (GMFCS IV–V) child where reconstruction is not feasible. **Selective dorsal rhizotomy (SDR)** is considered for ambulatory spastic diplegia (GMFCS II–III) with good selective motor control, typically age 3–8 years, to durably reduce spasticity. **Single-event multilevel surgery (SEMLS)** — correcting all levels of deformity (hip, knee, foot/ankle) in one anesthetic episode followed by one comprehensive rehabilitation period — is now the standard of care in ambulatory CP (replacing the older pattern of staged, repeated single-level procedures sometimes called "birthday syndrome" surgery), typically performed around age 7–10 once the gait pattern has matured and is amenable to instrumented gait analysis. Foot/ankle equinus surgery (gastrocnemius recession preferred over Achilles tendon lengthening, to reduce the risk of overlengthening and calcaneus gait) is generally deferred past age 4–5 except in severe fixed contracture. **Neuromuscular scoliosis** in non-ambulatory (GMFCS IV–V) CP is managed with posterior spinal fusion (pelvis-to-upper-thoracic, growing constructs if the child is young and curve severe) once the curve threatens sitting balance, pulmonary function, or caregiving — bracing is used only for seating comfort, not curve control.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Clubfoot (post-Ponseti):**
- Weeks 0–1 (post-tenotomy): final cast in maximum correction, non-weight-bearing not required (infants), cast removed at ~3 weeks.
- Weeks 3+ (post-cast removal): begin FAB full-time wear (23h/day) — parents counseled extensively on adherence, skin checks, and brace tolerance strategies, as adherence is the dominant modifiable relapse risk factor.
- Months 3–~48–60 (age 4–5y): transition to naps + nighttime brace wear (~12–14h/day); ongoing clinic follow-up every 2–4 months in year 1, then every 4–6 months, watching for early relapse signs (recurrent adductus, rising Pirani-equivalent findings, loss of dorsiflexion).
- Beyond brace discontinuation: annual follow-up recommended through skeletal maturity given late relapse and dynamic supination can present into the school-age years.

**Osteomyelitis/septic arthritis:**
- Acute phase (inpatient): IV antibiotics, immobilization/rest of the affected limb/joint for comfort, pain control; weight-bearing as tolerated once acute pain subsides for bone infection without joint involvement.
- Post-drainage (septic arthritis, esp. hip): protected weight-bearing 2–4 weeks depending on intraoperative findings/degree of cartilage injury, then progressive weight-bearing with physiotherapy focused on regaining joint ROM.
- Oral step-down phase: continue through total antibiotic course (see Treatment) with clinical/CRP monitoring; return to full activity/sport typically once CRP has normalized and the joint/limb has regained functional ROM and strength, generally by 6–8 weeks from presentation for uncomplicated cases.

**Osgood-Schlatter/apophysitides:**
- Symptom-guided activity modification rather than fixed timeline; most resolve within 12–24 months with apophyseal fusion.
- Physiotherapy emphasis: quadriceps and hamstring flexibility, eccentric quadriceps strengthening, and load management (graduated return to jumping/running sport) rather than immobilization.
- Return to sport: guided by symptom resolution with activity rather than a fixed calendar; brief in-season load reduction is usually sufficient, complete cessation rarely required.

**Juvenile OCD (non-operative):**
- Weeks 0–12: activity/impact restriction (crutches or protected weight-bearing as needed for pain), brace or cast in younger/more symptomatic patients; low-impact ROM/quad activation exercise permitted.
- Month 3: repeat imaging (radiograph ± MRI) to assess healing; if healing, begin graduated return to low-impact activity.
- Months 4–6: progressive strengthening and functional loading; full-impact/pivoting sport only after radiographic/clinical healing confirmed, typically not before month 4–6.
- Post-operative (drilling/fixation): protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks, progressive ROM and quad strengthening, return to sport generally 4–6 months after fixation/drilling once imaging confirms healing; longer (9–12 months) after OATS/cartilage restoration procedures.

**LLD (post-guided-growth/epiphysiodesis):** weight-bearing as tolerated immediately in most percutaneous techniques; routine follow-up every 4–6 months with standing radiographs to monitor correction rate and avoid overcorrection; implant removal timed to the desired correction endpoint.

**Blount disease (post-osteotomy/guided growth):**
- Guided growth: weight-bearing as tolerated, no formal rehab beyond routine activity; radiographic follow-up every 3–4 months to monitor correction rate (typically 0.5–1°/month) and plan implant removal.
- Osteotomy: protected weight-bearing 4–6 weeks (longer with external fixator until adequate callus), progressive weight-bearing and knee/ankle ROM physiotherapy through weeks 6–12, full activity typically by 3–4 months.

**CMT (post-surgical release):** stretching program and cervical range-of-motion exercises begin within days of surgery; soft collar or positioning device may be used briefly; home stretching continues for several months to prevent recurrent contracture; full activity typically by 6–8 weeks.

**Tarsal coalition (post-resection):** non-weight-bearing cast/boot 2–3 weeks, then progressive weight-bearing in a removable boot with early subtalar ROM exercises to prevent re-adhesion of the interposition graft; formal physiotherapy for peroneal strengthening and gait retraining weeks 6–12; return to sport typically 3–4 months.

**CP — SEMLS:** intensive multidisciplinary post-operative rehabilitation is the defining feature of the SEMLS model — typically 3–6 months of structured physiotherapy (progressive weight-bearing per procedure performed, strengthening, gait retraining), often with a single inpatient rehabilitation admission, aiming to consolidate gains from all corrected levels simultaneously rather than repeating rehab after sequential single-level surgeries.

## Complications

- **Clubfoot:** relapse (most common, strongly adherence-dependent), overcorrection (rare, valgus/pes planus from over-abduction or excessive tenotomy), dynamic supination requiring TATT, dorsal bunion (from an overactive/imbalanced first ray after correction), residual/rocker-bottom deformity from forceful mid-tarsal manipulation before hindfoot equinus is corrected (a classic technical error to avoid).
- **Osteomyelitis:** growth arrest/physeal bar formation (transphyseal vessel involvement in infants), chronic osteomyelitis with sequestrum/sinus tract, pathologic fracture, contiguous septic arthritis, and (rare but reported with virulent CA-MRSA) septic thrombophlebitis/DVT and multifocal disease.
- **Septic arthritis:** avascular necrosis of the femoral head (hip), articular cartilage destruction and secondary osteoarthritis, growth disturbance if the physis is involved, joint stiffness/contracture if drainage or rehabilitation is delayed.
- **Osgood-Schlatter:** rarely, an ununited ossicle persisting into adulthood with focal tenderness (may need excision); tibial tubercle avulsion fracture is a distinct, more acute injury rather than a complication of typical apophysitis.
- **Juvenile OCD:** progression to instability/loose body if non-operative treatment is inadequately monitored, non-union after fixation, donor-site morbidity after OATS, and premature osteoarthritis in large or lateral/weight-bearing lesions that fail to heal.
- **LLD surgery:** overcorrection or undercorrection from mistimed epiphysiodesis (multiplier miscalculation or unexpected growth variation), angular deformity from asymmetric physeal arrest with an 8-plate, pin/screw breakage or migration, and — with lengthening — pin-tract infection, joint contracture, premature consolidation, and neurovascular injury.
- **Blount disease:** recurrence after undercorrection or premature guided-growth implant removal, physeal arrest/bar formation after osteotomy near the growth plate, peroneal nerve palsy and compartment syndrome with rapid/large acute corrections (favoring gradual correction for large deformities).
- **CMT:** residual head tilt/facial asymmetry if treatment delayed, hypertrophic/keloid scarring after surgical release, recurrent contracture if post-operative stretching is inadequate.
- **Tarsal coalition:** recurrence of coalition/bar after resection, persistent pain despite adequate resection (consider undiagnosed additional coalition or degenerative change), interposition graft failure.
- **Flexible flatfoot surgery:** overcorrection (iatrogenic cavovarus) after lateral column lengthening, calcaneocuboid joint overload/subluxation, non-union of osteotomy, recurrence if concurrent gastrocnemius tightness is not addressed.
- **CP hip:** progression to painful dislocation if surveillance is missed or inadequate, avascular necrosis after reconstructive surgery (especially combined VDRO + pelvic osteotomy in severe subluxation), heterotopic ossification, recurrent subluxation requiring revision surgery.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[Coskun E, Bess LE, Bielski RJ — "The Outcomes of Guided Growth System in Juvenile and Adolescent Patients With Blount Disease," J Pediatr Orthop, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Blount+disease+treatment+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — reports effectiveness and safety of lateral proximal tibial guided-growth (8-plate) systems specifically in juvenile/adolescent Blount, including patients with severe deformity, reinforcing guided growth as a viable first-line option beyond the classic infantile-only indication when adequate growth remains.
- **[Tageldeen Mohamed M, et al. — "Percutaneous Transphyseal Screw Hemiepiphysiodesis (PETS) for Tibia Vara in Blount's Disease," J Pediatr Orthop, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=Blount+disease+treatment+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — adds outcome data for the PETS technique in tibia vara, broadening the guided-growth implant toolbox alongside tension-band 8-plates for Blount correction.
- **[Arvati S, et al. — "Brace non-adherence and relapse after Ponseti treatment for idiopathic clubfoot," J Pediatr Orthop B, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ponseti+clubfoot+relapse+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — further quantifies the strong, direct association between FAB non-adherence and relapse in a contemporary cohort, reinforcing brace-adherence counseling as the single highest-yield relapse-prevention intervention.
- **[Trees A, et al. — "Establishing the UK clubfoot research priorities," Bone & Joint Open, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=ponseti+clubfoot+relapse+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — a national consensus exercise identifying evidence gaps in relapse prediction, complex/atypical foot management, and long-term patient-reported outcomes, signaling where near-term trial evidence is expected.
- **[Canizares MF, Shore BJ, Sanders JS, Miller PE, Schoenecker JG — "The Immunothrombotic Phenotype: Early Inflammatory and Hematologic Markers that Identify Children at Increased Risk of Avascular Necrosis Following Septic Arthritis," JB JS Open Access, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=pediatric+septic+arthritis+diagnosis+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — proposes an early inflammatory/coagulation biomarker panel to risk-stratify AVN risk after hip septic arthritis, a step beyond Kocher-style diagnostic scoring toward prognostic stratification once the diagnosis is already established.
- **[Fortunato M, Tabard-Fougère A, et al. — "An Evaluation of Novel Inflammatory Biomarkers in Children with Kingella kingae Osteoarticular Infections," Pathogens, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=pediatric+septic+arthritis+diagnosis+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — refines biomarker-based recognition of *K. kingae* osteoarticular infection in young children, relevant given this organism's tendency toward culture-negative, low-CRP presentations that can be under-triaged by Kocher-style criteria alone.
- **[Domínguez-León V, et al. — "Expanding the Role of Dalbavancin in Children: A Case Series and Systematic Review," Pediatr Infect Dis J, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=pediatric+osteomyelitis+antibiotic+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — supports long-acting lipoglycopeptide (dalbavancin) step-down therapy for pediatric osteoarticular infection, including MRSA, as a strategy to shorten hospitalization/PICC-line duration while maintaining outcomes comparable to standard IV-to-oral step-down.
- **[Saavedra-Lozano J, Agüera M, Velasco-Arnaiz E — "Evolving Paradigms in Pediatric Osteomyelitis: Modern Insights Into an Old Disease," Curr Opin Infect Dis, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=pediatric+osteomyelitis+antibiotic+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — a contemporary review consolidating the shift toward shorter total antibiotic courses, early oral step-down guided by clinical/CRP response, and refined imaging/microbiologic diagnostics (PCR for *Kingella*) as current standard practice.
- **[Howard JJ, Graham HK, Johari A, Narayanan U, Bennett L, Presedo A, Shore BJ, Guerschman T, Aroojis A — "Hip displacement in children with cerebral palsy: surveillance to surgery — a current concepts review," SICOT-J, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=hip+surveillance+cerebral+palsy+program+consensus&sort=date)** — a multinational expert consensus synthesizing the evidence base for GMFCS-stratified hip surveillance and the reconstructive-to-salvage treatment ladder, reinforcing hip surveillance as a global standard-of-care priority rather than a regional/national program alone.
- **[Joseph PJS, Khattak M, Masudi ST, Minta L, Perry DC — "Radiological assessment of hip disease in children with cerebral palsy: development of a core measurement set," Bone & Joint Open, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=hip+surveillance+cerebral+palsy+program+consensus&sort=date)** — proposes a standardized core set of radiological measurements (anchored on migration percentage) for CP hip surveillance research and practice, aiming to improve comparability across surveillance programs and studies.
- **[Kemmeren LAM, Oei EHG, van Middelkoop M, Eygendaal D, Piscaer TM — "Juvenile osteochondritis dissecans of the knee in the general adolescent population: Prevalence and longitudinal trends," J ISAKOS, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=juvenile+osteochondritis+dissecans+knee+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — population-level epidemiologic data on JOCD prevalence and natural history in adolescents, informing counseling around incidental/asymptomatic lesions and the denominator for surgical versus non-operative management decisions.
- **[Rivarola H, Collazo C, Palanconi M, et al. — "Early arthroscopic fixation of low-grade juvenile osteochondritis dissecans with bioabsorbable pins achieves excellent clinical and imaging outcomes in high-demand athletes," J ISAKOS, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=juvenile+osteochondritis+dissecans+knee+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — supports earlier arthroscopic fixation (rather than prolonged non-operative trial) with bioabsorbable pins specifically in high-demand young athletes with low-grade instability, a nuance to the standard "non-operative first" algorithm for competitive athletes.
- **[Carabajal Mattar M, Kohan Fortuna Figueira S, Marangoni L, et al. — "Surgical management of unstable juvenile osteochondritis dissecans of the knee: clinical outcomes, return to sport, complications, and reoperation rates with an average follow-up of 6.5 years," J Pediatr Orthop B, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=juvenile+osteochondritis+dissecans+knee+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — mid-to-long-term (6.5-year) outcome data for surgically fixed unstable juvenile OCD, providing benchmark return-to-sport and reoperation rates for surgical counseling.
- **[Li et al. — "A Clinically Relevant Classification System for Pediatric Talocalcaneal Coalition," J Pediatr Orthop, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=tarsal+coalition+children+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — proposes a new classification for talocalcaneal coalition intended to better correlate imaging findings with surgical decision-making (resection versus arthrodesis) than prior purely anatomic descriptions.
- **[Li et al. — "Ultrasonography or Radiography for Suspected Pediatric Talocalcaneal Coalition," J Pediatr Orthop, 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=tarsal+coalition+children+AND+2023%3A2026%5Bpdat%5D&sort=date)** — compares ultrasonography against plain radiography as a lower-cost, radiation-free first-line screening step before proceeding to CT in suspected talocalcaneal coalition, particularly relevant in incompletely ossified feet.
