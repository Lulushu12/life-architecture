## Quick Reference

**Red flags mandating immediate imaging / neurosurgical-orthopedic co-management:** midline cervical or spinal tenderness after high-energy trauma; any focal neurologic deficit; priapism; incontinence; distracting injury with altered mental status/intoxication; ankylosing spondylitis or DISH with any neck trauma (treat as unstable until proven otherwise — fracture risk ~4x general population, frequently three-column, high rate of missed injury and epidural hematoma); GCS <15 with neck pain; high-risk mechanism (axial load diving, fall >1m/5 stairs, high-speed MVC, ejection, motorized recreational vehicle).

**Clearance rules — key numbers**

| Rule | High-risk criteria (any → imaging) | Low-risk criteria (all met → no imaging) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NEXUS** | — | No posterior midline C-spine tenderness; no focal neuro deficit; normal alertness; no intoxication; no painful distracting injury | Sensitivity ~99% for clinically significant injury; less specific than CCR; usable at any age including >65 |
| **Canadian C-Spine Rule (CCR)** | Age ≥65; dangerous mechanism (fall ≥3ft/5 stairs, axial load to head, high-speed MVC >100 km/h, rollover/ejection, motorized recreational vehicle, bicycle collision); paresthesias in extremities | Simple rear-end MVC, sitting position in ED, ambulatory at any time, delayed onset neck pain, no midline tenderness, AND able to actively rotate neck 45° left/right | Higher sensitivity (~99–100%) and better specificity than NEXUS in head-injured/alert trauma patients; not for GCS<15, unstable vitals, age <16, or known vertebral disease |

**Classification-to-management snapshot**

| Classification | Threshold that changes management |
|---|---|
| **TLICS** (thoracolumbar) | ≤3 = nonoperative; 4 = surgeon judgment; ≥5 = operative. Any PLC disruption or neuro deficit adds points and pushes toward surgery. |
| **SLIC** (subaxial cervical) | ≤3 = nonoperative; 4 = indeterminate; ≥5 = operative. Complete DLC disruption or facet perch/dislocation with cord compression are surgical regardless of score. |
| **Anderson–D'Alonzo** (odontoid) | Type II = unstable, highest nonunion risk → operative bias in fit patients, especially if displacement >5mm, angulation >10°, age >50, or posterior displacement. |
| **Levine-Edwards** (hangman) | Type I stable → collar; Type II/IIA and III → operative (halo often fails IIA due to distraction risk; III needs surgery for facet dislocation). |
| **Jefferson (C1 ring)** | Combined lateral mass displacement (Spence rule) >6.9mm (rule-of-Spence ~7mm surrogate for transverse ligament rupture) or ADI >3mm (adult) → transverse ligament incompetent → halo/surgery vs collar. |
| **ASIA (AIS)** | A (complete) → poorest prognosis for motor recovery; D → best; grade conversion at 72h–1yr is a core outcome measure and drives rehab intensity/prognosis counseling. |

**Treatment ladder (representative, by diagnosis)**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stable subaxial C-spine (no cord, no instability) | Rigid collar 6–8wk | Physiotherapy | Facet fracture-dislocation, SLIC ≥5, cord compression → ACDF/posterior fixation |
| Odontoid type II, young/fit | Rigid collar or halo | — | Anterior odontoid screw or posterior C1-C2 fusion if displaced, comminuted, or reducible-only-with-traction |
| Odontoid type II, elderly/frail | Rigid collar (accept fibrous nonunion) | Halo (relative contraindication >65 — high mortality/complication rate) | Posterior C1-C2 fusion if ambulatory, acceptable surgical risk, or progressive displacement |
| Hangman type I | Rigid collar/Miami-J | — | Rarely needed |
| Hangman type II/IIA | Halo (type II) — avoid traction in IIA | — | Anterior C2-C3 ACDF or posterior C2 pars/pedicle screw fixation |
| Jefferson, ligament intact | Rigid collar | Halo if ADI borderline | C1-C2 or occipitocervical fusion if transverse ligament ruptured/unstable |
| TLICS ≤3 burst fracture, neuro intact | TLSO brace 8–12wk, early mobilization | — | — |
| TLICS ≥5 / burst with PLC injury | — | — | Posterior segmental pedicle screw fixation ± decompression; short vs long-segment per bone quality/comminution |
| Chance/flexion-distraction | — | — | Posterior tension-band fixation (compression construct) — nonop only in isolated bony Chance with intact PLC and reliable follow-up |
| SCI with compression/deformity | — | — | Decompression <24h from injury (strong evidence for improved AIS conversion) |
| Central cord syndrome | — | Trial of early mobilization only in minimal cord compression + no instability | Early surgery (<24h) increasingly favored — improves ASIA motor recovery vs delayed, per AO Spine 2024 update |
| OVCF, no red flags | Analgesia, bracing, early mobilization, anti-osteoporotic therapy | — | Vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty for refractory pain, progressive collapse, or acute fracture <6–8wk with edema on MRI |

**Key numbers to memorize:**
- Rule of Spence (Jefferson): combined C1-C2 lateral mass overhang **>6.9mm** → TAL likely disrupted.
- Adult atlantodental interval (ADI) upper limit of normal: **3mm** (children: 4–5mm, growth-plate laxity).
- Odontoid type II operative-bias thresholds: displacement **>5mm**, angulation **>10°**, age **>50**, posterior displacement, comminution (IIA).
- TLICS/SLIC operative threshold: **≥5** operative, **4** judgment call, **≤3** nonoperative.
- Early SCI decompression window: **≤24 hours** (guideline-preferred); ultra-early **<12h/<8h** under active investigation for added benefit.
- SCI perfusion target: MAP **≥85–90 mmHg** for **5–7 days** post-injury.
- Ligamentotaxis (indirect fracture reduction via posterior distraction) loses effectiveness after roughly **72 hours** from injury.
- Vertebral fracture cascade risk: one OVCF raises risk of a subsequent vertebral fracture approximately **5-fold**.
- Chance fracture associated hollow-viscus injury: roughly **15–20%** of seatbelt-sign-positive patients.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Occipitocervical & upper cervical complex (C0–C2)
The **atlas (C1)** is a ring without a vertebral body; it transmits axial load through the lateral masses and articulates with the occipital condyles (flexion/extension) and C2 (rotation, ~50% of cervical rotation). The **transverse atlantal ligament (TAL)** is the primary restraint against anterior atlantoaxial translation and defines Jefferson-fracture stability. The **odontoid (dens)** is a cephalad extension of C2 stabilized by the TAL, alar ligaments, and apical ligament; its blood supply arises largely from an anastomotic ring off the vertebral artery entering at the base — a watershed zone at the waist (type II) that explains high nonunion rates. The pars interarticularis of C2 is the anatomic point of failure in hangman fractures (bilateral pars fracture, decoupling the vertebral body/dens from the posterior elements).

### Subaxial cervical spine (C3–C7)
Three-column concept (Denis, extended to cervical spine) plus the posterior ligamentous/discoligamentous complex (DLC — facet capsules, interspinous/supraspinous ligaments, ligamentum flavum, posterior anulus) determines stability. Facets are coronally oriented (~45°), permitting flexion-extension and translation injuries (unilateral/bilateral facet dislocation) more than in the thoracolumbar spine. The cervical spinal canal has generous reserve space above the cord (space available for the cord) at C3–C5, so central canal compromise here classically produces the **central cord syndrome** pattern via disproportionate injury to centrally-located cervical corticospinal fibers supplying the upper extremities.

### Thoracolumbar spine
Denis three-column model: anterior column (anterior longitudinal ligament [ALL], anterior 2/3 vertebral body/disc), middle column (posterior 1/3 body/disc, posterior longitudinal ligament [PLL]), posterior column (pedicles, facets, posterior ligamentous complex [PLC] — supraspinous/interspinous ligaments, ligamentum flavum, facet capsules). Middle-column failure is the hallmark of burst fractures (retropulsion risk); PLC failure is the hallmark of flexion-distraction (Chance) injuries and converts an otherwise stable-appearing burst fracture into an unstable three-column injury. The thoracolumbar junction (T10–L2) concentrates injury because it transitions from the rib-cage-stabilized, kyphotic thoracic spine to the mobile, lordotic lumbar spine, and because the spinal cord ends (conus medullaris, typically L1) here — injuries here can produce mixed conus/cauda patterns.

### Spinal cord injury pathophysiology
**Primary injury**: mechanical (compression, contusion, laceration, distraction/shear) at the moment of trauma. **Secondary injury cascade**: vascular injury and hemorrhage → ischemia (worsened by systemic hypotension and loss of autoregulation) → excitotoxicity (glutamate), free-radical generation, lipid peroxidation, inflammatory cell infiltration, apoptosis, and edema, evolving over hours to days and expanding the zone of injury beyond the initial mechanical insult. This time-dependent secondary cascade is the physiologic rationale for (1) maintaining MAP ≥85–90 mmHg for 5–7 days post-injury to preserve cord perfusion, and (2) early surgical decompression to arrest ongoing mechanical/ischemic injury before the secondary cascade matures.

### Central cord syndrome
Classically occurs after hyperextension injury in a patient with pre-existing cervical spondylosis/stenosis (older patient, low-energy fall) or after high-energy trauma with fracture-dislocation in a younger patient (worse prognosis subgroup). Historically attributed to a somatotopic lamination injury (upper-extremity fibers medial/central, lower-extremity fibers lateral in the corticospinal tract) — the classic Schneider concept — though contemporary imaging/pathologic work suggests the pattern may equally reflect diffuse cord injury with disproportionate white-matter (lateral corticospinal tract) involvement rather than pure somatotopic lamination. Clinically: disproportionate motor weakness in upper > lower extremities, distal > proximal, with variable sensory and bladder involvement.

### Osteoporotic bone biomechanics
Trabecular bone loss reduces vertebral body compressive strength disproportionately at the anterior column, producing wedge/biconcave/crush morphology under everyday axial loads (bending, lifting, even coughing) rather than high-energy trauma. Because the posterior elements and ligaments are usually spared, most OVCFs are mechanically stable (AO type A) — the clinical problem is pain, kyphotic deformity, and cascading fracture risk (each vertebral fracture increases risk of subsequent fracture ~5-fold), not instability.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**Primary/secondary survey (ATLS framework):** in-line cervical immobilization until cleared; assess airway (high cervical SCI risks diaphragmatic/intercostal paralysis); assess for **neurogenic shock** (hypotension + bradycardia from loss of sympathetic tone, distinct from hypovolemic shock — treat with vasopressors, not just fluids) versus **spinal shock** (transient loss of all reflex activity below the injury level, resolving over 24h–weeks; bulbocavernosus reflex return signals the end of spinal shock and that any persistent AIS A findings are more likely to represent a truly complete injury).

**Clinical clearance:** apply NEXUS or CCR (above) in alert, non-intoxicated patients without distracting injury. If criteria not met or patient cannot be clinically assessed (intubated, intoxicated, altered, distracting injury), proceed to imaging.

**Imaging algorithm:**
- **CT** (thin-cut, with sagittal/coronal reconstructions) is first-line for blunt trauma clearance in patients requiring imaging — far higher sensitivity than plain radiographs for fracture; plain films are largely obsolete for acute trauma screening in most trauma centers.
- **MRI** indicated for: any neurologic deficit not explained by CT; discordance between exam and CT; suspected ligamentous/discoligamentous injury with equivocal CT (assessing PLC/DLC integrity, especially when TLICS/SLIC scoring is borderline); cord signal characterization (edema vs hemorrhage — hemorrhage on T2*/GRE is a negative prognostic marker); pre-op planning for reduction of facet dislocations.
- **Obtunded patient with normal CT:** flexion-extension MRI or dynamic fluoroscopy to exclude ligamentous injury remains debated (EAST/multiple society guidance has shifted toward accepting a normal high-quality CT alone as sufficient to clear the obtunded patient in many protocols) — practice varies by institution; MRI within 48h is a reasonable compromise if CT is equivocal or exam cannot be obtained.
- **CT angiography (CTA) of the neck** for blunt cerebrovascular injury screening (Denver/modified Denver criteria) in cervical spine fractures involving the transverse foramen, subluxation, or C1–C3 fractures — vertebral artery injury risk.

**Neurologic examination — ASIA/ISNCSCI:** document bilateral motor (10 key muscle groups, 0–5 MRC scale each side), light touch and pinprick sensation (28 dermatomes each side), sacral sparing (deep anal pressure, voluntary anal contraction), and determine the **neurological level of injury** (most caudal level with normal motor AND sensory bilaterally), **single neurological level**, **motor level** each side, **sensory level** each side, and **zone of partial preservation** (only relevant if AIS A). Perform serial exams — grade can evolve, especially out of spinal shock.

**Diagnosis-specific evaluation pearls:**
- **Jefferson fracture**: measure combined overhang of C1 lateral masses on C2 on open-mouth odontoid view/coronal CT (rule of Spence: sum >6.9mm predicts TAL disruption); confirm on MRI if surgical decision hinges on ligament status.
- **Odontoid fracture**: assess displacement/angulation on CT; MRI for cord/ligament assessment if operative decision uncertain, or to rule out concomitant TAL injury.
- **Hangman fracture**: assess C2-C3 disc/ALL disruption and translation/angulation on CT and MRI — this differentiates Levine-Edwards types and predicts instability.
- **Central cord syndrome**: MRI mandatory to define cord edema/hemorrhage extent, degree of pre-existing stenosis, and presence of instability/fracture requiring surgical stabilization vs decompression alone.
- **OVCF**: MRI (STIR sequence) to confirm acute (marrow edema) vs chronic fracture and to identify the symptomatic level when multiple deformities coexist — essential before vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty.
- **Chance/flexion-distraction injury**: examine and document the seatbelt sign (abdominal wall ecchymosis) — a strong clinical marker for both the injury pattern and associated intra-abdominal injury; obtain abdominal/pelvic CT with IV contrast in any restrained occupant with this sign.
- **Thoracolumbar burst fracture**: measure sagittal (kyphotic) angle, percentage vertebral body height loss, and canal compromise (% of canal diameter occupied by retropulsed fragment) on CT — these feed directly into the operative-threshold discussion even when TLICS is borderline, since >50% canal compromise or >30° local kyphosis are commonly cited (though not universally validated) triggers favoring surgery in a neurologically intact patient.

**Differential and mimics:** always distinguish traumatic fracture from pathologic fracture (metastatic disease, myeloma, infection) in the elderly with "low-energy" vertebral collapse — MRI signal pattern (posterior element/pedicle involvement, epidural soft-tissue mass, abnormal signal in adjacent vertebrae) should prompt oncologic/infectious work-up rather than assuming osteoporotic etiology by default, especially in a first fracture without a clear mechanism.

## Classification

### AO Spine Subaxial Cervical Injury Classification (C3–C7)

| Component | Categories | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Morphology — A (compression)** | A0 minor/no bony injury; A1 compression fracture (single endplate); A2 split/vertical body fracture; A3 incomplete burst; A4 complete burst | A0–A1 generally nonoperative; A3/A4 evaluated with neuro status/facet injury — burst with canal compromise or neuro deficit trends operative |
| **Morphology — B (tension band)** | B1 monosegmental posterior tension band disruption (transdiscal) without perched/dislocated facets; B2 posterior/anterior tension band disruption with facet perch or subluxation; B3 hyperextension injury (bony or discoligamentous anterior tension-band disruption) | B injuries imply loss of tension-band integrity → higher instability; B2 with facet perch or B3 with cord compression favor surgery |
| **Morphology — C (translation)** | Displacement/translation in any plane (facet dislocation, fracture-dislocation) | Always considered unstable → operative reduction and fixation |
| **Facet injury (F) modifier** | F1 non-displaced fracture fragment; F2 fragment displaced into canal/foramen; F3 fracture of facet with ≥1cm displacement or >40% facet mass involvement; F4 floating lateral mass (ipsilateral pedicle + lamina fracture) | Higher F grade raises instability and often independently drives surgical fixation regardless of morphology grade |
| **Neurologic modifier (N)** | N0 intact; N1 transient deficit resolved; N2 radiculopathy; N3 incomplete SCI; N4 complete SCI; NX exam not assessable | Any neuro deficit (N2–N4) with structural compression = urgent decompression; N designation added to morphology to finalize the treatment recommendation |
| **Case-specific modifiers (M)** | M1 indeterminate DLC injury (MRI signal without confirmed instability); M2 critical disc herniation; M3 ankylosing spondylitis/DISH; M4 signs of vascular injury | M1 may tip a borderline case toward surgery; M3 (AS/DISH) mandates long-segment fixation and low threshold for surgery given inherent three-column instability |

### AO Spine Thoracolumbar Injury Classification

| Component | Categories | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Type A (compression)** | A0 minor, non-structural fracture (spinous/transverse process); A1 wedge-compression (single endplate, no posterior wall involvement); A2 split (pincer, both endplates, coronal split); A3 incomplete burst (one endplate, posterior wall fracture, no full-height involvement); A4 complete burst (both endplates, posterior wall, full-height involvement) | A0–A2 typically nonoperative (bracing); A3/A4 evaluated with neuro status and PLC — many A3/A4 with intact PLC and no deficit remain nonoperative per TLICS, but higher degree of canal compromise/kyphosis lowers surgical threshold |
| **Type B (tension band)** | B1 transosseous posterior tension band disruption (through-bone Chance); B2 posterior ligamentous tension band disruption (through-ligament/facet, often with an A-type body fracture); B3 hyperextension injury (anterior tension band failure) | B1/B2 = flexion-distraction pattern (Chance-type) — usually surgical (posterior tension-band fixation) unless purely bony with intact PLC and reliable patient for brace treatment |
| **Type C (translation)** | Displacement/dislocation in any plane, or severe multi-column disruption with rotational or shear displacement | Always unstable → operative stabilization, typically spanning fusion |
| **Neurologic modifier (N)** | N0 intact; N1 transient deficit; N2 radicular signs; N3 incomplete SCI or cauda equina (incomplete); N4 complete SCI; NX unassessable | Deficit with canal compromise = urgent decompression; drives operative decision especially in borderline A3/A4 |
| **Case-specific modifiers (M)** | M1 indeterminate PLC injury on MRI; M2 relevant comorbidities (e.g., ankylosing spondylitis) affecting treatment | M1 may push a borderline injury toward surgical stabilization; M2 (AS/DISH) → long-segment fixation, avoid closed reduction manipulation |

### TLICS — Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score

| Component | Points |
|---|---|
| **Morphology**: compression 1; burst 2; translation/rotation 3; distraction 4 | 1–4 |
| **Posterior ligamentous complex (PLC)**: intact 0; indeterminate (MRI signal only) 2; disrupted 3 | 0–3 |
| **Neurologic status**: intact 0; nerve root injury 2; complete cord/conus injury 2; incomplete cord/conus injury 3; cauda equina 3 | 0–3 |

**Total score → management**: ≤3 = **nonoperative** (brace); 4 = **surgeon/patient-specific judgment** (either operative or nonoperative reasonable); ≥5 = **operative**. A validated, widely used decision tool — though the 2023–2024 AO Spine treatment-algorithm validation studies show only moderate concordance with actual expert practice at the 4-point "gray zone," reinforcing that TLICS is a decision aid, not a mandate (see Recent Evidence).

### SLIC — Subaxial Cervical Spine Injury Classification

| Component | Points |
|---|---|
| **Morphology**: no abnormality 0; compression 1; burst +1 (=2 total); distraction (facet perch, hyperextension) 3; rotation/translation (facet dislocation, unstable teardrop/advanced flexion compression) 4 | 0–4 |
| **Discoligamentous complex (DLC)**: intact 0; indeterminate (isolated MRI signal, interspinous widening) 1; disrupted (widening of disc space, facet perch/dislocation) 2 | 0–2 |
| **Neurologic status**: intact 0; root injury 1; complete cord injury 2; incomplete cord injury 3; continuous cord compression with neuro deficit +1 | 0–4 |

**Total score → management**: ≤3 = **nonoperative** (collar); 4 = **indeterminate**, surgeon judgment; ≥5 = **operative**.

### Anderson–D'Alonzo Odontoid Fracture Classification

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Avulsion fracture of the tip of the odontoid (alar ligament avulsion) | Rare; usually stable — collar; must exclude occipitocervical dissociation |
| **II** | Fracture through the base/waist of the odontoid, above C2 body | Most common and least biologically favorable (watershed blood supply) — highest nonunion risk (up to 30–60% nonoperative in adverse-risk factors); operative bias if displacement >5mm, angulation >10°, comminution, posterior displacement, age >50, or delayed presentation |
| **IIA** | Type II with comminution at the fracture base (unstable subtype) | Poor candidate for halo/traction — high risk of distraction nonunion; favor surgical fixation |
| **III** | Fracture extends into the cancellous body of C2/superior articulating facets | Best healing potential (cancellous bone contact) — collar or halo often successful; surgery reserved for displacement/instability |

### Levine-Edwards Classification of Hangman (Traumatic Spondylolisthesis of C2) Fractures

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Bilateral pars fracture, <3mm translation, no angulation, C2-3 disc/ALL intact | Stable — rigid collar/Miami-J |
| **II** | Bilateral pars fracture with significant translation (>3mm) and/or angulation; C2-3 disc disrupted, PLL may remain intact | Unstable in flexion-extension — reducible with traction/extension → halo vest (cautious axial traction, avoid over-distraction) |
| **IIA** | Severe angulation with minimal translation; flexion-distraction mechanism, disc space opens anteriorly (hinge injury) | **Traction contraindicated** (risks catastrophic distraction through the disrupted disc) — treat with extension and compression in halo, or primary surgical fixation |
| **III** | Bilateral pars fracture with unilateral or bilateral C2-3 facet dislocation | Highly unstable — surgical reduction and fixation (posterior C2-3, or combined) mandatory |

### Jefferson Fracture (C1 Ring) — Stability Assessment
Not a formally numbered "type" system in routine use; classified descriptively by fracture pattern (unilateral or bilateral anterior/posterior arch fractures, 2-, 3-, or 4-part ring fracture) with stability determined functionally:
- **Rule of Spence**: combined lateral mass overhang on C2 >6.9mm (historically rounded to 7mm) on open-mouth/coronal CT correlates with transverse ligament rupture.
- **Direct MRI assessment** of TAL integrity is increasingly used to refine this indirect radiographic surrogate, particularly when overhang is borderline.
- **Management implication**: TAL intact (Dickman type I bony avulsion in continuity, or overhang <6.9mm) → rigid collar; TAL disrupted (Dickman type II midsubstance tear, or significant overhang) → halo immobilization or C1-C2/occipitocervical fusion, particularly with concomitant occipitocervical or C2 injury.

### ASIA Impairment Scale (AIS) — International Standards for Neurological Classification of SCI (ISNCSCI)

| Grade | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Complete** | No motor or sensory function preserved in sacral segments S4-S5 (no sacral sparing) | Worst prognosis; ~single digit % chance of meaningful motor recovery below injury level if truly complete at 72h post-spinal-shock resolution |
| **B — Sensory incomplete** | Sensory but not motor function preserved below the neurological level, including S4-S5, AND no motor function preserved more than 3 levels below the motor level on either side | |
| **C — Motor incomplete** | Motor function preserved below the neurological level, and more than half of key muscles below the neurological level have a muscle grade <3 | |
| **D — Motor incomplete** | Motor function preserved below the neurological level, and at least half of key muscles below the neurological level have a muscle grade ≥3 | Best prognosis for ambulation |
| **E — Normal** | Motor and sensory function normal in all segments, patient had a prior deficit | Used to document recovery, not baseline normal |

The exam should be repeated after resolution of spinal shock (bulbocavernosus reflex return) since a true "AIS A" designation immediately post-injury may reflect spinal shock rather than complete anatomic injury. **Zone of partial preservation (ZPP)** is recorded only for AIS A injuries — the number of dermatomes/myotomes caudal to the neurological level with some preserved function.

## Treatment

### Non-operative
- **Rigid cervical collar (Miami-J / Aspen)**: mainstay for stable subaxial injuries (SLIC ≤3, AO type A0/A1/N0), odontoid type I/III, Jefferson fractures with intact TAL, hangman type I. Typical duration 6–8 weeks with interval radiographs (flexion-extension films at 6–12 weeks once acute pain resolves, to confirm no delayed instability) before progressing out of the collar.
- **Halo vest immobilization**: historically used for unstable upper cervical injuries (displaced odontoid II in a patient unfit for surgery, Jefferson with TAL disruption, hangman type II) in patients able to tolerate it. Use has declined sharply, especially in patients >65, given pin-site infection, pneumonia risk, dysphagia, and notably **elevated mortality in elderly patients treated with halo** (aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause) — many centers now avoid halo in patients over ~65–70 and favor either collar-with-accepted-nonunion or surgical fixation instead.
- **TLSO/thoracolumbar orthosis (custom-molded or off-the-shelf hyperextension brace)**: for TLICS ≤3 thoracolumbar injuries (compression and most incomplete burst fractures with intact PLC and no neuro deficit). Typical duration 8–12 weeks, worn during upright activity, early mobilization encouraged (bed rest is not protective and increases complication rates). Kyphosis progression and interval imaging monitored at 2, 6, and 12 weeks.
- **OVCF conservative care**: analgesia (acetaminophen first-line; NSAIDs used cautiously given renal/GI risk in elderly; short-course opioids if needed; calcitonin has modest analgesic effect in the acute phase), early mobilization, extension bracing (soft or semi-rigid) for comfort, and — critically — initiation/optimization of osteoporosis therapy (calcium/vitamin D, bisphosphonate or anabolic agent per bone density and fracture risk) since the fracture itself is a sentinel event for future fracture risk. Most OVCFs (>80%) achieve satisfactory pain resolution with conservative care over 6–12 weeks.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures
- **Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP)**: cement injection without balloon creation of a cavity. Indications: persistent, function-limiting pain from an acute (typically <6–8 weeks, MRI-edema-confirmed) OVCF refractory to 2–4 weeks of conservative therapy, or in patients who cannot tolerate bed rest/bracing (e.g., cognitive impairment, high fall risk). Randomized trial evidence has been genuinely mixed: the 2009 sham-controlled trials (Buchbinder, Kallmes/INVEST) showed no benefit over sham; later, better-selected trials with acute, MRI-confirmed fractures (VAPOUR 2016) showed clinically meaningful early pain relief versus sham in a subgroup with acute severe pain. Contemporary network meta-analyses (2025) continue to favor vertebral augmentation (vertebroplasty or kyphoplasty) over non-surgical management for pain, disability (ODI), and quality of life (EQ-5D) in appropriately selected acute OVCF, while flagging adjacent-level fracture risk as a real trade-off.
- **Percutaneous balloon kyphoplasty (PKP)**: balloon tamp creates a cavity before cement injection, aiming to restore vertebral height/reduce kyphosis and lower extravasation risk versus PVP (lower cement-leakage rate, though clinical significance of leakage is usually low regardless of technique). Same core indications as PVP; typically preferred when height restoration/kyphosis correction is a clinical goal. A 2025 propensity-matched comparative analysis found a **higher one-year risk of subsequent (often adjacent-level) vertebral fracture after kyphoplasty compared with vertebroplasty**, an important counseling point when choosing between the two.
- **Technical pearls**: bipedicular or unilateral (extrapedicular/transpedicular) approach under biplanar fluoroscopy or CT guidance; cement viscosity control and slow injection under continuous lateral fluoroscopy are the key maneuvers to minimize leakage into the canal, disc space, or venous plexus; avoid injection if cement is too liquid or if venous filling is seen. Newer unilateral dual-plane puncture techniques (2025 literature) report reduced re-collapse rates by improving cement fill pattern.
- **Facet/medial branch blocks and epidural steroid injections** have no established role in acute spine trauma management; they are occasionally used for post-traumatic chronic axial pain once fracture healing/stability is confirmed, analogous to degenerative spine practice.

### Operative

#### Epidemiology and mechanism overview (context for operative decision-making)
Cervical spine injury accounts for roughly 2–4% of blunt trauma patients overall, with a bimodal age distribution: young adults (high-energy MVC, diving, sports) and older adults (ground-level falls, often with pre-existing spondylosis/stenosis or osteoporosis, lower-energy mechanism but disproportionately severe injury for the energy involved). Approximately half of cervical fractures involve the subaxial spine, with C2 injuries (odontoid + hangman) representing the single most common upper cervical fracture group in elderly falls. Thoracolumbar fractures cluster at the T10-L2 junction (~50-60% of all thoracolumbar fractures) for the biomechanical reasons described above. This bimodal, mechanism-dependent epidemiology is why the same radiographic pattern (e.g., a C2 fracture) is worked up and treated differently in a 25-year-old after a motorcycle crash versus an 80-year-old after a fall from standing — bone quality, frailty, comorbidity burden, and baseline functional status materially change the risk-benefit calculus of surgery even when the AO/SLIC/TLICS score is identical.

#### Odontoid fracture (type II, operative candidates)
- *Anterior odontoid screw fixation*: single or double cannulated screw across the fracture into the dens apex; preserves C1-C2 rotation. Best suited to fractures with a favorable fracture line (anteroinferior-to-posterosuperior obliquity, i.e., type IIA/horizontal-oblique patterns), reducible without a large fragment gap, and in patients with a body habitus/short neck allowing an adequate trajectory (barrel chest and severe kyphosis are relative contraindications). Requires intact posterior fragment/dens continuity — not suitable for comminuted (IIA) fractures or with reverse-oblique fracture lines.
- *Posterior C1-C2 fusion* (Harms/Goel-type C1 lateral mass–C2 pedicle/pars/translaminar screw-rod construct, or Magerl transarticular screws): preferred when anterior screw is not feasible (comminution, reverse obliquity, associated TAL injury, poor anterior trajectory), or as primary treatment in many elderly patients given lower nonunion risk with rigid segmental fixation and no dependence on precise fracture-line geometry. Trade-off: sacrifices ~50% of cervical rotation.
- *Elderly-specific decision-making*: multiple 2023–2025 series confirm nonoperative management of displaced type II fractures in octogenarians carries high (30–60%) fibrous nonunion rates but these are frequently painless and functionally tolerated; conversely, surgery (especially posterior fusion) achieves substantially higher fusion rates but carries real perioperative morbidity/mortality in frail patients. Shared decision-making incorporating frailty, baseline mobility, and patient goals is essential — current European survey data show a split practice pattern even among spine specialists, underscoring this remains a judgment call rather than an algorithmic one.

#### Hangman fracture (type II/IIA/III)
- Halo vest is often sufficient for reducible type II injuries; type IIA specifically should not be treated with axial traction.
- Surgical options: **anterior C2-3 discectomy and fusion (ACDF)** for injuries with significant disc/ALL disruption and translation, providing direct restoration of the C2-3 segment; **posterior C2 pars/pedicle screw fixation** (direct pars screw fixation of the fracture, or C1-C3 posterior instrumented fusion) for type III with facet dislocation or when posterior element integrity allows direct fracture fixation while preserving C2-3 motion.

#### Jefferson fracture with TAL disruption
- **C1-C2 posterior fusion** (Goel-Harms technique — C1 lateral mass and C2 pedicle/pars screws with rod, often supplemented with structural bone graft) is the workhorse when TAL is disrupted and the fracture pattern allows C1 lateral mass screw placement.
- **Occipitocervical fusion** reserved for fractures precluding safe C1 screw placement (severely comminuted lateral mass) or concomitant occipital condyle/occipitocervical dissociation.
- Isolated posterior arch fractures with intact anterior arch and TAL rarely need surgery.

#### Subaxial cervical fracture-dislocation / facet injury (SLIC ≥5, AO type C or B2/B3 with N deficit)
- *Closed reduction*: awake closed reduction with serial neurologic exams under fluoroscopy (Gardner-Wells tongs/traction) is favored by many centers for unifacet/bifacet dislocation in an awake, cooperative patient, historically without pre-reduction MRI when the patient can be reliably examined during traction (though practice is trending toward pre-reduction MRI when readily available, particularly if obtunded).
- *Anterior approach* (ACDF with plate): direct discectomy, decompression of retropulsed disc/bone, and fixation; preferred when the primary pathology is anterior column/disc compression, and provides direct access for corpectomy if a comminuted body fracture (AO A3/A4) is present.
- *Posterior approach* (lateral mass screw-rod fixation): preferred for predominantly posterior tension-band/facet injuries (B1/B2, unilateral/bilateral facet dislocation), allows direct facet reduction and is more robust for pure ligamentous instability.
- *Combined anterior-posterior fixation*: for highly unstable, multi-column injuries (AO type C, three-column disruption) or when anterior-only fixation is judged insufficient (e.g., comminuted body with posterior ligamentous disruption).
- *Ankylosing spondylitis/DISH (M3/M2 modifier)*: treat as long, rigid-lever-arm fractures — low threshold for imaging the entire spine (high rate of non-contiguous fractures), avoid manual reduction/extension maneuvers that can worsen displacement, and use long-segment posterior fixation spanning well above/below the fracture given the inherently unstable, brittle, poorly vascularized ankylosed bone.

#### Thoracolumbar burst fracture (TLICS ≥5, or ≥4 with surgeon judgment favoring surgery)
- *Posterior short-segment pedicle screw fixation* (one level above/below): workhorse for most burst fractures without severe comminution; often includes fixation at the fractured level itself ("index-level screw") to improve construct stiffness and reduce hardware failure/kyphosis recurrence versus classic short-segment constructs without index-level screws.
- *Posterior long-segment fixation* (2 levels above/below): reserved for severe comminution, osteoporotic bone, or when index-level screw placement is not feasible.
- *Decompression*: indirect decompression via ligamentotaxis (distraction/reduction through posterior instrumentation) is often sufficient for fractures without complete canal-blocking retropulsion; direct decompression (laminectomy, transpedicular corpectomy, or combined anterior corpectomy/strut graft or cage) is added when significant canal compromise persists with a neurologic deficit or when ligamentotaxis is judged unlikely to adequately clear the canal (fracture >72h old — ligamentotaxis efficacy drops sharply after the acute window).
- *Combined anterior-posterior reconstruction*: for severe (AO A4/C) fractures with major anterior column loss requiring structural reconstruction (corpectomy + cage/strut graft) in addition to posterior stabilization, particularly in younger patients where restoring anterior load-sharing and sagittal alignment is prioritized.
- *Minimally invasive percutaneous pedicle screw fixation* is increasingly used for TLICS 4–5 injuries without need for direct decompression, reducing blood loss/muscle injury versus open approaches with comparable fusion/alignment outcomes in appropriately selected patients.

#### Chance/flexion-distraction injury
- Purely bony (through-vertebral-body/lamina/spinous process) Chance fractures with an intact PLC on MRI can be treated nonoperatively in a hyperextension TLSO with close follow-up in a reliable patient — but the threshold for surgery is low because compliance/follow-up failure risks progressive kyphosis.
- Ligamentous or mixed bony-ligamentous flexion-distraction injuries (PLC disrupted) require **posterior segmental compression fixation** (pedicle screw-rod construct placed in compression across the injured segment) — a fusion-preserving alternative in patients without body-fracture comminution; the tension-band failure pattern makes compression instrumentation biomechanically ideal.
- Always screen for concomitant intra-abdominal (mesenteric, bowel/duodenal) injury — the seatbelt mechanism causing a Chance fracture has a well-described association with hollow viscus injury (up to 15-20% in some series); low threshold for abdominal CT/surgical consultation.

#### Spinal cord injury — timing and technique
- **Early surgical decompression (≤24 hours from injury)** is now the guideline-preferred approach across injury levels (cervical, thoracic, thoracolumbar) for patients with acute traumatic SCI and a compressive lesion amenable to decompression, per the updated AO Spine/GRADE-based clinical practice guideline (Fehlings et al., 2024 update to the 2017 guideline) — early surgery more than doubles the odds of ≥2-grade AIS improvement compared with later surgery (moderate-quality evidence). This applies specifically to central cord syndrome as well (see below), reversing older teaching that favored delayed surgery in degenerative-stenosis-related central cord injury.
- **Hemodynamic management**: maintain MAP ≥85–90 mmHg for the first 5–7 days post-injury to optimize spinal cord perfusion pressure, per the same guideline series; avoid/correct hypotension aggressively in the acute resuscitation phase.
- **High-dose methylprednisolone**: no longer recommended — current guidelines (including the 2024 AO Spine update) do not support steroid administration for acute SCI given an unfavorable risk-benefit profile (infection, GI hemorrhage, hyperglycemia) without consistent functional benefit; this is now standard of care in most North American and European trauma centers, a shift from 1990s-2000s NASCIS-era practice.
- **Technique** follows the level- and morphology-specific approaches above (anterior corpectomy/decompression, posterior laminectomy/instrumented fusion, combined), selected to achieve complete decompression of the compressive lesion (bone, disc, hematoma) while providing sufficient stability for early mobilization.

#### Central cord syndrome (operative)
- Historically managed with a trial of nonoperative care (collar, physiotherapy) reserving surgery for failure to improve, based on older literature suggesting comparable long-term outcomes with delayed surgery. Contemporary evidence and the 2024 AO Spine guideline update now favor **early surgery (within 24 hours)** for patients with central cord syndrome and radiographic cord compression, showing improved motor recovery and shorter hospital stay compared with delayed decompression — the timing recommendation has converged with that for SCI overall. Patients with minimal/no structural compression and no instability (pure "central cord physiology" without a compressive lesion) may still be reasonably managed nonoperatively with close monitoring.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Phase 1 — Acute (0–2 weeks):**
- Bracing/orthosis fitted (collar or TLSO) or post-operative wound care and pain control.
- SCI patients: ICU/step-down monitoring for neurogenic shock, autonomic dysreflexia risk (injuries T6 and above), respiratory support (especially C3-C5 — diaphragmatic function), DVT prophylaxis (mechanical + pharmacologic once hemostasis confirmed, typically started 24–72h post-injury/surgery per institutional protocol and bleeding risk), bowel/bladder program initiation (intermittent catheterization preferred over indwelling when feasible), pressure-injury prevention (turning schedule, specialized mattress) given profoundly elevated skin-breakdown risk with sensory loss.
- Early mobilization out of bed as soon as the construct/orthosis allows (typically within 24–72h for stable fixation or braced injuries) — bed rest is actively avoided given pneumonia, DVT, and deconditioning risk; this applies equally to elderly OVCF patients where prolonged bed rest is a major driver of complications (pneumonia, delirium, further bone loss).
- Physiotherapy: gentle ROM to uninvolved joints, respiratory physiotherapy (incentive spirometry, assisted coughing in cervical SCI), positioning/transfer training.

**Phase 2 — Subacute, protected loading (2–12 weeks):**
- Non-operative fractures: continue brace/collar for the prescribed duration (6–8wk cervical, 8–12wk TLSO); progressive out-of-bed tolerance, gait training, core/paraspinal isometric activation avoiding flexion/rotation stress on the injury per the specific fracture pattern.
- Post-operative fixation: typically allowed to mobilize with brace support per surgeon protocol; formal ROM restrictions (avoid combined flexion-rotation for facet injuries, avoid extension in extension-type injuries) until early fusion consolidation (~6–8 weeks).
- SCI-specific rehabilitation (inpatient rehabilitation facility, typically initiated once medically stable, often 1–3 weeks post-injury): task-specific strengthening of preserved musculature, spasticity management (early positioning/stretching, pharmacologic — baclofen, tizanidine — as needed), neurogenic bowel/bladder program optimization, wheelchair skills/seating assessment, psychological support/adjustment counseling, family/caregiver training.
- Interval imaging (flexion-extension radiographs or CT) at 6–12 weeks to confirm healing/stability before brace weaning in non-operative cases.

**Phase 3 — Progressive loading and functional restoration (3–6 months):**
- Brace weaning once radiographic union/stability confirmed; progressive resistance exercise, dynamic core stabilization, proprioceptive/balance training.
- SCI patients: continued outpatient or community-based rehabilitation, functional electrical stimulation where available, gait training with orthoses for incomplete injuries with lower-extremity motor potential (AIS C/D), ongoing spasticity and neuropathic pain management, driving and vocational assessment.
- OVCF patients: osteoporosis therapy reassessment (DEXA follow-up, medication adherence), fall-risk assessment/modification, balance and posture (extensor-strengthening) programs shown to reduce subsequent fracture risk.

**Return-to-work criteria:** radiographic union or stable fixation confirmed; pain controlled without escalating analgesic requirement; functional capacity (lifting, sitting tolerance, or job-specific demands) matched to occupational requirements — sedentary work often resumable by 6–8 weeks for stable, non-operative injuries, while heavy manual labor typically requires 3–6 months and formal work-conditioning/functional capacity evaluation, longer if fusion levels are extensive or if neurologic deficit persists.

**Return-to-sport / high-demand activity criteria:** solid radiographic fusion or confirmed bony union (typically ≥4–6 months for surgical fusion constructs); full, pain-free ROM; normalized strength (≥90% of contralateral/baseline on dynamometry where applicable); clearance imaging showing no hardware failure or progressive deformity; for cervical spine injuries, individualized clearance for contact/collision sports is required and remains controversial even after solid single-level fusion — most spine surgeons remain conservative about clearing patients for high-axial-load contact sports after cervical fracture-fixation, particularly involving C1-C2.

**Milestone summary table**

| Timepoint | Non-operative stable fracture | Operative fixation | SCI (any AIS grade) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–72h | Brace fitting, mobilize out of bed as tolerated | Wound care, mobilize per surgeon protocol (often POD1–2) | MAP goal 85–90mmHg, ICU/step-down, bowel/bladder program start |
| 2 weeks | Wean analgesia, gait training | Suture/staple removal, brace-protected ambulation | Transfer to inpatient rehab if medically stable |
| 6–8 weeks | Interval imaging; wean collar (cervical) | Interval imaging; begin gentle active ROM outside fixed plane | Continue task-specific strengthening, spasticity control |
| 12 weeks | Wean TLSO if union confirmed; begin strengthening | Progress to resistance exercise if fusion trending | Outpatient/community rehab transition, seating/wheelchair finalization |
| 3–6 months | Return to work per demand level; sport clearance individualized | Fusion assessment; return-to-work/sport if solid fusion | Continued FES/gait training if AIS C/D; long-term equipment and vocational planning |

## Complications

**Approximate complication rates (for counseling — ranges reflect study heterogeneity):**

| Complication | Approximate rate | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Odontoid type II nonunion (nonoperative, elderly) | 30–60% | Often stable fibrous nonunion, frequently asymptomatic |
| Halo vest pin-site infection/loosening | 20–30% | Higher in elderly, poor bone quality |
| Halo vest mortality (age >65) | Substantially elevated vs collar/surgery (exact rate series-dependent) | Aspiration pneumonia principal driver — key reason halo use has declined in elderly |
| VTE without prophylaxis after acute SCI | 40–100% (historic, no prophylaxis) | Near-universal indication for pharmacologic prophylaxis once safe |
| Adjacent-level fracture after vertebral augmentation | Meaningfully elevated vs nonoperative, higher after kyphoplasty than vertebroplasty in 2025 comparative data | Counseling point when choosing PVP vs PKP |
| Cement leakage (vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty) | Common radiographically (up to ~20–30% in some series) | Usually asymptomatic; rare symptomatic leak into canal/foramen |
| Hollow viscus/intra-abdominal injury with Chance fracture (seatbelt mechanism) | ~15–20% | Mandates abdominal CT/surgical consultation |
| Missed spine fracture in ankylosing spondylitis/DISH | Disproportionately high relative to general trauma population | Whole-spine imaging threshold should be low |

- **Odontoid nonunion**: reported up to 30–60% with nonoperative management of displaced type II fractures in older adults, most often fibrous/stable and asymptomatic, but can produce late myelopathy or instability requiring delayed fusion.
- **Halo vest complications**: pin-site infection/loosening (up to 20-30% in some series), dysphagia, aspiration pneumonia — a major driver of the well-documented elevated mortality when halo is used in patients >65-70.
- **Post-traumatic kyphosis / hardware failure**: after short-segment thoracolumbar fixation without index-level screws, especially in comminuted (A4) or osteoporotic fractures — a key reason for adding index-level screws or converting to long-segment constructs in comminuted/osteoporotic bone.
- **Adjacent segment fracture/degeneration**: after both fusion constructs and vertebral augmentation (elevated adjacent-level fracture risk post-kyphoplasty/vertebroplasty, higher reported after kyphoplasty in recent comparative data — see Recent Evidence).
- **Cement leakage (vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty)**: into the disc space, paravertebral veins, or canal; usually asymptomatic but can rarely cause radiculopathy, pulmonary cement embolism, or (rarely) cord compression — minimized with controlled, viscous cement injection under continuous fluoroscopy.
- **Respiratory complications in cervical/high-thoracic SCI**: pneumonia, atelectasis, ventilator dependence (C3 and above) — leading cause of early mortality after cervical SCI.
- **Venous thromboembolism**: markedly elevated risk in SCI (loss of muscle pump, immobility, hypercoagulable state) — DVT incidence historically 40-100% without prophylaxis; pharmacologic prophylaxis is standard once surgical hemostasis allows.
- **Autonomic dysreflexia**: in injuries T6 and above, a life-threatening hypertensive emergency triggered by noxious stimuli below the injury level (bladder distension, fecal impaction) — recognize and treat promptly (remove stimulus, sit patient upright, treat hypertension) to prevent stroke/seizure.
- **Pressure injuries**: high risk with sensory loss/immobility — proactive turning, specialized surfaces, and skin checks are essential rehab-phase tasks.
- **Neuropathic pain and spasticity**: common chronic sequelae of SCI, managed pharmacologically (gabapentinoids, baclofen) and with physical modalities.
- **Syringomyelia (post-traumatic syrinx)**: delayed neurologic deterioration months to years after SCI — requires MRI surveillance if new ascending deficit/pain develops; may require syrinx shunting or decompression of an underlying tethering/stenotic lesion.
- **Missed injury in ankylosing spondylitis/DISH**: disproportionately high rate of missed or under-treated fractures and epidural hematoma given a stiff, brittle spine and atypical mechanism (low-energy falls); vigilance and whole-spine imaging are essential.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **AO Spine / GRADE clinical practice guideline update on timing of decompression in acute SCI (2024)** — the updated guideline strengthens the recommendation for early surgical decompression (≤24 hours from injury) as the preferred approach for adult patients with acute traumatic SCI regardless of injury level, and extends this recommendation explicitly to **central cord syndrome**, moving away from the older practice of delayed surgery in that subgroup. It also addresses hemodynamic targets (MAP ≥85–90 mmHg for 5–7 days) and intraoperative SCI prevention/management. [Tetreault et al., introduction/rationale/scope, Global Spine Journal 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21925682231183969); [PubMed summary of decompression-timing recommendations](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38526922/); [Clinical Recommendations for the Acute Care of Patients With Traumatic SCI, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12693672/).
- **Narrative review of surgical timing evidence after SCI (2025)** — synthesizes the shift from the historic 24-hour benchmark toward investigation of ultra-early (<12h, even <8h/<5h) decompression, summarizing signal for further neurological benefit with earlier intervention but persistent logistical/system barriers to achieving it broadly. [Surgical Timing After Spinal Cord Injury: A Narrative Review, Korean J Neurotrauma 2025](https://kjnt.org/DOIx.php?id=10.13004%2Fkjnt.2025.21.e32).
- **Ultra-early (≤8h) decompression meta-analysis for thoracolumbar SCI (2023)** — systematic review/meta-analysis suggesting further neurological benefit from decompression within 8 hours compared with the standard 24-hour window, though based on heterogeneous, mostly non-randomized data; reinforces that "earlier is better" within feasible limits rather than 24h being a hard biological cutoff. [PMC10628804](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10628804/).
- **AO Spine Thoracolumbar Injury Classification treatment-algorithm validation study (2023, Spine)** — expert-panel review of 183 thoracolumbar burst fracture cases found the AO Spine algorithm's treatment recommendation aligned with actual contemporary expert surgical decision-making in the majority of cases, but concordance was notably lower in the "gray zone" injuries, prompting a published Letter to the Editor debate on whether the algorithm should be treated as prescriptive. Practical takeaway: use TLICS/AO algorithm as a structured decision aid, not a substitute for individualized judgment, especially at intermediate scores. [Validation study, Spine Journal (Ovid)](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/spinejournal/fulltext/10.1097/brs.0000000000004693~validation-of-the-ao-spine-thoracolumbar-injury); [Letter to the Editor, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37621229/); [Full-text discussion, PMC10867534](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10867534/).
- **ESTES (European Society for Trauma and Emergency Surgery) recommendation on thoracolumbar spine fractures (January 2023)** — European trauma-surgery consensus statement on evaluation and management of thoracolumbar fractures, complementing AO Spine/TLICS with a broader emergency-surgery perspective relevant to European practice patterns. [ESTES recommendation, PMC11458676](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11458676/).
- **AO Surgery Reference upper cervical injury classification update (May 2024)** — the occipitocervical trauma module was refreshed with a new upper cervical injury classification system intended to standardize description and treatment decision-making for C0-C2 injuries (complementing, not replacing, Anderson-D'Alonzo and Levine-Edwards for odontoid/hangman specifically). [AO Foundation announcement, June 2024](https://www.aofoundation.org/spine/about-aospine/news/2024/2024_06_aosr_upper-cervical-classification); [AO Spine Classification Systems overview](https://www.aofoundation.org/spine/clinical-library-and-tools/aospine-classification-systems).
- **Meta-analysis of operative vs nonoperative management of geriatric type II odontoid fractures, J Neurosurg Spine (2023)** — pooled analysis (20 comparative studies, n=8,296) found significantly lower nonunion rates with surgical fixation versus nonoperative management in patients >65, reinforcing an operative bias in fit elderly patients while confirming nonoperative management remains reasonable for frail patients given comparable functional/mortality outcomes in several cohorts. [Nonoperative versus operative management of type II odontoid fracture in older adults, PubMed 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37877937/); [Full text, J Neurosurg Spine](https://thejns.org/spine/view/journals/j-neurosurg-spine/40/1/article-p45.xml).
- **2025 systematic review/meta-analysis of comparative studies on operative vs nonoperative type II odontoid management in the elderly** — updated pooled analysis reaffirming higher fusion/lower nonunion rates with surgery, while emphasizing displacement, comorbidity burden, and age as the dominant decision-making variables rather than age alone. [ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0303846725004482).
- **European Association of Neurosurgical Societies (EANS) Spine Section survey on geriatric type II odontoid fracture treatment practices (2025)** — multinational survey confirming persistent practice variation: roughly 79% of respondents favor conservative treatment for non-displaced fractures in patients 70–80y (84% for 80–90y), highlighting that current European specialist practice remains individualized rather than protocol-driven. [PMC12210293](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12210293/); [ScienceDirect version](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772529425001146).
- **German Society for Orthopaedics and Trauma (DGOU) expert-consensus treatment algorithm for geriatric odontoid fractures (Global Spine Journal, 2023)** — a structured, evidence-plus-consensus algorithm from a major European trauma society explicitly incorporating frailty and fracture morphology into the operative/nonoperative decision for elderly type II odontoid fractures, directly relevant to European (including Romanian) practice patterns. [Osterhoff et al., Global Spine Journal 2023](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21925682231157316).
- **Network meta-analysis of vertebral augmentation vs nonsurgical management for OVCF (2025)** — pooled analysis of 46 studies (23 RCTs + 23 cohorts, n=5,660) comparing third-generation vertebral augmentation, kyphoplasty, vertebroplasty, and nonsurgical management found augmentation techniques (kyphoplasty/vertebroplasty) superior to nonsurgical management for pain (VAS), disability (ODI), and quality of life (EQ-5D), with augmentation trade-offs in adjacent-vertebral-fracture and cement-leakage rates. [Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13018-025-06233-w); [PMC12398152](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12398152/).
- **Propensity-matched comparison of long-term outcomes, vertebroplasty vs kyphoplasty (2025)** — found a higher one-year risk of subsequent (often adjacent-level) vertebral fracture after kyphoplasty compared with vertebroplasty, an important consideration when counseling patients on which augmentation technique to choose. [PMC12688411](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12688411/).
- **Unilateral dual-plane puncture percutaneous vertebroplasty technique study (2025)** — a refined unilateral puncture/cement-delivery technique reported reduced vertebral re-collapse rates versus conventional technique, reflecting ongoing technique-level refinement of augmentation procedures. [PMC12050179](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12050179/).
- **Updated evidence-based clinical guideline for diagnosis and treatment of adults with OVCF (Spine Journal, 2025 guideline summary)** — a contemporary guideline summary/update on OVCF diagnosis and treatment, building on the North American Spine Society's evidence-based framework and incorporating newer augmentation trial data. [Spine Journal guideline summary, 2025](https://www.thespinejournalonline.com/article/S1529-9430(25)00066-X/abstract); [NASS OVCF guideline document, spine.org](https://www.spine.org/Portals/0/assets/downloads/ResearchClinicalCare/Guidelines/Osteoporotic-Vertebral-Compression-Fractures.pdf).
