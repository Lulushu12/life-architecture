## Quick Reference


### Treatment ladders

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| **AIS** | Observation <20-25°, Schroth/SEAS PT | Bracing 20-25° to 40-45° with growth remaining (Risser 0-2) | Cobb >45-50° at skeletal maturity, or documented progression >5°/6mo despite bracing |
| **Adult spinal deformity** | Core PT, weight/bone-health optimization, NSAIDs, activity modification | Bracing as symptomatic bridge (limited role), epidural injection for concomitant stenosis pain | Refractory pain/disability with SRS-Schwab ++ modifiers, progressive neuro deficit, curve progression |
| **Scheuermann kyphosis** | Observation <50°, activity modification | Extension bracing (Milwaukee/TLSO) for flexible 50-70° curves with growth remaining | Rigid kyphosis >70-75°, pain, neuro symptoms, cardiopulmonary compromise |
| **Spondylodiscitis** | Empiric then culture-directed IV antibiotics 6-8wk, TLSO bracing | Percutaneous drainage of paraspinal/psoas collection | Neuro deficit, instability, epidural abscess with mass effect, failed medical therapy, sepsis despite antibiotics |
| **Spinal epidural abscess** | Selected non-op (no deficit, small abscess, close MRI/exam monitoring) + antibiotics | Percutaneous drainage in select cases | Any new/progressive neurologic deficit = emergent decompression |
| **Metastatic spine disease** | Systemic therapy, bone-modifying agents, analgesia, conventional EBRT if radiosensitive/low ESCC | Vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty for painful VCF without instability/high-grade ESCC | High-grade ESCC + radioresistant histology → separation surgery + SBRT; SINS 13-18 → stabilization |
| **Primary spine tumors (malignant)** | N/A (surgery is primary modality for most) | Denosumab downstaging (GCT), neoadjuvant chemo (Ewing/osteosarcoma) | En bloc resection with wide margins (chordoma, chondrosarcoma); adjuvant proton/high-dose RT for close margins |

### Red flags (any cluster)

- **New bowel/bladder dysfunction or progressive motor deficit** with a spinal infection or tumor on imaging — treat as an emergency, image and decompress without delay.
- **Fever + spine pain + escalating neurologic exam** — spinal epidural abscess until proven otherwise; do not wait for full triad (present together in <15%).
- **Night pain, weight loss, or pain unresponsive to rest** in a patient with a scoliotic curve — think tumor/infection, not idiopathic deformity, especially if the curve is left thoracic, painful, or rapidly progressive.
- **Mechanical (position-dependent) pain over a known bony metastasis** — treat as SINS-unstable until scored; mechanical instability is not a radiation-fixable problem.
- **CRP that fails to trend down after 3-4 weeks of appropriate antibiotics** in spondylodiscitis — reimage, reconsider organism/source control.

### Practice notes for European/Romanian context

- **Brucella and TB spondylitis** are meaningfully more prevalent in Romania and the broader Balkan/Mediterranean region than in most US series — include Brucella serology and TB-specific workup (interferon-gamma release assay, AFB culture, biopsy histology for caseating granulomas) in the differential for any atypical or slow-indolent spondylodiscitis, especially with a large paraspinal/psoas abscess and relatively preserved disc space.
- **EOS low-dose biplanar imaging** availability varies by center; where unavailable, minimize cumulative radiation in AIS follow-up by spacing conventional radiographs to the clinically necessary interval and using scoliometer screening to triage who needs a film at all.
- Terminology and thresholds in this monograph follow **SRS/AO Spine** convention (dominant in both European and US deformity literature); **NICE** and **BOAST** guidance is more relevant to acute infection pathways (sepsis recognition, time-to-imaging standards) than to elective deformity correction, where SRS/AO Spine and ESSKA/ESVS vascular-access guidance (for large vessel-adjacent anterior approaches) are the more applicable bodies.
- For metastatic disease, **NCCN** (US) and **ESMO** (Europe) supportive-care and bone-health guidelines both endorse bone-modifying agents and multidisciplinary tumor-board review before committing to a surgical vs. radiation-only pathway — practically interchangeable in their bottom-line recommendations for spine surgeons, so either can be cited depending on the audience.

### Key numbers

- BrAIST bracing benefit: treatment success (curve <50° at skeletal maturity) in **72% braced vs 48% observed**; effect dose-dependent (>12.9h/day wear = 90%+ success).
- AIS fusion threshold: **>45-50° Cobb** at/near skeletal maturity (thoracic); lower threshold (~40°) often used for lumbar curves given higher adult progression risk.
- Adult deformity sagittal targets: **PI-LL within ~10° (age-adjusted), SVA <4-5cm, PT <20-25°.**
- Spondylodiscitis antibiotic duration: **6 weeks non-inferior to 12 weeks** for most pyogenic cases (Bernard et al., Lancet 2015) — extend for undrained abscess, endocarditis, or slow responders.
- SEA time-to-decompression: neurologic recovery drops sharply once a complete deficit has been present **>24-36 hours**.
- Separation surgery + postoperative SBRT: **>85-90% one-year local control** for high-grade ESCC, regardless of histologic radiosensitivity.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Spinal deformity (AIS, ASD, Scheuermann)

**AIS** is a three-dimensional deformity (coronal curvature + sagittal plane change + axial vertebral rotation), not a purely coronal problem — apical vertebral rotation is what produces the rib prominence on forward bend. Etiology remains multifactorial (genetic predisposition — multiple candidate loci, neurohormonal/melatonin-signaling theories, asymmetric growth plate loading, connective tissue and platelet abnormalities proposed) without a single unifying cause. Prevalence of Cobb ≥10° is ~2-3% of adolescents; the female:male ratio rises steeply with curve magnitude (approaching 10:1 for curves reaching treatment thresholds), and progression risk tracks closely with remaining growth (peak height velocity), not chronologic age.

**Adult spinal deformity (ASD)** is either (a) progression of an unfused idiopathic curve into adulthood or (b) *de novo* degenerative scoliosis arising from asymmetric disc and facet degeneration in a previously straight spine. The dominant driver of adult disability is **loss of lumbar lordosis** from disc space collapse and facet arthropathy, producing a forward-shifted global sagittal axis. The body compensates (pelvic retroversion → increased pelvic tilt, hip extension, knee flexion, thoracic hypokyphosis) until compensatory reserve is exhausted, at which point positive sagittal vertical axis (SVA) becomes clinically apparent. HRQOL correlates more strongly with **sagittal** malalignment than with absolute coronal Cobb angle — this is the single most important conceptual shift from pediatric to adult deformity surgery.

**Scheuermann kyphosis** is a rigid, structural thoracic hyperkyphosis defined by anterior wedging ≥5° at three or more consecutive apical vertebrae, endplate irregularity, Schmorl's nodes, and disc space narrowing. Etiology is debated (mechanical/growth-plate injury theory, relative osteopenia theory); an atypical thoracolumbar variant exists with a lower apex and different bracing/surgical thresholds.

### Spinal infection

Vertebral osteomyelitis/spondylodiscitis is most commonly **hematogenous**, seeding the well-vascularized vertebral body metaphyseal end-arteries (Batson's valveless venous plexus is the classic route for pelvic/urinary sources) before spreading to the adjacent disc and contiguous vertebra — the disc is relatively avascular in adults, so primary discitis without vertebral involvement is more typical of children. *Staphylococcus aureus* is the leading organism overall, followed by streptococci and gram-negative rods (especially with a genitourinary source); **Brucella spondylitis** and **tuberculous (Pott's) spondylitis** remain relevant differentials in endemic regions of Central/Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean, and TB classically produces large paraspinal/psoas abscesses with relative disc preservation early. Risk factors: diabetes, IV drug use, immunosuppression, endocarditis, hemodialysis, and recent spinal instrumentation (postoperative discitis). **Spinal epidural abscess** most often arises by contiguous spread from spondylodiscitis, hematogenously from a skin/soft-tissue or IVDU source, or iatrogenically (epidural injection/catheter). The classic Heusner staging (back pain → radicular pain → motor/sphincter weakness → paralysis) can progress within hours, which is why imaging and surgical decision-making cannot be deferred once a deficit is present.

### Spinal tumors

The spine is the third most common site of skeletal metastasis (after lung and liver), reflecting the marrow-rich vertebral body and Batson's plexus drainage; roughly 10-20% of cancer patients develop symptomatic spinal metastasis over their disease course. Leading primaries: breast, lung, prostate, renal cell, thyroid, and hematologic malignancies (myeloma/lymphoma). Epidural extension causing metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC) is an oncologic emergency. **Primary spine tumors are rare** (<5% of primary bone tumors). Benign lesions cluster in the young and favor the posterior elements (osteoid osteoma, osteoblastoma, aneurysmal bone cyst, eosinophilic granuloma); giant cell tumor favors the vertebral body. Malignant primary tumors include **chordoma** (notochordal remnant, sacrum/clivus predominant, most common primary malignant spine tumor in adults, relatively radioresistant to conventional photon RT), chondrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma (pediatric), and solitary plasmacytoma. Enneking staging (benign: latent/active/aggressive; malignant: grade + compartment) and the Weinstein-Boriani-Biagini (WBB) surgical staging system guide en bloc resection planning for primary malignant tumors.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**AIS**: Adam's forward bend test with scoliometer (angle of trunk rotation ≥5-7° warrants radiograph); full-length standing PA/lateral scoliosis radiograph, low-dose biplanar (EOS) preferred to limit cumulative radiation in a population needing serial imaging. Neurologic exam should specifically look for asymmetric abdominal reflexes, café-au-lait spots, and foot deformity — any of these, plus atypical curve patterns (left thoracic curve, excessive kyphosis, rapid progression, age <10, significant pain) mandate **whole-spine MRI** to rule out syringomyelia, Chiari malformation, or tethered cord before labeling a curve idiopathic.

**ASD**: Standing full-length 36-inch (or whole-body EOS) films are mandatory — cropped lumbar-only films miss the pelvic and global sagittal picture. Supplement with supine bending/traction films for flexibility, CT for bony detail and pseudarthrosis assessment, MRI for neural element and stenosis evaluation, and **DEXA** before major reconstructive surgery — osteoporosis materially changes construct strategy (interbody support, cement augmentation, extending fixation) and is frequently under-recognized preoperatively. SRS-22r and ODI scores anchor the symptomatic burden and track outcome.

**Scheuermann**: standing lateral radiograph to measure T5-T12 kyphosis and wedging; hyperextension (prone or supine over a bolster) lateral film to assess flexibility — failure to correct below ~50° suggests a rigid curve that may need anterior release/osteotomy in addition to posterior instrumentation. MRI if neurologic symptoms or disc herniation at the apex are suspected.

**Spondylodiscitis**: CRP is the most useful marker for both diagnosis and treatment monitoring (more responsive than ESR, and far more sensitive than WBC, which is frequently normal). Obtain **2-3 sets of blood cultures before starting antibiotics** (positive in 50-70% of hematogenous cases) — if the patient is hemodynamically stable, holding empiric antibiotics 48-72h to allow a diagnostic CT-guided biopsy when blood cultures are negative meaningfully improves microbiologic yield. **Contrast-enhanced MRI is the imaging gold standard** (endplate destruction, T2-hyperintense disc, paraspinal/epidural enhancement). Consider Brucella serology and TB workup based on regional epidemiology and exposure history.

**Spinal epidural abscess**: whole-spine contrast MRI is required — skip lesions are common enough that limiting imaging to the symptomatic level risks missing a second focus. Serial, documented neurologic exams are as important as the imaging itself given the potential for rapid change. Do not let "atypical" presentation (fever and deficit are frequently absent) delay imaging in a patient with new back pain plus a plausible bacteremic source.

**Metastatic disease**: whole-spine MRI to screen for occult/asymptomatic skip metastases (changes surgical planning), CT for bony architecture and SINS scoring, bone scan or PET for staging, ASIA/Frankel grading for baseline neurologic status, and assessment of **ambulatory status**, which is one of the strongest prognostic and decision-driving variables. Multidisciplinary tumor board discussion (surgery, radiation/medical oncology) is the standard of care before committing to a treatment pathway.

**Primary tumors**: biopsy planning is a surgical decision, not a radiology convenience — the needle tract must be excisable en bloc at definitive surgery, so biopsy should be performed at (or in direct coordination with) the treating center, never as an unplanned outside referral, to avoid tumor seeding that compromises later margins. CT for bony detail, MRI for soft tissue/neural involvement, and pre-operative angiography/embolization for hypervascular lesions (renal cell metastasis, aggressive hemangioma).

## Classification

### Lenke Classification (Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis, 2001)

Built from three components: (1) curve type based on which curves are "structural" (Cobb ≥25° that does not correct to <25° on side-bending films, OR T5-T12 kyphosis >+20°, OR T10-L2 kyphosis >+20°), (2) a lumbar spine modifier, (3) a sagittal thoracic modifier.

| Curve Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Main thoracic** | Only the main thoracic (MT) curve is structural | Selective thoracic fusion often possible if lumbar modifier favorable |
| **2 — Double thoracic** | Proximal thoracic + MT both structural | Proximal thoracic curve must be included; watch shoulder balance |
| **3 — Double major** | MT + thoracolumbar/lumbar (TL/L) both structural | Both curves fused; selective fusion generally not appropriate |
| **4 — Triple major** | PT + MT + TL/L all structural | Most extensive fusion; all three curves included |
| **5 — TL/L only** | Only TL/L structural | Fusion limited to TL/L segment; thoracic left unfused |
| **6 — TL/L-MT** | TL/L structural and larger than MT, MT also structural | TL/L is the major curve driving fusion levels; MT usually included |

**Lumbar modifier** (relationship of the center sacral vertical line, CSVL, to the lumbar apex): **A** = CSVL passes between the pedicles of the apical vertebra; **B** = CSVL touches the apical vertebral body; **C** = CSVL is medial to the apex. C-modifier curves are less likely to tolerate a selective thoracic fusion (higher risk of postoperative coronal decompensation).

**Sagittal thoracic modifier** (T5-T12 kyphosis): **-** (<10°, hypokyphotic), **N** (10-40°, normal), **+** (>40°, hyperkyphotic) — affects rod contouring and osteotomy planning at the thoracic segment.

### Cobb Angle Thresholds

| Cobb angle | Clinical action |
|---|---|
| <10° | Not scoliosis by definition (spinal asymmetry); no follow-up needed |
| 10-24° | Observation; PT-based scoliosis-specific exercise (Schroth/SEAS); repeat radiograph every 4-6 months during growth |
| 25-40° (with significant growth remaining, Risser 0-2) | Bracing candidate per BrAIST criteria |
| >45-50° at/near skeletal maturity | Surgical fusion threshold (thoracic); lumbar curves often treated at a lower threshold (~40°) given higher documented adult progression risk |
| >90-100° (thoracic) | Risk of restrictive pulmonary physiology; consider pulmonary function testing |
| Progression >5°/6 months | Significant progression regardless of absolute magnitude — reassess treatment plan |

### SRS-Schwab Classification (Adult Spinal Deformity, 2012)

**Coronal curve type**

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **T** | Thoracic curve only (lumbar Cobb <30°) |
| **L** | Thoracolumbar/lumbar curve only (thoracic Cobb <30°) |
| **D** | Double curve (both ≥30°) |
| **N** | None (all Cobb <30°) — sagittal deformity present without significant coronal curve |

**Sagittal modifiers** (each graded 0 / + / ++)

| Modifier | 0 | + | ++ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PI-LL** (pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis mismatch) | <10° | 10-20° | >20° |
| **SVA** (sagittal vertical axis, C7 plumb to posterosuperior sacrum) | <4cm | 4-9.5cm | >9.5cm |
| **PT** (pelvic tilt) | <20° | 20-30° | >30° |

Higher-grade modifiers correlate directly with worse ODI and SRS-22 scores and are the primary driver for selecting more aggressive correction techniques (osteotomy grade, fusion length, need for anterior column support) — surgical planning targets restoring these toward "0" (ideally age-adjusted, since some PI-LL mismatch and PT elevation is physiologic with aging).

### Risser Sign

| Grade | Iliac apophysis ossification | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No ossification | High growth remaining — highest curve-progression risk; strong bracing candidate |
| 1 | 0-25% ossified | Substantial growth remaining |
| 2 | 25-50% ossified | Still meaningful growth remaining — BrAIST bracing window (Risser 0-2) |
| 3 | 50-75% ossified | Growth slowing |
| 4 | 75-100% ossified, not yet fused | Low progression risk; brace weaning typically begins |
| 5 | Fully fused to ilium | Skeletal maturity reached; progression risk low outside large (>50°) curves |

Note: **Sanders skeletal maturity staging** (hand radiograph, 8 stages) is increasingly used alongside or instead of Risser because it correlates more precisely with peak height velocity and captures the adolescent growth spurt earlier — worth using in parallel when planning bracing or fusion-sparing surgery (VBT) in a rapidly growing patient.

### SINS — Spinal Instability Neoplastic Score (0-18)

| Component | Finding | Points |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Junctional (occiput-C2, C7-T2, T11-L1, L5-S1) | 3 |
| | Mobile spine (C3-C6, L2-L4) | 2 |
| | Semi-rigid (T3-T10) | 1 |
| | Rigid (S2-S5) | 0 |
| **Pain** | Mechanical/positional pain | 3 |
| | Occasional non-mechanical pain | 1 |
| | Pain-free lesion | 0 |
| **Bone lesion quality** | Lytic | 2 |
| | Mixed lytic/blastic | 1 |
| | Blastic | 0 |
| **Spinal alignment** | Subluxation/translation present | 4 |
| | De novo deformity (kyphosis/scoliosis) | 2 |
| | Normal alignment | 0 |
| **Vertebral body collapse** | >50% collapse | 3 |
| | <50% collapse | 2 |
| | No collapse but >50% body involved | 1 |
| | None of the above | 0 |
| **Posterolateral element involvement** | Bilateral (facet/pedicle/costovertebral) | 3 |
| | Unilateral | 1 |
| | None | 0 |

**Total score → management**: **0-6 = stable** (no surgical stabilization needed on mechanical grounds); **7-12 = indeterminate** (potentially unstable — obtain spine surgical consultation); **13-18 = unstable** (surgical stabilization indicated regardless of radiosensitivity or systemic status considerations).

### Bilsky Epidural Spinal Cord Compression (ESCC) Scale

| Grade | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Tumor confined to bone | Low-grade — SBRT alone feasible |
| **1a** | Epidural extension without thecal sac deformation | Low-grade — SBRT alone feasible |
| **1b** | Deformation of thecal sac, no cord abutment | Low-grade — SBRT alone feasible |
| **1c** | Thecal sac deformation with cord abutment, no compression | Low-grade — SBRT alone feasible |
| **2** | Spinal cord compression, CSF still visible around cord | High-grade — separation surgery generally required before SBRT to allow safe cord dosing |
| **3** | Spinal cord compression, no CSF visible around cord | High-grade — separation surgery generally required before SBRT |

The grade-2/3 threshold is the pivotal decision point in the NOMS framework: SBRT alone cannot adequately treat the tumor without exceeding cord dose tolerance when there is high-grade compression, so surgical decompression to re-establish a CSF margin is required first.

### Tokuhashi / Tomita Scores (Reference — Prognostic Scoring for Metastatic Spine Disease)

**Revised Tokuhashi score** (0-15, six factors each scored 0-2): general condition (KPS), number of extraspinal bone metastases, number of vertebral metastases, metastases to major internal organs, primary tumor site, and neurologic status (Frankel grade).

| Total score | Estimated prognosis | Suggested strategy |
|---|---|---|
| 0-8 | <6 months | Palliative/conservative care |
| 9-11 | ~6-12 months | Palliative surgery (decompression/stabilization) reasonable |
| 12-15 | ≥12 months | Excisional/aggressive surgery reasonable |

**Tomita score** (2-10, three factors): grade of malignancy (primary growth rate: slow=1, moderate=2, rapid=4), visceral metastases (none=0, treatable=2, untreatable=4), bone metastases (solitary=1, multiple=2).

| Total score | Goal | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| 2-3 | Long-term local control | Wide/marginal excision |
| 4-5 | Middle-term control | Marginal/intralesional excision |
| 6-7 | Short-term palliation | Palliative decompression/stabilization |
| 8-10 | Terminal care | Supportive care, no surgery |

**Modern practice note**: both scores remain useful as prognostic reference points and are still cited in the literature, but decision-making in most contemporary centers has shifted to the **NOMS framework** (below), which is more directly actionable at the individual-patient level (integrates neurologic, oncologic, mechanical, and systemic axes) rather than producing a single survival-based surgical/non-surgical cutoff.

### Enneking / WBB staging (reference — primary tumor surgical planning)

Not one of the classifications formally requested for this monograph, but essential context for the primary-tumor operative section below, so summarized briefly here.

| System | Categories | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Enneking (benign)** | Latent (1), Active (2), Aggressive (3) | Guides curettage vs. wide excision decision |
| **Enneking (malignant)** | Low-grade (IA/IB), High-grade (IIA/IIB), Metastatic (III); A = intracompartmental, B = extracompartmental | Guides margin planning (wide vs. radical) |
| **Weinstein-Boriani-Biagini (WBB)** | Vertebra divided into 12 radiating zones (clock-face) + 5 layers (A-E, extraosseous soft tissue to dural involvement) | Maps tumor extent circumferentially to plan the en bloc resection corridor (which zones/layers must be sacrificed) |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **AIS**: observation with serial exam/radiograph for curves <20-25° with growth remaining. Physiotherapeutic scoliosis-specific exercise (Schroth method, SEAS) has modest but real evidence for reducing progression and is used as an adjunct to, not a replacement for, bracing in curves meeting bracing criteria. **Bracing** (Boston/Providence underarm TLSO, or Rigo-Cheneau for asymmetric 3D correction) is indicated for 25-40° curves in Risser 0-2 patients per **BrAIST** criteria (see Recent Evidence), worn 18-20h/day for full-time designs; nighttime-only designs (Charleston bending brace, Providence) are an option for single, flexible, lower-magnitude thoracolumbar/lumbar curves, with less robust evidence than full-time wear for larger or thoracic curves.
- **ASD**: core/paraspinal stabilization physiotherapy, weight optimization, NSAIDs, activity modification, and — critically — **preoperative bone health optimization** (vitamin D repletion, bisphosphonate or anabolic agent in osteoporotic patients) before any planned reconstruction, since poor bone quality is a leading cause of instrumentation failure. Bracing plays a limited, mostly symptomatic/temporizing role.
- **Scheuermann**: observation for curves <50°; extension bracing (Milwaukee brace or a well-molded TLSO) for flexible 50-70° curves in patients with significant growth remaining — efficacy is inferior to AIS bracing and requires a flexible, growing patient to have a meaningful effect.
- **Spondylodiscitis**: empiric IV antibiotics (typically vancomycin + a broad-spectrum agent such as a third/fourth-generation cephalosporin) pending cultures, then narrowed to organism-directed therapy; standard duration is **6-8 weeks**, guided by serial CRP trend, with extension for undrained abscess, endocarditis, or slow clinical response. A TLSO brace is commonly used for pain control and provisional stability while bony healing occurs, though evidence for brace efficacy specifically is limited.
- **Spinal epidural abscess**: selective non-operative management (antibiotics alone, with serial neurologic exams and repeat MRI) can be appropriate in carefully chosen patients without neurologic deficit and with a small, non-compressive abscess and acceptable surgical risk — but the threshold to convert to surgery for any new or progressive deficit must remain very low.
- **Metastatic disease**: systemic therapy directed at the primary tumor, bone-modifying agents (zoledronic acid, denosumab) to reduce skeletal-related events, conventional external beam radiotherapy for radiosensitive histologies (multiple myeloma, lymphoma, seminoma) and for pain palliation in low-grade ESCC disease, and multimodal analgesia.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **CT-guided percutaneous biopsy** — central to diagnosis in both spinal infection (when blood cultures are negative) and tumor (mandatory before treating an undiagnosed lesion, and must be planned so the tract can be excised en bloc if the lesion proves to be a primary sarcoma).
- **Percutaneous abscess/paraspinal collection drainage** — an option for select spondylodiscitis or epidural abscess cases without significant cord compression, often combined with prolonged antibiotics.
- **Vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty** — effective for mechanical pain from a pathologic vertebral compression fracture (metastatic or myeloma) without significant posterior wall compromise or high-grade epidural extension; provides rapid pain relief and facilitates earlier mobilization, but is not a substitute for stabilization when SINS scores as unstable.
- **Radiofrequency ablation (RFA)** — curative for osteoid osteoma in the majority of cases (CT-guided, less morbid than open excision); used as a local-control adjunct (often combined with cementoplasty) alongside SBRT for selected spinal metastases.
- **Preoperative embolization** — important for hypervascular lesions (renal cell carcinoma metastasis, thyroid metastasis, aggressive hemangioma) to reduce intraoperative blood loss before resection/decompression.
- **Epidural steroid or facet injection** — no primary role in deformity correction itself, but useful as a diagnostic/symptomatic bridge for concomitant stenosis-related radicular pain in adult deformity patients awaiting or deferring surgery.

### Operative

**AIS**
- **Indications**: Cobb >45-50° at/near skeletal maturity, progressive curve despite bracing, documented progression >5°/6mo, or significant curve-related pulmonary/cosmetic concern.
- **Posterior spinal fusion with segmental pedicle screw instrumentation** is the current standard (has replaced hook and hybrid constructs). Fusion levels follow the Lenke type and modifiers — all structural curves must be included; the lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) is generally the "stable" or "neutral" vertebra to minimize risk of decompensation and distal adding-on. **Selective thoracic fusion** (sparing the lumbar curve) is appropriate in favorable Lenke 1A/1B/1C patterns with an A or B lumbar modifier; direct vertebral derotation techniques improve axial-plane correction beyond simple rod rotation. Anterior approaches (open or thoracoscopic release) are now rarely needed, reserved for very rigid curves (>75-90°, correction <50% on side-bending).
- **Vertebral body tethering (VBT)**: a fusionless, motion-preserving alternative using anterior vertebral body screws connected by a tensioned polymer cord that applies asymmetric compression to modulate growth on the convex side. Received FDA **Humanitarian Device Exemption** approval for skeletally immature patients with progressive idiopathic scoliosis of 30-65°. Ideal candidates: Sanders stage 2-4/Risser 0-2 with substantial growth remaining, flexible curves correcting to <30° on bending/traction films, thoracic curves (best-studied pattern). Known risks include tether breakage (reported in a meaningful minority of cases in early to mid-term series), overcorrection, and conversion to fusion in a non-trivial proportion of patients at longer follow-up. It is best framed as a **narrow, well-selected alternative**, not a general substitute for fusion — long-term (10-year+) durability data are still maturing.
- **Pitfalls**: crankshaft phenomenon if a very immature spine (open triradiate cartilage) undergoes posterior-only fusion without anterior growth arrest; incorrect LIV selection causing distal adding-on; coronal decompensation after overly aggressive selective fusion.

**Adult spinal deformity**
- **Planning target**: restore age-adjusted alignment — PI-LL mismatch toward <10° (or age-adjusted per published formulas), SVA <4-5cm, PT <20-25°. The Roussouly/Lafage sagittal shape classification is a useful adjunct for individualizing the target rather than applying one absolute number to every patient.
- **Technique ladder** (escalating with deformity magnitude and rigidity): decompression alone (rare — only for pure stenosis without progression risk) → short-segment fusion for focal pathology → interbody techniques (ALIF/LLIF/OLIF with hyperlordotic cages) for anterior column support, indirect decompression, and lordosis restoration → long fusion to the pelvis (S2-alar-iliac or iliac screws) for larger deformity → posterior column (Smith-Petersen) osteotomies (~10-15° correction/level) → pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO, ~30-35°/level, single-level correction of fixed sagittal deformity, higher blood loss and complication burden) → vertebral column resection for severe, rigid, multiplanar deformity. Circumferential minimally invasive (cMIS) strategies are increasingly used in lower-grade deformity to reduce blood loss and length of stay in appropriately selected patients.
- **Pearls**: multi-rod (accessory rod) constructs at the lumbosacral junction reduce rod fracture; preserve the posterior ligamentous complex at the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) and UIV+1, and avoid correcting beyond age-adjusted targets, to reduce proximal junctional kyphosis/failure (PJK/PJF); optimize bone health preoperatively (bisphosphonate, teriparatide, or romosozumab in osteoporotic patients) to reduce screw loosening and junctional fracture.

**Scheuermann kyphosis**
- **Indications**: kyphosis >70-75° with pain or cosmetic concern, progression despite bracing, or neurologic symptoms from apical disc herniation/cord compression.
- **Technique**: posterior-only instrumentation with multilevel Ponte (posterior column) osteotomies has largely replaced combined anterior release plus posterior fusion for most curves; correction is targeted to a physiologic kyphosis of ~20-40° — overcorrection risks junctional breakdown.
- **Pitfall**: proximal junctional kyphosis is well recognized if fusion does not extend to a stable sagittal vertebra or if correction is excessive.

**Spondylodiscitis — surgical triggers and technique**
- **Triggers**: neurologic deficit/cord compression, mechanical instability or significant vertebral body destruction/collapse, failed medical management (persistent bacteremia/sepsis or progressive bone destruction despite appropriate antibiotics), a significant epidural or paraspinal abscess with mass effect, progressive kyphotic deformity, or need for open tissue diagnosis when percutaneous biopsy is non-diagnostic.
- **Technique**: debridement (historically anterior corpectomy, now frequently posterior-only or combined approaches depending on level and abscess location) with structural reconstruction (titanium cage or allograft) and posterior instrumentation for stability. Instrumenting in the setting of active infection is now widely accepted practice — provided adequate debridement and appropriate antibiotic coverage — contrary to older teaching that avoided hardware in infected fields. Percutaneous posterior stabilization alone (without formal debridement) is a reasonable damage-control option in frail, high-risk patients.

**Spinal epidural abscess**
- **Emergent surgical decompression** (laminectomy ± instrumentation) is indicated for any new or evolving neurologic deficit; time-to-decompression correlates directly with neurologic recovery, with outcomes worsening sharply once a deficit has been present beyond roughly 24-36 hours — this should not be delayed for additional workup once the decision is made.
- Combine with abscess drainage and the antibiotic course described above for concurrent spondylodiscitis, which frequently coexists.

**Metastatic spine surgery (NOMS-driven)**
- **Separation surgery**: posterior debulking of epidural tumor to re-establish ~2-3mm of circumferential CSF space around the thecal sac (deliberately not a gross-total-resection goal), followed by **postoperative SBRT** typically within 2-3 weeks once the wound has healed. This combination achieves high (>85-90%) one-year local control even for classically radioresistant histologies (renal cell, sarcoma, melanoma, thyroid, hepatocellular, NSCLC).
- **NOMS decision logic**: high-grade ESCC (Bilsky 2-3) + reasonable surgical/systemic candidacy → separation surgery + SBRT, regardless of radiosensitivity; mechanical instability (SINS 13-18) → stabilization (open or percutaneous) regardless of ESCC grade or radiosensitivity; low-grade ESCC + radiosensitive tumor → conventional EBRT alone often sufficient; low-grade ESCC + radioresistant tumor → SBRT alone (dose escalation is feasible without a cord-abutment constraint); poor systemic status/short life expectancy → favor radiation/systemic therapy/best supportive care and avoid surgery.
- **Minimally invasive approaches** (percutaneous pedicle screw stabilization ± mini-open decompression) are increasingly favored in the metastatic population specifically to reduce wound complications and blood loss and to allow earlier resumption of systemic therapy — an important consideration given limited life expectancy and radiation/chemotherapy-related wound-healing concerns.

**Primary spine tumor surgery**
- **Benign latent/active** (osteoid osteoma, small ABC): intralesional excision/curettage ± adjuvant (phenol, cryotherapy), or CT-guided RFA (curative in the majority of osteoid osteomas, less invasive than open excision).
- **Benign aggressive** (giant cell tumor): extended intralesional curettage with adjuvant ± preoperative denosumab to downstage and facilitate resection (with attention to rebound/recurrence risk after discontinuation); en bloc resection reserved for recurrent or anatomically inaccessible-for-curettage disease.
- **Malignant** (chordoma, chondrosarcoma): **en bloc resection with wide margins**, per Enneking/WBB staging, remains the mainstay — both are relatively resistant to conventional photon radiotherapy, so high-dose proton/carbon-ion RT or SBRT is used as an adjunct for close/positive margins or unresectable disease (notably sacral chordoma). Osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma require multimodal treatment (neoadjuvant chemotherapy plus resection, with radiotherapy added for Ewing sarcoma).
- **Sacral chordoma** specifics: en bloc sacrectomy with the resection level dictated by proximal tumor extent; sacral nerve root sacrifice creates a direct trade-off between oncologic margin and bowel/bladder/sexual function that must be discussed explicitly preoperatively. Intralesional surgery carries a high local recurrence rate and should be avoided when en bloc resection is feasible.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**AIS — post posterior spinal fusion**
- **0-2 weeks**: inpatient mobilization from postoperative day 1; segmental pedicle screw fixation generally does not require formal bracing (surgeon-dependent); bend/lift/twist (BLT) precautions; walking as tolerated.
- **2-6 weeks**: gradual increase in ambulation; no PE or sports; avoid prolonged sitting; driving typically deferred until ~4 weeks and off opioid analgesia.
- **6-12 weeks**: full return to school typical by 6 weeks; light activities resumed; precautions gradually lifted.
- **3-6 months**: non-contact sports and swimming typically permitted around 3 months; begin core strengthening.
- **6-12 months**: full return to contact/collision sports generally allowed at 9-12 months once fusion is confirmed solid on imaging.

**Adult spinal deformity — post long fusion to pelvis**
- **0-2 weeks**: inpatient stay commonly 4-7 days; early mobilization to reduce ileus/DVT risk; bracing per surgeon preference (often optional with rigid segmental instrumentation); multimodal pain control.
- **2-6 weeks**: progressive ambulation with formal PT for gait/functional training; BLT precautions maintained; continued VTE prophylaxis per individualized risk.
- **6-12 weeks**: gentle core activation exercises introduced; no high-impact activity; standing alignment films at 6 weeks to screen early for PJK.
- **3-6 months**: progressive strengthening; return to sedentary work often achievable by 6-12 weeks, physical labor deferred to 4-6 months.
- **6-12+ months**: CT fusion assessment around 1 year; individualized clearance for heavy labor/sport; ongoing PJK surveillance with standing films at 3, 6, and 12 months, then annually.

**Spondylodiscitis / spinal epidural abscess (operative or non-operative)**
- Early mobilization within pain limits once medically stable is prioritized over prolonged bed rest, which primarily adds deconditioning risk without proven infection-control benefit.
- TLSO bracing for pain and provisional stability, typically 6-12 weeks in non-instrumented cases, weaned as CRP normalizes and imaging shows healing.
- Outpatient parenteral antibiotic therapy (OPAT) is standard once the patient is clinically stable, allowing rehabilitation to proceed outside the acute hospital setting.
- In post-neurologic-deficit patients, formal inpatient or outpatient neurorehabilitation (gait training, strengthening) proceeds in parallel with the antibiotic course.

**Metastatic spine disease — post-surgical**
- Early mobilization (goal: ambulate postoperative day 1) is a priority given the emphasis on preserving quality of life and function within a limited life expectancy.
- Coordinate postoperative SBRT timing (~2-3 weeks after surgery, once the wound is healed) directly with radiation oncology.
- Rehabilitation goals are functional/palliative (ADLs, pain control, safe mobility) rather than sport- or work-return-oriented; early involvement of palliative care and home health/hospice services as appropriate.

## Complications

- **AIS**: pseudarthrosis (~1-3% with modern segmental screw constructs), surgical site infection (~1-2%), neurologic injury (<1%, reduced further with intraoperative neuromonitoring), proximal junctional kyphosis (less frequent than in adult deformity but reported), distal adding-on from suboptimal LIV selection, implant prominence, crankshaft phenomenon in skeletally immature posterior-only fusion.
- **Adult spinal deformity**: high overall complication burden (commonly cited in the 30-70% range depending on definition and procedure complexity) — PJK/PJF (radiographic PJK in a substantial minority, revision-requiring PJF in a smaller subset), pseudarthrosis and rod fracture (concentrated at the lumbosacral junction), surgical site infection (higher than in degenerative spine surgery generally), neurologic deficit (particularly with three-column osteotomy, several percent for PSO), major intraoperative blood loss, and significant medical complication rates given the older, more comorbid population; revision surgery is common at mid-term follow-up.
- **Scheuermann kyphosis**: PJK, junctional pain, pseudarthrosis, neurologic injury related to osteotomy correction, flatback syndrome if overcorrected.
- **Spondylodiscitis**: relapse/chronic infection, progression to epidural abscess, vertebral collapse with kyphotic deformity, sepsis, and clinically meaningful mortality in elderly/comorbid populations; delayed diagnosis remains the leading modifiable driver of poor outcomes.
- **Spinal epidural abscess**: permanent neurologic deficit when decompression is delayed beyond the critical window, recurrent/residual abscess, and historically significant mortality — outcomes have improved with earlier MRI access and more aggressive surgical thresholds but delayed diagnosis remains the dominant risk factor.
- **Metastatic spine disease**: local recurrence after separation surgery plus SBRT (a minority at one year, still meaningfully lower than surgery or conventional radiation alone), wound complications (elevated by systemic therapy and radiation effects — preoperative radiation is a particular risk factor, which is part of why SBRT is sequenced postoperatively), venous thromboembolism (elevated baseline cancer-associated risk), and instrumentation failure with rapidly progressive disease.
- **Primary spine tumors**: local recurrence with inadequate (intralesional) margins — historically a major problem for chordoma — functional deficits from planned nerve root sacrifice in sacrectomy, wound complications from large resections, and late metastasis (notably chordoma).

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **Vertebral body tethering (VBT) — FDA IDE cohort outcomes** (2024–2025): The first prospective, multicenter U.S. FDA Investigational Device Exemption study of VBT in skeletally immature patients reported ~75% "successful" outcomes at 2 years, with inadequate intraoperative curve correction predicting later failure; accompanying commentary tracking the same population further out found success declining over time (85% at 1 year, 75% at 2 years, 62% at a mean 3.8 years) with roughly 50% cumulative tether breakage by 36 months — tempering earlier enthusiasm and reinforcing that VBT durability past skeletal maturity remains unresolved. [Vertebral Body Tethering in Skeletally Immature Patients: Results of a Prospective U.S. FDA IDE Study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39719007/); [Prospective Vertebral Body Tethering Data — Commentary](https://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/fulltext/2025/02050/prospective_vertebral_body_tethering_data_.16.aspx)
- **VBT — umbrella review** (2024): An umbrella review of VBT for AIS concluded the underlying evidence base is still heterogeneous and of low-to-moderate quality overall, supporting continued restriction of VBT to well-selected, flexible thoracic curves in patients with substantial growth remaining rather than broad adoption as a fusion alternative. [Current Status of Vertebral Body Tethering for Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis: An Umbrella Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39679276/)
- **AIS bracing — dose-response beyond BrAIST's original threshold** (2025): Extending BrAIST's wear-time analysis, a 2025 study found that brace wear above 18 h/day continues to show a dose-response benefit on treatment success and end-of-treatment Cobb angle <30°, suggesting benefit may not plateau at the level originally described in BrAIST. [Wearing a brace above 18 hrs/day shows a dose–response effect](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00586-025-09124-0)
- **AIS bracing — sensor-based adherence monitoring** (2025): A pilot study integrated force and temperature sensors into braces to more accurately estimate real-world wear-time compliance than temperature sensing alone (the method used in BrAIST), addressing known limitations of single-modality adherence tracking. [Integrated Force and Temperature Sensors to Enhance Orthotic Treatment Monitoring in AIS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11819978/)
- **GAP score validated against SRS-Schwab classification** (2024): A direct comparison found the Global Alignment and Proportion (GAP) score and SRS-Schwab ASD classification both predict surgical outcomes, with mechanical complication rates rising sharply from ~6% in proportioned spinopelvic states to 47% (moderately disproportioned) and 95% (severely disproportioned) by GAP category. [Comparison of GAP Score and SRS-Schwab ASD Classification](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11130083/)
- **GAP score meta-analysis** (2025): A systematic review and meta-analysis reaffirmed the GAP score's predictive value for mechanical complications after adult spinal deformity surgery across multiple cohorts, supporting its continued use in preoperative planning alongside age-adjusted targets. [Predictive value of the GAP score for mechanical complications — systematic review/meta-analysis](https://www.nassopenaccess.org/article/S2666-5484(25)00236-7/fulltext)
- **Age-adjusted vs. normative sagittal correction** (2023): A comparative study of adult spinal deformity patients corrected to normative versus functional (age-adjusted) alignment targets reported differences in clinical outcomes and proximal junctional failure rates, adding outcomes-based support to the shift away from fixed, uniform PI-LL/SVA/PT cutoffs. [Clinical outcomes and proximal junctional failure: normative vs. functional alignment](https://thejns.org/spine/view/journals/j-neurosurg-spine/39/6/article-p757.xml)
- **Spondylodiscitis — SPILF clinical practice guidelines** (2022, published 2023): Updated French Infectious Diseases Society (SPILF) guidelines for diagnosis and treatment of disco-vertebral infection in adults were published, prompting reconsideration of the syndrome in patients with recent or worsening febrile spinal pain — a more recent, dedicated guideline update than the still-referenced 2015 IDSA document. [2022 SPILF Clinical Practice Guidelines for disco-vertebral infection](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S266699192300009X)
- **Native vertebral osteomyelitis — toward a unified definition** (2024): A framework proposal in the *Journal of Bone and Joint Infection* highlighted the lack of a consensus case definition for native vertebral osteomyelitis despite rising incidence and persistently poor outcomes (frequent relapse, long-term sequelae), and called for standardization to enable better comparative research. [It is time for a unified definition of native vertebral osteomyelitis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11262020/)
- **Separation surgery + SBRT vs. 3D-conformal RT** (2025): A single-center retrospective comparison found separation surgery followed by SBRT produced superior local control and overall survival compared with separation surgery followed by conventional 3D-conformal radiotherapy, further consolidating SBRT as the preferred adjuvant modality after separation surgery for metastatic epidural spinal cord compression. [Role of Separation Surgery and Advanced Radiotherapy: SBRT vs. 3D-CRT](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1878875025008472)
- **Separation surgery + SBRT — access barriers** (2024): A study on obstacles to receiving postoperative radiation after separation surgery found timely SBRT delivery is frequently delayed by scheduling conflicts, insurance denials, and travel logistics, flagging a real-world gap between the evidence-based NOMS paradigm and its reliable execution. [Obstacles to receiving postoperative radiation therapy following separation surgery](https://thejns.org/spine/view/journals/j-neurosurg-spine/41/3/article-p416.xml)
- **Carbon fiber-reinforced (CFR-PEEK) instrumentation in spine oncology** (2024–2025): A case-matched series comparing CFR-PEEK to titanium instrumentation in spinal oncology patients found carbon fiber implants at least equivalent in clinical efficacy while improving postoperative imaging quality (reduced artifact), aiding radiotherapy planning and surveillance for local recurrence; the multicenter randomized CARBOMETASPINE trial is now underway to test this prospectively against titanium in metastatic disease. [Impact of instrumentation material on local recurrence: CFR-PEEK vs. titanium](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11685248/); [CARBOMETASPINE trial protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12403952/)
- **Denosumab for spinal giant cell tumor** (2024): A 2024 meta-analysis of neoadjuvant denosumab in surgical patients with spinal GCT and epidural disease reported no local recurrences in that surgical cohort, while a separate 2024 study of denosumab de-escalation (extending dosing intervals) found recurrences in a subset of patients during the de-escalated phase — reinforcing that discontinuation/de-escalation, not surgical neoadjuvant use itself, carries the recognized rebound risk. [Neoadjuvant denosumab and neurological deterioration risk — meta-analysis](https://www.thespinejournalonline.com/article/S1529-9430(24)00033-0/abstract); [Efficacy and safety of denosumab de-escalation in giant cell tumor](https://www.spandidos-publications.com/10.3892/ol.2024.14520)
- **Minimally invasive stabilization in metastatic spine disease** (2024): A systematic review of MIS techniques (percutaneous pedicle screw fixation, vertebral augmentation, radiofrequency ablation) across 50 studies and >3,000 metastatic spine disease patients over a decade confirmed good pain control and reduced perioperative morbidity versus open surgery, supporting continued growth of MIS approaches to minimize time off systemic therapy. [Role of minimally invasive surgery in metastatic spine disease — systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10912012/)
