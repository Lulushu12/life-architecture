## Quick Reference

**Epidemiology**: distal radius fracture (DRF) accounts for 8–15% of all adult fractures; bimodal distribution — young males (high energy) and postmenopausal women (low energy, osteoporotic). Concomitant TFCC injury reported in up to 40–60% of intra-articular DRFs on MRI/arthroscopy (often clinically silent); ulnar styloid fracture accompanies ~50–65% of DRFs.

### Treatment ladder by diagnosis

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extra-articular DRF, acceptable alignment** | Closed reduction (if displaced) + below/above-elbow cast 4–6 wk | Removable orthosis after 2–3 wk if stable | K-wire or VLP only if redisplacement |
| **Extra-articular/simple intra-articular DRF, unstable or elderly with poor alignment tolerance** | Closed reduction + cast, weekly films first 3 wk | K-wire fixation (esp. <65 y, dorsal displacement) | Volar locking plate (VLP) |
| **Complex intra-articular DRF (die-punch, comminuted, articular step >2 mm)** | — (reduction rarely holds) | — | ORIF VLP ± dorsal plate/fragment-specific fixation, ± bridging ex-fix, ± arthroscopic assist |
| **DRUJ instability, acute, reducible, stable after DRF/ulnar styloid fixation** | Immobilize forearm in neutral-to-supination, long-arm cast/orthosis 4–6 wk | — | — |
| **DRUJ instability, irreducible or grossly unstable after fracture fixation** | — | Closed reduction + long-arm cast | K-wire trans-fixation of DRUJ (4–6 wk) ± TFCC repair |
| **Acute TFCC tear (Palmer 1B peripheral, traumatic)** | Immobilization 4–6 wk + NSAIDs | Structured hand therapy | Arthroscopic/open repair if failed 3 mo conservative care or associated DRUJ instability |
| **Acute TFCC tear (Palmer 1A/1C/1D central/radial/distal)** | Immobilization, activity modification | Corticosteroid injection (diagnostic/therapeutic) | Debridement (1A) rarely repair (poor vascularity centrally) |
| **Ulnar styloid fracture, isolated, tip, DRUJ stable** | Splint/cast 2–3 wk, early motion | — | Rarely indicated |
| **Ulnar styloid fracture, base, large fragment, DRUJ unstable** | — | — | Fixation (tension band/screw) if DRUJ remains unstable after radius fixation |

### Key numbers to memorize

- **Acceptable post-reduction alignment (adult, functional-demand threshold)**: radial inclination >15°, radial height loss <5 mm, dorsal tilt <10° (neutral-to-slightly dorsal acceptable), articular step/gap ≤2 mm, no radial shortening >5 mm relative to ulna.
- **Elderly/low-demand acceptable alignment (more permissive)**: dorsal angulation up to 10–20°, radial shortening up to 5 mm, if extra-articular — radiographic alignment correlates poorly with PRWE/functional outcome in low-demand patients; grip strength and ROM are stronger predictors (2025 systematic review).
- **Normal radiographic parameters**: radial inclination 22–23°, radial height 11–12 mm, volar tilt 11° (range 0–22°), ulnar variance neutral ±2 mm.
- **BOAST/NICE timing**: intra-articular and re-displaced fractures → surgery within 72 h; extra-articular fractures needing surgery → within 1 week; fracture clinic review within 72 h of injury or first ED presentation.
- **Acute carpal tunnel syndrome (ACTS)** complicates 4.5–9% of DRFs; median nerve ultrasound CSA/height-width-ratio thresholds under study but not yet standard of care — clinical serial exam remains gold standard.
- **DRAFFT2 (2022, still the reference RCT)**: cast vs K-wire — no PRWE difference at 12 mo, but 13% of cast patients needed rescue fixation for loss of position vs 0.4% revision in the K-wire arm.
- **TFCC repair vs debridement**: peripheral (Palmer 1B) tears heal well with repair (good vascularity, outer 10–20%); central (1A) tears are avascular and are debrided, not repaired.

### DRUJ stress testing — quick technique note

Perform pronation/supination stress under fluoroscopy with the elbow at 90° and forearm rotated to neutral, then stressed in pronation and supination while an assistant applies a dorsal/volar translation force to the ulna relative to the radius; compare translation and the "clunk" endpoint to the contralateral, uninjured side. Document the arc of forearm rotation (if any) within which the joint remains reduced and stable — this arc becomes the immobilization position if nonoperative/pin management is chosen.

### Classification cheat-sheet — which system to reach for

| Situation | Reach for | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Coding/administrative, research comparability | AO/OTA 23 | Universal, hierarchical, most widely used in trials |
| Deciding fixation strategy at the bedside | Fernandez | Mechanism-based, maps directly to buttress vs bridging vs combined fixation |
| Describing associated ulnar-sided/DRUJ injury burden | Frykman | Explicitly tracks ulnar styloid + joint involvement (largely historical now) |

| Communicating/coding a TFCC tear found at MRI or arthroscopy | Palmer | Clinical reference standard distinguishing traumatic (1) from degenerative (2) pathways |

### Red flags — do not miss

- **Open fracture / compartment syndrome** — urgent debridement, do not delay for imaging perfection.
- **Progressive median nerve deficit or disproportionate pain after reduction/casting** → acute carpal tunnel syndrome; emergent carpal tunnel release, do not wait for EMG.
- **Irreducible DRUJ or persistent gross instability after anatomic radius fixation** → suspect TFCC/interosseous membrane disruption or entrapped ECU/extensor tendon in the DRUJ (irreducible dislocation) — needs open exploration.
- **Galeazzi-equivalent pattern** (DRF + gross DRUJ disruption, especially with radial shaft involvement) — treat as unstable DRUJ injury, low threshold for pinning.
- **Bilateral/high-energy wrist fracture in a young patient with tight cast and increasing analgesia requirement** → compartment syndrome of forearm, not just ACTS.
- **Positive ulnar fovea sign + persistent ulnar-sided pain after adequate radius reduction** → occult TFCC/DRUJ pathology, image/arthroscope before attributing to "just a sprain."

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The distal radius articulates with the scaphoid and lunate (radiocarpal joint) and with the ulnar head (DRUJ, sigmoid notch). Load transmission across the wrist is ~80% radius / 20% ulna in neutral variance; this ratio shifts with radial shortening (increasing ulnocarpal load) — the biomechanical basis for symptomatic ulnar impaction after malunion.

**Key stabilizers of the DRUJ**: the TFCC (triangular fibrocartilage complex) is the primary soft-tissue stabilizer, comprising the articular disc, the volar and dorsal radioulnar ligaments (the deep, "ligamentum subcruentum" fibers insert onto the fovea — the critical stabilizing structure, distinct from the more peripheral, better-vascularized superficial fibers that insert onto the ulnar styloid base), the ulnocarpal ligaments (ulnolunate, ulnotriquetral), the ECU subsheath, and the meniscus homologue. Secondary stabilizers: pronator quadratus, interosseous membrane (particularly its distal oblique bundle), and the DRUJ capsule/bony sigmoid notch congruity.

**Vascular zonation of the TFCC** (Palmer/Bednar): peripheral 10–20% (ulnar attachment) is well vascularized from ulnar and dorsal/palmar branches → heals with repair; the central 2/3 to 80% is relatively avascular → poor healing potential, managed by debridement when symptomatic.

**Fracture mechanics**: dorsally displaced/angulated fractures (Colles pattern) result from a fall on an outstretched, dorsiflexed wrist — the classic osteoporotic fragility fracture. Volarly displaced (Smith pattern) results from a fall on a flexed wrist or direct dorsal blow. Barton fractures are marginal shear fractures (volar or dorsal rim) with carpal subluxation and are inherently unstable — nearly always operative. Die-punch fractures represent axial load driving the lunate facet into the metaphysis, producing central articular depression — a key target for fragment-specific/dorsal buttress fixation.

**Median nerve** runs immediately volar to the fracture site under the flexor tendons/pronator quadratus — vulnerable to direct contusion, traction from dorsal displacement, hematoma, or compartment pressure; this anatomic proximity is the rationale for mandatory serial neurologic exams.

**Sigmoid notch morphology (Tolat classification)**: the radial sigmoid notch — the ulnar head's articular seat on the radius — is categorized by depth/shape (flat, ski-slope, C-type, S-type) and influences intrinsic bony DRUJ stability independent of soft tissue; a shallow or dysplastic notch (flat/ski-slope types) confers less inherent bony stability and a lower threshold for suspecting/treating soft-tissue (TFCC) instability after fracture, which is why the CUP and other contemporary TFCC classification schemes explicitly incorporate sigmoid notch type alongside the soft-tissue tear pattern.

**Fragility fracture significance**: a low-energy distal radius fracture in a postmenopausal woman or man over 50 is a sentinel event for underlying osteoporosis and a substantially elevated risk of subsequent hip and vertebral fracture. Regardless of treatment modality chosen for the wrist itself, referral for bone health assessment (DEXA, FRAX, fracture liaison service pathway where available) should be initiated at the index presentation — a widely endorsed secondary-fracture-prevention principle (IOF/national osteoporosis guidelines) that is frequently missed when attention is focused solely on the fracture itself.

### Diagnosis-specific pathophysiology notes

- **DRUJ instability**: results from disruption of the foveal (deep) TFCC insertion, a displaced basilar ulnar styloid fracture (avulsing the foveal ligament attachment), sigmoid notch fracture, or gross interosseous membrane/Essex-Lopresti-type injury. Instability may be occult until the radius is anatomically reduced and fixed — always reassess DRUJ stability intraoperatively after radius fixation.
- **Ulnar styloid fracture**: tip fractures generally spare the foveal ligament insertion (deep fibers) and rarely destabilize the DRUJ; base fractures more often involve the foveal attachment and correlate with DRUJ instability and higher nonunion-associated morbidity.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (low- vs high-energy), hand dominance, occupation/activity demand (drives acceptable-alignment threshold and operative threshold), comorbidities/bone health status, anticoagulation, prior wrist symptoms (osteoarthritis, prior TFCC injury).

**Examination**: inspect for deformity ("dinner fork"), open wounds, swelling; palpate radial vs ulnar-sided tenderness (anatomic snuffbox — rule out concomitant scaphoid fracture); assess full neurovascular exam with explicit **median nerve** sensory (2-point discrimination, light touch in median distribution) and motor (thenar/ APB) documentation at presentation, post-reduction, and at each follow-up until resolved — this baseline is medicolegally and clinically essential given the 4.5–9% ACTS incidence.

**DRUJ-specific tests**: ballottement test (piano-key sign), ulnar fovea sign (sensitivity ~95%, specificity ~86% for TFCC/foveal pathology per published series), press test, supination/pronation stress comparison to contralateral wrist under fluoroscopy intraoperatively.

**Imaging**:
- **Plain radiographs**: PA, lateral, oblique wrist. Measure radial inclination, radial height, volar/dorsal tilt, articular step-off/gap, ulnar variance. Compare to contralateral wrist if baseline anatomy uncertain.
- **CT**: recommended for intra-articular fractures with step/gap >2 mm on plain film, comminuted die-punch patterns, or pre-operative planning for fragment-specific fixation; superior for quantifying articular incongruity and detecting occult sigmoid notch or DRUJ fractures.
- **MRI**: not routine acutely for DRF itself, but the modality of choice for suspected TFCC tear when clinical suspicion persists after fracture healing/reduction, or pre-operatively if repair being planned; sensitivity for TFCC tears improves substantially with MR arthrography (approaches arthroscopy).
- **Wrist arthroscopy**: diagnostic and therapeutic gold standard for TFCC tears and DRUJ instability assessment; increasingly used intraoperatively during DRF ORIF ("fluoroscopically assisted arthroscopic reduction," FAAR) to directly visualize articular reduction and TFCC/ligamentous status, particularly in young, high-demand patients with intra-articular fractures.
- **Fluoroscopic DRUJ stress exam under anesthesia**: after any DRF or ulnar styloid fixation, actively test DRUJ stability in pronation, neutral, and supination before closing — this is the operative moment that converts "occult" instability into a treated diagnosis.

**Suggested follow-up imaging schedule**:

| Treatment | Imaging timepoints |
|---|---|
| Cast/nonoperative | Post-reduction, then weekly to 3 wk, then 6 wk (union check), then as clinically indicated |
| K-wire | Post-op, 2 wk, 4–6 wk (pin removal decision), then as clinically indicated |
| VLP | Post-op, 2 wk, 6 wk, 3 mo (union confirmation); further imaging only if clinically indicated |
| DRUJ K-wire/TFCC repair | Post-op, then at wire removal (~4–6 wk), then at return-to-rotation milestones |

### Diagnosis-specific evaluation notes

- **Distal radius fracture**: always screen for associated scaphoid tenderness (anatomic snuffbox) and, in high-energy patterns, for a floating wrist/segmental forearm injury; document skin integrity carefully around the dorsal and volar wrist since blistering can delay definitive fixation by 5–10 days.
- **DRUJ instability**: perform the ballottement test, press test, and fovea sign bilaterally for comparison — subtle instability is easily missed without a side-to-side reference, especially in a hypermobile patient. Always reassess intraoperatively after radius fixation, not just preoperatively, since anatomic radius restoration can unmask or resolve apparent instability.
- **TFCC injury (acute)**: reproduce ulnar-sided click/pain with the TFCC "grind" (axial load + ulnar deviation + rotation) or supination lift test; correlate with mechanism (fall on pronated, extended wrist with axial load is classic). MRI arthrography is preferred over standard MRI when repair is being considered, given superior sensitivity for peripheral tears.
- **Ulnar styloid fracture**: on radiographs, distinguish tip (distal to the fovea) from base/basilar fractures (through or proximal to the fovea) — this distinction, more than fragment size alone, predicts DRUJ instability risk and should be explicitly documented in the fracture description.
- **Essex-Lopresti-type injury (differential to keep in mind)**: a comminuted radial head fracture with disruption of the interosseous membrane and DRUJ — if a DRF-pattern presentation includes elbow pain or radial head tenderness, image the ipsilateral elbow; missed proximal radius migration is a well-known pitfall when attention is anchored on the wrist.

## Classification

### AO/OTA 23 (distal radius)

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **23-A** (extra-articular) | A1 ulna fractured, radius intact; A2 radius extra-articular, simple/impacted; A3 radius extra-articular, multifragmentary | Usually amenable to closed reduction/casting if reducible to acceptable alignment; K-wire or VLP if unstable/redisplaces, especially A3 |
| **23-B** (partial articular) | B1 sagittal split (radial styloid); B2 dorsal rim (Barton); B3 volar rim (volar Barton/reverse Barton) | Inherently unstable due to shear mechanism — nearly always operative (ORIF with buttress/volar locking plate); B3 volar Barton is the classic VLP indication |
| **23-C** (complete articular) | C1 articular simple + metaphyseal simple; C2 articular simple + metaphyseal multifragmentary; C3 articular multifragmentary (comminuted, e.g., die-punch) | C1/C2 generally ORIF VLP ± dorsal augmentation; C3 often needs fragment-specific fixation, dorsal plating, external fixation as adjunct, or combined approaches; highest risk of post-traumatic arthritis |

The current AO/OTA classification also carries **universal qualifiers** (e.g., displacement, comminution, articular impaction, associated ulnar fracture) that further refine the A/B/C group and are useful for structured operative note documentation and outcome research, though day-to-day surgical decision-making usually still centers on the A/B/C/subgroup level shown above.

### Frykman classification

| Type | Articular involvement | Ulnar styloid |
|---|---|---|
| I | Extra-articular | Absent |
| II | Extra-articular | Present |
| III | Radiocarpal joint involved | Absent |
| IV | Radiocarpal joint involved | Present |
| V | Distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) involved | Absent |
| VI | DRUJ involved | Present |
| VII | Both radiocarpal and DRUJ involved | Absent |
| VIII | Both radiocarpal and DRUJ involved | Present |

Management implication: increasing Frykman grade (odd→even pairs add ulnar styloid fracture; ascending numbers add joint involvement) correlates with higher rates of DRUJ instability, worse functional outcome with nonoperative treatment, and a lower threshold for operative fixation — types VII–VIII (both joints + styloid) carry the highest risk of chronic DRUJ instability and post-traumatic arthrosis and warrant careful intraoperative DRUJ stress testing regardless of radius fixation method. Frykman is largely of historical/descriptive value today (superseded by AO/OTA and Fernandez for surgical planning) but remains widely referenced in outcome literature.

### Fernandez classification (mechanism-based)

| Type | Mechanism | Pattern | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Bending (metaphyseal) | Extra-articular fracture (Colles/Smith) | Closed reduction/cast if stable; percutaneous pinning or plating if unstable |
| II | Shearing | Articular fracture of the joint surface (Barton, radial styloid/Chauffeur) | Inherently unstable — ORIF (buttress plating) essentially always required |
| III | Compression | Articular impaction/die-punch, comminuted central depression | Requires elevation/bone graft or substitute, fragment-specific or dorsal plating; arthroscopic-assisted reduction useful |
| IV | Avulsion | Radiocarpal fracture-dislocation with ligamentous avulsion (styloid processes) | High association with DRUJ/carpal ligament instability — fix bony fragments and reassess/treat ligamentous stability |
| V | Combined/high-energy | Combination of above + severe soft tissue injury (high-velocity, e.g., MVC, fall from height) | Often needs staged management, external fixation, soft tissue coverage planning; highest complication risk |

Fernandez is favored by many surgeons for **surgical decision-making** because it explicitly separates injury mechanism/soft-tissue implication (unlike AO/OTA's purely morphologic scheme), directly informing fixation strategy (buttress vs bridging vs combined).

### Melone classification (intra-articular, four-part concept — die-punch/complex fractures)

Describes the distal radius articular surface as **four principal fragments**: the radial shaft, the radial styloid, and the medial complex split into **dorsomedial (dorsal lunate facet)** and **volomedial (volar lunate facet)** fragments — the medial complex is the key load-bearing articular block.

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Stable, minimally displaced 4-part pattern | Closed reduction/cast may suffice; monitor closely |
| II | Unstable, displaced medial complex ("die-punch") — reducible (IIa) or irreducible/comminuted (IIb) | ORIF required; IIb often needs fragment-specific fixation as the medial complex will not reduce closed |
| III | Type II + volar spike fragment | Volar buttressing (plate) mandatory to prevent carpal translation |
| IV | Split medial fragment with rotation (dorsopalmar articular rotation) | Complex fixation, may require dorsal + volar combined approach or fragment-specific system to control rotated fragments |
| V | Severe explosive comminution with metaphyseal-diaphyseal extension | Bridging external fixation ± limited internal fixation, often staged; highest risk of malunion/nonunion |

Management implication overall: Melone's value is in emphasizing the **medial (lunate) facet** as the critical stability determinant — failure to control the volar ulnar/lunate facet fragment (often too small/distal for a standard volar plate's most distal row) is a classic cause of secondary carpal subluxation; drives use of fragment-specific plates/spike plates for the medial complex.

### Palmer classification (TFCC injuries)

| Class | Subtype | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Traumatic** | 1A | Central perforation/tear of the articular disc | Avascular zone — debridement (arthroscopic) if symptomatic; not repaired |
| | 1B | Peripheral (ulnar) avulsion, ± ulnar styloid fracture | Well-vascularized zone — primary repair (arthroscopic or open) if DRUJ unstable or symptoms persist after conservative trial |
| | 1C | Distal avulsion (ulnocarpal ligaments — ulnolunate/ulnotriquetral) | Repair if causing ulnocarpal instability; can be technically demanding arthroscopically |
| | 1D | Radial-sided avulsion, ± sigmoid notch fracture | Repair (transosseous technique to radius) if DRUJ instability present |
| **2 — Degenerative (ulnocarpal impaction spectrum)** | 2A | TFCC wear, no perforation | Conservative (activity modification, ulnar shortening not usually indicated yet) |
| | 2B | TFCC wear + lunate and/or ulnar chondromalacia | Conservative first; consider ulnar shortening osteotomy if positive ulnar variance and impaction symptoms |
| | 2C | TFCC perforation + lunate/ulnar chondromalacia | Debridement ± ulnar shortening |
| | 2D | TFCC perforation + chondromalacia + lunotriquetral ligament perforation | Debridement, possible LT stabilization, ulnar shortening |
| | 2E | Above + ulnocarpal arthritis | Salvage (e.g., wafer procedure, ulnar shortening, or arthroplasty/fusion depending on severity) |

Note for this monograph's scope (acute trauma): **Palmer Class 1 (traumatic)** is the clinically relevant category for acute DRF/DRUJ-associated TFCC injury; Class 2 (degenerative/ulnocarpal impaction) is a separate chronic overload pathway, included here for completeness of the classification but managed along a different, non-trauma pathway. Note: newer arthroscopic classification schemes (e.g., the 2022–2023 "CUP" — Central/Ulnar/Peripheral — classification, and a disc/reins/wall "D-R-W" scheme) have been proposed to address Palmer's limitations in guiding repair technique, but Palmer remains the clinical reference standard for communication and coding.

## Treatment

**Shared decision-making framework (elderly, borderline-unstable DRF)**: the trial evidence above (DRAFFT2, WRIST, VOLCON, DRIFT, DART) supports presenting patients with a genuinely balanced choice rather than a default recommendation in most borderline cases:
- Casting/nonoperative care: avoids surgical risk, generally similar 12-month PRWE/functional outcome to surgery in most elderly cohorts, but slower early recovery and a meaningful (~13% in DRAFFT2's cast arm, lower with VLP as the fallback) chance of needing rescue surgery for redisplacement.
- Surgical fixation (VLP first-line, K-wire for simpler patterns): faster early functional recovery and, per WRIST secondary analysis, a lower overall complication rate than pinning/external fixation/casting — but carries surgical risk (tendon injury, infection, anesthesia) and is a bigger initial commitment.
- The factors that should tip the decision are **pre-injury activity level, comorbidity burden, cognitive status/ability to comply with cast care, hand dominance and occupational demands, and explicit patient preference for speed of recovery versus avoidance of surgery** — not chronologic age in isolation.

### Non-operative

**Distal radius fracture**: closed reduction under hematoma block or procedural sedation for displaced fractures, followed by well-molded below- or above-elbow cast/splint in slight flexion and ulnar deviation (avoid extreme flexion — "Cotton-Loder" position is obsolete and associated with median nerve compression and stiffness). Weekly radiographs for the first 3 weeks are standard practice to detect early redisplacement (highest risk window), then at 6 weeks. Total immobilization typically 4–6 weeks, transitioning to a removable orthosis for the final 1–2 weeks in cooperative, stable patients to permit early elbow/finger motion. Acceptable alignment thresholds (see Quick Reference) should be defined **in advance** based on patient age/functional demand — the same displacement tolerated in a low-demand 80-year-old is an indication for surgery in an active 45-year-old.

**DRUJ instability (acute, reducible, stable in a defined arc)**: immobilize the forearm in the rotational position of maximal stability (commonly supination for volar TFCC/ligament injury, or the position found stable on intraoperative/clinical stress testing) in a long-arm cast or Muenster-type orthosis for 4–6 weeks, then progressive rotational ROM.

**Acute TFCC tear (Palmer 1B and other peripheral/traumatic types without gross DRUJ instability)**: first-line management is immobilization (wrist splint/cast) 4–6 weeks + NSAIDs, followed by structured hand therapy emphasizing progressive ROM and forearm rotator strengthening. A substantial proportion improve without surgery; surgical referral is reserved for those failing 3–6 months of conservative management or presenting with objective DRUJ instability.

**Isolated ulnar styloid fracture**: tip fractures — early protected motion in a wrist splint 2–3 weeks is sufficient; the vast majority unite or become an asymptomatic fibrous nonunion without functional consequence, since the foveal (deep) ligament insertion is typically preserved. Base fractures without DRUJ instability follow the same non-operative pathway but warrant explicit DRUJ stability assessment before committing to non-op care.

**Cast technique notes**: a well-molded three-point (interosseous, dorsal, volar) short-arm cast is as effective as a long-arm cast for most extra-articular fractures and is better tolerated; long-arm/Muenster casting is reserved for cases needing rotational control (e.g., DRUJ instability co-management, some pediatric-adjacent young-adult patterns) since forearm rotation control, not elbow immobilization, is the actual biomechanical goal. Avoid circumferential cast application in the first 24–48 hours in a swelling-prone injury — use a well-padded sugar-tong or volar/dorsal splint initially, converting to a definitive cast once acute swelling has plateaued (typically 5–10 days), which also reduces compartment syndrome/ACTS risk from an overly tight primary cast.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Hematoma block** (intra-fracture lidocaine injection) or **Bier block/procedural sedation** for closed reduction of displaced DRF in the ED — hematoma block is adequate for most closed reductions and avoids sedation risk, particularly relevant in elderly, comorbid patients.
- **Diagnostic/therapeutic corticosteroid injection into the DRUJ or ulnocarpal space** for chronic or subacute TFCC-pattern ulnar-sided wrist pain when the diagnosis is uncertain or as a bridge while conservative therapy is trialed — response supports (but does not confirm) TFCC/DRUJ origin and can defer arthroscopy in lower-demand patients.
- **Ulnar shortening is not an acute-injury procedure** — it belongs to the degenerative Palmer Class 2 impaction pathway, not acute Class 1 trauma; mentioned here only to avoid conflating pathways when a chronic TFCC picture is encountered.

### Operative

**Volar locking plate (VLP) fixation — the workhorse for unstable/displaced DRF**
- **Indications**: failure to achieve/maintain acceptable alignment by closed means, Fernandez II/III/IV/V patterns, AO/OTA 23-B3 (volar Barton) and most 23-C patterns, articular step/gap >2 mm in an active patient, dorsally comminuted fractures with volar cortical continuity.
- **Approach**: modified Henry (flexor carpi radialis) approach, plate placed proximal to the "watershed line" to avoid flexor tendon irritation/rupture (a well-documented late complication of distal plate malposition — FPL rupture from a prominent distal plate edge is the classic pitfall).
- **Technique pearls**: restore radial length/inclination first (often via ligamentotaxis with a provisional distraction/traction tower), then address volar tilt; use locking screws in the distal (subchondral) row for osteoporotic/comminuted metaphyseal bone; confirm distal screw length under multiple fluoroscopic views (including a "skyline"/tangential view) to avoid dorsal cortex penetration and extensor tendon injury (EPL rupture is a recognized delayed complication even with correctly measured screws, from direct trauma or attritional wear); consider fragment-specific plates or a dorsal buttress/spanning plate for die-punch (Melone medial complex) fragments not controlled by the volar plate alone.
- **Pitfalls**: over-reliance on VLP for dorsally comminuted, volarly deficient fractures without addressing the dorsal defect (risk of loss of volar tilt); missing a small volar-ulnar (lunate facet) fragment too distal/small for standard plate purchase — a leading cause of early fixation failure and carpal subluxation.

**K-wire (percutaneous pinning) fixation**
- **Indications**: extra-articular or simple intra-articular fractures, especially in younger patients (<65 years per DRAFFT2 eligibility) with dorsally displaced patterns amenable to closed/limited-open reduction; useful where VLP is not readily available or as definitive treatment when reduction is stable after pinning.
- **Evidence**: DRAFFT2 (multicentre UK RCT, 500 adults) showed no significant PRWE difference between cast and K-wire fixation at 12 months (mean 21.2 vs 20.7, adjusted difference −0.34, p=0.87), but 13% of the cast group required rescue surgical fixation for loss of position versus essentially none in the K-wire group — meaning K-wire fixation trades an upfront procedure for a substantially lower redisplacement/rescue-surgery rate, an important shared-decision-making point.

**External fixation** (bridging or non-bridging)
- **Indications**: severely comminuted metaphyseal-diaphyseal fractures, open fractures with soft tissue compromise, damage-control in polytrauma, as an adjunct/augment to internal fixation in Fernandez V patterns.
- **Evidence**: recent meta-analyses of RCTs (2024–2025) comparing VLP to external fixation generally favor VLP for earlier functional recovery and fewer pin-site complications, with external fixation reserved for cases unsuitable for plate fixation (soft tissue, comminution, damage control).

**Volar locking plate vs cast in the elderly — the current evidence base**
This is one of the most extensively re-litigated questions in trauma surgery over the past decade, and the practicing surgeon should be able to cite the trial landscape:
- **WRIST trial** (multicentre US/international RCT, adults ≥60 y, unstable DRF; 4 arms — VLP, external fixation ± pinning, percutaneous pinning, casting): at 12 and 24 months, functional outcome scores converged across groups by 1 year, but internal fixation (VLP) was associated with significantly fewer complications than the other treatment arms in secondary analysis, and patients with high pre-injury activity level and low comorbidity burden showed clinically meaningful early benefit from surgical treatment — supporting an individualized, activity/comorbidity-stratified rather than purely age-stratified decision.
- **VOLCON trial** (Netherlands RCT, patients >65 y with unstable DRF, VLP vs casting): informed European practice toward more selective/conservative use of VLP in the elderly given comparable functional outcomes with fewer surgical complications in the cast group, while noting cast patients accept greater residual deformity.
- **DRIFT trial** (RCT, ≥65 y, fractures failing to maintain acceptable alignment after closed reduction, n=291, VLP vs nonoperative; 12-month PRWE primary outcome, 2025 publication) — the most direct, contemporary answer to "what do I do when an elderly patient's fracture is truly unstable/unacceptable after reduction": adds to the evidence that even in this scenario, differences in PRWE between fixation and acceptance of malalignment are often small at 12 months, though surgical patients tend to recover function faster in the early months.
- **DART trial** (RCT, noninferiority design, cast vs surgery for displaced intra-articular DRF in the elderly, JBJS Open Access 2025) — reinforces noninferiority of casting for many displaced intra-articular patterns in lower-demand elderly patients.
- **Bottom line for practice**: across this trial body (VOLCON, WRIST, DRIFT, DART), 12-month patient-reported outcomes converge between VLP and nonoperative treatment for most elderly patients with unstable/malaligned DRF; the real, reproducible surgical advantages are **faster early functional recovery** and (in WRIST) a **lower overall complication rate with internal fixation versus other treatment modalities** — not a durable long-term PRWE superiority. Decision-making should therefore weight **pre-injury activity level, comorbidity burden, cognitive status/ability to tolerate a cast, and patient preference for speed of recovery** over chronologic age alone; a 2023 secondary WRIST analysis explicitly recommends stratifying by physiologic rather than chronologic age.

**TFCC repair (arthroscopic or open)**
- **Indications**: Palmer 1B peripheral tears with DRUJ instability or failed 3–6 months conservative treatment; 1C/1D tears causing instability; intraoperative finding of TFCC disruption with persistent DRUJ instability after anatomic DRF/ulnar styloid fixation.
- **Technique**: arthroscopic outside-in or inside-out peripheral repair is favored for isolated 1B tears (well-vascularized zone, minimally invasive, allows concurrent assessment/treatment of associated chondral or ligamentous injury); open repair (transosseous foveal reattachment) is preferred for foveal (deep-fiber) disruption not adequately addressed by peripheral capsular repair, and for combined bony (styloid base) + ligamentous injury requiring direct fixation. Recent systematic reviews (2024–2025) show broadly equivalent outcomes between open and arthroscopic TFCC repair, so approach selection should follow tear pattern/foveal involvement and surgeon experience rather than a presumed superiority of one technique.
- **1A central tears**: debrided, not repaired, given the avascular central zone — repair attempts here have historically poor healing rates.

**DRUJ stabilization**
- **Acute reducible instability persisting after anatomic radius/styloid fixation**: transfix the DRUJ with 1–2 smooth K-wires (radius-to-ulna, proximal to the joint, avoiding the sigmoid notch) with the forearm held in the stable rotational position, combined with immobilization for 4–6 weeks; wires typically removed at 4–6 weeks once soft-tissue healing (TFCC/capsule) is presumed adequate.
- **Irreducible DRUJ dislocation**: open reduction is required — most commonly due to entrapment of the ECU tendon (dorsal dislocation) or extensor tendons/periosteum within the joint; a systematic review of acute isolated DRUJ dislocations (2024–2025) emphasizes that irreducibility is the key trigger for urgent open exploration rather than repeated closed attempts.
- **Basilar ulnar styloid fracture with confirmed DRUJ instability**: fix with tension-band wiring, a headless compression screw, or a small hook plate, restoring the foveal ligament attachment; isolated tip fractures or fractures without demonstrated instability generally do **not** require fixation (see ulnar styloid rehabilitation notes below) even though they radiographically appear un-united at follow-up — a large body of meta-analytic evidence in DRF-associated styloid nonunion shows no functional penalty from an ununited tip fragment when the DRUJ is clinically stable, though more recent (2024) data on **isolated** base-of-styloid nonunions specifically show reduced motion, lower functional scores, more pain, and a higher rate of positive ballottement testing in the nonunion group — a distinction the surgeon should draw explicitly between DRF-associated tip nonunions (benign) and isolated/basilar nonunions (clinically consequential).

### Special scenarios

- **Open distal radius fracture**: irrigation and debridement per standard open-fracture protocol (antibiotics on presentation, timely debridement), with definitive fixation (usually VLP) either at the index procedure or staged depending on wound contamination/soft-tissue status; low threshold for plastic surgery input if there is a volar soft-tissue defect over the planned plate site.
- **Bilateral distal radius fractures**: common in high-energy falls/skiing-type injuries in younger patients and in low-energy same-level falls with poor protective reflexes in the elderly; strongly favor operative fixation of at least one side to preserve independent function (feeding, hygiene, transfers) during recovery, particularly in patients living alone.
- **Anticoagulated/antiplatelet patients**: closed reduction and splinting should not be delayed for anticoagulation reversal; definitive surgical timing follows standard peri-procedural anticoagulation-bridging protocols, balanced against BOAST/NICE timing targets — early multidisciplinary (hematology/cardiology) input avoids unnecessary delay past the 72-hour/1-week windows.
- **Neglected/missed DRUJ instability presenting late (weeks to months out)**: options narrow to soft-tissue reconstruction (e.g., tendon graft ligament reconstruction of the DRUJ) or, in low-demand/degenerative presentations, salvage procedures (Sauvé-Kapandji, Darrach, or DRUJ arthroplasty) — outside the acute-injury scope of this monograph but worth flagging as the downstream consequence of an unrecognized acute instability, reinforcing why intraoperative and follow-up DRUJ assessment matters.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

### Phase overview

| Phase | Timeline | Cast/nonoperative | K-wire | VLP (operative) | DRUJ K-wire/TFCC repair |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0–2 wk | Immobilized; digit/elbow/shoulder AROM day 1 | Immobilized; digit AROM day 1; protect pin sites | Protected splint; digit AROM day 1 | Immobilized in stable rotational position; digit AROM |
| 1 | 2–6 wk | Weekly films to wk 3; removable orthosis + gentle wrist AROM from ~wk 4–5 | Pins in place to wk 4–6; no wrist ROM until pin removal | Protected active wrist ROM from wk 1–2 | Forearm rotation blocked until wire removal (~wk 4–6) |
| 2 | 6–12 wk | Progressive AROM/AAROM, light ADLs, grip strengthening from wk 6–8 | Pins removed ~wk 4–6, then progressive wrist ROM | Progressive strengthening, scar mobilization | Progressive rotational ROM after wire removal; TFCC repair protected rotation to wk 6 |
| 3 | 12–16+ wk | Progressive resistive strengthening, proprioception, occupational/sport-specific reconditioning | Same as Phase 3 general pathway | Same as Phase 3 general pathway | Full strengthening once union/repair maturity confirmed |

**Phase 0 (0–2 weeks, post-reduction/post-op)**: elevation, edema control, active finger/thumb and elbow/shoulder ROM from day 1 regardless of treatment method (cast, K-wire, or plate) to prevent stiffness and disuse; cryotherapy; strict avoidance of forearm rotation and wrist loading. Weekly radiographs (non-operative arm) for redisplacement surveillance through week 3. Continued explicit documentation of median nerve status at each visit.

**Phase 1 (2–6 weeks)**:
- Cast/nonoperative: maintain immobilization; transition to removable orthosis around week 4–5 in cooperative patients with stable radiographic position, beginning gentle active wrist ROM within the orthosis.
- K-wire: pins typically remain 4–6 weeks; protect pin sites, active digit motion continues; wrist ROM deferred until pin removal.
- VLP (operative): given rigid fixation, early protected active wrist ROM typically begins at 1–2 weeks post-op (surgeon/construct-dependent), progressing gently; this early-motion advantage is one of the practical benefits of internal fixation over casting.
- DRUJ K-wire transfixation: forearm rotation is blocked until wire removal (~4–6 weeks); focus therapy on digit/elbow/shoulder motion.

**Phase 2 (6–12 weeks)**: progressive active and active-assisted wrist flexion/extension, radial/ulnar deviation, and forearm pronosupination; begin light functional activities of daily living; scar mobilization if operative; initiate gentle grip strengthening around week 6–8 once union is radiographically progressing. For TFCC repairs, forearm rotation is typically restricted to protected/limited arcs until 6 weeks post-repair, then progressed. Formal physiotherapy per **AAOS/APTA (Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy) 2024 CPG on distal radius fracture rehabilitation** emphasizes early active ROM, edema management, and graded strengthening with ICF-linked outcome tracking (PRWE, DASH/QuickDASH, grip dynamometry).

**Phase 3 (12–16+ weeks)**: progressive resistive strengthening (grip, wrist flexor/extensor, forearm rotators), proprioceptive/weight-bearing exercises reintroducing axial load through the wrist, sport- or occupation-specific reconditioning.

**Weight-bearing / loading milestones**: light functional loading (typing, light ADLs) generally permitted from ~6 weeks once early union present; unrestricted loading/impact activity or weight-bearing through the wrist (push-ups, weightlifting) typically deferred to ~12 weeks and only after radiographic union confirmed and adequate strength/ROM regained; return to contact/collision sport generally 3–4 months, individualized to fracture pattern, fixation stability, and sport demands.

**Return-to-work criteria**: sedentary/desk work often feasible within 1–2 weeks post-reduction (non-dominant hand) or once splinted comfortably; manual labor/heavy lifting typically deferred until ~12 weeks with radiographic union and functional grip strength (commonly targeting ≥70–80% of contralateral grip) and near-full pronosupination.

**Return-to-sport criteria**: pain-free full composite ROM, grip strength ≥85–90% of contralateral side, radiographic union, and sport-specific functional testing (e.g., push-up/weight-bearing tolerance for gymnastics, racquet-loading for tennis) — generally not before 3 months for high-demand/impact sport, individualized for TFCC repairs where forearm rotation loading is the rate-limiting recovery domain.

**Outcome measures to track longitudinally**: Patient-Rated Wrist Evaluation (PRWE) — the primary outcome in most modern DRF trials (DRAFFT2, WRIST, VOLCON, DRIFT); DASH/QuickDASH for broader upper-limb function; grip strength via dynamometer (compare to contralateral, express as percentage); goniometric wrist flexion/extension, radial/ulnar deviation, and forearm pronation/supination; visual analog pain scale. Document at baseline (where feasible), 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months to match the timepoints used in the trial literature cited above, which supports meaningful comparison of an individual patient's trajectory against published benchmarks.

## Complications

- **Malunion**: the most common complication of nonoperative DRF treatment, particularly dorsal angulation/radial shortening. Symptomatic malunion (pain, restricted rotation, weakness, ulnocarpal impaction, cosmetic deformity) in a functionally demanding patient is addressed with corrective (usually opening-wedge, volar-approach) osteotomy, restoring radial length/inclination/tilt with structural bone graft or substitute and plate fixation; concomitant ulnar-sided procedures (ulnar shortening osteotomy or, less commonly, distal ulna resection) are added when ulnar impaction or DRUJ incongruity persists after radius realignment. Corrective osteotomy is generally deferred until the malunion is symptomatic and functionally limiting rather than performed for radiographic appearance alone.
- **Median nerve injury / acute carpal tunnel syndrome**: 4.5–9% incidence; requires vigilant serial exam and low threshold for urgent carpal tunnel release + fracture stabilization if progressive.
- **Tendon rupture**: EPL rupture (spontaneous, delayed, even in nonoperative fractures — attritional over a healing dorsal ridge, or from a prominent dorsal screw) and FPL rupture (classically from a volar plate placed distal to the watershed line, mechanically abrading against the tendon) are the two hallmark late tendon complications; index of suspicion should trigger urgent surgical exploration/tendon transfer planning.
- **Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)**: reported in a meaningful minority of DRF patients (historically cited ~1–37% depending on diagnostic criteria and population, more consistently in the low single digits to low teens with modern criteria); early mobilization and adequate analgesia are the principal modifiable mitigations; vitamin C prophylaxis has mixed but historically supportive evidence in some fracture populations.
- **DRUJ instability / chronic ulnar-sided pain**: if unrecognized/untreated, leads to chronic instability, painful clicking, weakness in grip and forearm rotation, and accelerated DRUJ arthrosis.
- **TFCC nonunion/persistent tear after repair**: peripheral repairs generally heal well given vascularity; failure more likely with missed foveal (deep) component or inadequate immobilization compliance.
- **Post-traumatic osteoarthritis**: strongly correlated with residual articular incongruity (>2 mm step/gap) — the principal justification for anatomic articular reduction in intra-articular (23-B/C, Fernandez II/III, Melone II–V) fracture patterns.
- **Ulnar styloid nonunion**: generally benign when the DRUJ is stable (no functional penalty per pooled DRF-associated meta-analytic data); isolated basilar nonunions are the exception, with 2024 data showing reduced motion, function, and higher DRUJ instability rates — warranting closer follow-up and a lower threshold for delayed fixation if symptomatic.
- **Stiffness**: the dominant complication of prolonged immobilization or inadequate early therapy engagement — underscores the rationale for early digit/elbow motion regardless of fixation method.
- **Infection/hardware complications**: pin-site infection with K-wires/ex-fix (generally superficial, managed with local care ± oral antibiotics); deep infection with VLP is uncommon but requires standard fracture-related infection principles (debridement, culture-directed antibiotics, hardware retention vs removal based on stability/timing).
- **Delayed union / nonunion of the radius**: uncommon in metaphyseal DRF given the rich cancellous bone bed, but should be suspected with persistent pain and lack of radiographic progression beyond 3–4 months, particularly in smokers, uncontrolled diabetics, or after high-energy comminuted patterns; may require revision fixation with bone grafting.
- **Loss of forearm rotation**: most often from DRUJ incongruity/malunion (radial shortening or malrotation), heterotopic ossification of the interosseous membrane after high-energy injury, or under-recognized TFCC/DRUJ pathology — pronosupination deficit should always trigger a specific search for DRUJ-level pathology rather than being attributed to generic "stiffness."
- **Extensor/flexor tendon irritation short of rupture (tenosynovitis)**: common with prominent hardware; monitor clinically and consider elective hardware removal (typically not before 1 year, once union is secure) if symptomatic.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[DRIFT trial — Nonoperative treatment versus volar locking plating for distal radius fracture in patients aged 65 years or older](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pmed.1004728)** (PLOS Medicine, 2025): RCT of 124 elderly patients whose fractures failed to maintain acceptable alignment after closed reduction; adds directly to the "truly unstable elderly fracture" decision point, reinforcing that even here, 12-month PRWE differences between VLP and accepted malalignment are modest, while surgery still confers faster early recovery.
- **[Volar Locking Plate versus Closed Reduction and Immobilization for Distal Radius Fracture in the Elderly: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of RCTs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11606675/)** (2024): pooled RCT data in patients ≥60 confirming comparable functional outcomes between VLP and casting with a shift in emphasis to complication rates and speed of recovery as the true discriminators — practice-changing in de-emphasizing "operate on every displaced elderly fracture."
- **[VOLCON RCT — complications and functional outcome of volar plating vs casting in patients >65 years](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9705678/)**: foundational European RCT (with continued citation/secondary analyses through the 2023–2025 window) supporting selective, rather than routine, surgical fixation in the elderly.
- **[DART Study — cast versus surgery for displaced intra-articular distal radius fractures in the elderly](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12425212/)** (JBJS Open Access, 2025): noninferiority RCT extending the casting-is-often-sufficient conclusion specifically to displaced intra-articular patterns in lower-demand elderly patients, broadening the population in which nonoperative care is defensible.
- **[DRAFFT2 — Surgical fixation with K-wires versus casting in adults with fracture of distal radius](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8767805/)** (BMJ 2022, with continued 2023–2024 secondary radiographic analyses e.g. [Bone & Joint Open](https://boneandjoint.org.uk/Article/10.1302/2633-1462.52.BJO-2023-0149)): no 12-month PRWE difference cast vs K-wire, but a clinically important 13% vs ~0% rescue-fixation rate for redisplacement in the cast arm — the key number to quote when counseling adults on cast-first management with close radiographic surveillance.
- **[WRIST trial secondary analyses — complication rates by treatment arm](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2720916) and [physiologic vs chronologic age stratification](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9926326/)** (JAMA Network Open): internal fixation (VLP) shows significantly fewer complications than pinning, external fixation, or casting, and outcomes stratify better by pre-injury activity level/comorbidity burden than by chronologic age — together reframing the operative-vs-nonoperative debate around complication burden and physiologic (not chronologic) age.
- **[A New Arthroscopic Classification of TFCC Disorders](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10803146/)** and **[TFCC injuries — limitations of current classification systems and the proposed 'CUP' classification](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36113053/)** (2023–2024): proposed refinements to Palmer's scheme intended to better link tear morphology to arthroscopic repair technique; not yet a replacement for Palmer in routine clinical communication but relevant when planning arthroscopic repair.
- **[Arthroscopic algorithm for acute traumatic TFCC tears](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11626739/)** (Journal of Hand Microsurgery, Jan 2025): consolidates a decision algorithm for acute Palmer Class 1 tears (repair vs debridement vs observation) based on tear location and DRUJ stability — useful as a structured operative decision aid.
- **[Understanding isolated ulnar styloid fractures — nonunion and healing outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41438372/) and [should the ulnar styloid be fixed following distal radius fracture fixation?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37210301/)** (2023–2025): together, these distinguish isolated (non-DRF-associated) basilar ulnar styloid nonunion — associated with reduced motion, lower function scores, more pain, and higher DRUJ instability rates — from the generally benign DRF-associated tip nonunion, and reinforce that fixation should be reserved for demonstrated post-radius-fixation DRUJ instability rather than performed routinely.
- **[Systematic Review of Acute Isolated Distal Radioulnar Joint Dislocation: Treatment Options](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11728307/)** (2024–2025): clarifies that irreducibility (typically ECU or extensor tendon entrapment) is the key indicator for open exploration versus continued closed management of acute DRUJ dislocation.
- **[Assessment Strategies for DRUJ Instability: Current State and a Need for Improved Tools](https://www.jhandsurg.org/article/S0363-5023(25)00375-2/fulltext)** (Journal of Hand Surgery, 2025): highlights that despite ballottement/fovea sign/press test being in routine use, objective, reproducible instability grading remains an unmet need — relevant caveat when documenting "stable" vs "unstable" intraoperatively.
- **[Timely Fixation of Distal Radius Fractures: Improving Compliance with NICE and BOAST Guidelines](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12741471/)** (2024–2025 audits): confirms and operationalizes the BOAST/NICE timing standards (72-hour fracture clinic review; surgery within 72 h for intra-articular/re-displaced fractures, within 1 week for extra-articular) as the current UK benchmark, with ongoing system-level compliance gaps documented in contemporaneous audits ([System-Level Barriers to NICE Guideline Compliance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12728452/)).
- **[Distal Radius Fracture Rehabilitation — AAOS/APTA Clinical Practice Guidelines linked to ICF](https://www.jospt.org/doi/10.2519/jospt.2024.0301)** (JOSPT, Aug 2024): the current reference rehabilitation CPG from the Academy of Orthopaedic Physical Therapy/Academy of Hand and Upper Extremity Physical Therapy, structuring post-fracture therapy content and outcome tracking (PRWE/DASH) used in the Rehabilitation Protocol section above.
- **[Does Radiographic Alignment Predict Functional Outcomes in Elderly Patients With Distal Radius Fractures? — comprehensive review](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12535427/)** (2025): reinforces that in low-demand elderly patients, grip strength and ROM are more clinically meaningful predictors of patient-reported outcome than strict radiographic alignment parameters — supports a more permissive alignment-acceptance strategy in this population when counseled appropriately.
- **[Utility of Ultrasound for Identifying Median Nerve Changes Indicative of Acute Carpal Tunnel Syndrome After Distal Radius Fracture](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10382869/)** (JHS Global Online, 2023): early biomechanical/imaging data proposing quantitative median nerve cross-sectional-area/height-width-ratio thresholds for ACTS — investigational, not yet a replacement for serial clinical exam, but worth knowing as ultrasound-based ACTS monitoring is likely to mature over the next several years.
