## Quick Reference

**Anatomic zones** (proximal femur, distal to proximal): femoral head → femoral neck → intertrochanteric region (between greater and lesser trochanters) → subtrochanteric region (lesser trochanter to 5 cm distal, or by AO definition to the isthmus). Femoral head fractures are intra-articular shear/impaction injuries almost always associated with hip dislocation.

**Timing standard (all fragility hip fractures):** surgery within **24–48 h** of presentation (AAOS 2021 CPG, strong recommendation); UK NICE/BOAST Best Practice Tariff target is **<36 h from arrival**. Delay beyond 48 h is associated with roughly 20% higher 12-month mortality. Medical optimization should never exceed 24–48 h except for correctable, specific contraindications (e.g., reversible anticoagulation, active cardiac ischemia, uncontrolled electrolyte/glucose derangement) — "fit for surgery," not "medically perfect," is the standard.

**Universal fragility-fracture bundle:**

- Orthogeriatric co-management from admission.
- Same-day nerve block or regional analgesia (fascia iliaca/femoral nerve block).
- Tranexamic acid (strong AAOS recommendation) unless contraindicated.
- VTE chemoprophylaxis.
- Early mobilization POD0–1.
- Bone-health assessment and anti-osteoporotic therapy initiated before discharge.
- Delirium screening (CAM).
- Nutrition assessment and pressure-injury prevention.

### Classification-driven treatment ladder

| Diagnosis | Non-operative | First-line operative | Alternative/second-line |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Femoral neck, non-displaced/valgus-impacted (Garden I–II)** | Rarely in non-ambulatory, low-demand, high-risk patients | In situ percutaneous fixation (cannulated screws or FNS) | Sliding hip screw ± derotation screw |
| **Femoral neck, displaced (Garden III–IV)**, young/biological age <60 | Not appropriate | Urgent (<6–12 h ideal) anatomic closed/open reduction + internal fixation to limit AVN risk | If reduction fails/comminuted → arthroplasty |
| **Femoral neck, displaced, elderly (>60–65, low-demand)** | Not appropriate (except comfort-care) | Hemiarthroplasty (cemented) | THA if active/independent, good cognition, low dislocation risk |
| **Intertrochanteric, stable (31-A1)** | Non-ambulatory/end-of-life patients only | Sliding hip screw (SHS) or cephalomedullary nail — equivalent outcomes | — |
| **Intertrochanteric, unstable (31-A2/A3, reverse oblique, subtrochanteric extension)** | Not appropriate | Cephalomedullary nail (CMN) | SHS with trochanteric stabilization plate if nail unavailable |
| **Subtrochanteric** | Not appropriate (traction is temporizing only) | Long cephalomedullary nail with anatomic/near-anatomic reduction | Locking plate if nail contraindicated (severe deformity/implant in situ) |
| **Femoral head (Pipkin)** | Type I, minimally displaced (<2 mm), congruent joint, stable | ORIF or fragment excision (Type I/II) via surgical hip dislocation or anterior approach | Arthroplasty for comminuted/elderly/delayed presentation |

**Red flags:**

- Shortened/externally rotated limb after low-energy fall in the elderly = femoral neck or IT fracture until proven otherwise.
- Hip fracture + ipsilateral knee pain → screen for femoral shaft/distal femur fracture ("floating knee" or segmental pattern).
- Posterior hip dislocation with groin pain and inability to fully extend/internally rotate → suspect Pipkin femoral head fracture and sciatic nerve injury.
- Subtrochanteric fracture in a patient on long-term bisphosphonate/denosumab with a lateral cortex "beak" and transverse pattern → atypical femur fracture (AFF) — image the contralateral femur.
- Normal radiograph with persistent groin pain and inability to weight-bear → occult fracture, obtain MRI within 24–48 h.

**Key numbers:**

- Pauwels angle >50° = high shear, fixation-failure risk.
- Garden alignment index target 155–180° in both AP and lateral.
- Acceptable varus reduction in IT fractures — neutral to slight valgus; avoid >5° malreduction.
- Tip-apex distance (TAD) <25 mm predicts screw cutout <2% (vs. >20% above threshold).
- Neck-shaft angle restoration within 5° of the contralateral side for subtrochanteric fixation.
- Nail entry point for antegrade CMN — tip of greater trochanter (piriformis-entry nails largely obsolete for standard trauma nailing).
- Transfusion threshold: generally Hb <8 g/dL (restrictive strategy), most centers now favor this over the liberal <10 g/dL threshold per the FOCUS trial framework.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The proximal femur's blood supply is the dominant determinant of fracture behavior and treatment. The **medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA)**, via its deep branch running along the posterosuperior femoral neck within the retinacular (Weitbrecht) fibers, supplies the majority of the femoral head in adults; the **lateral femoral circumflex artery** contributes anteroinferiorly; the **artery of the ligamentum teres** (foveal artery, from obturator/MFCA) is a minor, often insufficient source in adults, more important in children. Displaced intracapsular (subcapital/transcervical) fractures tear the retinacular vessels, producing a variable but clinically significant risk of **femoral head osteonecrosis (AVN)** and nonunion — the rationale for both urgent anatomic reduction in younger patients and for arthroplasty (bypassing the vascular problem entirely) in low-demand elderly patients with displaced fractures.

The femoral neck experiences combined bending and shear loads; the inferomedial neck (calcar femorale) is the primary compressive trabecular buttress. Loss of calcar/medial cortical continuity (comminution) is the chief determinant of mechanical instability in both femoral neck and intertrochanteric fractures and drives implant selection (sliding/compressive devices vs. fixed-angle constructs).

The intertrochanteric region is extracapsular and richly vascularized (metaphyseal/cancellous bone) — union rates are high (>95%) regardless of implant, and the clinical problem is mechanical (implant cutout, varus collapse, medialization) rather than vascular. The subtrochanteric region is biomechanically the highest-stress zone in the femur: the medial cortex sustains massive compressive load and the lateral cortex tensile load, largely cortical (not cancellous) bone with a comparatively poor blood supply — hence high rates of delayed union/nonunion if reduction or fixation is suboptimal, and the classic deforming forces (iliopsoas flexes/externally rotates the proximal fragment, abductors abduct it, adductors pull the distal fragment into varus/shortening) must be neutralized with the nail/plate, not merely by traction.

Femoral head fractures are shear or impaction injuries of the articular surface, virtually always occurring with a hip dislocation (typically posterior, occasionally anterior with an indented anterior/inferior head fragment). Sciatic nerve injury (peroneal division especially) accompanies 10–20% of posterior fracture-dislocations and must be documented pre- and post-reduction.

### Diagnosis-specific pathophysiology summary

- **Femoral neck fracture:** intracapsular; vascular (AVN/nonunion) risk dominates decision-making, not mechanical stability. Synovial fluid pressure from an intracapsular hematoma may itself tamponade retinacular flow — a rationale (of debated magnitude) for urgent capsulotomy/reduction in young displaced fractures.
- **Intertrochanteric fracture:** extracapsular; vascular risk is negligible, mechanical stability (lateral wall integrity, posteromedial calcar continuity) dominates implant selection and failure modes.
- **Subtrochanteric fracture:** extracapsular but poorly vascularized cortical bone under the highest biomechanical stress in the femur; deforming muscular forces, not vascularity, are the central technical problem.
- **Femoral head fracture:** intra-articular shear/impaction injury coupled to a dislocation event; AVN risk is driven by dislocation severity and time-to-reduction rather than the fracture pattern alone, and articular congruity determines long-term osteoarthritis risk independent of AVN.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History/mechanism:**

- Low-energy fall in an osteoporotic elderly patient → femoral neck or intertrochanteric pattern.
- High-energy trauma in a young patient → subtrochanteric fracture or femoral head fracture/dislocation.
- Atraumatic thigh pain with prodromal groin/thigh discomfort in a patient on prolonged antiresorptive therapy → screen for atypical femur fracture.

**Exam:**

- Shortened, externally rotated, adducted limb — extracapsular fracture or displaced intracapsular fracture.
- Inability to straight-leg raise.
- Groin pain with axial load/log-roll test — suggests an occult/non-displaced fracture.
- Full neurovascular exam of the limb, with explicit sciatic nerve documentation for any fracture-dislocation.
- Screen for associated injuries: ipsilateral femoral shaft ("floating knee" pattern), pelvic ring injury in high-energy trauma.

**Imaging:**

- AP pelvis plus AP and cross-table lateral hip as the minimum series; an internal-rotation AP view improves neck visualization when pain allows.
- CT of the pelvis/hip is standard for surgical planning in intertrochanteric/subtrochanteric fractures — assesses comminution, posteromedial fragment, lateral wall integrity, and coronal-plane (Hoffa-type) intertrochanteric fragments — and is mandatory for femoral head fractures to characterize fragment size/location and detect intra-articular loose bodies after dislocation reduction.
- MRI (or, if unavailable within 24 h, CT with thin cuts/bone scan) is indicated when plain radiographs are negative but clinical suspicion for occult femoral neck fracture remains high — MRI sensitivity approaches 100% and should be obtained within 24–48 h rather than delaying care empirically.
- Full-length femur imaging is recommended before instrumenting any subtrochanteric or atypical fracture, to rule out a contralateral or ipsilateral incomplete AFF and to plan nail length/bow-matching.

## Classification

### Garden classification (femoral neck fracture)

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Incomplete/valgus-impacted fracture | Lowest AVN risk; in situ percutaneous screw fixation; can be treated somewhat less urgently but still within the 24–48 h window |
| **II** | Complete fracture, non-displaced (trabecular alignment intact) | Behaves similarly to Type I; in situ fixation; watch for occult displacement on follow-up films |
| **III** | Complete fracture, partially displaced (trabeculae malaligned, but femoral head trabeculae still partially engage acetabular trabeculae) | Substantially higher AVN/nonunion risk; urgent anatomic reduction + fixation in younger patients; arthroplasty favored in elderly low-demand patients |
| **IV** | Complete fracture, fully displaced (femoral head trabeculae rotate to realign with acetabulum, but no bony contact with neck) | Highest AVN risk; arthroplasty is standard of care in patients >60 with displaced fractures; fixation reserved for young/biologically young patients accepting AVN/reoperation risk |

*In practice Garden is often collapsed into "non-displaced (I–II)" vs. "displaced (III–IV)" for treatment decisions because inter-observer reliability of the 4-way split is poor; the binary split is highly reproducible and clinically dominant.

### Pauwels classification (femoral neck fracture — shear angle)

| Type | Fracture line angle from horizontal | Biomechanical implication | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | <30° | Predominantly compressive load across fracture | Lower shear stress on fixation; standard parallel cannulated screw or SHS construct usually adequate |
| **II** | 30–50° | Mixed shear/compression | Moderate failure risk; meticulous screw positioning (inverted triangle, posteroinferior screw) |
| **III** | >50° | High shear force, fracture prone to sliding into varus | Highest fixation-failure/nonunion rate with standard screws; consider fixed-angle device (sliding hip screw ± anti-rotation screw, or femoral neck locking plate/FNS) especially in younger patients where head preservation is the goal |

Pauwels angle is measured on the AP radiograph and is highly reduction-dependent — an anatomically reduced fracture converts an unfavorable native Pauwels III pattern into a mechanically favorable construct; reduction quality often matters more than raw implant choice.

### AO/OTA 31 classification (proximal femur, comprehensive)

| Group | Region | Subgroups | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **31-A** | Trochanteric (intertrochanteric) | A1: 2-part, stable (simple, along the intertrochanteric line, medial/posterior cortex intact); A2: multifragmentary with posteromedial comminution, no lateral wall/subtrochanteric extension involving the lateral cortex as a fixed reference; A3: reverse obliquity or transverse, lateral cortex fractured (lateral wall incompetent) | A1/most A2 = SHS or CMN equivalent; A2 with thin/fractured lateral wall (<20.5 mm) and A3 = CMN mandatory (SHS mechanically fails against reverse-obliquity/lateral-wall-deficient patterns) |
| **31-B** | Femoral neck | B1: subcapital, minimal displacement; B2: transcervical; B3: subcapital, displaced | Mirrors Garden/Pauwels logic — B1 often fixation, B3 usually arthroplasty in the elderly |
| **31-C** | Femoral head | C1: split (Pipkin I/II equivalent); C2: split with depression/impaction; C3: comminuted | Guides ORIF vs. arthroplasty; C3 in the elderly favors arthroplasty over reconstruction |

AO/OTA 32-A3 (subtrochanteric, extending distally) fractures are formally classified within the femoral shaft (32) chapter but are clinically managed with the trauma/subtrochanteric algorithm below; many centers use the **Russell-Taylor** or simply "subtrochanteric zone" designation clinically alongside Seinsheimer.

### Evans-Jensen classification (intertrochanteric fracture — stability-based, practical alternative/complement to AO)

| Type | Description | Stability | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Undisplaced, 2-part | Stable | SHS or CMN; low failure risk with either |
| **II** | Displaced, 2-part, reducible to stable configuration | Stable after reduction | Anatomic reduction restores medial/posterior cortical buttress; SHS or CMN |
| **III** | Comminuted, posteromedial fragment, irreducible to stable position, lesser trochanter detached | Unstable | Fixed-angle/load-sharing device preferred (CMN); anticipate controlled collapse |
| **IV** | Comminuted with subtrochanteric extension | Unstable | Long CMN mandatory to span the subtrochanteric zone |
| **V (reverse obliquity)** | Fracture line runs from proximomedial to distolateral (opposite obliquity) | Highly unstable | SHS biomechanically contraindicated (fragment slides medially, fixation cuts out); CMN or fixed-angle blade plate required |

### Seinsheimer classification (subtrochanteric fracture)

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Non-displaced (<2 mm) or minimally displaced, any pattern | Rare; may be treated with standard CMN, low complexity |
| **II** | Two-part fracture: IIA transverse; IIB spiral, lesser trochanter attached to proximal fragment; IIC spiral, lesser trochanter attached to distal fragment | Reduction strategy depends on which fragment retains the lesser trochanter (rotational control reference); CMN standard |
| **III** | Three-part: IIIA with a posteromedial (calcar) fragment including lesser trochanter; IIIB with a proximal lateral cortical (butterfly) fragment | Comminution destabilizes medial buttress; meticulous indirect reduction (avoid stripping the fragment), CMN with attention to length/rotation/alignment restoration rather than perfect anatomic fragment reduction |
| **IV** | Comminuted, four or more fragments | High complexity; long CMN, may need cerclage cable/wire assistance for reduction (biology-preserving indirect technique), or plate augmentation |
| **V** | Subtrochanteric-intertrochanteric fracture combination (extension into the greater trochanter/piriformis fossa) | Long cephalomedullary nail essential to control both the trochanteric and subtrochanteric components as a single construct |

### Pipkin classification (femoral head fracture, associated with hip dislocation)

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Fracture fragment caudal/inferior to the fovea centralis (below the ligamentum teres attachment) | Best prognosis; non-displaced/minimally displaced → closed reduction + non-operative trial; displaced or non-congruent joint → excision of small fragment or ORIF via surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach, preserves MFCA) |
| **II** | Fracture fragment cephalad/superior to the fovea (includes weight-bearing dome) | ORIF required if displaced (fragment is load-bearing) — anatomic reduction and screw fixation (often countersunk/headless screws from the articular surface); surgical hip dislocation approach preferred over Kocher-Langenbeck alone to protect blood supply |
| **III** | Type I or II combined with an ipsilateral femoral neck fracture | Worst prognosis (highest AVN rate); femoral neck fracture takes priority for fixation urgency in young patients (or arthroplasty if elderly); treat as combined injury — many advocate arthroplasty in older patients given very high AVN risk with fixation of both components |
| **IV** | Type I or II combined with an acetabular fracture (posterior wall/rim) | Combined reconstruction needed; acetabular congruency and hip stability drive approach selection (often posterior/Kocher-Langenbeck when the acetabular component predominates, or combined/surgical dislocation approach) |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

Non-operative management of proximal femur fractures in ambulatory adults is exceptional and reserved for:

- Non-ambulatory, severely cognitively impaired, or end-of-life/comfort-care patients where surgery would not change functional trajectory.
- Truly non-displaced/impacted Garden I femoral neck fractures in extremely high anesthetic-risk patients — with the understanding that a meaningful minority still displace and require rescue surgery, so serial radiographs and protected weight-bearing are mandatory if this path is chosen.
- Select minimally displaced (<2 mm) Pipkin I femoral head fractures with a congruent, stable joint on exam and CT.

Even in these scenarios, early mobilization and pain control are prioritized over strict bed rest, since prolonged immobility itself drives the mortality risk this population is most vulnerable to.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Regional anesthesia** (fascia iliaca compartment block or femoral nerve block) is a standard-of-care analgesic adjunct from ED presentation through the perioperative period, reducing opioid requirement and delirium risk, and is now embedded in most hip-fracture care pathways/BOAST-type bundles.
- **Intra-articular or periarticular injections** have no established therapeutic role in acute fracture management (as opposed to post-traumatic osteoarthritis, addressed elsewhere).
- **Tranexamic acid** (topical or IV, typically 1 g at incision ± a second dose) is a strong-recommendation adjunct across fixation and arthroplasty procedures to reduce blood loss/transfusion, applied with individualized caution in patients with recent VTE or high thrombotic risk.

### Operative

#### Femoral neck fracture — fixation vs. arthroplasty

In situ or reduced percutaneous fixation (three cannulated screws in an inverted-triangle configuration, or the Femoral Neck System [FNS] — a fixed-angle sliding blade-and-screw construct) is used for Garden I–II and for displaced fractures in young/biologically young patients (generally <60, extending case-by-case to physiologically young patients into their 60s–70s) where head preservation and avoidance of arthroplasty is the priority despite AVN/nonunion risk (nonunion 10–30%, AVN 10–40% depending on displacement and reduction quality).

- Reduction must be anatomic and achieved urgently — most surgeons target reduction within 6–12 h in young displaced fractures, though evidence for a hard cutoff (as opposed to general urgency) is weaker than commonly taught; regardless, this population should not be deprioritized within the standard 24–48 h pathway.
- Recent trials (FNS vs. cannulated screws, 2023–2025) show FNS achieves comparable or modestly better functional scores and time-to-union with a shorter learning-curve-independent fixed-angle mechanism, at the cost of a single larger implant and higher hardware cost.
- Cannulated screws remain a valid, cheaper, and widely available default, particularly relevant in resource-variable European practice settings.

For displaced (Garden III–IV) fractures in patients over roughly 60–65 with low-to-moderate functional demand, **arthroplasty** is standard of care — fixation in this group carries unacceptable reoperation rates (30–40% for AVN/nonunion/screw cutout vs. ~5–10% arthroplasty revision at comparable follow-up).

**Hemiarthroplasty vs. THA.** The landmark **HEALTH trial** (NEJM 2019, with continued influence through 2023–2025 registry and cost-effectiveness follow-up analyses) randomized 1495 patients ≥50 years with displaced femoral neck fractures:

- Primary outcome (secondary hip procedure within 24 months) occurred in ~8% of both groups — no significant difference.
- Serious adverse events trended higher with THA (41.8% vs. 36.7%).
- Functional/pain scores modestly favored THA but fell below the threshold of clinical importance.
- Practical synthesis: **hemiarthroplasty** remains the appropriate default for most low-demand, older (>75–80), or cognitively impaired patients; **THA** is favored for patients who were ambulatory without aids, cognitively intact, with reasonable life expectancy and higher functional demand, accepting a modestly higher dislocation/reoperation-for-instability risk in exchange for better long-term function.
- Approach (posterior vs. anterolateral/direct anterior) is surgeon-preference-driven; posterior approach with capsular repair has an acceptably low dislocation rate and remains the most widely used.

**Cemented vs. uncemented stems.** The WHiTE 5 trial (NEJM 2022) and subsequent 2023 meta-analyses (24 RCTs, ~3,470 patients) consistently favor **cemented** fixation for hemiarthroplasty in the elderly fragility-fracture population — better Harris Hip Scores, lower periprosthetic fracture rates, and no significant increase in mortality with modern third-generation cementing technique and anesthesia vigilance around cementation (bone cement implantation syndrome). Uncemented stems are reserved for situations where cementation is relatively contraindicated (severe cardiopulmonary instability) or in some younger/biologically robust patients undergoing THA.

#### Intertrochanteric fracture — implant selection

Stable patterns (AO 31-A1, most reducible A2/Evans I–II) can be treated with either a **sliding hip screw (SHS)** or a **cephalomedullary nail (CMN)** with statistically equivalent union and functional outcomes per multiple RCTs and the 2023 INSITE trial (large UK pragmatic RCT: no clinically meaningful 1-year EQ-5D difference between SHS and IMN, no significant revision difference, no interaction by fracture stability) — SHS remains a reasonable, lower-cost, evidence-supported choice for these patterns.

**Unstable patterns** — reverse obliquity (Evans V), transverse, subtrochanteric extension, thin/fractured lateral wall (<20.5 mm), or gross comminution — mandate a **CMN**: the SHS's sliding mechanism depends on an intact lateral wall to resist medialization of the shaft, and reverse-oblique/lateral-wall-deficient fractures fixed with SHS have unacceptably high cutout/fixation-failure rates.

Multiple 2023 meta-analyses converge on a nuanced picture:

- CMN and SHS show similar surgical-failure and mortality rates overall.
- CMN carries a modest excess of implant-related peri-implant (interprosthetic) femur fractures with older nail designs, largely mitigated by modern nail geometry with reduced proximal diameter/stiffness mismatch.
- CMN offers faster operative time and less blood loss in some series, while SHS remains cheaper.
- Practical algorithm: default to CMN for any unstable pattern or where availability makes it equally accessible; SHS is entirely acceptable, arguably preferable on cost grounds, for simple stable patterns.

**Technical pearls for CMN:**

- Restore length/alignment/rotation before final locking — do not rely on the nail alone to reduce the fracture.
- Tip-apex distance (TAD) <25 mm (Baumgaertner) minimizes cutout risk (<2% below this threshold vs. >20% above).
- Consider a construct with two lag screws (integrated screw-and-blade or dual-screw nails) in osteoporotic bone with rotationally unstable patterns.
- Obtain a lateral fluoroscopic view to confirm central-central or slightly inferior lag screw position within the femoral head.

#### Subtrochanteric fracture

**Long cephalomedullary nailing** is the standard of care. The dominant technical challenge is reduction, not fixation — deforming muscular forces (iliopsoas flexion/external rotation of the proximal fragment, abductor pull into abduction, adductor-driven varus/shortening of the distal fragment) must be neutralized before/during nail passage.

**Reduction techniques:**

- Lateral decubitus or supine positioning with a bump.
- Blocking (Poller) screws to control the reduction as the nail passes through a wide metaphyseal canal.
- Percutaneous clamps or joysticks.
- Cerclage cabling for long spiral/comminuted patterns — biology-preserving, minimally invasive placement to avoid devascularizing fragments.
- Open reduction only when closed/percutaneous methods fail to restore length-alignment-rotation.

Restore anatomic or near-anatomic alignment — varus/valgus and rotational malalignment >10–15° predispose to nonunion and abductor limp. In patients with an existing ipsilateral hip implant, marked deformity, or a periprosthetic pattern precluding nailing, a **locking plate** (proximal femoral locking plate) with indirect reduction technique is the alternative, accepting a load-sharing rather than intramedullary load-sharing construct and generally a longer weight-bearing restriction.

Always screen for and image the contralateral femur when the pattern (transverse, lateral cortical beak, minimal comminution) or history (bisphosphonate/denosumab >3–5 years, prodromal thigh pain) suggests an **atypical femur fracture (AFF)**. AFFs are managed with the same nailing principle but require special attention: some advocate longer nails to the level of the physeal scar, consideration of a teriparatide adjunct, and a drug-holiday discussion — AFFs carry a materially higher nonunion/delayed-union rate than typical subtrochanteric fractures.

#### Femoral head fracture (Pipkin)

After emergent closed reduction of the hip dislocation (within 6 h to minimize AVN risk) and post-reduction CT, definitive management depends on fragment size, location, and joint congruency.

- Small, non-weight-bearing (Pipkin I, inferior to fovea) fragments causing no mechanical block can be excised or, if the joint is stable and congruent, observed.
- Larger or weight-bearing-surface (Pipkin II) fragments require anatomic ORIF, ideally via a **surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach)** with trochanteric flip osteotomy, which preserves the MFCA and provides 360° visualization of the femoral head and acetabulum — superior to a standard posterior (Kocher-Langenbeck) approach for anterior/superior head fragments, and avoids the AVN risk of an isolated posterior approach disrupting an already-compromised blood supply.
- Fixation uses small fragment or headless compression screws countersunk below the articular cartilage.
- Combined injuries (Pipkin III with femoral neck fracture, Pipkin IV with acetabular fracture) carry the worst prognosis — AVN rates reported up to 50%+ in some Pipkin III series. In older patients with a Pipkin III pattern, primary arthroplasty is a reasonable alternative to attempting fixation of both the head and neck components.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Weight-bearing status** is implant- and fracture-stability-dependent, but the overarching goal across all fragility hip fractures is **immediate weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT)** whenever the construct allows — restrictive weight-bearing in the elderly correlates with loss of independence and higher mortality, and most modern fixed-angle/arthroplasty constructs are designed to tolerate it.

- **Hemiarthroplasty/THA:** WBAT postoperative day 0–1; hip precautions (posterior approach: avoid combined flexion >90° + adduction + internal rotation) for 6 weeks if posterior approach without robust capsular repair, often relaxed sooner with modern capsular repair techniques and anterior approaches; assisted ambulation with walker/crutches transitioning to cane by 2–4 weeks as balance allows; formal PT for gait, transfers, and strengthening starting POD1.
- **Femoral neck fixation (screws/FNS), stable pattern:** WBAT is increasingly favored even for fixation in the elderly (fixed-angle devices tolerate it); touch-down/partial weight-bearing for 6 weeks is still used by many surgeons for Pauwels III or comminuted patterns, advancing to full weight-bearing by 8–12 weeks with radiographic union.
- **Intertrochanteric (SHS or CMN), stable pattern:** WBAT immediately; the sliding/telescoping mechanism is designed for it.
- **Intertrochanteric/subtrochanteric, unstable or comminuted:** WBAT is still generally pursued with a CMN construct (load-sharing device), but with closer radiographic follow-up (2, 6, 12 weeks) to detect early collapse/varus migration; touch-down weight-bearing for 4–6 weeks is reserved for cases with poor bone quality, inadequate fixation, or major comminution where the surgeon judges the construct marginal.
- **Femoral head fracture (ORIF):** protected/touch-down weight-bearing 6–8 weeks given articular fixation, then progressive loading to full weight-bearing by 10–12 weeks with radiographic evidence of healing; hip precautions per the surgical approach used (trochanteric flip osteotomy patients also need trochanteric-osteotomy-specific abductor precautions until osteotomy union, typically 6–8 weeks).

**Physiotherapy content, by phase:**

- **POD0–1:** bed mobility, transfers, seated/standing balance, ankle pumps/VTE prophylaxis exercises.
- **Week 1–2:** gait training with the appropriate assistive device, hip/knee active ROM, isometric quadriceps/gluteal sets.
- **Week 2–6:** progressive resistive exercise, gait-device weaning as strength/balance allow, stair training.
- **Week 6–12:** hip abductor and extensor strengthening, single-leg stance work, balance/proprioceptive training, community ambulation distance goals.
- **Beyond 12 weeks:** continued strengthening, fall-prevention program, functional/occupational therapy for ADL independence.

**Radiographic and clinical milestones:**

- AP/lateral films at 2, 6, and 12 weeks (more frequent for unstable/comminuted fixation constructs).
- Union typically confirmed by bridging callus (IT/subtrochanteric fixation) or trabecular consolidation (femoral neck) by 12–16 weeks.
- Delayed-union workup (labs, CT) if no radiographic progression by 4–6 months.

**Return to work/activity:**

- Sedentary work — typically 4–8 weeks post-arthroplasty or stable fixation, once pain-controlled and independent with transfers/short-distance ambulation.
- Physically demanding work — 3–6 months, contingent on radiographic union (fixation constructs) and functional strength testing.
- Driving — typically resumes at 4–6 weeks post-arthroplasty (earlier for non-operative/contralateral-limb surgery, later if right-sided posterior-approach THA with braking-reflex concerns), per surgeon assessment and local licensing regulations.
- High-demand recreational activity or sport (relevant mainly to younger femoral neck/subtrochanteric/femoral head fracture patients) — generally deferred until full radiographic union and symmetric strength/hop testing, typically 4–9 months depending on fracture complexity.

**Universal secondary-prevention rehabilitation component:** every fragility hip fracture patient should receive falls-risk assessment (vision, home hazards, orthostatics, polypharmacy review), bone mineral density testing (or presumptive treatment initiation given the fracture itself qualifies as a major osteoporotic fracture), and initiation of anti-osteoporotic pharmacotherapy (bisphosphonate, denosumab, or anabolic agent depending on renal function/fracture severity/prior therapy) ideally before discharge or within weeks — this is now considered a core, auditable quality metric in national hip fracture registries (UK NHFD, and analogous programs) alongside the surgical-timing metric.

## Complications

**Femoral neck fracture (fixation):**

- Avascular necrosis — 10–40% depending on displacement/reduction quality; presents 1–3 years post-injury, requiring long-term surveillance.
- Nonunion — 10–30% in displaced fractures.
- Femoral neck shortening, associated with inferior hip function in the FAITH trial cohort.
- Screw cutout/failure of fixation requiring conversion to arthroplasty.

**Femoral neck fracture (arthroplasty):**

- Dislocation — higher with THA than hemiarthroplasty, further reduced with dual-mobility constructs or robust posterior capsular repair.
- Periprosthetic fracture — lower with cemented stems.
- Periprosthetic joint infection.
- Leg-length discrepancy.
- Acetabular erosion — a hemiarthroplasty-specific long-term complication in more active patients, part of the rationale for THA in higher-demand patients.
- Aseptic loosening — long-term, more relevant to THA given longer expected implant life.

**Intertrochanteric fracture:**

- Loss of fixation/cutout, minimized by TAD <25 mm and central-inferior screw placement.
- Varus collapse and medialization of the shaft (particularly SHS in unstable patterns).
- Z-effect/reverse Z-effect with dual-screw nail systems.
- Peri-implant femur fracture at the nail tip — more common with older, shorter, stiffer nail designs; mitigated with modern nails and by avoiding a stress riser at the tip in osteoporotic bone.
- Nonunion — uncommon, <5%, given the metaphyseal/cancellous vascularity of this region.

**Subtrochanteric fracture:**

- Nonunion/delayed union — higher than intertrochanteric fractures owing to cortical, lower-vascularity bone and greater deforming forces; reported nonunion up to 10–20% in comminuted or malreduced cases.
- Malunion — varus, rotational malalignment from failure to control deforming forces.
- Implant failure — nail breakage at the screw hole if union is delayed and load cycling continues.
- In the AFF subset: contralateral fracture and delayed healing independent of fixation quality.

**Femoral head fracture (Pipkin):**

- Avascular necrosis — the dominant long-term concern, correlating with dislocation-to-reduction time and fracture-dislocation severity.
- Post-traumatic osteoarthritis — very common by 5–10 years given the inherent articular injury.
- Heterotopic ossification, particularly after surgical hip dislocation or extensile posterior approaches; perioperative NSAID or single-fraction radiotherapy prophylaxis considered in high-risk cases.
- Sciatic nerve palsy — assess pre- and post-reduction; most peroneal-division injuries have a guarded but non-zero recovery potential over 12–18 months.
- Recurrent instability if the acetabular/capsular injury is not adequately addressed.

**Cross-cutting geriatric complications (all proximal femur fractures in the fragility population):**

- 30-day mortality 5–10%; 1-year mortality historically 20–30%, materially reduced by orthogeriatric co-management and prompt surgery.
- Delirium — 20–60% incidence, minimized by regional analgesia, avoidance of high-risk medications, and early mobilization.
- Venous thromboembolism.
- Pressure injuries.
- Loss of independent living status — roughly half of previously independent patients do not return to their prior living situation.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2023 — INSITE trial (UK, pragmatic RCT)** found no clinically meaningful 1-year EQ-5D difference and no significant revision-rate difference between intramedullary nail and sliding hip screw for intertrochanteric fractures, with no interaction by fracture stability, reinforcing that SHS remains a valid evidence-supported option for stable (AO 31-A1, select A2) patterns while broader safety data show older nail designs carry more implant-related peri-implant femur fractures. [Comparison of Cephalomedullary Nails with Sliding Hip Screws — cumulative meta-analysis of RCTs (PMC)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10060774)
- **2023 — Systematic review/meta-analysis** confirms cephalomedullary nailing has a **higher reoperation and refracture rate** compared with sliding hip screw fixation for intertrochanteric fractures overall, tempering blanket "nail-for-everything" practice and supporting a pattern-based (stable vs. unstable) implant selection algorithm. [Cephalomedullary Nailing has a Higher Reoperation Rate Compared to Sliding Hip Screw Fixation — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37873525/)
- **2023 — Meta-analysis of 24 RCTs (~3,470 patients), cemented vs. uncemented hemiarthroplasty** reaffirms cemented fixation's superiority in hip function, pain, and complication profile (lower periprosthetic fracture, subsidence/loosening) for femoral neck fracture hemiarthroplasty in the elderly, consolidating cemented stems as the preferred default in this population. [Cemented vs. Uncemented Hemiarthroplasty — systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs, Orthopaedic Surgery (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/os.13716)
- **2023 — Femoral Neck System (FNS) vs. cannulated screws meta-analysis/systematic review** shows comparable-to-favorable functional and radiographic outcomes for FNS, supporting its adoption as a fixed-angle alternative to conventional multi-screw constructs, particularly for higher-shear (Pauwels III) patterns in younger patients. [Femoral neck system vs. cannulated screws — meta-analysis and systematic review (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10390772/)
- **2023–2025 — Continued HEALTH trial follow-up and registry/cost-effectiveness analyses** (propensity-matched cohort studies, American Joint Replacement Registry analyses) continue to show no significant difference in secondary-procedure rates between hemiarthroplasty and THA for displaced femoral neck fracture, with THA carrying a modestly higher complication burden — supporting individualized selection (THA for higher-functioning, cognitively intact patients) rather than a uniform default. [Comparative Effectiveness of THA and Hemiarthroplasty — propensity-score-matched cohort study (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36758068/); [Hemiarthroplasty Versus THA — American Joint Replacement Registry analysis, Journal of Arthroplasty](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(25)00907-6/abstract)
- **2024 — AAOS reaffirms strong evidence that surgery within 24–48 hours reduces mortality and complications** in geriatric hip fracture, with data specifically highlighting a 20% lower 12-month mortality risk when surgery occurs under 48 hours — used to justify continued investment in "hip fracture within 24 hours" institutional programs across US trauma centers. [Getting Geriatric Hip Fractures to the OR within 24 Hours Is Critical — AAOS Now, Dec 2024](https://www.aaos.org/aaosnow/2024/dec/clinical/clinical07/)
- **2024–2025 — Systematic review on tranexamic acid in hip fracture management** synthesizes evidence supporting TXA's blood-loss/transfusion benefit while flagging the need for individualized VTE risk assessment given inconsistent exclusion criteria across the underlying trials — refining rather than reversing the AAOS strong recommendation for routine TXA use. [Assessing the Effectiveness of Tranexamic Acid in Hip Fracture Management — systematic review (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12851818/)
- **2025 — Meta-analysis comparing proximal femoral nail (PFN) and dynamic hip screw (DHS)** for intertrochanteric fractures reports shorter operative time and significantly lower intraoperative blood loss favoring PFN, adding a perioperative-efficiency argument (distinct from the union/reoperation equivalence shown in earlier RCTs) that is increasingly cited when centers standardize on nailing for logistical/OR-efficiency reasons. [Comparative Effectiveness of the Proximal Femoral Nail and Dynamic Hip Screw Fixation — systematic review and meta-analysis (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12620960/)
- **2024–2025 — Emerging orthogeriatric co-management cohort data** continue to refine the mortality/complication benefit signal: recent retrospective cohorts show orthogeriatric liaison care significantly reduces infection-related complications and infection-associated mortality (with less consistent effect on all-cause mortality across studies), reinforcing interdisciplinary co-management as a core, guideline-endorsed process measure rather than solely a mortality lever. [Orthogeriatric co-management of fragility fractures — retrospective cohort study on one-year mortality and functional outcomes (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12831399/); [Orthogeriatric Care Following Hip Fracture — Improving Post-Operative Outcomes in an Aged Population (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11050858/)
- **2023–2025 — Complication-rate database analyses comparing SHS and CMN** for intertrochanteric fractures at scale (large administrative datasets) largely corroborate RCT-level equivalence in major outcomes while identifying implant-specific complication signatures (nail-tip fracture vs. screw-cutout/medialization), supporting the pattern-based selection algorithm over a one-size-fits-all implant policy. [Complication Rates in Intertrochanteric Fractures — database analysis comparing SHS and CMN (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11283303/)
- **2025–2026 — Randomized and cohort comparisons of femoral neck locking plate/FNS-type constructs vs. multiple cannulated screws** in young femoral neck fracture patients continue to show functional and union-time advantages for fixed-angle devices, with cannulated screws retaining a role where implant cost or availability is limiting — relevant directly to resource-variable practice settings including much of Europe. [Femoral neck locking plate versus multiple cannulated screws — randomized controlled trial (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12359918/)
