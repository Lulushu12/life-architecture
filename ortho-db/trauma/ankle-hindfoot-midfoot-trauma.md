## Quick Reference

**Ankle fracture treatment ladder**

| Pattern | First-line | Second-line | Surgical indication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weber A, stable | Walking boot/cast, WBAT | — | Displaced medial malleolus, talar shift on stress view |
| Weber B, stable (no medial injury, negative stress) | Functional brace/cast, WBAT | — | Positive stress test, talar shift >2 mm, bimalleolar equivalent |
| Weber B, unstable / bi- or trimalleolar | — | — | ORIF fibula ± syndesmosis ± posterior malleolus |
| Weber C / Maisonneuve | — | — | Always ORIF fibula + syndesmosis stabilization |

**Syndesmosis injury**: intraoperative stress test after fibula fixation (Cotton/external rotation) in every Weber B/C → if positive, stabilize. Suture-button vs screw — see Treatment. Malreduction on CT reported in **>25–50%** of screw-only cohorts when fluoroscopy alone is used to judge reduction — use contralateral-comparison fluoroscopy or intraoperative CT/O-arm when available.

**Talus fracture (Hawkins) ladder**: Type I → non-op (nondisplaced, cast NWB) or percutaneous screws if minimally displaced; Type II–IV → **emergent closed/open reduction** (within hours, especially with skin tenting or dislocation) followed by definitive ORIF within days once soft tissue allows.

**Calcaneus ladder**: Sanders I → non-op always. Sanders II/III → shared decision: ORIF (better short/mid-term functional scores in meta-analysis) vs non-op (UK HeFT: no 60-month functional difference, non-op has fewer secondary procedures) vs primary subtalar fusion in older/lower-demand or Sanders IV. Sanders IV → primary subtalar arthrodesis strongly considered.

**Lisfranc ladder**: Nunley-Vertullo Stage I → non-op (boot/cast, progressive WB). Stage II/III or any frank diastasis/instability → operative (ORIF vs primary arthrodesis — outcomes broadly equivalent in RCTs; PA favored for purely ligamentous injury and comminuted 1st–3rd TMT).

**Red flags** (any diagnosis): open fracture, dislocation with skin tenting/vascular compromise (reduce **immediately**, do not wait for imaging), compartment syndrome (Lisfranc and calcaneus are classic culprits — check with tense midfoot/hindfoot swelling and pain out of proportion), floating talus, bilateral calcaneus fractures (screen for thoracolumbar burst fracture — ~10% incidence), fall from height (screen spine/pelvis).

**Key numbers**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Normal Böhler angle | 20–40° (calcaneus) |
| Normal Gissane (crucial) angle | 120–145° |
| Tibiofibular clear space (mortise view) | ≤6 mm |
| Tibiofibular overlap (mortise view) | ≥1 mm |
| Medial clear space | ≤4 mm (equal to superior clear space) |
| Posterior malleolus fixation threshold | ≥25% articular surface (lateral XR) or CT-defined incisural/step-off involvement per Mason & Molloy morphology, displacement >2 mm |
| Lisfranc diastasis (1st–2nd metatarsal base) | >2 mm abnormal on AP/WB view; >5 mm = Nunley-Vertullo Stage III |
| Hawkins II/III/IV AVN rate | ~20–50% / ~50–100% / ~70–100% |
| WAX trial early WB | 2 weeks post-ORIF non-inferior (and trending superior) to 6 weeks |
| Time-to-fixation for talar neck | Historically "emergent"; contemporary data show timing to definitive fixation (<24 h vs later) does **not** clearly change AVN rate once reduction is achieved — urgent **reduction** (not necessarily final fixation) is the priority |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Ankle mortise & syndesmosis

The distal tibiofibular syndesmosis is stabilized by four structures: the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (**AITFL**), posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (**PITFL**, superficial and deep components — the deep component is the transverse tibiofibular ligament and the strongest restraint to posterior fibular translation), the interosseous ligament (**IOL**, continuous with the interosseous membrane), and the interosseous membrane itself. The deltoid ligament (superficial and deep bands) is the primary medial stabilizer and the deep component is the key restraint to talar external rotation/lateral translation — deltoid competence (clinically or on stress imaging) is central to deciding whether a Weber B ankle fracture is stable.

Maisonneuve fracture pattern deserves explicit mention: a proximal fibular fracture (often near the fibular neck, at risk to the common peroneal nerve) combined with disruption of the interosseous membrane and syndesmosis, presenting with a subtle or even radiographically unremarkable ankle beyond medial tenderness/deltoid injury or a posterior malleolus fracture. Always palpate the proximal fibula in any ankle injury with disproportionate medial-sided pain and obtain full-length tibia-fibula radiographs when suspected — missing it and treating only the "ankle" leaves an unstable syndesmosis.

### Talus

~60% of the surface is articular cartilage, with no muscular/tendinous attachments — blood supply is entirely through capsuloligamentous vessels: the **artery of the tarsal canal** (branch of posterior tibial artery, supplies the bulk of the body via an anastomotic sling with the artery of the tarsal sinus), the **deltoid branch** of the posterior tibial artery (often the sole surviving supply after a displaced neck fracture — must be protected during medial exposure), and dorsal branches from the anterior tibial/dorsalis pedis artery supplying the head/neck and superior body. Fracture displacement disrupts these anastomoses in a graded fashion, which is the physiologic basis of the Hawkins classification.

### Calcaneus

The posterior facet is the key weight-bearing articulation with the talus; **Böhler's angle** (drawn on lateral XR between the highest points of the anterior process and posterior facet, and the tuberosity) reflects posterior facet height/collapse, and **Gissane's (crucial) angle** reflects the lateral wall/posterior facet relationship. Most fractures result from axial loading (fall from height/high-energy) driving the lateral talar process into the posterior facet, producing a primary fracture line (constant fragment stays with the sustentaculum via strong interosseous ligaments) and a secondary fracture line whose location/comminution defines the Sanders type. Lateral wall blowout causes peroneal tendon impingement/subfibular impingement, and heel widening causes hindfoot malalignment.

### Lisfranc complex

The tarsometatarsal (TMT) joint complex is stabilized osseously by the "Roman arch" configuration (recessed 2nd metatarsal base locked between the medial and lateral cuneiforms — the keystone) and ligamentously by dorsal, interosseous, and plantar TMT ligaments, the strongest being the plantar **Lisfranc ligament** running from the medial cuneiform to the base of the 2nd metatarsal (no equivalent ligament exists between the 1st and 2nd metatarsal bases dorsally, which is the structural "weak point"). Injury spectrum ranges from high-energy crush/direct injury to low-energy axial-load/twisting injuries (athletes) causing purely ligamentous "subtle Lisfranc" injury without fracture.

### Navicular & cuboid

The navicular sits in the medial column between talar head and cuneiforms; its central-third watershed zone is relatively hypovascular, predisposing to stress fracture and nonunion. Cuboid sits in the lateral column and functions as a structural strut ("nutcracker" mechanism — compressed between calcaneus and 4th/5th metatarsal bases in forced abduction injuries, classically the "nutcracker fracture").

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

- **Ankle fracture**: assess skin/soft-tissue envelope, deformity requiring urgent reduction, distal neurovascular status. Ottawa Ankle Rules to triage radiographs in low-energy presentations. Standard views: AP, mortise (15–20° internal rotation), lateral. CT for intra-articular extension, comminution, posterior malleolus morphology (essential for surgical planning per Mason & Molloy), and any complex/rotational pattern.
- **Syndesmosis**: **squeeze test** (proximal calf compression reproducing distal pain), **external rotation stress test** (pain/gapping with external rotation of the foot with knee flexed), **Cotton test** (intraoperative — anterior-posterior fibular translation with the reduction clamp/pointed instrument). Gravity or manual external rotation stress radiographs help decide operative stabilization in isolated (no-fracture) syndesmotic sprains. Weight-bearing CT increasingly used to detect subtle malreduction/instability not seen on fluoroscopy.
- **Talus**: look for skin tenting over the medial malleolus/talar body (dictates emergent reduction), **Hawkins sign** (subchondral lucency on AP/mortise view at 6–8 weeks indicating intact revascularization — a positive sign is reassuring though its negative predictive value has been questioned in recent series). CT (1 mm cuts) mandatory for surgical planning; MRI reserved for occult fracture or AVN surveillance.
- **Calcaneus**: assess for compartment syndrome of the foot (up to 10% of displaced intra-articular fractures — measure compartment pressures if clinical suspicion), blistering/soft-tissue status (drives timing of surgery), and **screen the contralateral calcaneus and thoracolumbar spine** (axial-load mechanism, spine fracture co-incidence ~10%). Lateral, axial (Harris) and Broden views; CT (coronal, sagittal, axial) is the gold standard for Sanders classification and surgical planning.
- **Lisfranc**: weight-bearing AP/oblique/lateral foot radiographs (non-weight-bearing films miss subtle injury); look for the "fleck sign" (avulsion off the medial cuneiform or 2nd metatarsal base), loss of alignment of the medial border of the 2nd metatarsal with the medial border of the middle cuneiform, and loss of arch height on lateral view. CT for bony detail/comminution; MRI for suspected purely ligamentous injury with normal/equivocal weight-bearing radiographs (high sensitivity for Lisfranc ligament rupture) — bone scan historically used, largely supplanted by MRI/WBCT.
- **Navicular/cuboid**: standard foot series; CT for body fractures/comminution and surgical planning; MRI or bone scan for suspected stress fracture with normal radiographs (classic in track/jumping athletes with vague dorsal midfoot pain — the "N spot" tenderness).

**Imaging pearls / common pitfalls**: a normal non-weight-bearing radiograph does not exclude a Lisfranc injury — insist on weight-bearing or gravity/manual stress views before discharging a patient with midfoot tenderness and a normal supine film. An isolated, apparently benign fibula fracture with disproportionate medial ankle tenderness should trigger a full-length tibia-fibula film to exclude Maisonneuve. Any calcaneus fracture from a fall from height warrants a screening lumbar spine radiograph/CT and examination of the contralateral heel. Subtle navicular and cuboid fractures are frequently missed on initial radiographs in athletes with vague midfoot pain — a low threshold for CT or MRI in a patient with persistent dorsal midfoot pain and a normal radiograph avoids delayed diagnosis and nonunion.

## Classification

### Weber/Danis (AO/Danis-Weber) — level of fibular fracture relative to syndesmosis

| Type | Definition | Syndesmosis status | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Fibula fracture below/at the level of the plafond (infrasyndesmotic), usually transverse | Almost always intact | Often stable/non-op; associated medial-sided injury (vertical medial malleolus or deltoid) suggests supination-adduction pattern — check for talar dome impaction |
| **B** | Fibula fracture at the level of the syndesmosis (transsyndesmotic), oblique/spiral | Variable — disrupted in up to ~50% | Requires clinical/intraoperative stress testing after fibula fixation to decide on syndesmosis stabilization |
| **C** | Fibula fracture above the syndesmosis (suprasyndesmotic) | Disrupted by definition | Always requires syndesmosis stabilization; proximal fibula fracture (Maisonneuve) mandates full-length tibia/fibula films and knee exam |

### Lauge-Hansen — mechanism-based, foot position + deforming force, sequential stages

| Pattern (relative frequency) | Stage I | Stage II | Stage III | Stage IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Supination-External Rotation (SER)** ~40–75% | AITFL rupture | Distal fibula fracture, short oblique/spiral (Weber B) | PITFL rupture or posterior malleolus avulsion | Medial malleolus fracture or deltoid rupture |
| **Pronation-External Rotation (PER)** ~5–20% | Medial malleolus fracture or deltoid rupture | AITFL rupture | Fibula fracture above syndesmosis (Weber C, often proximal/Maisonneuve) | PITFL rupture or posterior malleolus fracture |
| **Supination-Adduction (SA)** ~10–20% | Transverse distal fibula avulsion below joint, or lateral ligament rupture | Vertical medial malleolus fracture ± medial talar dome impaction | — | — |
| **Pronation-Abduction (PA)** ~5–20% | Medial malleolus fracture or deltoid rupture | Syndesmotic ligament rupture(s) (AITFL/IOL/PITFL) | Short oblique/comminuted "butterfly" fibula fracture above syndesmosis | — |
| **Pronation-Dorsiflexion (PD)** rare | Medial malleolus fracture | Anterior tibial margin fracture | Supramalleolar fibula fracture | Posterior tibial (plafond) fracture — transitional to pilon |

**Management implication:** Lauge-Hansen predicts the sequence of soft-tissue and bony injury and guides intraoperative order of reduction (fix fibula first in most patterns, restoring length/rotation before assessing medial and syndesmotic stability); SER-IV and PER patterns are the ones most likely to need syndesmosis stabilization; SA patterns require careful inspection of the medial talar dome for impaction (missed cause of post-traumatic arthritis).

### Hawkins classification — talar neck fractures (with Canale-Kelly type IV modification)

| Type | Definition | Reported AVN rate | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Nondisplaced vertical talar neck fracture | ~0–13% | Non-op (NWB cast) or percutaneous screw fixation if any doubt about stability |
| **II** | Displaced with subtalar joint subluxation/dislocation; tibiotalar joint intact | ~20–50% | Urgent reduction (closed if possible) + ORIF; anatomic reduction is the strongest modifiable predictor of AVN/outcome |
| **III** | Displaced with both subtalar and tibiotalar dislocation | ~50–100% (commonly cited ~70–100%) | Emergent reduction (open if closed fails) to relieve skin tension and vascular kink; ORIF once soft tissues allow; high complication rate (AVN, infection, malunion) regardless of timing |
| **IV** (Canale-Kelly) | Type III plus talonavicular subluxation/dislocation | ~70–100% | Same urgency as III; worst prognosis; counsel on high likelihood of AVN/arthrosis and possible salvage fusion |

**Hawkins sign** (subchondral lucency at 6–8 weeks) — presence suggests preserved vascularity/lower AVN risk; recent cohort data note its **absence does not reliably predict AVN** (in one series, 38% of Hawkins-sign-negative tali still avoided AVN, and correlation with 1-year AVN was weaker than classically taught) — treat it as supportive, not definitive.

### Sanders classification — calcaneus, based on coronal CT through the widest part of the posterior facet

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Nondisplaced (any number of fracture lines, <2 mm displacement) | Non-operative regardless of fragment count |
| **II** (IIA/IIB/IIC by location of primary fracture line — lateral/central/medial) | Two-part fracture of the posterior facet | ORIF (typically via sinus tarsi or extensile lateral approach) if displaced/depressed; best functional outcomes among displaced patterns |
| **III** (IIIAB/IIIAC/IIIBC) | Three-part fracture, central depressed fragment between two facet fracture lines | ORIF with careful posterior facet reconstruction; higher complication rate than II |
| **IV** | Comminuted, four or more articular fragments | ORIF with high wound/nonunion/post-traumatic arthritis risk, or primary subtalar arthrodesis (increasingly favored, union ~94% in recent series) — shared decision, favor fusion in high comminution, low-demand or older patients |

### Myerson classification — Lisfranc, based on direction/pattern of TMT displacement

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Total incongruity — all five metatarsals displaced together in the same plane (sagittal or coronal) | Usually high-energy; needs full reduction of the entire TMT row |
| **B1** | Partial incongruity — medial displacement isolated to the 1st ray | Medial column fixation (screw or plate) |
| **B2** | Partial incongruity — lateral displacement of the 2nd–5th rays | Lateral/lesser TMT fixation, bridge plating or K-wires for mobile 4th–5th rays |
| **C1** | Divergent — partial (1st ray displaces medially, lesser rays displace laterally) | Combined medial screw/plate + lateral bridging fixation |
| **C2** | Divergent — complete divergent pattern of all rays | Most unstable/comminuted pattern; often favors primary arthrodesis of the medial 3 rays |

### Nunley-Vertullo classification — subtle/low-energy (athletic) Lisfranc injury, staged by exam + imaging

| Stage | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Sprain; normal weight-bearing radiograph alignment and arch height; MRI/bone scan positive for Lisfranc ligament injury | Non-operative: protected weight-bearing (boot/cast) 4–6 weeks, progressive return to sport |
| **II** | 1–5 mm diastasis between 1st–2nd metatarsal bases on weight-bearing view; no loss of arch height | Operative (ligamentous instability without bony collapse) — anatomic reduction and screw fixation or primary fusion depending on surgeon/joint condition |
| **III** | Diastasis >5 mm plus loss of arch (decreased calcaneal–5th metatarsal height on weight-bearing lateral) | Operative — ORIF or primary arthrodesis; worst prognosis of the three stages, higher risk of post-traumatic arthritis |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Ankle fracture**: isolated Weber A, and Weber B with a negative stress test and no medial-sided injury (intact deltoid clinically/radiographically) — short leg cast or removable boot, weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) once comfortable, 6 weeks with serial radiographs at 1–2 weeks to confirm maintained reduction (talar shift is the failure mode to watch for).
- **Syndesmosis (isolated sprain without fracture)**: West Point grade I–II (stable on stress exam) — functional rehabilitation, protected weight-bearing, taping/bracing; grade III (frank instability) trends operative in athletes but a trial of immobilization is reasonable in low-demand patients.
- **Talus**: Hawkins I only — NWB cast/boot 6–8 weeks until radiographic union, then progressive weight-bearing.
- **Calcaneus**: Sanders I always; displaced Sanders II/III in patients who are poor surgical candidates (diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, smokers, open wounds/soft-tissue compromise) or who prioritize avoiding surgical risk — per **UK HeFT** long-term data, non-operative treatment gives equivalent 60-month Kerr-Atkins functional scores to ORIF with a lower rate of additional surgery. Non-op protocol: bulky Jones dressing/splint, elevation, NWB until swelling controlled, early subtalar/ankle ROM exercises, progressive WB from ~8–10 weeks guided by radiographic consolidation.
- **Lisfranc**: Nunley-Vertullo Stage I only, and select truly nondisplaced (<2 mm) injuries confirmed on weight-bearing imaging — NWB cast/boot 6 weeks then progressive WB with repeat weight-bearing films to confirm no late diastasis.
- **Navicular/cuboid**: nondisplaced avulsion and tuberosity fractures, and most nondisplaced stress fractures without sclerosis/nonunion signs — NWB cast 6–8 weeks (navicular stress fractures are notoriously slow/prone to nonunion given the central watershed zone — err toward NWB immobilization over a boot).

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Corticosteroid injection** has no role in acute fracture management; it is used later for isolated post-traumatic subtalar or TMT joint pain as a diagnostic/temporizing tool before arthrodesis, or for symptomatic sinus tarsi syndrome after calcaneus fracture.
- **Diagnostic intra-articular local anesthetic injection** (subtalar or TMT joints) is a useful adjunct to localize the pain generator in chronic post-traumatic hindfoot/midfoot pain before committing to fusion.
- **Bone stimulation (LIPUS/electrical)** may be considered as an adjunct for navicular stress fracture nonunion or delayed union, though evidence is mixed and it is not a substitute for adequate immobilization/offloading.
- **Botulinum toxin/regional nerve blocks** have niche roles in perioperative pain control (popliteal/ankle blocks) rather than disease-modifying treatment — standard multimodal regional anesthesia is appropriate for all operative cases in this cluster.

### Operative

**Ankle fracture / fibula**
- Standard lateral approach, anatomic reduction of the fibula (restore length, rotation, and coronal alignment against the incisura), fixed with a one-third tubular or precontoured locking plate (neutralization or lag-screw-plus-neutralization construct depending on fracture obliquity); intramedullary fibular nailing is a good option in osteoporotic bone or compromised soft tissue (diabetics, elderly) with lower wound complication rates.
- Medial malleolus: two partially threaded cancellous screws or a tension band construct for small/avulsion fragments; a small buttress plate for vertical shear (supination-adduction) patterns.
- Restore the mortise anatomically — malreduction of even 1–2 mm of fibular shortening/rotation measurably increases contact pressure and accelerates post-traumatic arthritis; always compare intraoperative fluoroscopy to contralateral uninjured mortise views when in doubt.

**Syndesmosis stabilization**
- After fibula fixation, stress-test every Weber B/C ankle (Cotton test + external rotation) — if positive, stabilize.
- **Screw fixation**: one or two 3.5/4.5 mm quadricortical (or tricortical) screws, 2–4 cm above the joint line, angled 25–30° anterior-to-posterior (parallel to the joint), through or just above the plate. Historically debated whether to remove screws prophylactically (routine removal not required if asymptomatic; a rigid screw allows no physiologic fibular motion during dorsiflexion, and breakage is common but usually benign).
- **Suture-button (dynamic) fixation**: increasingly preferred based on **level-I evidence** — a 2024 meta-analysis of RCTs found suture-button fixation associated with lower syndesmotic malreduction (1% vs 12%), fewer broken implants, lower reoperation and implant-failure rates, and faster/earlier return to work versus screws; a 6-year RCT follow-up (pronation-external-rotation injuries) showed sustained suture-button benefit. Suture-button allows physiologic micromotion, avoids a planned second surgery for hardware removal, and is preferred in athletes and higher-demand patients; screws remain reasonable/cost-effective, particularly where suture-button implants are unavailable or in comminuted/very unstable syndesmoses where added rigidity is desired.
- **Reduction verification** is the technical crux — fluoroscopy alone under-detects malreduction; use direct visualization/reduction clamp technique referencing the anterior and posterior tibial tubercles, contralateral-comparison fluoroscopy, or intraoperative CT/O-arm/3D imaging where available, since malreduction (reported in a substantial minority to majority of screw-fixed cases judged by fluoroscopy alone) is strongly linked to worse patient-reported outcomes at 2 years.
- Fixing an adequately sized **posterior malleolus fragment** (it carries the PITFL insertion) often restores syndesmotic stability directly and can obviate a separate trans-syndesmotic screw/suture-button — assess syndesmosis stability *after* posterior malleolus fixation, not before.

**Posterior malleolus (Mason & Molloy classification and Liverpool treatment algorithm)**
| Type | Morphology | Approach / fixation |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Small extra-incisural posterolateral fragment, syndesmosis (PITFL) usually not attached to a large enough fragment to warrant direct fixation | Standard lateral/anterolateral approach for fibula; stabilize syndesmosis directly (screw/suture-button) rather than fixing the malleolus fragment itself |
| **2A** | Posterolateral fragment extending into the incisura, two-part | Posterolateral approach, direct reduction and buttress/antiglide or lag screw fixation |
| **2B** | Same as 2A plus an additional (comminuted/intermediate) fragment | Posterolateral approach ± accessory posteromedial exposure to address the intermediate fragment; may need two plates/fragment-specific fixation |
| **3** | Postermedial extension, often a large "die-punch"-type fragment | Posteromedial approach, direct buttress plating; frequently combined with medial malleolus fixation |

**Fixation threshold**: direct fixation is favored when the fragment involves **≥25% of the articular surface** on lateral radiograph, or by CT-defined criteria (incisural involvement, articular step-off >2 mm, comminution, or syndesmotic instability persisting after fibula/medial fixation) per the Mason & Molloy framework — CT morphology now drives the decision more than a single fragment-size cutoff, since posterior malleolus fractures are increasingly understood as syndesmosis-ligament-avulsion equivalents rather than purely a "size" problem. Direct buttress fixation of the posterior malleolus restores articular congruity, reduces the incidence of anterior talar subluxation, and — as above — frequently restores syndesmotic stability without an additional syndesmotic implant.

**Talus fracture**
- Emergent closed reduction (sedation, longitudinal traction with foot plantarflexed, then correction of deformity) for any dislocated talar fracture with skin compromise — this is a surgical/reduction emergency independent of when definitive fixation occurs.
- Definitive ORIF once soft tissues allow (typically within days): dual (anteromedial + anterolateral) approaches to visualize both columns of the neck without further devascularizing the body; anatomic reduction is the single most important modifiable determinant of outcome. Fixation with headless compression screws (posterior-to-anterior preferred biomechanically, or anterior-to-posterior when posterior approach is impractical) ± a medial or lateral neutralization/buttress plate for comminuted neck fractures to prevent varus malunion (the most common malunion pattern, causing lateral column overload).
- Body/comminuted fractures may need a medial malleolar osteotomy for adequate visualization.
- Counsel patients on high AVN and post-traumatic arthritis rates even with anatomic reduction, particularly Hawkins III/IV; core decompression is not evidence-supported for established talar AVN — collapse is managed with staged bracing/protected weight-bearing and, if progressive, tibiotalocalcaneal fusion or talar body allograft/prosthetic replacement.

**Calcaneus fracture**
- Timing: delay surgery until soft-tissue swelling permits (positive "wrinkle sign", typically 10–14 days) unless using the sinus tarsi approach, which tolerates earlier surgery.
- **Extensile lateral approach**: full visualization of the posterior facet and lateral wall, plate fixation — historically standard for Sanders II/III/IV but associated with a meaningful wound complication rate (historically cited up to ~25% in some series, lower in contemporary practice with careful soft-tissue technique/no-touch flap handling).
- **Sinus tarsi (minimally invasive) approach**: direct visualization of the posterior facet through a smaller incision, percutaneous screw ± limited plate fixation; lower wound complication rates than extensile lateral, comparable reduction quality in experienced hands for II/III patterns, now the preferred approach at many high-volume centers.
- **Non-operative vs ORIF (Sanders II/III)**: UK HeFT long-term (60-month) data show **no significant difference in Kerr-Atkins functional score** between ORIF and non-operative treatment (79.2 vs 76.4), no difference in walking difficulty or shoe-fitting, but **more additional surgical procedures in the ORIF group** (10 vs 4 patients, p=0.043) — use this to counsel patients that surgery does not guarantee superior long-term function and carries its own reoperation burden, while acknowledging earlier/shorter-term series and meta-analyses of Sanders II/III favor ORIF for functional scores at ~2 years. Decision should be individualized (occupation, activity demand, soft tissue, comorbidities, patient preference) rather than protocolized.
- **Primary subtalar arthrodesis**: favored for Sanders IV (comminuted, unreconstructable posterior facet) — recent series report subtalar union rates around 94% with good functional outcomes and avoidance of a staged secondary fusion; also reasonable in older/lower-demand patients with Sanders III as a way to avoid a later arthrodesis. ORIF still edges out primary arthrodesis on pooled functional scores for Sanders II/III specifically.
- Restore Böhler's angle, heel height/width, and hindfoot alignment; address lateral wall exostosis/peroneal impingement at index surgery if present.

**Lisfranc injury**
- Anatomic reduction is the dominant predictor of outcome regardless of construct chosen — nonanatomic reduction (>2 mm residual displacement) is strongly associated with post-traumatic arthritis and poor functional scores.
- **ORIF**: transarticular screws (medial 3 TMT joints: 1st TMT and 2nd TMT ± 3rd TMT with 3.5/4.0 mm screws) and/or dorsal bridge plating across the 1st–2nd and 2nd–3rd TMT joints (avoids traversing articular cartilage with a screw); K-wires or a bridge plate for the more mobile, less-reducible 4th–5th rays (avoid rigid screw fixation of the lateral column, which tolerates and requires more physiologic motion). Hardware is often removed electively at 3–6 months, especially transarticular screws, given documented breakage/loosening rates.
- **Primary arthrodesis** of the medial 3 TMT joints: favored for purely ligamentous injury (no reconstructable articular surface), comminuted/unreconstructable fracture-dislocations, and delayed presentation; theoretical advantage of avoiding a planned second hardware-removal surgery and directly addressing the joints most prone to post-traumatic arthritis (1st–3rd TMT).
- **Evidence comparing the two**: a 2024 RCT-only meta-analysis (5 RCTs, 241 patients) found **no significant difference** in patient-reported outcomes or complication rates between primary arthrodesis and ORIF, though PA trended toward fewer secondary procedures/hardware removals; a separate 2024 meta-analysis of 18 studies (mixed RCT/observational) found **better AOFAS and VAS scores with primary arthrodesis** (AOFAS 84.4 vs 75.7); a large multicenter RCT found no difference in VAS-FA between arms. Practical synthesis: neither approach is clearly superior across all injury patterns — favor ORIF when the joint surfaces are reducible/preservable (especially in younger, high-demand patients where joint preservation is desirable), favor PA when injury is purely ligamentous, comminuted, or presentation is delayed.
- Lateral column (4th–5th TMT) injuries are essentially never fused primarily — preserve motion with temporary K-wire or bridge-plate fixation.

**Navicular & cuboid fracture**
- Displaced navicular body fractures: ORIF via dorsomedial/dorsolateral approach with screws ± plating to restore the medial column; comminuted/impacted fractures may need structural bone graft to restore length. Recent series report much higher fracture-healing/lower refracture rates with operative treatment of displaced fractures (~98% success, ~1% refracture) versus non-operative management of the same displaced injuries (~78% success, ~24% refracture) — displacement is the key trigger for surgery.
- Cuboid ("nutcracker") fractures with lateral column shortening: ORIF/structural bone grafting to restore lateral column length, since shortening leads to forefoot abduction and midfoot collapse; associated with Lisfranc and calcaneocuboid injuries frequently enough that the entire midfoot must be imaged and stressed intraoperatively.
- Isolated navicular/cuboid stress fractures failing conservative management: percutaneous or open screw fixation, particularly in athletes for faster/more reliable return to sport.

### Special populations & perioperative pearls

- **Elderly/osteoporotic ankle fractures**: consider fibular intramedullary nailing over plating (less periosteal stripping, lower wound complication rate in thin, fragile skin), locked plating constructs, or augmenting medial malleolar fixation with a buttress plate rather than screws alone in osteoporotic bone; a syndesmosis-only or tibiotalocalcaneal nailing construct is a recognized salvage option in the frail, non-ambulatory, or severely osteoporotic patient where standard ORIF is high-risk.
- **Diabetic neuropathy/Charcot risk**: treat with a low threshold for prolonged immobilization, extended NWB periods, and "overtreat" fixation (bridge plating, additional screws, or combined intramedullary + plate constructs) — diabetic ankle fractures have substantially higher rates of malunion, nonunion, and wound complications; involve a multidisciplinary (vascular/endocrine/wound-care) team early.
- **Open fractures** (most common in talus, calcaneus, and high-energy Lisfranc injuries): follow standard open-fracture principles — urgent IV antibiotics, irrigation and debridement, provisional stabilization (external fixation or spanning frame if definitive fixation is not immediately safe), staged definitive fixation once the soft-tissue envelope allows; involve plastic surgery early for Gustilo IIIB/IIIC injuries requiring flap coverage.
- **Compartment syndrome of the foot**: nine-compartment anatomy; when suspected (Lisfranc, calcaneus, crush injuries), perform fasciotomy through a dorsal (2-incision) ± medial approach without delay — missed compartment syndrome produces claw-toe deformity and permanent disability disproportionate to the index fracture.
- **Damage control in polytrauma**: spanning external fixation of the ankle/hindfoot is appropriate when the patient is not a physiologic candidate for definitive fixation or when soft tissues are too swollen/compromised — convert to definitive fixation once the patient and soft tissues stabilize, generally within 2–3 weeks before pin-tract considerations and soft-tissue changes complicate later surgery.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Ankle fracture (post-ORIF, stable fixation)**
- 0–2 weeks: splint/boot, strict elevation, NWB or toe-touch, early toe/knee ROM, wound check at 10–14 days.
- 2–6 weeks: transition to removable boot, begin protected weight-bearing per contemporary evidence — the **WAX trial** (UK RCT) found weight-bearing initiated at **2 weeks** post-ORIF was non-inferior (trending superior) to the traditional 6-week protocol for functional outcomes, with no increase in complications and favorable cost-effectiveness; the 2024 AAOS guideline update likewise supports early protected weight-bearing after anatomically reduced, rigidly plated bimalleolar/isolated lateral malleolar fractures. Begin active/active-assisted ankle ROM (dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, avoid aggressive inversion in medial-sided injuries) once wound healed.
- 6–12 weeks: progressive WBAT to full weight-bearing, gait training, proprioception/balance work, progressive strengthening (theraband, calf raises as tolerated).
- 12 weeks onward: return to impact/sport once radiographic union confirmed, full painless ROM, and strength ≥90% of contralateral side; return to sedentary work typically 2–6 weeks, manual labor 3–4 months depending on fixation stability and job demands.

**Syndesmosis-specific**: suture-button constructs generally tolerate the same early WB protocols as isolated fibula fixation given their dynamic nature; screw constructs historically prompted more conservative/delayed WB until screw removal or radiographic consolidation, though this gap is narrowing in contemporary practice with rigid, well-reduced constructs.

**Talus fracture**
- 0–6 weeks: strict NWB, splint/cast, elevation; watch closely for wound issues given thin soft-tissue envelope.
- 6–12 weeks: progressive WB guided by radiographic union (talar fractures unite more slowly than most foot/ankle fractures) — begin only once trabecular bridging is evident; premature WB risks talar body collapse in AVN-prone fractures.
- 3–6 months: continue AVN surveillance with serial radiographs (Hawkins sign timing, subchondral collapse); if AVN develops, protected weight-bearing/bracing until revascularization or collapse dictates salvage surgery.
- Return to sport/heavy labor generally deferred to 6–12 months given slow union and AVN risk window.

**Calcaneus fracture (post-ORIF or non-op)**
- 0–2 weeks: elevation, NWB, early subtalar/ankle active ROM exercises started as soon as pain allows (even before WB) to combat stiffness — this is one of the most important modifiable elements of hindfoot rehab.
- 2–8 weeks: continued NWB with ROM progression; wound surveillance especially with extensile lateral approach.
- 8–12 weeks: progressive WB introduced once radiographic consolidation present (~10–12 weeks is typical), sinus tarsi approach patients may progress slightly earlier than extensile-lateral patients.
- 3–6 months: full WB, strengthening, gait normalization; heavy manual labor/return to sport often delayed 6–12 months; a meaningful minority (10–15%+) require secondary procedures (hardware removal, subtalar fusion for post-traumatic arthritis) in the first 1–2 years, higher after ORIF than after non-op per HeFT long-term data.

**Lisfranc injury (post-fixation)**
- 0–6 weeks: NWB, splint/cast, elevation.
- 6–10 weeks: transition to boot, begin protected/progressive WB guided by fixation construct stability and radiographic maintenance of reduction; bridge-plated/K-wire lateral column fixation generally allows earlier progressive WB than transarticular screws across weight-bearing medial rays.
- 10–12 weeks: full WB, gait retraining, arch-support orthotic consideration.
- 3–6 months: hardware removal (if transarticular screws used) typically performed at ~4–6 months before full return to high-demand activity, since retained screws across a mobile joint risk breakage under load.
- Return to sport for athletes: typically 6–9 months, later for high-demand cutting/jumping sports; counsel patients that post-traumatic arthritis is common even with anatomic reduction and good early function.

**Navicular/cuboid fracture**: NWB cast 6–8 weeks for displaced/operatively fixed fractures (navicular in particular given watershed vascularity and historically slow/unreliable union), then progressive WB in boot over 2–4 weeks, full activity by 4–6 months; stress fractures treated operatively may return to sport somewhat faster (10–12 weeks) than those treated with prolonged non-operative immobilization.

**Physiotherapy content common to this cluster**: early phase focuses on edema control (elevation, compression, ankle pumps within the cast/boot as permitted), and active-assisted ROM in planes not stressing the healing structure (e.g., avoid inversion after medial-sided ankle injury, avoid forefoot abduction stress after Lisfranc fixation). Mid phase introduces closed-chain proprioception (single-leg stance progression, wobble board once WBAT), gastrocnemius-soleus stretching (contracture is near-universal after prolonged immobilization), and progressive resistance (theraband, then closed-chain leg press/calf raise). Late phase adds plyometrics, agility drills, and sport-specific retraining only after painless full ROM and ≥85–90% strength symmetry versus the contralateral limb.

**Return-to-work/return-to-sport summary**

| Diagnosis | Sedentary work | Manual labor | Return to sport |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ankle fracture (stable ORIF) | 2–6 weeks | 3–4 months | 3–5 months |
| Syndesmosis injury (fixed) | 2–6 weeks | 3–4 months | 4–6 months |
| Talus fracture | 6–12 weeks (sedentary) | 6–12 months | 6–12 months, if at all for high-impact sport |
| Calcaneus fracture | 6–10 weeks | 6–12 months | 6–12 months |
| Lisfranc injury | 6–10 weeks | 4–6 months | 6–9 months |
| Navicular/cuboid fracture | 4–8 weeks | 3–5 months | 4–6 months |

## Complications

- **Post-traumatic arthritis**: the dominant long-term complication across this entire cluster — ankle (from malreduction/incongruity), subtalar (calcaneus, talus), and TMT joints (Lisfranc) — driven primarily by residual articular incongruity/step-off at index treatment.
- **Avascular necrosis**: defining complication of talar neck/body fractures (rates by Hawkins type above); managed with surveillance, protected weight-bearing during the revascularization window (creeping substitution can take 1–3 years), and staged fusion/reconstruction for collapse.
- **Malunion**: varus malunion is the classic talar neck complication (overload of the lateral column, requires corrective osteotomy if symptomatic); calcaneal malunion (loss of height/width, lateral wall impingement on peroneals, hindfoot valgus) may require lateral wall exostectomy, peroneal tenolysis, or corrective osteotomy/fusion.
- **Syndesmotic malreduction**: strongly associated with inferior patient-reported outcomes at 2 years; the single most preventable technical error in ankle fracture surgery — mitigate with meticulous intraoperative reduction technique and low threshold for advanced intraoperative imaging.
- **Wound complications/infection**: highest with the extensile lateral calcaneal approach (historically up to ~25% in some series, lower with modern soft-tissue technique and appropriate timing) and in talus/pilon-adjacent injuries with compromised soft-tissue envelopes; sinus tarsi approach for calcaneus reduces this risk.
- **Compartment syndrome (foot)**: under-recognized in high-energy Lisfranc and calcaneus injuries; missed compartment syndrome leads to clawing, contracture, and chronic pain — maintain a low threshold to measure compartment pressures in tense, disproportionately painful injuries.
- **Nonunion**: uncommon in most of this cluster with adequate fixation, but a recognized risk in navicular fractures (central watershed hypovascularity) and comminuted talar neck fractures.
- **Hardware failure/prominence**: syndesmotic screw breakage (usually asymptomatic, does not mandate removal), Lisfranc transarticular screw breakage under physiologic load (a rationale for either bridge-plating or planned removal).
- **Chronic regional pain/stiffness**: hindfoot and midfoot injuries in particular carry a meaningfully higher rate of persistent stiffness and reduced patient-reported function than ankle-only fractures, independent of radiographic outcome — set expectations accordingly at the first visit.

|---|---|---|

## Recent Evidence

- **2024 level-I meta-analysis (syndesmosis)**: pooled RCT data show suture-button fixation achieves lower syndesmotic malreduction (1% vs 12%), fewer implant breakages, and lower reoperation rates than screw fixation — reinforces suture-button as the preferred construct where available. [Better outcomes using suture button compared to screw fixation in talofibular syndesmotic injuries — PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38740648/)
- **6-year RCT follow-up (Injury, pronation-external-rotation injuries)**: confirms durable clinical benefit of suture-button over screw fixation persists to long-term follow-up, not just early recovery. [Suture button versus syndesmosis screw fixation — minimum 6-year follow-up RCT](https://www.injuryjournal.com/article/S0020-1383(21)00588-X/fulltext)
- **UK HeFT long-term (60-month) outcomes (Bone & Joint Journal)**: ORIF and non-operative treatment of displaced intra-articular calcaneal fractures show no difference in Kerr-Atkins functional scores at 5 years, but ORIF carries a significantly higher rate of additional surgical procedures (p=0.043) — the key long-term evidence for individualized, preference-sensitive counseling in Sanders II/III calcaneus fractures. [Open reduction and internal fixation versus nonoperative treatment for calcaneal fractures — long-term HeFT follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34058883/)
- **2024 RCT-only meta-analysis, Lisfranc (Foot & Ankle Orthopaedics)**: 5 RCTs/241 patients show no significant difference in patient-reported outcomes between primary arthrodesis and ORIF, refining the "either is reasonable" position with actual randomized-trial pooling rather than observational data. [Primary Arthrodesis or ORIF for Lisfranc Injuries — meta-analysis of RCTs, 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/24730114241286892)
- **2024 comprehensive meta-analysis, Lisfranc (Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery)**: pooled data (18 studies) show better AOFAS/VAS scores with primary arthrodesis versus ORIF, adding nuance/tension to the RCT-only finding above and supporting individualized selection by injury pattern (ligamentous vs reconstructable articular injury). [Primary arthrodesis versus ORIF for acute Lisfranc injuries — systematic review and meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00402-024-05700-z)
- **Mason & Molloy interobserver reliability study (Cureus, 2023)**: 20 raters across general trauma and dedicated foot/ankle surgeons classifying 38 CT scans — establishes that the Mason & Molloy system, while more clinically actionable than fragment-size cutoffs, still has meaningful interobserver variability, particularly distinguishing 2A/2B — a caution for relying on it in isolation for surgical planning. [Enhancing Consistency in Posterior Malleolus Fracture Classification — Mason & Molloy interobserver reliability study](https://www.cureus.com/articles/203473-enhancing-consistency-in-posterior-malleolus-fracture-classification-a-comprehensive-interobserver-reliability-study-with-20-raters-using-the-mason--molloy-classification)
- **2024 AAOS guideline update on early weight-bearing after ankle ORIF**: supports early protected weight-bearing for stable fixation (bimalleolar or isolated lateral malleolar fractures with anatomic reduction and rigid plating), with data showing faster recovery and no increase in fixation failure. [Early Weight-Bearing After ORIF of Ankle Fractures — evidence behind the AAOS guideline update](https://fractured.ai/articles/early-weight-bearing-after-orif-of-ankle-fractures-evidence-behind-the-2024-aaos/)
- **WAX trial (UK RCT, referenced in 2025 BOAST-compliance literature)**: weight-bearing started at 2 weeks post-ankle-ORIF is non-inferior (trending superior) to the traditional 6-week protocol, with no increase in complications and a high probability of cost-effectiveness — the primary evidence base behind the BOAST early-weight-bearing standard. [Early Weight-Bearing Following Ankle Fixation in a UK Major Trauma Centre — real-world adoption vs the WAX RCT and BOAST standards](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12710795/)
- **2025 closed-loop audit (Cureus)**: documents real-world compliance gaps with BOAST early-weight-bearing standards after ankle fixation at a UK district general hospital, useful as a benchmark for practice-improvement/QI initiatives. [Improving Compliance With BOAST Guidelines for Early Weight Bearing Following Ankle Fracture Fixation — closed-loop audit](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12356356/)
- **2024–2025 primary subtalar arthrodesis literature (Bone & Joint Journal and systematic review)**: reinforces primary subtalar fusion as a viable, durable option in selected comminuted (Sanders IV, some Sanders III) calcaneal fractures, with union rates in contemporary series around 94%. [Primary subtalar arthrodesis is a viable option for selected patients with a calcaneal fracture](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41763241/); [Primary subtalar arthrodesis in displaced intra-articular calcaneal fracture — systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40375052/)
- **Hawkins II/III complication series (2024, Journal of Foot & Ankle Surgery/PMC)**: confirms Hawkins III fractures carry significantly higher rates of AVN, infection, and reoperation than Hawkins II, while nonunion rates do not significantly differ between types — refines counseling on which specific complications scale with fracture severity. [Hawkins II and III Talus Neck Fractures — complication rates and utility of Hawkins sign](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11686673/)
- **Osteonecrosis risk-factor study (2023, PubMed)**: identifies patient- and injury-level risk factors for AVN after talar fracture beyond Hawkins type alone, supporting a more individualized AVN-risk conversation with patients rather than relying on classification type in isolation. [Identifying Risk Factors for Osteonecrosis After Talar Fracture](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37735752/)
- **Navicular fracture management outcomes (2023 data cited in StatPearls/clinical review)**: operative treatment of displaced navicular fractures shows markedly higher union/success rates (~98%) and lower refracture rates (~1%) than non-operative treatment of displaced fractures (~78% success, ~24% refracture), sharpening the threshold for surgery in displaced navicular body fractures. [Tarsal Navicular Fractures — StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK542221/)
