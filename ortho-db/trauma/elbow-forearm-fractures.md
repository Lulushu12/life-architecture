## Quick Reference

**Red flags — do not miss:**
- **Posterior interosseous nerve (PIN)** palsy with any Monteggia/radial neck fracture — document pre-reduction; usually neurapraxia, observe 3 months before exploring.
- **Compartment syndrome** — highest risk with both-bone forearm fracture, floating elbow, high-energy Monteggia; disproportionate pain + pain on passive stretch trumps pulse status.
- **Open fracture** — olecranon and proximal ulna fractures are frequently subcutaneous/open; treat per BOAST open fracture standard (debridement <24h for low-grade, IV antibiotics within 1h of arrival).
- **Missed DRUJ injury in Galeazzi** — always image wrist; ulnar styloid base fracture, DRUJ widening on true lateral, or instability on exam after radius fixation.
- **Missed proximal radioulnar/interosseous membrane injury in radial head fracture (Essex-Lopresti)** — wrist pain out of proportion, tenderness over DRUJ; do not resect the radial head without excluding this.
- **Redislocation risk after "simple" elbow dislocation** — recheck concentric reduction on true lateral; > 50% widening of ulnohumeral joint on AP or lateral = instability, not "simple."
- **Vascular injury** — brachial artery at risk in high-energy distal humerus and elbow fracture-dislocations; document pulses/Doppler pre- and post-reduction.

**Key numbers:**
- Coronoid: fragments below the **O'Driscoll "tip" level** are often not visible on standard lateral radiograph but critical to anterior capsular/brachialis attachment.
- Radial head excision alone is relatively contraindicated when there is **associated LCL injury, coronoid fracture, or interosseous membrane/DRUJ injury** (converts a stable injury into a chronically unstable one).
- Terrible triad "stability arc" intraoperative test: elbow should track concentrically through **30°–130°** flexion without subluxation before closing.
- Acceptable both-bone forearm fracture reduction (children, not applicable to adults who need anatomic ORIF): not applicable — **adults require anatomic reduction and rigid fixation**, malunion of >10° angulation or >10° loss of rotation measurably reduces forearm rotation.
- Olecranon fracture displacement threshold for surgery: **>2 mm** articular step-off or extensor mechanism disruption.
- Monteggia Bado I is ~**70%** of adult Monteggia equivalents; missed proximal radial head dislocation is the most common cause of poor outcome.
- Essex-Lopresti: radial head resection without addressing the injury → proximal radial migration and **ulnocarpal impaction**; average proximal migration can exceed 5–10 mm over months if unrecognized.
- Elbow stiffness: functional arc for most ADLs = **30°–130° flexion-extension, 50° pronation/50° supination** (Morrey's functional arc).

**Treatment ladder (first-line → second-line → surgical) by diagnosis:**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical (definitive) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distal humerus fracture (AO 13A/B, non/minimally displaced) | Cast/splint, early motion at 1–2 wk if stable | — | ORIF (dual-plate 90-90 or parallel) |
| Distal humerus fracture, elderly comminuted intra-articular (AO 13C) | — | — | ORIF vs primary **total elbow arthroplasty (TEA)** in low-demand elderly |
| Radial head fracture, Mason I | Sling, early AROM within days | Aspiration of hemarthrosis for pain | Rarely surgical |
| Radial head fracture, Mason II | Early motion if mechanical block absent | — | ORIF if block to rotation/>2mm step + >30% articular surface |
| Radial head fracture, Mason III/IV | — | — | ORIF if reconstructable (≤3 fragments); **radial head arthroplasty (RHA)** if comminuted/non-reconstructable, especially with associated instability |
| Olecranon fracture, non-displaced | Long-arm splint 1–2 wk then hinged brace | — | Tension-band wiring (transverse, low-energy) |
| Olecranon fracture, displaced/comminuted (Mayo IIB/III) | — | — | Plate fixation (comminuted, oblique/unstable, osteoporotic) or IM screw (simple transverse) |
| Elbow dislocation, simple | Closed reduction + early (3–5 day) protected AROM | Hinged brace if mild instability | Ligament repair if grossly unstable/re-dislocates in brace |

| Monteggia (Bado I–IV) | — | — | Anatomic ulna fixation (plate) restores radial head reduction; address radial head/neck fracture as needed |
| Galeazzi | — | — | Anatomic radius plating restores DRUJ; pin DRUJ if unstable after fixation |
| Both-bone forearm fracture (adult) | — | — | Dual plating (gold standard); IM nailing selected cases |
| Essex-Lopresti | — | — | Radial head fixation/arthroplasty + IOM/TFCC management + DRUJ stabilization (temporary pin) |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The elbow is a highly congruent trocho-ginglymoid joint stabilized by a combination of **bony architecture** (olecranon-trochlear and radiocapitellar articulations), the **medial (ulnar) collateral ligament (MCL)** complex — anterior bundle is the primary restraint to valgus stress — and the **lateral collateral ligament (LCL) complex**, principally the **lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL)**, the primary restraint to posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI). The **radial head** is a secondary valgus stabilizer and the primary restraint to proximal radial migration (longitudinal forearm stability) via its relationship to the interosseous membrane (IOM) central band and the triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) at the wrist — the "three-ring" concept of longitudinal forearm stability (proximal: radiocapitellar joint; middle: IOM central band; distal: TFCC/DRUJ).

The **coronoid process** is a critical anterior buttress against posterior subluxation/dislocation; it is also the attachment of the anterior capsule and the anterior bundle of the MCL. Loss of coronoid height, even a small "tip" fragment, disproportionately destabilizes the elbow because it removes the anterior bony block and the soft-tissue sleeve attached to it.

**Terrible triad** = elbow dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture — the injury pattern reflects a single valgus-posterolateral rotatory mechanism: the forearm supinates and the elbow is axially loaded in valgus, disrupting the LCL first (usually off the humeral origin), then the anterior capsule and coronoid, with the radial head fracturing from impaction against the capitellum. Understanding this "circle of Horii" injury progression (lateral to medial soft-tissue disruption) explains why LCL repair is essential even when the radial head and coronoid are fixed.

**Monteggia** injuries (proximal ulna fracture + radiocapitellar dislocation) and **Galeazzi** injuries (distal-third radius fracture + DRUJ disruption) are both "one-bone-fracture-plus-joint-dislocation" injuries of forearm ring integrity — restoring the length and alignment of the fractured bone is usually necessary and sufficient to reduce the associated joint, provided the fracture is anatomically fixed.

**Essex-Lopresti** is a longitudinal radioulnar dissociation: radial head fracture + IOM (central band) disruption + DRUJ injury. Because the radius is the primary axial load-bearing structure of the forearm distal to the elbow, an unrecognized IOM injury after radial head excision allows the radius to migrate proximally, transferring load to the ulnocarpal joint (ulnar impaction) and destabilizing the DRUJ and radiocapitellar joint chronically.

**Both-bone forearm fractures** disrupt the radius-ulna "ring" and the interosseous membrane tension that maintains the radial bow (critical for pronosupination); anatomic length, rotation, and the radial bow must be restored or forearm rotation is permanently compromised — this is why adult diaphyseal forearm fractures are treated operatively far more consistently than most other long-bone fractures.

**Distal humerus fractures** occur through the thin "dumbbell"-shaped supracondylar bone stock connecting the medial and lateral columns to the humeral shaft — the trochlea and capitellum form the articular block suspended between two columns that converge proximally. Comminution here is common because the bone is thin and largely cancellous distally; this is why dual-column plating (rather than single-plate or intramedullary fixation) is required to neutralize the bending and torsional forces across both columns simultaneously, and why elderly osteoporotic comminution can render ORIF biomechanically unreliable, favoring TEA.

**Elbow (soft-tissue) stability hierarchy** — useful conceptual framework for surgical decision-making: primary stabilizers are the ulnohumeral articulation, the MCL anterior bundle, and the LUCL; secondary stabilizers are the radial head, the common flexor-pronator and extensor origins, and the joint capsule. An injury pattern that disrupts only secondary stabilizers with primary stabilizers intact (e.g., isolated radial head fracture without LCL injury) tends to remain stable; injury combinations that breach both a primary and secondary stabilizer (terrible triad = LUCL + radial head + coronoid/anterior capsule) produce gross instability, which is why the treatment goal in these injuries is not merely fracture healing but restoring the full stabilizer envelope.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History/mechanism:** Fall on outstretched hand (FOOSH) with elbow extended/forearm supinated → terrible triad/simple dislocation pattern. Direct blow to forearm ("nightstick") → isolated ulna fracture (consider non-accidental/assault). Fall with forced pronation + axial load → Monteggia. High-energy axial load through extended wrist with forearm loaded → Essex-Lopresti (classically a fall onto outstretched hand with a "crunch" felt at the wrist, or industrial/vehicular crush).

**Exam:**
- Full neurovascular exam before and after any reduction: radial, median (including AIN — thumb/index tip pinch), ulnar (including PIN — thumb/finger extension, no sensory component) function; radial and ulnar pulses, capillary refill.
- Palpate the **entire forearm and wrist** in every elbow fracture, and the **entire elbow** in every forearm/wrist fracture — mandatory to avoid missing Monteggia, Galeazzi, or Essex-Lopresti.
- Assess skin integrity/open wounds circumferentially (olecranon fractures are frequently open due to subcutaneous location).
- Stability testing (varus/valgus stress, forearm rotation with elbow at 30° flexion for PLRI/"pivot-shift" apprehension) — best performed under anesthesia intraoperatively for terrible triad/complex dislocations, not in the acute ED setting.
- DRUJ ballottement and TFCC stress testing in every radius/ulna fracture.

**Imaging:**
- AP/lateral elbow, AP/lateral/oblique forearm, AP/lateral wrist — **image the joint above and below** any forearm fracture as standard practice.
- True lateral elbow radiograph is essential to judge concentric reduction (ulnohumeral joint space should be parallel/symmetric) and coronoid fragment size.
- **CT with 2D/3D reconstruction** for essentially all intra-articular distal humerus fractures (AO 13B/C), comminuted radial head fractures, and any coronoid fracture where size/comminution changes the fixation plan (O'Driscoll classification requires CT).
- Contralateral wrist radiograph for comparison of ulnar variance when Essex-Lopresti or chronic Galeazzi/DRUJ injury is suspected (radial shortening >2–3 mm relative to contralateral side is diagnostic).
- MRI selectively for equivocal IOM injury (Essex-Lopresti) or LCL/MCL integrity in low-energy "simple" dislocations being considered for non-operative management, though clinical stress exam under fluoroscopy is often more practical intraoperatively.
- Post-reduction films mandatory for all dislocations to confirm concentricity before splinting.

**Diagnosis-specific evaluation pearls:**
- **Terrible triad / complex fracture-dislocation:** examination under anesthesia (EUA) with fluoroscopic stress testing is the definitive stability assessment and should be planned as part of the operative workup rather than relied upon in the awake ED patient, where guarding limits accuracy.
- **Monteggia:** radial head dislocation direction on the initial films (anterior/posterior/lateral) determines Bado type and should be specifically documented; a "missed Monteggia" (chronic radial head dislocation after an ulna fracture treated as isolated) is a recognized and largely preventable diagnostic failure — always trace a line through the radial shaft/head (radiocapitellar line) on every elbow and forearm view.
- **Galeazzi:** the "rule of thumb" — any distal-third radius shaft fracture with >5mm radial shortening, >5° angulation, or a fracture of the ulnar styloid base should raise suspicion for DRUJ instability even if the joint appears reduced on initial films.
- **Essex-Lopresti:** obtain a true AP wrist film and measure ulnar variance; compare to the contralateral wrist. A radial head fracture with ANY wrist symptoms warrants this comparison as a low-cost, high-yield screening step.
- **Olecranon:** assess triceps continuity actively (resisted extension against gravity) — a positive extensor lag with a minimally displaced fracture on static imaging still requires surgical fixation because the fracture is functionally unstable regardless of the millimeters of displacement measured on a static film.

## Classification

### AO/OTA 13 — Distal Humerus Fracture

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **13A** | Extra-articular (epicondylar avulsion, supracondylar/metaphyseal) | Often amenable to single/dual column plating with simpler columnar reconstruction; good bone stock often allows earlier motion |
| **13B** | Partial articular (one column, capitellum/trochlea fractured, other column intact) | Column-specific fixation (headless screws for capitellar shear, posterolateral plate); preserve the intact column as a stable strut |
| **13C** | Complete articular (both columns + articular surface fractured, dissociated from shaft) | Requires dual-plate fixation (orthogonal 90-90 or parallel 3.5mm locking plates) restoring articular block first, then fixing to shaft; in elderly with unreconstructable comminution (C3) → primary TEA is an accepted alternative |

Subgroups (.1/.2/.3) further stratify simple vs. wedge vs. multifragmentary — increasing subgroup number correlates with more comminution and lower likelihood of a stable ORIF construct, raising the threshold to consider TEA in low-demand elderly patients.

### Mason Classification — Radial Head Fracture (modified Hotchkiss)

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Non-displaced or minimally displaced (<2mm), no mechanical block | Non-operative: sling, early AROM within days once comfortable |
| **II** | Displaced (>2mm) marginal fracture involving <30–50% of the head, or with a mechanical block to rotation | ORIF if block to motion or displacement threatens stability; screws (often headless, buried below cartilage) or a small fragment/mini plate outside the "safe zone" |
| **III** | Comminuted fracture involving the whole head, often not reconstructable | ORIF if ≤3 major fragments and reconstructable; radial head arthroplasty (RHA) if comminuted/non-reconstructable, particularly with associated elbow instability (terrible triad, Essex-Lopresti) |
| **IV (modified)** | Any Mason type associated with elbow dislocation | Treat radial head per fragment count/reconstructability, but bar for RHA over ORIF drops given the added instability — RHA restores the stabilizing strut more reliably than a marginal ORIF in the unstable elbow |

Radial head excision alone (without replacement) is now reserved for isolated, low-demand, mechanically-blocking fractures with a **confirmed intact LCL, coronoid, and IOM** — essentially rare in current practice given how easily instability is under-recognized acutely.

### Mayo Classification — Olecranon Fracture

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Non-displaced (<2mm), stable | Non-operative: brief splint then hinged brace, early motion |
| **IA** | Non-displaced, non-comminuted | Cast/splint 1–2 weeks |
| **IB** | Non-displaced, comminuted | Cast/splint, monitor for displacement |
| **II** | Displaced (>2mm or extensor mechanism disrupted), stable ulnohumeral joint | Surgical fixation required to restore extensor mechanism |
| **IIA** | Displaced, non-comminuted | Tension-band wiring (transverse fracture at the level of the trochlear notch, low-demand patient) |
| **IIB** | Displaced, comminuted | Plate fixation preferred (comminution makes TBW constructs fail); pre-contoured locking olecranon plate |
| **III** | Displaced, unstable (associated with ulnohumeral instability, e.g., fracture-dislocation, Monteggia-variant "transolecranon" injury) | Plate fixation with restoration of the coronoid/trochlear notch; may require addressing associated radial head/coronoid injury as in Monteggia-equivalent |

**Fixation selection pearl:** tension-band wiring is biomechanically a dynamic compression construct that works only with an intact anterior cortical hinge under axial (extensor) load — it fails/loses fixation in oblique, comminuted, or distally-extending fracture lines, where a plate (or an intramedullary screw ± suture augmentation for simple transverse patterns) is preferred to reduce reoperation for hardware prominence and loss of fixation.

### Bado Classification — Monteggia Injury (with Jupiter modification for adult variants)

| Type | Definition | Frequency | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Ulna shaft fracture (proximal/mid) with **anterior** dislocation of the radial head, apex-anterior ulnar angulation | Most common (~60–70%) | Anatomic ulna plate fixation typically reduces the radial head closed; confirm concentric reduction on true lateral post-fixation; explore radiocapitellar joint if not concentric |
| **II** | Ulna fracture with **posterior/posterolateral** dislocation of the radial head, apex-posterior angulation | Second most common in adults | Often associated with coronoid and/or radial head fractures (Monteggia "variant"/equivalent) — treat as a complex elbow fracture-dislocation, address coronoid/radial head as in terrible triad principles |
| **III** | Ulna metaphyseal fracture with **lateral/anterolateral** dislocation of the radial head | Predominantly pediatric | Anatomic ulna fixation; assess LCL integrity |
| **IV** | Fracture of **both radius and ulna** with anterior dislocation of the radial head | Rare | Fix both forearm bones anatomically; radial head reduction follows ulna length/alignment restoration |

**Key principle across all Bado types:** anatomic restoration of ulnar length and alignment is the primary maneuver that reduces the radial head; if the radial head does not reduce concentrically after solid anatomic ulna fixation, suspect an interposed annular ligament, an unaddressed radial head/neck fracture, or malreduction of the ulna and re-address before closing.

### O'Driscoll Classification — Coronoid Fracture

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Type 1 — Tip fracture** | Subtype 1: <2mm height; Subtype 2: ≥2mm height | Small tip fragments (<2mm) often left alone if elbow stable after LCL/radial head fixation; larger tip fragments (≥2mm) — suture fixation through drill holes to capture the anterior capsule attachment even when the bone fragment itself is unfixable |
| **Type 2 — Anteromedial facet fracture** | Subtype 1: rim only; Subtype 2: rim + tip; Subtype 3: rim + sublime tubercle | Associated with **varus posteromedial rotatory instability (VPMI)**, a distinct pattern from terrible triad; often requires buttress plating of the anteromedial facet (medial approach) because this fragment is critical for varus stability and is NOT adequately addressed by LCL repair alone |
| **Type 3 — Basal fracture** | Subtype 1: coronoid body/base, no involvement of olecranon; Subtype 2: transolecranon basal fracture (coronoid fracture extends into and through the proximal ulna/olecranon) | Large structural fragment requiring formal fixation (screws antegrade/retrograde, or plate) — essential for anterior stability; Type 3.2 (transolecranon) requires olecranon fixation that indirectly captures the coronoid fragment |

**Clinical use:** O'Driscoll type dictates surgical approach — Type 1/most Type 2 subtype-1 injuries are addressed through the lateral approach as part of terrible-triad sequencing; anteromedial facet fractures (Type 2, especially subtype 2/3) and large basal fractures (Type 3) usually require a separate medial approach for direct buttress fixation, because lateral-only fixation cannot adequately capture these fragments.

### Elbow Dislocation — Directional and Stability Classification

Not part of the five required classification systems above, but essential complementary framework for the elbow dislocation diagnosis in this cluster:

| Category | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple dislocation** | No associated fracture; ulnohumeral and radiocapitellar joints dislocate together (>90% are posterior/posterolateral) | Closed reduction, brief splint, early protected motion (see Treatment/Rehabilitation) |
| **Complex dislocation** | Dislocation with associated fracture (radial head, coronoid, or both = terrible triad; or with olecranon = transolecranon variant) | Operative stabilization per fracture-specific and terrible-triad protocols above |
| **Posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI)** | LUCL-deficient pattern; forearm subluxates/rotates around an intact MCL as the elbow extends and supinates | LUCL repair/reconstruction; the "pivot-shift" test is the clinical correlate |
| **Varus posteromedial rotatory instability (VPMI)** | Anteromedial facet coronoid fracture + LCL injury; forearm subluxates in varus/internal rotation | Anteromedial facet buttress plating (medial approach) is essential — LCL repair alone is inadequate, as noted under O'Driscoll type 2 above |
| **Valgus posterolateral instability** | MCL-dominant pattern, often from a valgus-overload mechanism (e.g., overhead throwing, distinct from the acute dislocation spectrum) | Usually managed non-operatively acutely; chronic valgus instability in overhead athletes is a separate entity (UCL reconstruction), outside the scope of the acute fracture-dislocation cluster |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Radial head Mason I:** sling for comfort 3–5 days, early active ROM as tolerated (start within the first week); aspiration of a tense hemarthrosis can reduce pain and may modestly improve early motion, though evidence for functional benefit is mixed.
- **Non-displaced/minimally-displaced olecranon (Mayo IA/IB):** posterior long-arm splint at 45–90° flexion for comfort 1–2 weeks, then hinged brace and progressive AROM; weekly radiographs for first 3 weeks to confirm no displacement.
- **Simple elbow dislocation (no fracture, concentric and stable reduction):** brief splint (≤1 week, often just days) followed by early protected active motion — this is now the standard of care; prolonged immobilization (>3 weeks) is associated with worse ROM and higher rates of chronic stiffness without improving stability. A hinged elbow brace (blocking terminal extension initially, progressively opened) can be used if mild instability is noted but full ligament repair is not indicated.
- **Non/minimally displaced distal humerus fracture (AO 13A, stable pattern) in a low-demand or medically unfit patient:** collar-and-cuff or splint with very early gentle motion, accepting some residual stiffness as a trade-off against surgical risk.
- Non-operative management is **not appropriate** as definitive treatment for displaced both-bone forearm fractures, Galeazzi, Monteggia, terrible triad, or Essex-Lopresti in adults — anatomic restoration of length/rotation/alignment is required for these to avoid functional loss.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Hemarthrosis aspiration** (radial head/Mason I-II, and acutely after simple dislocation) — intra-articular local anesthetic instillation can assist early ROM initiation and pain control in the first days; not disease-modifying, purely symptomatic/functional.
- **Botulinum toxin to biceps/brachialis** — emerging adjunct in select post-traumatic stiffness protocols to reduce spasm and facilitate stretching before contracture release surgery; evidence remains limited/case-series level, use selectively in refractory stiffness.
- **Heterotopic ossification (HO) prophylaxis** is the most clinically important adjunctive consideration in this cluster: indomethacin (or other NSAID) for 2–3 weeks, or single-fraction radiotherapy (700 cGy) in very high-risk cases (head injury + elbow fracture-dislocation, delayed fixation, associated burns), reduces clinically significant HO after complex elbow fracture-dislocations and terrible triad; routine single-dose NSAID prophylaxis is reasonable in high-risk terrible triad/distal humerus cases, balanced against bone-healing and GI/renal risk.
- Corticosteroid injection has **no role** in the acute fracture/dislocation setting; reserve for post-traumatic degenerative sequelae years later.

### Operative

**Distal humerus fracture (AO 13B/C):**
- **Approach:** posterior triceps-sparing (Bryan-Morrey/paratricipital), triceps-splitting, or olecranon osteotomy (best articular exposure for complex C-type fractures with comminution extending to the trochlea) — osteotomy adds a healing burden (nonunion risk of the osteotomy itself) so reserve for cases where exposure is genuinely inadequate otherwise.
- **Fixation:** dual-plating is the standard — either **orthogonal (90-90)** or **parallel** 3.5mm locking plate constructs on the medial and lateral columns; the AO principle is to achieve a construct rigid enough for **early active motion**, since stiffness (not fixation failure) is the dominant long-term complication. Restore the articular block first with interfragmentary screws, then fix the reconstructed block to the shaft with plates that converge distally to capture as many articular fragments as biomechanically possible.
- **Primary TEA:** indicated in low-demand elderly patients (typically >65–70 years) with unreconstructable comminution (C3), severe osteoporosis, or when ORIF cannot achieve a stable enough construct for early motion — accepts permanent lifting restrictions (~2 kg / 5 lb repetitive, ~5 kg / 11 lb single lift) but yields more reliable early functional recovery than a failed/salvage ORIF converted to TEA later. Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty is an alternative in younger, higher-demand patients with unreconstructable fractures where TEA's lifting restrictions and long-term loosening risk are unattractive, preserving the option of later conversion.
- **Pitfall:** ulnar nerve — routinely identify, protect, and consider anterior transposition (especially with medial plating or pre-existing ulnar symptoms); failure to protect is a leading cause of iatrogenic neuropathy.

**Surgical approach selection — practical comparison:**

| Approach | Best for | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Kocher (lateral, between anconeus and ECU)** | Radial head ORIF/RHA, terrible triad lateral-sided work (radial head + LCL) | PIN at risk if dissection strays anterior/proximal to the radiocapitellar joint with forearm in pronation — pronate the forearm during exposure to move PIN away |
| **Extensor digitorum communis (EDC)-splitting lateral** | Radial head access with less capsular disruption than Kocher | Slightly less extensile than Kocher for combined coronoid work |
| **Anterior (Henry-type) to elbow** | Coronoid-first approach in select terrible triad protocols; also access to brachialis-coronoid interval | Neurovascular structures (median nerve, brachial artery) require careful protection; less familiar to most trauma surgeons than lateral/posterior approaches |
| **Posterior triceps-sparing (paratricipital/Bryan-Morrey)** | Distal humerus ORIF, preserves extensor mechanism | Slightly reduced articular exposure vs. osteotomy for very comminuted C3 patterns |
| **Olecranon osteotomy** | Distal humerus C-type fractures with comminution extending into the trochlea, maximal articular exposure | Adds an osteotomy nonunion/hardware-irritation risk; avoid in patients where TEA might later be needed (violates the extensor mechanism TEA depends on) |
| **Medial (over the flexor-pronator origin, ulnar nerve identified)** | Anteromedial facet coronoid buttress plating, MCL repair | Requires ulnar nerve identification/protection or transposition; combine with lateral approach for combined medial-lateral instability |

**Radial head fracture:**
- ORIF technique: place screws/plates in the **"safe zone"** (roughly the 110° non-articulating arc when the forearm is held in neutral rotation, extending from the radial styloid to Lister's tubercle line) to avoid impingement on the PRUJ during rotation; headless compression screws buried beneath the articular cartilage for marginal fragments.
- RHA technique: modular metal (titanium/cobalt-chrome) monopolar or bipolar head; **correct sizing is critical** — oversizing is a well-documented cause of capitellar erosion, stiffness, and pain (overstuffing the radiocapitellar and PRUJ joints), so size to the resected native head fragments/trial down rather than up when uncertain. Meta-analyses of prospective trials in Mason III fractures (2024) show RHA and ORIF achieve comparable functional/clinical outcomes overall, with ORIF trending toward more reoperations/hardware removal and worse rotation in some series, while RHA trends toward more reliable early stability in comminuted or instability-associated patterns — supporting the practical rule: **ORIF if ≤3 reconstructable fragments and no gross instability; RHA if comminuted or if the elbow/forearm requires the radial head as a stabilizing strut** (terrible triad, Essex-Lopresti, Monteggia-variant).
- Radial head excision without replacement: only for isolated Mason II/III with a confirmed stable elbow and forearm (no LCL, coronoid, or IOM injury) — increasingly rare as a definitive choice given diagnostic under-recognition of instability.

**Olecranon fracture:**
- **Tension-band wiring (TBW):** two parallel 1.6mm K-wires + 18-gauge figure-eight wire (or a cannulated screw + tension-band wire), for simple transverse fractures at the trochlear notch level in low-demand patients; converts extensor pull into compression across the fracture. Highest reported hardware-irritation/removal rates (literature reports a wide range, roughly 9–80% depending on technique/series) — counsel patients that a second surgery for wire removal is common.
- **Plate fixation:** pre-contoured locking olecranon (proximal ulna) plate for comminuted, oblique, or distally-extending fracture lines, and for osteoporotic bone — provides angularly stable fixation independent of an intact anterior cortex, allowing more reliable early motion; current comparative literature and biomechanical studies continue to favor plating for comminuted patterns, with intramedullary screw (± suture-tension band augmentation, "hybrid" constructs) as a lower-profile alternative for simple transverse fractures that reduces hardware prominence while preserving fixation strength.
- **Transolecranon fracture-dislocation (Monteggia/olecranon variant):** reduce and fix the olecranon anatomically first to restore the trochlear notch and thereby reduce the ulnohumeral joint; address any associated coronoid or radial head injury as in terrible triad.

**Terrible triad injury — fixation sequence (the must-cover technical core):**
1. **Examine under anesthesia and reduce the elbow first**, confirm concentric reduction on fluoroscopy/true lateral.
2. **Coronoid** — address before the radial head when accessible: for anterior-approach exposures or fragments visible through the radial head defect, fix with suture lasso through drill holes (capturing anterior capsule) for small/comminuted tip fragments, or screws for larger O'Driscoll type 1 subtype-2/type 3 fragments; anteromedial facet fractures (O'Driscoll type 2) need a separate medial buttress approach.
3. **Radial head** — ORIF if reconstructable (≤3 fragments), RHA if comminuted; the radial head must be addressed (fixed or replaced, essentially never excised alone) because it is a key lateral/valgus and longitudinal stabilizer in this unstable pattern.
4. **LCL/LUCL repair** — through the lateral (Kocher or extensor-digitorum-splitting) approach, suture anchor or transosseous tunnel repair back to the isometric point on the lateral epicondyle (near the center of the capitellum's arc of rotation); this step is essential in virtually all terrible triads since the LUCL is the injury's primary soft-tissue lesion.
5. **Intraoperative stability test** — take the elbow through a full flexion-extension arc under fluoroscopy/direct vision after lateral-sided repair; if concentric and stable through 30°–130°, close.
6. **If still unstable** (subluxates or requires >30–45° flexion to stay reduced): repair the MCL (medial approach), or apply a **hinged external fixator** to maintain concentric reduction while allowing protected early motion, or both.
7. Post-fixation: hinged brace, begin protected active motion within the first 1–2 weeks per rehabilitation protocol below — motion, not prolonged immobilization, is what prevents the dreaded stiffness that historically gave this injury its name.

**Monteggia/Galeazzi:**
- **Monteggia:** anatomic ulna plating (compression or bridge plating depending on comminution) restores length/rotation and typically reduces the radial head; confirm concentric radiocapitellar reduction on true lateral fluoroscopy before closing — if not reduced, check for interposed annular ligament/capsule, malreduced ulna, or an unaddressed radial head/neck fracture or coronoid fracture (Bado II/"Monteggia variant") requiring separate fixation per terrible-triad principles.
- **Galeazzi:** anatomic radius plating (volar approach, compression plate) restores length and typically reduces the DRUJ; stress-test the DRUJ under fluoroscopy in full pronation/supination after fixation — if unstable, immobilize forearm in supination in a long-arm cast/splint for 4–6 weeks, or place a temporary DRUJ pin (0.062in K-wire, radius to ulna, proximal to the sigmoid notch) for 4–6 weeks if grossly unstable; open TFCC repair reserved for cases with a large ulnar styloid base fracture/fragment blocking reduction or persistent instability after pinning.

**Both-bone forearm fracture (adult):**
- **Dual plating** (separate incisions for radius and ulna, 3.5mm dynamic compression or locking plates) remains the gold-standard, achieving union rates generally >95% with anatomic restoration of the radial bow — critical for full pronosupination.
- **Interlocking IM nailing** (radius and/or ulna) is a selected alternative — smaller incisions, lower infection risk in open/contaminated wounds, useful in segmental/comminuted fractures and in patients where soft-tissue coverage or multiple injuries make extensive dissection unattractive; recent meta-analyses (2024) comparing IMN vs ORIF in adult diaphyseal forearm fractures show broadly comparable union rates with IMN offering shorter operative time/smaller incisions, though it may be technically less able to restore the radial bow precisely and has been associated with a higher malrotation/malunion risk in some series if not meticulously performed — plating remains preferred when precise anatomic restoration of rotation is paramount (young, high-demand patients).
- Restoring the **radial bow** and correct rotational alignment (use the bicipital tuberosity–radial styloid relationship and any preserved cortical landmarks) is the single most important technical determinant of postoperative pronosupination.

**Essex-Lopresti — operative principles:**
- Acute recognition is paramount: treat the radial head (fix if reconstructable, RHA if comminuted — a spacer/RHA is essentially mandatory to maintain the proximal radial buttress, since resection alone guarantees proximal migration).
- Address the **DRUJ**: reduce and provisionally pin the DRUJ (radioulnar K-wire) for 4–6 weeks to protect the healing/reconstructing IOM and TFCC while the radial head implant/fixation matures; repair the TFCC if a large fragment or frank instability is present.
- IOM reconstruction (autograft, e.g., bone-patellar tendon-bone or triceps fascia, or synthetic graft) is reserved for chronic/missed cases with symptomatic longitudinal instability, since acute anatomic reduction and RHA alone can be sufficient if the diagnosis is made early.
- Missed/chronic Essex-Lopresti with established ulnar impaction and radiocapitellar arthrosis is a salvage problem (ulnar shortening osteotomy, radial head revision arthroplasty, or IOM reconstruction) — underscores why acute recognition changes the entire trajectory.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Distal humerus ORIF (stable dual-plate construct):**
- **0–2 weeks:** posterior splint for comfort only if construct is stable; begin gentle active-assisted ROM (AAROM) within days to 2 weeks — early motion is the priority given rigid fixation is specifically designed to permit it.
- **2–6 weeks:** progress AROM, gentle stretching into extension/flexion, avoid resisted loading.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive strengthening once radiographic union trending (typically 8–12 weeks); light functional use.
- **3–6 months:** full strengthening; expect a residual extension deficit of 10–15° in many patients as a realistic outcome even with good technique.
- TEA: lifelong lifting restriction (~2 kg repetitive/5 kg single lift), earlier return to light ADLs (2–4 weeks) given implant stability, avoid impact/repetitive loading permanently.

**Radial head ORIF/RHA:**
- **0–1 week:** sling for comfort, begin AROM within the first few days.
- **1–6 weeks:** progressive AROM including forearm rotation; avoid resisted supination against the healing/implant construct in the first 6 weeks.
- **6–12 weeks:** strengthening once healed/implant stable; return to full activity by 3 months in most patients.

**Olecranon (TBW or plate):**
- **0–1 week:** splint immobilization for comfort/soft-tissue rest.
- **1–2 weeks:** transition to hinged brace, begin gentle AROM (flexion typically well-tolerated early; extension against gravity/resisted triceps activity delayed).
- **6 weeks:** avoid resisted elbow extension (triceps loading against the fixation) until this point to protect the tension-band/plate construct from extensor-mechanism pull-off, then progress resisted strengthening.
- **3 months:** full strengthening, return to contact/manual labor once radiographically healed and strength restored.

**Elbow dislocation (simple, stable) and terrible triad/complex fracture-dislocation:**
- **Simple dislocation:** brief splint (days, rarely beyond 1 week) → hinged brace with progressive extension block release → begin active motion within 3–5 days of injury. Randomized/systematic-review evidence (2023–2024) consistently shows early mobilization produces less extension deficit and faster return of function/return to work than immobilization beyond 1–3 weeks, without increasing redislocation risk in truly "simple" (stable, concentric) dislocations.
- **Terrible triad/post-fixation complex instability:** hinged elbow brace set to block the last 20–30° of extension initially (arc progressively opened over 2–4 weeks as stability is confirmed clinically), begin protected AROM within 1–2 weeks of fixation; this is the single most important stiffness-prevention measure — prolonged immobilization is the dominant modifiable risk factor for the contracture that historically defined this injury's poor reputation. Static progressive or dynamic splinting introduced at 6–8 weeks if a flexion or extension contracture is developing despite therapy.
- Formal hand/elbow physiotherapy: AAROM → AROM progression, edema control, scar mobilization once wounds healed, gentle overpressure stretching from 3–4 weeks once fixation/repair is confirmed stable.
- **Stiffness prevention bundle** (applies across this whole cluster): rigid-enough fixation to permit early motion, HO prophylaxis in high-risk cases, early (days, not weeks) initiation of motion, hinged bracing rather than static casting whenever stability allows, and aggressive early physiotherapy engagement — each element reinforces the others; omitting any one (e.g., a technically excellent fixation followed by 4 weeks of casting "to be safe") is a common preventable cause of poor outcome.
- **Return to work:** light/sedentary duty at 2–4 weeks (simple dislocation, ORIF with stable fixation); manual labor/heavy lifting typically 3–4 months, contingent on radiographic union and functional strength testing.
- **Return to sport:** non-contact sport once painless functional arc (30–130° flexion, adequate rotation) and satisfactory strength achieved, typically 3–4 months; contact/collision sport or activities risking a fall onto the elbow deferred to ~4–6 months and individualized to fixation stability and residual laxity on exam.

**Both-bone forearm fracture / Monteggia / Galeazzi (plate fixation):**
- **0–2 weeks:** splint/sling for comfort; begin gentle elbow and wrist AROM early (adjacent joints), guarded forearm rotation.
- **2–6 weeks:** progressive AROM of forearm rotation and adjacent joints; avoid axial loading (no weight-bearing through the arm) until union is progressing.
- **6–12 weeks:** progressive strengthening once radiographic union (typically 8–12 weeks for diaphyseal forearm fractures with rigid plate fixation); light functional use.
- **DRUJ-pinned Galeazzi/Essex-Lopresti:** pin removal at 4–6 weeks in clinic, then begin forearm rotation AROM progressively.
- **Return to work/sport:** light duty ~6 weeks, full manual labor/contact sport 3–4 months contingent on union and rotational strength.

## Complications

- **Post-traumatic elbow stiffness** — the dominant long-term complication across nearly every diagnosis in this cluster; strongly correlated with duration of immobilization, degree of initial soft-tissue injury, and heterotopic ossification. Prevention (early motion, adequate fixation rigidity, HO prophylaxis in high-risk patients) is far more effective than treatment; established stiffness may eventually require arthroscopic or open capsular release/contracture release if refractory to therapy beyond 6–12 months.
- **Heterotopic ossification** — most common after high-energy elbow fracture-dislocations (terrible triad, distal humerus with associated dislocation), especially with delayed fixation, concomitant head injury, or burns; graded by Hastings-Graham; prophylaxis as above, excision reserved for functionally limiting, radiographically mature HO (typically ≥6–12 months post-injury).
- **Post-traumatic osteoarthritis** — long-term sequela of any intra-articular fracture (distal humerus, radial head, coronoid); more common with residual articular incongruity.
- **Nonunion** — olecranon (particularly after inadequate fixation of comminuted/oblique patterns with TBW), distal humerus (especially at the supracondylar column junction or with inadequate fixation rigidity/motion too soon), forearm diaphysis (uncommon with modern plating, <5% in most series, higher with nailing in some comparative series if rotational stability is inadequate).
- **Radioulnar synostosis** — heterotopic bone bridging the radius and ulna, causing loss of rotation; risk factors include associated coronoid + olecranon fracture, high-energy/comminuted forearm fractures, delayed fixation, and single-incision approaches to both-bone fractures (hence separate-incision dual plating is preferred); prophylactic considerations mirror HO prophylaxis in high-risk cases.
- **Chronic elbow instability / recurrent subluxation** — under-recognized or inadequately repaired LCL/coronoid injury in terrible triad; presents as recurrent apprehension, "clicking," or frank redislocation — often requires revision ligament reconstruction ± coronoid reconstruction (autograft/allograft).
- **DRUJ instability/chronic pain** — missed Galeazzi-associated DRUJ injury or Essex-Lopresti; may progress to ulnocarpal impaction, TFCC degeneration, and radiocapitellar arthrosis requiring salvage procedures (ulnar shortening, radial head revision, Sauvé-Kapandji, or DRUJ arthroplasty in severe chronic cases).
- **PIN/ulnar nerve injury** — iatrogenic (Monteggia ORIF exposure, distal humerus medial plating without transposition) or injury-related (usually neurapraxic with radial head/neck fracture-dislocation, observe 8–12 weeks with serial exam/EMG before considering exploration).
- **Radial head arthroplasty-specific:** overstuffing (oversized implant) causing capitellar erosion, stiffness, and pain — reinforces the sizing pearl above; implant loosening/wear over long-term follow-up in a minority of patients.
- **Infection** — higher risk with open fractures (common with olecranon given subcutaneous location) and prolonged/complex surgical exposures; manage per standard open-fracture principles (early debridement, antibiotics, staged fixation if grossly contaminated).
- **Compartment syndrome (acute/missed)** — most feared in high-energy both-bone forearm and floating-elbow patterns; if missed, evolves to Volkmann's ischemic contracture — a devastating, largely preventable complication that underscores the importance of serial exam (or compartment pressure monitoring in the obtunded/intubated patient) in the first 24–48 hours.
- **Malunion/malrotation of forearm fractures** — even modest loss of the radial bow or rotational malalignment measurably reduces pronosupination arc; corrective osteotomy is the salvage option for symptomatic malunion once healed, timed after full radiographic consolidation (typically not before 6–12 months to allow remodeling assessment in younger patients, though adults rarely remodel meaningfully and earlier correction is often reasonable once union is solid).
- **Avascular necrosis of the trochlea/capitellum** — rare but reported after high-energy distal humerus fracture-dislocation with extensive soft-tissue stripping; minimizing periosteal stripping during ORIF reduces risk.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[Radial head arthroplasty vs. ORIF in Mason 3 radial head fractures: meta-analysis of prospective trials (2024, JSES International)](https://jsesinternational.org/article/S2666-6383(24)00372-4/fulltext)** — pooled prospective-trial data show comparable overall clinical/functional outcomes between RHA and ORIF for Mason III fractures, reinforcing that fragment reconstructability (not a blanket "always replace") should drive the choice.
- **[Isolated Mason III radial head fractures: RHA or ORIF — outcomes with 5–14 years follow-up (2025, International Orthopaedics)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00264-025-06445-z)** — long-term (5–14yr) data show no significant difference in most functional scores between RHA and ORIF, but better pronation with RHA and worse functional outcomes when ORIF later required secondary resection/removal — supports low threshold to choose RHA when reconstruction is marginal.
- **[Plate or Arthroplasty for complex Mason III radial head fractures? Mid-to-long-term results from a blinded-assessor study (2024, Injury)](https://www.injuryjournal.com/article/S0020-1383(24)00710-1/fulltext)** — blinded outcome assessment (reducing bias present in earlier retrospective comparisons) continues to support individualized fragment-based decision-making between plate ORIF and arthroplasty for complex Mason III injuries.
- **[Early Mobilization of Simple Elbow Dislocations (2023, JBJS)](https://journals.lww.com/jbjsjournal/fulltext/2023/10040/early_mobilization_of_simple_elbow_dislocations_.12.aspx)** and the accompanying **[Treatment strategies for simple elbow dislocation — systematic review (PMC, 2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874000/)** — confirm early active mobilization (days, not weeks) after simple elbow dislocation produces less extension deficit and faster return of function/work than immobilization ≥3 weeks, without increased instability — cements early motion as standard of care rather than a case-by-case option.
- **[Early Joint Use Following Elbow Dislocation Limits ROM Loss and Tissue Pathology in Posttraumatic Joint Contracture (JBJS, basic-science/translational, 2023–2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10578297/)** — mechanistic data supporting that active motion beginning within days of dislocation reduces capsular/tissue pathology underlying contracture, giving a biological rationale for the clinical early-mobilization findings above.
- **[Conservative and surgical management of simple elbow dislocations — SMDA-SEC consensus and guidelines (Orthopedic Reviews)](https://orthopedicreviews.openmedicalpublishing.org/article/133988-conservative-and-surgical-management-of-simple-elbow-dislocations-smda-sec-consensus-and-guidelines)** — recent multidisciplinary consensus formalizing early-mobilization-first pathways and criteria for when surgical stabilization is warranted in "simple" dislocations.
- **[Terrible Triad Injuries around the Elbow: It is Still a Puzzle? Prospective study (2024, PMC/OrthoScience)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11458208/)** — prospective outcome data confirming that systematic, sequential fixation (coronoid → radial head → LCL) yields good 1-year functional results (MEPS improving from a mean of ~41 to ~90; DASH improving from ~66 to ~17), supporting the standard sequence taught above.
- **[Outcomes of coronoid-first repair through an anterior approach in terrible triad injury — prospective study, minimum 2-year follow-up (2024, PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11582235/)** — demonstrates a viable alternative sequencing (anterior coronoid-first) to the classic lateral-approach sequence, with good mid-term results, useful when coronoid fragment morphology favors direct anterior exposure.
- **[Total elbow arthroplasty for distal humeral fractures in the elderly: functional outcomes and implant survival (2026, Frontiers in Surgery)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/surgery/articles/10.3389/fsurg.2026.1855944/full)** — contemporary series reporting good functional outcomes with high implant survival, supporting primary TEA as a durable option in appropriately selected elderly patients with unreconstructable distal humerus fractures.
- **[Distal humeral hemiarthroplasty compared to total elbow replacement for distal humeral fractures: registry analysis of 906 procedures (2023, Journal of Shoulder and Elbow Surgery)](https://www.jshoulderelbow.org/article/S1058-2746(23)00647-X/fulltext)** — large registry data comparing hemiarthroplasty and TEA for acute distal humerus fractures, informing patient selection between the two arthroplasty options, particularly relevant for younger patients wishing to avoid TEA's lifting restrictions.
- **[Total Elbow Arthroplasty Versus ORIF for Distal Humerus Fractures: Propensity-Matched 30-Day Complications (2023–2024, PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11159534/)** — propensity-matched analysis showing broadly similar short-term complication profiles between TEA and ORIF once patient factors are controlled, with TEA associated with higher transfusion risk — useful for informed consent discussions.
- **[Intramedullary nail fixation versus ORIF for adult diaphyseal forearm fractures — systematic review and meta-analysis (2024, Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11533272/)** — contemporary pooled comparison confirms plating remains the reference standard for anatomic radial-bow restoration, while IMN offers a viable, less-invasive alternative with broadly comparable union rates in appropriately selected patterns.
- **[The Essex-Lopresti Injury (2024, Journal of Orthopaedic Trauma)](https://journals.lww.com/jorthotrauma/fulltext/2024/09001/the_essex_lopresti_injury.4.aspx)** — updated review reinforcing that early recognition (high index of suspicion with any radial head fracture plus wrist pain) and acute treatment (radial head reconstruction/arthroplasty plus DRUJ stabilization) produce substantially better outcomes than delayed/missed diagnosis managed as a chronic salvage problem.
- **[Open fractures of the upper limb — do the BOAST guidelines need an update? (2023, Injury)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37080881/)** — scoping review questioning whether current BOAST open-fracture standards (relevant to the frequently-open olecranon and proximal ulna fracture) remain optimally calibrated for upper-limb-specific injury patterns, flagging an area to watch for future guideline revision.
