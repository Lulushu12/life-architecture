## Quick Reference

**Hemodynamic algorithm (high-energy unstable pelvis) — sequence, not menu:**

1. **Binder/sheet** at greater trochanters (not iliac crests) immediately on suspicion, before imaging.
2. **Primary survey** — if hemodynamically unstable despite binder + 1–2 L crystalloid → activate MTP, pelvic AP film, FAST.
3. **FAST positive (intraperitoneal blood)** → laparotomy first; address pelvis intra-/post-op.
4. **FAST negative, pelvis is the source, unstable** → **preperitoneal pelvic packing (PPP)** ± **REBOA Zone III** as bridge, concurrent with external fixation/C-clamp for mechanical stability, in the OR — not the angio suite, for the exsanguinating patient.
5. **Hemodynamically stable (or responds transiently) and CT shows contrast blush** → **angioembolization (AE)**, non-selective or selective, in the interventional suite.
6. Definitive posterior/anterior fixation staged after resuscitation (damage-control orthopaedics), typically 2–10 days once physiology normalizes (lactate clearing, base deficit corrected, not coagulopathic).

**Key numbers**

| Parameter | Threshold / value |
|---|---|
| Pelvic volume gain enabling tamponade loss | Ring disruption can double pelvic volume; binder/ex-fix restores tamponade |
| Mortality, hemodynamically unstable pelvic fracture | 20–40% historically; contemporary protocolized care ~15–20% |
| Transfusion trigger for PPP/REBOA escalation | Ongoing instability after 2 U PRBC / persistent base deficit >-6 despite binder |
| REBOA Zone III inflation time | Keep <60 min if possible; ischemia risk rises sharply beyond this |
| Acetabular "gull sign" / roof arc angles | Medial, anterior, posterior roof arc <45° → weight-bearing dome involved, favors surgery |
| Acceptable acetabular residual displacement (Matta) | ≤1 mm anatomic; 1–3 mm imperfect (acceptable); >3 mm poor (higher PTOA risk) |
| Posterior wall fracture "unstable" threshold | >40–50% wall involvement or arc <25° on Judet views, or hip subluxation/dislocation |
| Sacral dysmorphism prevalence | ~30–40% of population — screen before transiliac-transsacral screw placement |
| Time to weight-bear, isolated stable pubic rami fragility fracture | Immediate WBAT with analgesia; goal ambulation within 24–48h |
| FFP Type I → conservative; Type III/IV → operative | See Classification |

**Red flags (any diagnosis in this cluster)**

- Hemodynamic instability with pelvic ring disruption → treat as pelvic hemorrhage until proven otherwise.
- **Open pelvic fracture** (perineal, vaginal, rectal wound, degloving) — mortality up to 25–50%; mandates diverting colostomy consideration, aggressive debridement, external fixation.
- **Urethral/bladder injury** — blood at meatus, high-riding prostate, perineal ecchymosis → retrograde urethrogram before Foley.
- **Lumbosacral plexus/nerve root injury** with displaced sacral (especially Denis III) or vertically unstable posterior ring fractures.
- Isolated "low-energy" pubic rami fracture in the elderly with disproportionate pain, inability to mobilize, or new neurologic deficit → CT to exclude occult posterior ring (FFP II–IV) or vascular injury (corona mortis, obturator vessels).
- Acetabular fracture with irreducible hip dislocation or marginal impaction — urgent reduction to protect femoral head blood supply and articular cartilage.

**Treatment ladder (compact)**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pelvic ring injury (Tile A / stable LC I) | Protected weight-bearing, analgesia, early mobilization | Symptomatic bracing | Rarely; ex-fix/percutaneous screws if progressive displacement |
| Pelvic ring injury (Tile B/C, high-energy) | Binder + resuscitation (ED) | PPP/REBOA/AE per algorithm | Percutaneous iliosacral/transiliac-transsacral screws ± anterior plate/ex-fix; symphyseal plating for APC |
| Acetabular fracture, young/active | Traction/analgesia if nondisplaced & congruent | — | ORIF via Kocher-Langenbeck, ilioinguinal, Stoppa, or combined; goal anatomic reduction |
| Acetabular fracture, elderly (>60–65y) | ORIF if reconstructible with good bone stock and congruent dome | — | ORIF + acute THA ("fix-and-replace") or acute THA with cup reconstruction for comminuted/impacted dome, associated both-column, marked osteoporosis |
| Sacral fracture, traumatic (young, displaced) | — | — | Percutaneous iliosacral or transiliac-transsacral screws; lumbopelvic fixation for vertically unstable/U-type or spinopelvic dissociation |
| Sacral fracture, fragility (FFP II) | Analgesia, mobilization trial | Percutaneous sacroplasty (cement augmentation) if pain refractory | Percutaneous SI/TITS screw (± cement augmentation) if trial fails or progressive displacement |
| Sacral fracture, fragility (FFP III/IV) | — | — | Percutaneous posterior fixation (TITS screw, often cement-augmented) ± anterior fixation |
| Pubic rami fracture (fragility, isolated FFP I) | Analgesia, early WBAT, falls/osteoporosis workup | Bone-forming agent per fracture-liaison pathway | Rarely; consider ramus screw if refractory pain or displacement/instability on follow-up imaging |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Pelvic ring biomechanics

The **pelvic ring** is a closed osseous-ligamentous ring formed by two innominate bones (ilium, ischium, pubis fused at the triradiate cartilage) and the sacrum, linked anteriorly at the **pubic symphysis** and posteriorly at the paired **sacroiliac (SI) joints**. Stability is predominantly **posterior**: the posterior SI ligament complex (posterior SI, interosseous SI, sacrotuberous, sacrospinous ligaments) carries the majority of load transfer from spine to lower limbs and is the principal determinant of vertical and rotational stability — disruption here, not the anterior ring, defines a truly "unstable" pelvis. The pelvic floor musculature and iliolumbar ligaments contribute secondary stability. Load transmission runs from the lumbar spine through the sacral ala, across the SI joints, into the posterior iliac columns and acetabula, and out through the femora during stance — the "sacrosciatic buttress" concept explains why posterior ring integrity, not anterior ring integrity, dictates whether a patient can bear weight and mobilize.

### Vascular anatomy driving hemorrhage risk

Pelvic ring disruption bleeds from three sources — cancellous bone surfaces (always), the **presacral venous plexus** (major volume source, often unresponsive to embolization because it is a low-pressure venous network, not an arterial target), and arterial branches of the internal iliac (superior gluteal, obturator, internal pudendal, lateral sacral) which are the target of angioembolization. This anatomic distinction is the entire rationale for the sequenced algorithm in Quick Reference: mechanical stabilization (binder, ex-fix, packing) controls venous/cancellous bleeding by restoring tamponade and reducing pelvic volume, while angioembolization specifically targets arterial sources identified on CTA blush. The **corona mortis** (aberrant obturator-to-external iliac vascular anastomosis, present in 20–30% of patients) crosses the superior pubic ramus roughly 5–9 cm from the symphysis and is at risk during anterior ramus/Stoppa exposure and with displaced anterior ring fractures — it is a recognized, if rare, source of life-threatening hemorrhage even from an isolated nondisplaced pubic ramus fracture in the elderly. Disruption of the ring increases retroperitoneal/pelvic volume — an unstable "open book" pattern can more than double potential space — which impairs the natural tamponade effect and allows ongoing venous/cancellous hemorrhage to proceed largely unchecked; this is the rationale for immediate circumferential compression (binder) before any further workup in a suspected unstable pelvis.

### Acetabulum

An inverted-Y (triradiate) articular structure comprising an **anterior column** (iliopubic, running from the iliac crest to the pubic symphysis, and including the anterior wall) and a **posterior column** (ilioischial, running from the greater sciatic notch to the ischial tuberosity, and including the posterior wall and the weight-bearing dome). The weight-bearing **dome** is the confluence of both columns superiorly, and its integrity (assessed via roof-arc angles) is the single most important determinant of whether a fracture pattern tolerates nonoperative treatment. The femoral head derives blood supply chiefly from the **medial femoral circumflex artery** via the posterosuperior retinacular vessels running along the femoral neck beneath the piriformis and obturator internus tendons — posterior wall fracture-dislocations and posterior surgical approaches place this supply at direct risk, underlying both the urgency of reducing a dislocated hip (ideally <6 hours) and meticulous care during posterior dissection (preserve the piriformis–obturator internus sling, avoid excessive retraction on the short external rotators, avoid stripping the quadratus femoris insertion which carries the terminal retinacular branches).

### Sacrum and neural elements

The sacrum functions as the posterior keystone of the pelvic ring and the terminal segment of the spinal column, transmitting axial load through the sacral ala into the SI joints. The **lumbosacral plexus** (L4–S4 nerve roots, contributing to the sciatic and superior/inferior gluteal nerves) traverses the sacral foramina and ala — fracture location relative to the foramina (Denis zones, below) directly predicts neurologic injury risk, rising from ~6% in Zone I to ~57% in Zone III. **Sacral dysmorphism** — a common developmental variant featuring an oblique or narrow upper sacral segment, a residual S1 ala slope, mammillary body prominences, and an acute S1/S2 disc angulation visible on lateral sacral imaging — occurs in roughly a third of the general population and materially constrains safe percutaneous S1 screw trajectories (a "normal" S1 corridor may not exist). Always assess pelvic inlet/outlet radiographs and the lateral sacral view (or thin-cut CT with sagittal reconstructions) before planning any iliosacral or transiliac-transsacral screw, since an S2 corridor or an alternate trajectory may be required.

### Fragility fracture pathophysiology

In osteoporotic bone, low-energy mechanisms (a fall from standing height) that would cause an isolated pubic ramus fracture in a young patient frequently also cause an occult, nondisplaced posterior ring injury (sacral ala impaction or incomplete SI joint/sacral fracture) that is invisible on plain radiographs — CT or MRI upstages roughly 15–40% of apparently isolated "pubic rami fractures" to a combined anterior-posterior FFP pattern. This distinction is clinically decisive: posterior involvement predicts failure of conservative treatment, prolonged immobility, and materially higher 1-year mortality, which is why any elderly patient with disproportionate pain or failure to mobilize after an apparently "simple" rami fracture warrants cross-sectional imaging of the posterior ring rather than reassurance from a normal pelvic radiograph alone.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**Primary survey (high-energy)**: pelvic ring injury is assessed as part of ATLS circulation — a single manual "spring test" of the pelvis is discouraged (may dislodge clot) once instability is suspected clinically (leg-length discrepancy, rotational deformity, perineal/scrotal/labial hematoma, blood at urethral meatus, high-riding prostate) or on the trauma pelvic AP film. Apply a **binder** empirically in any suspected unstable pattern before further workup. Log-roll to inspect perineum, rectum (blood, high-riding prostate), and vagina for open fracture; a **rectal/vaginal exam is mandatory** to exclude open injury, which changes management (contamination control, diverting colostomy consideration).

**Imaging**:
- **AP pelvis** (trauma series) — screens for gross diastasis, vertical displacement, sacral fracture lucency.
- **Inlet view** — best for anterior-posterior (sagittal plane) displacement and sacral ala fractures.
- **Outlet view** — best for cephalad-caudad (vertical) displacement and foraminal integrity.
- **CT pelvis with fine cuts** — mandatory for all but the most trivial stable injuries; defines posterior ring pattern (SI joint diastasis vs. crescent fracture vs. sacral fracture zone), dysmorphism, comminution, and is essential for acetabular fracture classification.
- **Judet views (45° oblique, obturator and iliac oblique)** for acetabular fractures — obturator oblique profiles the anterior column and posterior wall; iliac oblique profiles the posterior column and anterior wall/iliac wing. Roof arc measurements on AP and both obliques (medial, anterior, posterior) quantify dome involvement.
- **3D CT reconstruction** for all displaced acetabular fractures and complex pelvic ring injuries — critical for surgical planning.
- **MRI** for suspected occult sacral/pelvic fragility fracture with negative or equivocal CT and disproportionate pain (STIR sequence sensitive for marrow edema); also for suspected sacral insufficiency fracture and to characterize neural element involvement.
- **CT angiography** when an arterial blush or vascular injury is suspected and the patient is stable enough to acquire it.

**FAST** to triage intraperitoneal hemorrhage source before committing to pelvic-specific interventions (a positive FAST in an unstable patient generally directs to laparotomy first). Serial **hemoglobin, lactate, base deficit** trend resuscitation adequacy and threshold for escalation along the hemorrhage-control algorithm.

### Diagnosis-specific evaluation points

- **Pelvic ring injury**: examine for leg-length discrepancy and rotational deformity with the patient supine before any manual stress testing; document neurovascular status of both lower limbs (L5/S1 dermatomes, dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial pulses) given proximity of the lumbosacral plexus and iliac vessels to displaced posterior ring fragments; a single gentle manual stability assessment is acceptable only once, early, before a binder is applied — repeated stress testing risks dislodging clot and precipitating hemorrhage.
- **Acetabular fracture**: assess hip range of motion and stability only after excluding an unreduced dislocation (reduce urgently under sedation/GA if present); document sciatic nerve function (ankle dorsiflexion/plantarflexion, sensation over the dorsum of the foot) before and after any reduction maneuver; obtain post-reduction CT to assess for intra-articular fragments and congruency.
- **Sacral fracture**: perform a full lower-extremity motor/sensory/reflex exam plus perianal sensation and rectal tone, since Denis Zone II/III injuries carry substantial cauda-equina-pattern risk; ask specifically about new bowel/bladder/sexual dysfunction, which is frequently under-reported by patients focused on pain.
- **Pubic rami fracture (fragility)**: assess ability to stand and take a few steps (or attempt a straight-leg raise) at presentation — inability to mobilize despite an apparently isolated anterior-ring injury on plain film is the single strongest clinical trigger for CT to exclude an occult FFP II–IV posterior component; screen for anticoagulant use and check hemoglobin trend given rare but reported vascular complications from the corona mortis/obturator vessels.

## Classification

### Young-Burgess (mechanism-based, pelvic ring)

| Type | Subtype | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lateral Compression (LC)** | LC-I | Sacral ala compression (buckle) fracture, ipsilateral rami fractures | Usually stable; nonoperative unless progressive |
| | LC-II | Crescent (iliac wing) fracture or SI joint disruption + rami fractures | Often stable to partially unstable; posterior fixation if displaced crescent |
| | LC-III | LC-I/II + contralateral open-book (APC) component ("windswept pelvis") | Unstable; highest association with severe associated injury, higher mortality |
| **Anteroposterior Compression (APC)** | APC-I | Symphyseal widening <2.5 cm, minimal posterior ligament injury | Stable; nonoperative or symphyseal plate if symptomatic |
| | APC-II | Symphyseal widening >2.5 cm, anterior SI ligament disruption, posterior SI ligaments intact | Rotationally unstable, vertically stable; symphyseal plate ± anterior SI fixation |
| | APC-III | Complete SI joint disruption (anterior + posterior ligaments) | Rotationally AND vertically unstable; highest hemorrhage risk (torn posterior venous plexus/arteries); posterior + anterior fixation |
| **Vertical Shear (VS)** | — | Vertical displacement of hemipelvis through SI joint, sacrum, or iliac wing | Vertically and rotationally unstable; posterior fixation (iliosacral/TITS screws or plating) mandatory |
| **Combined Mechanism (CM)** | — | Combination of above patterns (e.g., LC + VS) | Individualized; treat per most unstable component |

Young-Burgess correlates strongly with **transfusion requirement and mortality** (APC-III and VS patterns carry the highest hemorrhage risk) and is the most clinically useful classification for the acute hemodynamic algorithm.

### Tile/AO (stability-based, pelvic ring)

| Type | Subtype | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** — Stable | A1 | Avulsion fracture (ASIS, AIIS, ischial tuberosity), ring intact | Symptomatic treatment, protected weight-bearing |
| | A2 | Stable, minimally displaced ring fracture (isolated rami, iliac wing) | Nonoperative |
| | A3 | Transverse sacral/coccygeal fracture without ring instability | Nonoperative |
| **B** — Rotationally unstable, vertically stable | B1 | Open-book (external rotation), unilateral | Anterior ± posterior fixation depending on posterior ligament integrity |
| | B2 | Lateral compression, ipsilateral | Usually nonoperative unless displaced/crescent |
| | B3 | Bilateral type B | Bilateral anterior fixation ± posterior stabilization |
| **C** — Rotationally and vertically unstable | C1 | Unilateral complete posterior ring disruption | Posterior fixation (screws or plate) + anterior fixation |
| | C2 | Bilateral, one side C-type, other side B-type | Bilateral posterior fixation strategy tailored to each side |
| | C3 | Bilateral complete disruption | Most unstable pattern; often requires combined posterior + anterior fixation, staged |

Tile/AO is the preferred classification for **surgical decision-making** (it maps directly onto whether posterior fixation is required), while Young-Burgess better predicts **hemorrhage magnitude**. Use both in combination in practice.

### Judet-Letournel (acetabular fracture)

**Elementary (simple) patterns:**

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Posterior wall | Fracture of the posterior articular rim, often with hip dislocation | Most common pattern; Kocher-Langenbeck approach; assess for marginal impaction and femoral head injury |
| Posterior column | Oblique fracture from greater sciatic notch to ischial tuberosity/obturator ring | Kocher-Langenbeck approach |
| Anterior wall | Isolated anterior rim fracture (uncommon) | Ilioinguinal or Stoppa approach |
| Anterior column | Fracture from iliac crest/wing through anterior column to pubic ramus, variable height | Ilioinguinal, Stoppa, or combined depending on height of fracture |
| Transverse | Fracture separating superior (iliac) from inferior (ischiopubic) fragment, both columns, through the acetabulum | Approach depends on displacement pattern (anterior vs posterior predominant); may need combined |

**Associated (complex) patterns:**

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| T-shaped | Transverse component + vertical split separating anterior and posterior columns | Often requires combined anterior + posterior approach |
| Posterior column + posterior wall | Posterior column fracture with separate posterior wall fragment | Kocher-Langenbeck, address both components |
| Transverse + posterior wall | Most common associated pattern | Kocher-Langenbeck usually sufficient |
| Anterior column/wall + posterior hemitransverse | Anterior injury with a posterior transverse component | Ilioinguinal/Stoppa ± posterior fixation |
| Both-column ("floating acetabulum") | All articular fragments detached from intact ilium; entire articular surface separated from axial skeleton | Most complex; combined anterior + posterior approach or single extensile approach; high rate of secondary arthroplasty in elderly |

**Approach selection logic**: Kocher-Langenbeck for predominantly posterior patterns (posterior wall, posterior column, transverse+posterior wall); ilioinguinal or anterior intrapelvic (Stoppa) ± lateral window for predominantly anterior patterns (anterior column/wall, most both-column); combined or extensile (extended iliofemoral) approaches reserved for complex, delayed (>3 weeks), or both-column patterns not amenable to single-window fixation. Anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) has largely supplanted ilioinguinal in many centers for anterior column/both-column patterns due to direct quadrilateral surface visualization and lower risk of femoral nerve/vessel injury.

### Denis Classification (sacral fracture, zone-based)

| Zone | Description | Neurologic injury rate | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zone I** | Fracture lateral to the neural foramina (through the sacral ala) | ~6% (typically L5 root, from ala fracture/hematoma) | Usually amenable to percutaneous iliosacral/TITS screw; monitor for L5 root symptoms |
| **Zone II** | Fracture through the neural foramina, not entering the central canal | ~28% (L5–S1 roots, unilateral) | Screw fixation feasible; higher radiculopathy risk; consider decompression if progressive deficit |
| **Zone III** | Fracture extending into the central sacral canal | ~57% (bilateral, may include bowel/bladder/sexual dysfunction — cauda equina pattern) | Often requires decompression (laminectomy) + stabilization (lumbopelvic fixation) beyond simple percutaneous screws, especially with displacement or neuro deficit |

Denis zone correlates directly with neurologic risk and should be documented before any percutaneous posterior fixation is planned; Zone III with deficit and displacement is a relative indication for **open reduction with foraminal/canal decompression and segmental (lumbopelvic) fixation** rather than percutaneous screws alone.

### FFP / Rommens Classification (Fragility Fractures of the Pelvis)

| Type | Subtype | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Ia / Ib | Anterior ring only: unilateral (Ia) or bilateral (Ib) pubic rami fracture(s), no posterior involvement | Conservative: analgesia, early mobilization, osteoporosis workup |
| **II** | IIa | Nondisplaced posterior compression fracture of the sacral ala (impaction) + anterior ring fracture | Trial conservative treatment first; percutaneous fixation if pain refractory or mobilization fails |
| | IIb | Minimally displaced (<10 mm) unilateral sacral fracture (incomplete) + anterior ring | As IIa; lower threshold for fixation if instability on standing films |
| | IIc | Bilateral nondisplaced posterior lesions + anterior ring | As IIa/b, closer monitoring for progression |
| **III** | IIIa/b/c | Displaced unilateral posterior lesion (iliac fracture, SI joint dislocation, or sacral fracture) + anterior ring | Surgical: percutaneous posterior fixation (SI screw/TITS, often cement-augmented) |
| **IV** | IVa | Bilateral displaced posterior lesions + anterior ring | Surgical: bilateral percutaneous posterior fixation |
| | IVb | Spinopelvic dissociation (bilateral vertical sacral fractures + transverse component — "H" or "U" pattern) | Surgical: TITS screw(s) or lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation; consider decompression if neurologic deficit |

**Practical caveat (2024–2025 literature, see Evidence section)**: recent series challenge the strict Rommens algorithm — some FFP II patients do well with fixation up front (avoiding a failed conservative trial in a frail patient who cannot tolerate re-hospitalization), while select FFP III/IV patients without significant displacement or intractable pain have been managed successfully nonoperatively. Individualize based on frailty, pain trajectory, mobilization capacity, and displacement on serial imaging rather than applying the type strictly.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Stable pelvic ring injuries (Tile A, LC-I without progression, APC-I)**: protected weight-bearing as tolerated with analgesia, early mobilization with physiotherapy, serial standing/flamingo radiographs at 1–2 weeks to confirm no occult instability.
- **FFP Type I and select Type II**: analgesia (multimodal, avoid long-term opioids in the elderly — regional blocks such as pericapsular or fascia iliaca useful adjuncts), early supervised mobilization with walker/frame, osteoporosis secondary-prevention pathway initiation (DXA, vitamin D/calcium, anti-resorptive or anabolic agent — see Recent Evidence), thromboprophylaxis, and inpatient geriatric co-management (orthogeriatric model) given the substantial 1-year mortality associated with these "minor" injuries.
- **Nondisplaced/minimally displaced, congruent acetabular fractures** (roof arcs >45° all planes, no marginal impaction, hip congruent and stable on exam/dynamic fluoroscopy under anesthesia if borderline): protected touch-down weight-bearing 6–8 weeks with serial radiographs to confirm no secondary displacement; this includes many low-demand elderly patients with anterior column or both-column patterns whose fracture geometry maintains secondary congruence ("roof-intact" both-column pattern).
- **Sacral fragility fractures without significant displacement or neurologic deficit**: trial of conservative management per Rommens IIa/IIb — analgesia, mobilization, admission for pain and functional monitoring; escalate to fixation if failing at 3–7 days.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Angioembolization (AE)** — image-guided, non-selective (internal iliac trunk) or selective (named branch) embolization for arterial blush on CTA or ongoing bleeding after mechanical stabilization in a hemodynamically stable-enough patient; performed in interventional radiology, effective for arterial sources but not for venous/cancellous bleeding, which is the dominant source in most pelvic hemorrhage — hence its complementary rather than substitutive role relative to packing.
- **REBOA (Resuscitative Endovascular Balloon Occlusion of the Aorta), Zone III** — temporizing aortic occlusion below the renal arteries via femoral access, used as a bridge in extremis to buy time for definitive source control (packing, ex-fix, OR); minimize inflation time (<60 min target) given distal ischemia risk; requires trained personnel and immediate access to definitive hemorrhage control.
- **Percutaneous cement augmentation (sacroplasty)** — CT- or fluoroscopy-guided cement injection into the sacral ala for FFP II with refractory pain despite analgesia and mobilization attempts, or as an adjunct alongside percutaneous screw fixation ("screw-in-cement" / cement-augmented iliosacral screws) to improve fixation strength in severely osteoporotic bone — increasingly used given high rates of screw loosening/cutout with standard screws alone in this population.
- **Nerve blocks** (fascia iliaca, pericapsular nerve group [PENG]) as opioid-sparing analgesia adjuncts for both pelvic ring and acetabular fracture pain control, particularly valuable in the frail elderly perioperative pathway.
- **Diverting colostomy** — not an "injection" but a mandatory adjunctive procedure consideration in open pelvic fractures with rectal/perineal contamination, performed early to reduce pelvic sepsis.

### Operative

**Pelvic ring — acute hemorrhage control sequence** (see Quick Reference algorithm): binder → resuscitation → (FAST-directed) laparotomy vs. pelvic-specific control → **preperitoneal pelvic packing** (via 6–8 cm lower midline incision, packing lap sponges into the true pelvis along the pelvic brim, typically 3 per side) combined with **external fixation or pelvic C-clamp** for mechanical stability, performed together in the OR for the exsanguinating patient; **angioembolization** reserved for patients stable enough to reach the angio suite (or as staged next step after packing if bleeding persists). REBOA as an extremis bridge. Definitive internal fixation is staged once physiology normalizes.

**Pelvic ring — definitive fixation**:
- **Anterior ring**: symphyseal plating (6–8 hole plate, 2 screws per side minimum) for symphyseal diastasis; percutaneous retrograde or antegrade ramus screws for ramus fractures, especially useful in obese patients or as a minimally invasive alternative to plating.
- **Posterior ring**: percutaneous **iliosacral screws** (S1 ± S2, 7.0–7.3 mm cannulated) for SI joint disruption or sacral fracture amenable to closed/percutaneous reduction; **transiliac-transsacral (TITS) screws** for bilateral posterior instability, U/H-type sacral fractures, or when maximal fixation strength is needed (particularly in osteoporotic bone) — requires confirmed safe corridor (screen for dysmorphism); **lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation** with iliac screws + pedicle screws connected by rods, ± transsacral bar, for spinopelvic dissociation, highly comminuted sacral fractures, or when percutaneous screw purchase is inadequate; open reduction of crescent fractures/SI dislocation via anterior or posterior approach when closed reduction fails.
- **Timing**: definitive fixation typically 2–10 days post-injury after damage-control resuscitation is complete, balancing early stabilization (reduces pain, ICU length of stay, pulmonary complications) against physiologic readiness.

**Acetabular fracture — operative principles**:
- **Indications for ORIF**: displaced fracture with dome/weight-bearing surface involvement (roof arc <45°), articular step-off >2 mm, marginal impaction, incongruent or unstable hip (post-reduction), associated patterns (both-column, T-shaped, transverse+posterior wall) in patients with reasonable bone stock and functional demand.
- **Approach selection**: as detailed under Judet-Letournel above — Kocher-Langenbeck for posterior patterns, ilioinguinal/anterior intrapelvic (Stoppa) for anterior/both-column, combined for complex delayed presentations.
- **Fixation**: interfragmentary lag screws + neutralization/buttress plating along the columns; pelvic reconstruction plates contoured to the pelvic brim and posterior column; quadrilateral surface buttressing (spring plate or dedicated quadrilateral plate) critical in both-column and medial wall comminution to prevent central protrusio.
- **Technical pearls**: obtain intraoperative fluoroscopy (obturator oblique, iliac oblique, and Judet views) to confirm reduction before final fixation; use a ball-spike pusher and Schanz pins for indirect reduction; protect the sciatic nerve throughout Kocher-Langenbeck exposure (hip flexed, knee extended, avoid excessive retraction); watch for the corona mortis during Stoppa/ilioinguinal dissection of the superior pubic ramus; achieve anatomic (≤1 mm) reduction — this is the single strongest predictor of long-term joint survival.
- **Pitfalls**: missed marginal impaction (elevate and bone-graft before column fixation), incomplete reduction of the posterior wall leading to residual instability, iatrogenic sciatic injury, heterotopic ossification risk with extensile/Kocher-Langenbeck approaches (consider indomethacin or single-fraction radiotherapy prophylaxis in high-risk patients — extensive soft tissue stripping, male sex, head injury).

**Fix vs. THA in the elderly acetabulum — decision framework**:
- **ORIF alone** remains preferred when the fracture is reconstructible (limited comminution, preservable dome congruence, adequate bone stock) even in older patients — outcomes depend far more on reduction quality and comminution than age alone; 7-year joint survival after ORIF is reported around 80% when reduction is anatomic, dropping to ~65–70% with imperfect reduction.
- **Acute THA (with or without concurrent fixation — "fix-and-replace")** is favored when: severe articular comminution/impaction precluding a congruent, durable reduction; both-column or transverse+posterior wall patterns with marked osteoporosis and dome destruction; pre-existing ipsilateral hip osteoarthritis; femoral head injury (impaction, osteonecrosis risk); patient age/frailty such that a second surgery for post-traumatic arthritis (which develops in a substantial proportion of elderly ORIF patients) would be poorly tolerated.
- **"Fix-and-replace" technique**: column fixation (typically plating to restore the columns and posterior wall/quadrilateral surface) followed by acute THA using a cementless or, per recent RCT-level evidence, **cemented** revision-type or dual-mobility cup for maximal early stability and reduced dislocation risk in compromised acetabular bone; dual-mobility or constrained liners are favored given elevated dislocation risk from posterior wall/column deficiency and abductor compromise.
- Contemporary comparative series show acceptable complication profiles for fix-and-replace (reoperation ~8–9%, predominantly infection or dislocation) with earlier mobilization and lower rates of secondary arthroplasty conversion compared with staged ORIF-then-THA; the literature increasingly favors this combined approach in carefully selected patients ≥60–65 years with unreconstructible or high PTOA-risk patterns, while ORIF alone remains standard for reconstructible fractures regardless of age.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Pelvic ring injury (surgically stabilized, stable fixation construct):**
- **Weeks 0–6**: touch-down/toe-touch weight-bearing on the operated side(s) if posterior fixation is percutaneous screws with good bone quality and anatomic reduction; non-weight-bearing if fixation is tenuous (severe osteoporosis, comminution, lumbopelvic construct); DVT prophylaxis continued; bed mobility, transfer training, seated exercises; pain control transitioning off opioids.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive weight-bearing as radiographs confirm healing (callus bridging, no progressive displacement on serial films), gait training with assistive device, hip/core strengthening avoiding resisted hip flexion/adduction against fixation in the first weeks.
- **Weeks 12+**: full weight-bearing once union confirmed (typically by 12 weeks for percutaneous posterior fixation with good bone, longer — 4–6 months — for lumbopelvic constructs or severely comminuted patterns); return to low-impact activity; hardware removal not routine unless symptomatic.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work 6–12 weeks; manual labor typically 4–6 months, individualized to fracture pattern and fixation stability.

**Fragility pelvic/sacral fractures (FFP, conservative or minimally invasive fixation):**
- **Days 0–3**: aggressive multimodal analgesia, orthogeriatric co-management, mobilization attempt within 24–48 hours (weight-bearing as tolerated — restricting weight-bearing in this population drives the deconditioning/mortality spiral more than it protects the fracture).
- **Weeks 1–6**: physiotherapy focused on gait, balance, transfers; falls-risk assessment and home safety evaluation; if operated (percutaneous screws), similarly WBAT is generally permitted given fixation is chosen specifically to allow immediate loading in this population — this is a key advantage of surgical fixation over conservative care for FFP II–IV.
- **Weeks 6–12**: continued strength/balance training; osteoporosis treatment initiated/optimized (anabolic agent if very high fracture risk, otherwise anti-resorptive) per fracture-liaison service pathway; reassess ambulatory status against pre-injury baseline.
- **Milestone**: return to pre-fracture ambulatory level and living situation is the primary rehabilitation goal; only 40–60% of FFP patients regain pre-fracture mobility level at 1 year, underscoring the value of a low threshold for early fixation in patients failing conservative trials.

**Sacral fracture (traumatic, young/high-energy, percutaneous or lumbopelvic fixation):**
- **Weeks 0–6**: weight-bearing status dictated by construct — touch-down/toe-touch if percutaneous screws in good bone with anatomic reduction; non-weight-bearing on the affected side(s) for 6 weeks if lumbopelvic (triangular) fixation was required for a highly unstable or comminuted pattern; monitor for evolving neurologic deficit at each visit if the initial injury involved Denis Zone II/III.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive weight-bearing guided by radiographic callus and absence of secondary displacement; initiate core and hip girdle strengthening; neurogenic bowel/bladder rehabilitation if a foraminal/canal injury caused deficit, coordinated with urology/neurorehabilitation as needed.
- **Months 3–4**: full weight-bearing typically achieved once union is confirmed; return to desk work 6–10 weeks, manual labor or heavy lifting deferred to 4–6 months.
- **Return to sport**: low-impact activity at 3–4 months once union confirmed; contact/axial-load sport deferred to 6–9 months and contingent on resolution of any neurologic deficit.

**Acetabular fracture (post-ORIF):**
- **Weeks 0–6**: touch-down weight-bearing (10–15 kg / foot-flat) on the operated limb; early passive/active-assisted hip ROM (avoid resisted abduction if abductors were taken down); CPM not routinely required; hip precautions if posterior wall repair (avoid combined flexion-adduction-internal rotation, analogous to posterior THA precautions, for 6 weeks) or anterior approach (avoid extension-external rotation).
- **Weeks 6–12**: progress to partial then full weight-bearing guided by radiographic union and surgeon assessment; closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, pool therapy; continue HO prophylaxis monitoring if extensile approach was used.
- **Months 3–6**: full weight-bearing, progressive resistance training, return to low-impact recreational activity; assess for early PTOA signs.
- **Return to sport**: non-contact low-impact activity (cycling, swimming) 4–6 months; pivoting/contact sport 9–12 months, contingent on radiographic union, full painless ROM, and functional strength testing — many surgeons counsel against return to high-impact pivoting sport indefinitely given articular cartilage injury burden.
- **Post-THA (fix-and-replace)**: standard THA rehabilitation protocol accelerated relative to ORIF-alone — weight-bearing as tolerated typically permitted from the outset given implant stability supersedes fracture-fixation concerns, with dislocation precautions tailored to approach and construct (dual-mobility/constrained liner may relax standard precautions).

## Complications

- **Hemorrhagic death** — the dominant early mortality driver in unstable pelvic ring injury; mitigated by protocolized binder-to-OR/angio pathways (see algorithm).
- **Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (PTOA)** — the leading long-term complication of acetabular fracture; strongly correlated with reduction quality (anatomic reduction ~10–15% conversion to THA at 10 years vs. >40–50% with imperfect/poor reduction), initial marginal impaction, femoral head injury, and posterior wall involvement.
- **Avascular necrosis of the femoral head** — associated with fracture-dislocation, delayed reduction (>6–12h), and posterior wall injury; incidence roughly 5–10% overall, higher with delayed treatment.
- **Heterotopic ossification** — most common after Kocher-Langenbeck and extended iliofemoral approaches (up to 20–30% without prophylaxis, higher-grade HO less common); mitigated with indomethacin or single-fraction radiotherapy in high-risk patients.
- **Sciatic nerve injury** — iatrogenic (retraction/traction during posterior approach) or fracture-related (posterior wall/column, fracture-dislocation); peroneal division more vulnerable than tibial.
- **Nonunion/malunion of pelvic ring** — more common with inadequate posterior fixation of vertically unstable (Tile C, APC-III, VS) patterns; presents as chronic pain, gait asymmetry, sitting intolerance.
- **Symptomatic hardware, screw loosening/cutout** — particularly in osteoporotic bone with standard iliosacral screws; drives increasing use of cement-augmented constructs and TITS screws in the fragility population.
- **Infection** — deep SSI rates higher with extensile approaches, open pelvic fractures, and combined fix-and-replace acetabular procedures (reported ~5–6% in recent fix-and-replace series).
- **DVT/PE** — pelvic and acetabular trauma carry among the highest VTE risk of all orthopaedic trauma; mechanical + pharmacologic prophylaxis per institutional protocol, extended duration in immobilized patients.
- **Loss of independence and 1-year mortality in fragility fractures** — pubic rami and FFP fractures carry 1-year mortality comparable to hip fractures (roughly 10–20% in several series) when posterior ring involvement or failure to remobilize occurs, reinforcing the case for an orthogeriatric co-management pathway analogous to hip fracture care.
- **Dislocation after fix-and-replace THA** — elevated relative to elective THA due to posterior column/wall deficiency and abductor compromise; mitigated by dual-mobility or constrained liner selection.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2026 — Joint Trauma System, Pelvic Fracture Care Clinical Practice Guideline**: updated military/civilian-adapted CPG codifying the binder → resuscitation → FAST-directed → preperitoneal packing/REBOA/angioembolization sequence for hemodynamically unstable pelvic fractures, with explicit escalation criteria; reinforces packing + external stabilization performed together in the OR for the exsanguinating patient. [Pelvic Fracture Care CPG](https://jts.health.mil/assets/docs/cpgs/Pelvic_Fracture_Care_17_Feb_2026_ID34v1.1.pdf)
- **2024 — "Pelvic packing – status 2024" (PMC)**: contemporary review confirms preperitoneal pelvic packing remains a cornerstone hemorrhage-control option, particularly where angiography is not immediately available, and should be performed in conjunction with mechanical pelvic stabilization to maximize efficacy — does not replace, but complements, angioembolization for arterial sources. [Pelvic packing – status 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11724799/)
- **2024 — "Emergency treatment of pelvic ring injuries: state of the art" (Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery)**: synthesizes updated ESVS vascular trauma guidance and AAST/ACS-COT damage-control resuscitation principles, emphasizing an explicit, protocolized sequence from resuscitation to operative/endovascular/hybrid source control rather than an either/or choice between packing and embolization. [Emergency treatment of pelvic ring injuries: state of the art](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00402-024-05447-7)
- **2025 — Retrospective cohort, THA vs. ORIF for acetabular fractures in older adults (Medicare Limited Data Set)**: large administrative-database comparison reinforcing that acute THA carries a distinct complication/reoperation profile from ORIF in the elderly, informing shared decision-making on fixation strategy at index surgery. [THA vs ORIF acetabular fractures in older adults](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40759001/)
- **2025 — "To Fix or Replace? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis on Surgical Management of Acetabular Fractures in the Elderly"**: pooled analysis supports acute fix-and-replace for unreconstructible/high-PTOA-risk patterns, showing comparable or improved functional outcomes and mobility recovery versus ORIF alone in appropriately selected patients ≥60 years, while ORIF alone remains preferred when the joint is reconstructible. [To Fix or Replace? meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41106706/)
- **2025 — Mid-term (minimum 5-year) multicentric study, fix-and-replace vs. ORIF in elderly acetabular fractures**: implant survival and functional scores favor fix-and-replace in comminuted/impacted dome patterns; reinforces that reduction quality (not age per se) drives ORIF success, refining patient-selection criteria for combined procedures. [Mid-term outcomes fix-and-replace vs ORIF](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12078442/)
- **2025 — 3-year follow-up, acute fixation + primary THA in 70 elderly acetabular fracture patients**: reoperation rate 8.6% (infection 5.7%, dislocation 2.9%), supporting fix-and-replace as a reproducible strategy with an acceptable complication profile across multiple centers. [Acetabular fractures elderly, fixation + THA, 3-year follow-up](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00402-025-05941-6)
- **2023 — Systematic review/meta-analysis, primary THA + limited ORIF vs. ORIF alone for geriatric acetabular fractures**: earlier pooled evidence establishing the fix-and-replace approach as non-inferior or superior for functional recovery in select geriatric patterns, foundational to the 2024–2025 confirmatory studies above. [THA + limited ORIF vs ORIF, geriatric acetabular fractures](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37395715/)
- **2023 — Systematic review/meta-analysis, acute vs. delayed THA after prior acetabular fracture fixation**: clarifies timing strategy when arthroplasty is anticipated but not performed acutely, relevant to counseling patients treated initially with ORIF alone who progress to symptomatic PTOA. [Acute vs delayed THA after acetabular fixation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10504200/)
- **2024/2025 — "Transiliac-Transsacral Screw Fixation is Superior to Lumbopelvic Stabilization" for minimally displaced sacral H-/U-type fragility fractures**: comparative outcomes study finding TITS screws achieve comparable or better stabilization with less surgical morbidity than lumbopelvic (triangular) constructs for this specific pattern, supporting a shift toward percutaneous-first strategy for fragility spinopelvic dissociation when the pattern permits. [TITS fixation superior to lumbopelvic stabilization](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39058691/)
- **2025 — Biomechanical analysis of combined iliosacral + transiliac-transsacral screw fixation in dysmorphic sacrum, Denis II vertical shear**: combined ISS + TTS constructs significantly outperform TTS alone in reducing vertical displacement in dysmorphic anatomy, informing construct choice when dysmorphism limits a purely transsacral trajectory. [Biomechanical analysis ISS/TTS combined fixation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12517282/)
- **2025 — "Classifications and treatment management of fragility fracture of the pelvis: A scoping review"**: maps current conservative-treatment trends and outcomes for FFP, and — notably — cites monocentric series challenging the strict Rommens/Hofmann algorithm, with good outcomes reported for operatively treated FFP II and nonoperatively treated select FFP III/IV, supporting individualized rather than rigid type-based decision-making. [Classifications and treatment management of FFP: scoping review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002013832500066X)
- **2025 — AO Foundation clinical update, "Fragility Fractures of the Pelvis (FFP): Diagnosis, Classification & Treatment Options"**: practice-oriented synthesis reaffirming CT/MRI upstaging of apparently isolated pubic rami fractures to combined anterior-posterior FFP patterns, and reinforcing minimally-invasive percutaneous fixation (often cement-augmented) as the preferred surgical strategy when operative treatment is indicated. [AO Foundation FFP update](https://www.aofoundation.org/trauma/about-aotrauma/blog/2025_03_blog_fragility_fractures_of_pelvis)
- **2023 — "Clinical Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Fragility Fractures of the Pelvis" (Orthopaedic Surgery, Zhang et al.)**: consensus guideline formalizing dynamic assessment of fracture morphology/instability for pubic rami and FFP fractures (Grade 1B recommendation) and codifying the type-stratified conservative-vs-operative algorithm summarized in the Classification section above. [Clinical Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of FFP](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/os.13755)
- **2024 — "Can Nondisplacement Osteoporotic Pubic Rami Fracture Be Life-Threatening Injuries? A Case Report, Overview, and Algorithm Protocol for Management"**: case-based review proposing a structured vascular-risk-screening algorithm for isolated pubic rami fractures in the elderly, given rare but reported occult corona mortis/obturator vessel injury with nondisplaced fractures — supports maintaining a low threshold for hemodynamic monitoring even in "minor" fragility ring injuries. [Nondisplaced osteoporotic pubic rami fracture algorithm](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23247096241231648)
