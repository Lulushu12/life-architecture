## Quick Reference

### Caprini score → VTE risk category → prophylaxis (management implication)

| Total points | Risk category | Approx. VTE risk (no ppx) | Recommended prophylaxis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Very low | ~0.5% | Early ambulation only, no mechanical/pharmacologic |
| 1–2 | Low | ~1.5% | Mechanical prophylaxis (IPC) alone |
| 3–4 | Moderate | ~3% | Pharmacologic prophylaxis (LMWH) ± IPC |
| ≥5 | High | 6%–≥60% in unprotected series | Pharmacologic **+** mechanical combined; consider extended-duration ppx |

**Note:** elective major lower-extremity arthroplasty and hip/pelvis/femur fracture are each independently worth **5 points** — essentially every arthroplasty and hip-fracture patient defaults into the "high risk" tier, which is why guideline-based pharmacologic prophylaxis is near-universal in these populations rather than optional.

### ASA Physical Status → perioperative implication

| Grade | Definition | Typical implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Normal healthy patient | Routine anesthetic risk counseling |
| II | Mild systemic disease (well-controlled HTN, DM, smoker, obesity) | Standard optimization; no special workup usually needed |
| III | Severe systemic disease, functional limitation | Formal preop medical/cardiac optimization; consider regional over GA; higher complication/mortality benchmarking |
| IV | Severe systemic disease, constant threat to life | Multidisciplinary preop review; ICU/HDU bed planning; discuss goals of care |
| V | Moribund, not expected to survive without the operation | Emergent/life-saving indication only; explicit consent re: mortality risk |
| VI | Declared brain-dead, organs procured for donation | Not applicable to elective orthopedics |
| "E" suffix | Emergency case | Added to any grade; independently raises complication/mortality risk |

### Clavien-Dindo complication grading

| Grade | Definition | Example in orthopedics |
|---|---|---|
| I | Deviation from normal course, no pharmacologic/surgical/endoscopic/radiologic intervention needed (antiemetics, antipyretics, analgesics, physiotherapy allowed; bedside wound opening allowed) | Postop ileus managed expectantly, superficial seroma opened at bedside |
| II | Requires pharmacologic treatment beyond Grade I drugs, or blood transfusion, or TPN | Transfusion for symptomatic anemia, antibiotics for cellulitis |
| IIIa | Intervention (surgical/endoscopic/radiologic) **not** under general anesthesia | Bedside/local I&D, percutaneous drainage under sedation |
| IIIb | Intervention under general anesthesia | Return to OR for hematoma evacuation, irrigation & debridement |
| IVa | Life-threatening, single-organ dysfunction, ICU-level care | Acute MI, single-organ renal failure requiring dialysis |
| IVb | Life-threatening, multi-organ dysfunction | Sepsis with multi-organ failure |
| V | Death | — |
| suffix "d" | Patient discharged with the complication (disability) | Persistent nerve palsy at discharge |

### Treatment ladders (first-line → second-line → escalation)

| Problem | First-line | Second-line | Escalation/surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| VTE prophylaxis, standard-risk TJA | Aspirin 81mg BID (post-DOAC/LMWH lead-in in trauma per PREVENT-CLOT-style protocols, or as monotherapy per CRISTAL/EPCAT II) + IPC | LMWH (enoxaparin 40mg SC daily) or DOAC (rivaroxaban 10mg daily) | Therapeutic anticoagulation, IVC filter (rare, only if anticoagulation contraindicated + acute proximal DVT) |
| Surgical site infection prophylaxis | Cefazolin 2g IV (3g if ≥120kg) within 60 min of incision | Vancomycin or clindamycin (true beta-lactam anaphylaxis) within 120 min | Extend to gram-negative coverage only if documented colonization/high MRSA-negative gram-negative risk; add vancomycin adjunct in known MRSA carriers |
| Intraoperative blood loss reduction | IV TXA 1g at incision (± 1g at closure, or 10–15mg/kg) | Topical/intra-articular TXA 1–3g if IV relatively contraindicated | Cell salvage, staged/hypotensive anesthesia, transfusion per restrictive threshold (Hb <8g/dL symptomatic or <7g/dL asymptomatic) |
| Postoperative pain (arthroplasty/trauma) | Scheduled acetaminophen + NSAID (if no contraindication) + peripheral nerve block/periarticular LIA | Add low-dose oral opioid, gabapentinoid only if neuropathic component | PCA opioid, ketamine infusion, revisit surgical cause (hematoma, malposition, infection) |
| BCIS during cementation | Preemptive: canal lavage/vent hole, warn anesthesia, IV fluid loading, vasopressor primed | IV fluids + vasopressor (phenylephrine/ephedrine) bolus at first hypotension | ACLS-level resuscitation, abort cementation sequence, consider uncemented conversion in future cases |
| Suspected early PJI / wound drainage | Prolonged elevation, dressing optimization, address anticoagulation-driven ooze | Wound review at 5–7 days if drainage persists; consider incisional NPWT | DAIR (debridement, antibiotics, implant retention) if <4 weeks from index surgery/acute hematogenous, stable implant |

### Red flags (any warrants same-day escalation)

- **Persistent wound drainage beyond postoperative day 5–7** — leading trigger for early PJI workup and possible DAIR.
- **Acute hypotension/hypoxia/arrhythmia within minutes of cement pressurization or component impaction** — treat as BCIS until proven otherwise.
- **Calf pain/swelling, sudden dyspnea, or pleuritic chest pain postoperatively** — treat as DVT/PE until excluded, especially on/after chemoprophylaxis discontinuation.
- **New confusion in a patient >65y postoperatively** — screen for delirium (CAM), do not assume "normal for age"; check for occult infection, hypoxia, medication effect, urinary retention.
- **Fever + tachycardia + wound erythema before POD 3** — consider necrotizing/aggressive early infection, not routine post-surgical inflammation.
- **True anaphylaxis history to penicillin (urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension within minutes)** — the only scenario that should redirect away from cefazolin; a remote/vague rash history should NOT.

### Key numbers

- Cefazolin: **2g IV** (**3g if ≥120kg**), infuse to complete **within 60 min before incision**; redose **every 4h intraoperatively** or after **>1500mL blood loss**.
- Vancomycin/fluoroquinolones: begin infusion **within 120 min before incision** (slow infusion time).
- Antibiotic prophylaxis duration: **stop within 24h of surgery** — no benefit to prolonged courses.
- TXA IV dosing: **1g bolus at incision ± 1g at closure**, or weight-based **10–15mg/kg**; topical **1–3g in 100mL saline** intra-articular before closure.
- Transfusion threshold: **restrictive strategy (Hb 7–8g/dL trigger)** is non-inferior to liberal in hip fracture surgery (FOCUS trial).
- Tourniquet pressure: **limb occlusion pressure + 50–100mmHg** (commonly 250–300mmHg thigh); **max inflation time 90–120 min**, release and reperfuse if longer needed.
- VTE chemoprophylaxis duration: minimum **10–14 days**, extended **28–35 days** for THA/hip fracture per ACCP/ICM guidance, especially in high-risk patients.
- Neuraxial/regional block timing after LMWH: **12h after prophylactic dose, 24h after therapeutic dose** (ASRA); DOACs typically **2–3 days** depending on agent/renal function.
- Time to surgery in hip fracture: **<24–36h** from admission associated with reduced mortality (NICE/NHFD benchmarks).
- Perioperative glucose target: **<200mg/dL** to reduce SSI risk regardless of diabetic status.

## Classifications (fully enumerated)

### Caprini Risk Assessment Model — full point enumeration

| Points | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| **1 point** | Age 41–60y; minor surgery; BMI >25; swollen legs; varicose veins; pregnancy or postpartum (<1 month); history of unexplained/recurrent miscarriage; oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy; sepsis (<1 month); serious lung disease incl. pneumonia (<1 month); abnormal pulmonary function; acute MI; congestive heart failure (<1 month); history of inflammatory bowel disease; medical patient currently at bed rest |
| **2 points** | Age 61–74y; arthroscopic surgery; malignancy (present or previous); major open surgery (>45 min); laparoscopic surgery (>45 min); confined to bed >72h; immobilizing plaster cast; central venous access |
| **3 points** | Age ≥75y; personal history of VTE; family history of VTE; Factor V Leiden; prothrombin gene mutation 20210A; lupus anticoagulant; elevated anticardiolipin antibody; elevated serum homocysteine; heparin-induced thrombocytopenia; other congenital or acquired thrombophilia |
| **5 points** | Elective major lower-extremity arthroplasty; hip, pelvis, or leg fracture; acute stroke (<1 month); multiple trauma (<1 month); acute spinal cord injury/paralysis (<1 month) |

**Management implication:** score is additive; totals map to the four risk categories in Quick Reference. In elective adult reconstruction, essentially all patients present already in the ≥5-point "high risk" band by virtue of the procedure alone, so Caprini's chief clinical utility in arthroplasty is refining agent selection (aspirin-eligible "standard risk" vs LMWH/DOAC-mandatory "elevated risk" per additional VTE-specific factors — prior VTE, active cancer, thrombophilia, morbid obesity, poor mobility) rather than deciding whether to give prophylaxis at all.

### ASA Physical Status Classification — full detail

| Grade | Definition | Illustrative example | Perioperative implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Normal healthy patient | Non-smoker, no/minimal alcohol use | Minimal added risk |
| **II** | Mild systemic disease, no functional limitation | Controlled HTN/DM, smoker, social alcohol use, pregnancy, obesity 30<BMI<40 | Standard preop optimization |
| **III** | Severe systemic disease with functional limitation | Poorly controlled DM/HTN, COPD, morbid obesity BMI≥40, active hepatitis, ESRD on dialysis, cardiac stent >3 months ago | Formal cardiac/pulmonary workup; consider regional anesthesia; higher expected LOS/complication rate |
| **IV** | Severe systemic disease, constant threat to life | Recent (<3 months) MI/CVA/TIA, ongoing cardiac ischemia, severe valve dysfunction, sepsis, DIC | Multidisciplinary/anesthesia-led risk discussion; ICU bed reserved; shared decision-making on elective vs deferral |
| **V** | Moribund, not expected to survive without surgery | Ruptured aneurysm, massive trauma, ischemic bowel with multi-organ failure | Emergent/life-saving indication only |
| **VI** | Declared brain-dead, organs being procured | — | Not applicable to elective orthopedic practice |
| **E** modifier | Appended for emergency surgery (delay would significantly increase risk to life or organ) | ASA IIIE, ASA IVE | Independently increases morbidity/mortality risk versus the same grade performed electively |

### Clavien-Dindo Classification of Surgical Complications — full detail

| Grade | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Any deviation from the normal postoperative course without need for pharmacologic treatment (beyond antiemetics, antipyretics, analgesics, diuretics, electrolytes) or surgical/endoscopic/radiologic intervention; wound infections opened at the bedside are included | Observation, symptomatic management, does not change discharge planning materially |
| **II** | Requires pharmacologic treatment with drugs other than those allowed for Grade I, including blood transfusions and total parenteral nutrition | Extends length of stay, triggers medical consult (e.g., hematology, ID) |
| **IIIa** | Requires surgical, endoscopic, or radiologic intervention **not** performed under general anesthesia | Bedside procedures, interventional radiology drainage |
| **IIIb** | Requires intervention under general anesthesia | Return to OR |
| **IVa** | Life-threatening complication with single-organ dysfunction requiring IC/ICU management | Escalation of care level, prognosis discussion |
| **IVb** | Life-threatening complication with multi-organ dysfunction | ICU, multidisciplinary critical care |
| **V** | Death of the patient | Mortality/morbidity review |
| **suffix "d"** | Patient is discharged with the complication present (disability) | Flags need for outpatient follow-up/rehab planning |

## VTE Prophylaxis in Orthopedic Surgery

**Risk stratification** combines procedure-inherent risk (arthroplasty and hip/pelvis/femur fracture are automatically high-risk per Caprini) with patient-specific factors: personal/family VTE history, active malignancy, inherited/acquired thrombophilia, immobility, morbid obesity, and estrogen therapy.

**Agent selection — the aspirin-vs-anticoagulant question:**
- **EPCAT II** (Anderson et al.) established that after an initial 5-day course of rivaroxaban post-THA/TKA, transitioning to **aspirin 81mg BID** for the remainder of the prophylaxis period is non-inferior for symptomatic VTE, with a lower bleeding rate — this "anticoagulant-then-aspirin" sequencing became the practical template for many North American arthroplasty protocols.
- **CRISTAL trial** (elective and trauma hip/knee arthroplasty populations, largely hip fracture surgery) compared **aspirin monotherapy** against enoxaparin/DOAC for the full prophylaxis course and found aspirin **non-inferior** for the composite of symptomatic VTE, death, and major bleeding at 90 days — reinforcing that low-risk, average-BMI, non-thrombophilic arthroplasty patients can be managed on aspirin alone from the outset rather than requiring a LMWH/DOAC lead-in.
- **Major trauma / extremity and pelvic-acetabular fracture** patients (a distinct population from elective arthroplasty) were addressed by a large pragmatic RCT comparing **aspirin vs. low-molecular-weight heparin** for thromboprophylaxis; aspirin was **non-inferior** for the primary outcome of death from any cause, with similar rates of DVT/PE and no meaningful safety penalty — this now supports aspirin as a reasonable default agent even in operative trauma populations, not just elective reconstruction.
- **AAOS Clinical Practice Guideline** for VTE prophylaxis in elective hip/knee arthroplasty explicitly lists aspirin as an acceptable agent for patients without elevated VTE risk factors, alongside LMWH, DOACs, warfarin, and mechanical devices — deliberately avoiding a single mandated agent and instead framing this as a risk-stratified, shared decision.
- **ICM (International Consensus Meeting on VTE)** consensus statements similarly support aspirin as sufficient in standard-risk primary TJA, reserving LMWH/DOAC-grade anticoagulation for patients with prior VTE, active cancer, hypercoagulable state, or inflammatory arthritis on high-dose steroids.

**Mechanical prophylaxis:** intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) should be used in essentially all arthroplasty and hip fracture patients regardless of pharmacologic agent, and as monotherapy when pharmacologic prophylaxis is contraindicated (active bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, recent intracranial hemorrhage).

**Duration:** minimum 10–14 days for TKA; extended prophylaxis to **28–35 days** is recommended for THA and hip fracture surgery given persistent post-discharge VTE risk.

**Neuraxial/regional anesthesia timing (ASRA guidance):** prophylactic LMWH — needle/catheter placement or removal ≥12h after last dose; therapeutic LMWH — ≥24h; DOACs — typically 2–3 days depending on agent and renal function; aspirin alone imposes no restriction on neuraxial timing.

**IVC filters** have a narrow role: acute proximal DVT/PE with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation in the immediate perioperative window — not a substitute for pharmacologic prophylaxis.

## Surgical Antibiotic Prophylaxis

**Standard regimen:** **cefazolin 2g IV** (**3g if body weight ≥120kg**), infusion completed within **60 minutes before incision**; intraoperative redosing every **4 hours** or after **>1500mL blood loss**. Discontinue within **24 hours** of surgery — prolonged "prophylaxis" beyond this window does not reduce SSI rates and increases resistant-organism and *C. difficile* risk (CDC/WHO SSI prevention guidance).

**The penicillin-allergy cross-reactivity myth:** the historically taught ~10% cross-reactivity between penicillins and cephalosporins is now understood to be a substantial overestimate driven by older, impure cephalosporin preparations and shared testing methodology flaws. True cross-reactivity is mediated by shared R1 side chains, not the shared beta-lactam ring — and **cefazolin has a unique side-chain structure not shared with any penicillin**, making cross-reactivity with cefazolin essentially negligible even in patients with a well-documented penicillin allergy. Contemporary allergy/ID literature (Blumenthal and colleagues, among others) supports cefazolin use even in patients reporting penicillin allergy, reserving avoidance for those with a history of **true anaphylaxis, Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN, or other severe immediate-type reaction**. A vague reported rash, GI upset, or unknown-mechanism reaction should **not** trigger diversion to vancomycin/clindamycin.

**Alternative regimen (true beta-lactam anaphylaxis):** vancomycin or clindamycin, infusion started within **120 minutes before incision** given the slower required infusion rate for vancomycin. Vancomycin should not be used as a default "safe" substitute — routine unnecessary vancomycin use is associated with **higher SSI rates** in some cohorts (inferior gram-negative and staphylococcal coverage relative to cefazolin, plus red-man reaction/nephrotoxicity risk) and drives resistance pressure.

**MRSA-colonized or high-risk patients:** consider adding a single dose of vancomycin as an **adjunct** to (not replacement for) cefazolin, informed by preoperative nasal MRSA screening; universal or targeted decolonization (nasal mupirocin + chlorhexidine bathing) reduces SSI in elective arthroplasty cohorts.

**Antibiotic stewardship & allergy delabeling:** formal preoperative allergy delabeling pathways — using validated risk-stratification tools (e.g., PEN-FAST) to identify patients at low risk of true penicillin allergy, followed by direct oral amoxicillin challenge or referral for penicillin skin testing — measurably shift patients back onto cefazolin, reducing vancomycin/clindamycin utilization, operative-day delays, and associated SSI risk. This is increasingly built into pre-admission joint-replacement clinics rather than left to day-of-surgery clarification.

## Tranexamic Acid

**Mechanism:** synthetic lysine analog that competitively blocks the lysine-binding sites on plasminogen, preventing its conversion to plasmin and thereby inhibiting fibrinolysis — reduces surgical blood loss without directly promoting new clot formation.

**Routes and dosing:**
- **IV:** 1g bolus at incision, optionally repeated as 1g at wound closure (or a 3-hour infusion), or weight-based 10–15mg/kg per dose.
- **Topical/intra-articular:** 1–3g diluted in 50–100mL normal saline instilled into the joint before closure, with a dwell time before drain release (if a drain is used) — favored when systemic exposure is a specific concern.
- **Oral:** ~1.95–2g given 2 hours preoperatively; comparable efficacy to IV in several arthroplasty trials with lower cost and no IV-line requirement.
- **Combined IV + topical regimens** are used in high-blood-loss cases (revision arthroplasty, bilateral procedures, complex trauma) for additive effect.

**Safety breadth:** the aggregate arthroplasty and hip-fracture literature — spanning many thousands of randomized patients — has consistently failed to show an increased rate of DVT, PE, MI, or stroke with TXA at standard doses, including in patients with a **prior history of VTE**, coronary disease, or stroke, once considered relative contraindications. This safety signal, however, is **procedure- and dose-context dependent**: a large noncardiac surgery trial in a broader (non-orthopedic-specific, higher cardiovascular-risk) population found an increased risk of the composite of major vascular events (including nonfatal MI and VTE) with high-dose IV TXA compared with placebo — a reminder that the reassuring orthopedic-specific safety data should not be extrapolated uncritically to markedly higher cardiovascular-risk patients or non-orthopedic contexts without individualized risk-benefit discussion.

**Trauma-specific evidence:** major hemorrhage trials in the broader trauma population (not ortho-specific) established a mortality benefit from early TXA administration in bleeding trauma patients, and a separate large trial in traumatic brain injury patients found TXA safe with a signal toward reduced head-injury-related death when given early. These findings, alongside orthopedic-specific hip fracture data showing reduced transfusion requirement, support routine TXA use in operative hip fracture management barring a genuine contraindication.

**Relative contraindications:** history of seizure disorder (TXA, especially at high IV bolus doses or via inadvertent intrathecal exposure, is proconvulsant); active arterial thromboembolic event within the preceding ~3 months (individualized discussion); renal impairment requires dose reduction (renally cleared). Active malignancy and remote (>12 months, stable) VTE history are **not** considered contraindications in current practice.

## Blood Management

**Preoperative optimization:** iron-deficiency anemia should be identified and treated (oral or IV iron depending on severity/timeline to surgery) weeks before elective arthroplasty; erythropoiesis-stimulating agents are reserved for select cases (religious objection to transfusion, severe anemia with insufficient time for iron repletion) given cost and thrombotic risk considerations.

**Intraoperative strategies:** TXA (above) is now the single highest-yield blood-conservation intervention in elective arthroplasty; adjuncts include controlled hypotensive anesthesia (in appropriately selected ASA I–II patients), meticulous hemostasis, and cell salvage/reinfusion in high-blood-loss cases (revision arthroplasty, deformity correction, polytrauma).

**Transfusion threshold:** a **restrictive transfusion strategy** (transfuse for Hb <8g/dL or symptomatic anemia, versus a liberal threshold near 10g/dL) was shown in a large hip fracture surgery RCT to be **non-inferior** for 60-day mortality and functional recovery, while meaningfully reducing transfusion exposure — this restrictive approach is now the default across most arthroplasty and hip fracture protocols, with liberalization reserved for symptomatic patients or those with significant cardiac ischemic disease where individualized thresholds may be higher.

## Tourniquet Use

**Technique:** cuff pressure set at the **limb occlusion pressure plus a 50–100mmHg safety margin** (commonly translating to ~250–300mmHg at the thigh, lower for calf-level or pediatric use), applied after limb exsanguination (Esmarch or elevation). Maximum single inflation time is conventionally **90–120 minutes**; if additional time is needed, deflate and reperfuse for several minutes before reinflating.

**Complications:** tourniquet-related nerve palsy (typically neurapraxia, usually reversible but occasionally prolonged), skin injury/chemical burns from prep solution pooling under the cuff, post-tourniquet syndrome (transient stiffness/weakness/swelling), and — rarely — deep vein thrombosis or compartment syndrome exacerbation.

**Tourniquet-less TKA debate:** contemporary comparative literature, largely in the context of routine TXA use, shows that **avoiding the tourniquet (or using a "short" tourniquet only during cementation)** reduces postoperative pain scores, quadriceps inhibition/early strength deficit, thigh bruising, and wound complication rates, with **no clinically meaningful difference** in total blood loss, transfusion rate, or long-term functional outcome once TXA is used as the primary hemostatic strategy. Proponents of tourniquet use point to a clearer, drier surgical field (particularly valued by lower-volume surgeons or in cementation) and shorter operative time. The pragmatic, increasingly common compromise is a **"cementation-only" short tourniquet time** — inflate immediately before cement application, deflate after prosthesis seating — capturing the visualization benefit during the critical cementing step while minimizing total ischemia time and its downstream pain/strength penalties.

## Cement & Bone Cement Implantation Syndrome (BCIS)

**Mechanism:** during medullary canal preparation and cement/component pressurization, fat, marrow contents, air, and cement monomer are forced into the venous circulation, producing pulmonary microembolization, complement activation, and histamine/thromboxane release. The clinical syndrome comprises acute hypoxia, hypotension, and/or loss of consciousness/cardiac arrest occurring around the time of cementation, prosthesis insertion, joint reduction, or (rarely) tourniquet deflation.

**Donaldson grading:**

| Grade | Features |
|---|---|
| **1** | Moderate hypoxia (SpO₂ <94%) or hypotension (drop >20% of baseline systolic) |
| **2** | Severe hypoxia (SpO₂ <88%) or hypotension (drop >40%), or unexpected loss of consciousness |
| **3** | Cardiovascular collapse requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation |

**Risk factors:** hip **hemiarthroplasty for fracture** (substantially higher risk than elective THA — osteoporotic, unprepared canal, often frailer/higher ASA cohort), male sex, ASA grade III–IV, pre-existing cardiopulmonary disease/pulmonary hypertension, pathologic or heavily sclerotic/osteoporotic bone, long-stem or revision femoral components (more medullary content displaced), and diminished physiologic reserve generally.

**Risk mitigation:**
- **Communicate explicitly with anesthesia** immediately before cementation so hemodynamic monitoring is heightened and vasopressors are drawn up and ready.
- **Femoral canal lavage** (pulsatile lavage) before cement introduction to clear fat/marrow debris and reduce intramedullary pressure at the time of pressurization.
- **Vent hole / retrograde ("distal-to-proximal") cement filling technique** to allow egress of marrow contents and air ahead of the cement column, avoiding a closed-system pressure spike.
- **Avoid excessive/forceful pressurization**, particularly in osteoporotic canals where compliance is high.
- In the **very highest-risk patients** (severe cardiopulmonary disease, ASA IV, frail hip-fracture patients where a hemodynamic hit could be catastrophic), consider an **uncemented stem** as an alternative — accepting a modestly higher periprosthetic fracture risk in exchange for eliminating BCIS risk; national joint registry data generally favor cemented fixation for hip fracture hemiarthroplasty on aggregate revision/mortality outcomes, so this substitution should be individualized rather than routine.
- If Grade 2–3 BCIS occurs: stop the cementing/impaction sequence if not yet complete, give 100% oxygen, IV fluid bolus, vasopressor (phenylephrine or ephedrine) for hypotension, and escalate to full ACLS if cardiovascular collapse develops.

## ERAS in Arthroplasty & Trauma

**Core elements** (ERAS Society and allied consensus frameworks for hip/knee arthroplasty):
- Preoperative patient education and expectation-setting; "prehabilitation" where feasible.
- Minimizing preoperative fasting; carbohydrate loading up to 2h before surgery in appropriate patients.
- Avoidance of long-acting sedative premedication.
- Preference for **spinal/regional anesthesia** over general anesthesia where suitable.
- **Multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia** built around periarticular local infiltration and/or peripheral nerve blocks.
- **TXA** as standard blood-conservation practice.
- Avoidance of routine surgical drains and urinary catheters where not specifically indicated.
- Intraoperative normothermia and goal-directed (rather than liberal) fluid therapy.
- **Early mobilization — same calendar day as surgery** wherever feasible, with weight-bearing as tolerated for cemented/standard constructs.
- Early resumption of oral intake and early discontinuation of IV fluids/catheters.
- Discharge based on objective functional criteria rather than a fixed postoperative day.

**Outcome data:** ERAS pathway implementation in elective hip/knee arthroplasty is consistently associated with shortened length of stay (commonly from a historical 4–5 days down to **1–2 days**, with same-day/next-day discharge achievable in selected outpatient-eligible patients), reduced total opioid consumption, reduced postoperative nausea/vomiting, earlier achievement of physiotherapy milestones, and complication/readmission rates that are equivalent or improved relative to traditional pathways — importantly, LOS reduction has **not** come at the cost of increased 30/90-day readmission in the bulk of the published cohort and registry literature.

**ERAS/co-management in trauma (hip fracture):** the trauma analogue centers on **expedited time to surgery (<24–36h)**, orthogeriatric co-management from admission, avoidance of unnecessary preoperative fasting delays, standardized multimodal analgesia (notably fascia iliaca or PENG blocks to reduce preoperative opioid need), TXA use, and immediate postoperative mobilization — bundled programs built on these principles (formalized in UK national hip fracture audit "Best Practice Tariff"-style frameworks) are associated with reduced 30-day mortality and shorter length of stay compared with unstructured care.

## Regional Anesthesia Considerations

**Neuraxial (spinal/epidural) vs. general anesthesia:** neuraxial techniques are associated with reduced intraoperative blood loss, lower VTE rates, and improved early pain control compared with general anesthesia in lower-extremity arthroplasty and hip fracture surgery. Large contemporary randomized comparisons in hip fracture surgery specifically have found **no significant difference in 60-day mortality or functional recovery** between spinal and general anesthesia — meaning the choice should be individualized (patient anticoagulation status, spinal deformity, hemodynamic stability, patient preference) rather than mandated, even though regional retains clear secondary benefits (analgesia, nausea, delirium signal in some cohorts).

**Peripheral nerve blocks:**
- **Adductor canal block** is preferred over traditional femoral nerve block for TKA — comparable analgesia while substantially better preserving quadriceps strength, supporting earlier and safer ambulation (a key ERAS enabler).
- **IPACK block** (interspace between the popliteal artery and capsule of the knee) is frequently combined with adductor canal block to address posterior knee pain not covered anteriorly.
- **Fascia iliaca compartment block / PENG (pericapsular nerve group) block** are favored for hip fracture preoperative and perioperative analgesia — effective anterior hip/femoral coverage with a lower motor-block burden than a full femoral block, reducing fall risk.
- **Periarticular local infiltration analgesia (LIA)**, injected directly by the surgeon intraoperatively, is a core ERAS multimodal component, often combined with (not replacing) a peripheral block.

**Anticoagulation timing (ASRA consensus principles):** neuraxial or deep peripheral block placement/catheter removal should respect the same drug-specific windows used for VTE prophylaxis timing (see Quick Reference/VTE section) — prophylactic LMWH 12h, therapeutic LMWH 24h, DOACs typically 2–3 days depending on agent and renal clearance; aspirin alone does not require a hold.

## Opioid-Sparing Multimodal Analgesia

**Foundational components**, layered rather than sequential:
- **Scheduled (not PRN) acetaminophen**, IV or oral.
- **NSAID** (COX-2 selective agents favored where GI/bleeding concern exists) unless contraindicated (renal impairment, active GI bleed, high fracture-nonunion concern in select trauma contexts).
- **Peripheral nerve block and/or periarticular local infiltration** as above — the single most effective opioid-sparing intervention in the immediate postoperative window.
- **Perioperative dexamethasone** — dual role as antiemetic and analgesic adjunct, shown to reduce PONV and modestly extend block/analgesic duration.
- **Low-dose ketamine infusion** as an adjunct in opioid-tolerant or high-pain-burden patients.
- **Gabapentinoids** (gabapentin/pregabalin): evidence for meaningful analgesic benefit in routine arthroplasty is modest, while sedation, dizziness, and **fall risk in elderly patients** are real costs — current guidance (including AAOS-aligned practice) favors selective rather than routine use, reserving them for patients with a clear neuropathic pain component.
- **Short-acting oral opioid** reserved as breakthrough medication on top of the above, not as the primary analgesic strategy.

**Preoperative opioid exposure:** patients who are opioid-tolerant preoperatively have measurably worse pain control, higher postoperative opioid requirements, and worse functional outcomes after arthroplasty; where feasible, preoperative opioid-weaning counseling/referral before elective surgery is worthwhile. Procedure-specific consensus recommendations (PROSPECT-style working groups) provide analgesic-regimen guidance tailored to specific orthopedic procedures rather than a single generic protocol.

## Frailty & Orthogeriatric Co-Management

**Frailty assessment tools:**
- **Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS, 1–9):** 1 (very fit) through 9 (terminally ill); scores ≥5 ("mildly frail" and beyond) are associated with meaningfully higher perioperative complication and mortality risk and should trigger orthogeriatric involvement.
- **Modified Frailty Index (mFI-5):** a 5-item comorbidity-count tool (functional status, diabetes, COPD/pneumonia, CHF, hypertension requiring medication) derived from NSQIP variables; higher scores predict complications, LOS, and discharge-to-facility risk in orthopedic cohorts.
- **FRAIL scale:** Fatigue, Resistance, Ambulation, Illnesses, Loss of weight — a brief self-report screen usable in outpatient pre-assessment clinics.

**Orthogeriatric co-management:** structured models in which geriatric medicine is embedded in hip fracture (and increasingly elective arthroplasty) care from admission — covering medical optimization, delirium prevention, polypharmacy review, bone health, and discharge planning — are associated with reduced inpatient mortality, shorter length of stay, and reduced 30-day readmission compared with traditional orthopedics-only models, and underpin national hip fracture audit "Best Practice Tariff"-style programs.

**Delirium prevention:** routine **CAM (Confusion Assessment Method)** screening, avoidance of benzodiazepines and strongly anticholinergic medications, aggressive pain control (untreated pain is itself a delirium driver), early mobilization, sleep-wake cycle preservation, hydration/nutrition, and prompt correction of hypoxia/electrolyte derangement.

**Bone health:** hip fracture and fragility-fracture patients should be assessed and, where appropriate, started on **osteoporosis pharmacotherapy** before discharge or via a **Fracture Liaison Service** referral — this "secondary fracture prevention" step is repeatedly identified as an under-delivered but high-yield component of orthogeriatric bundles.

**Time-to-surgery:** expedited surgery, generally **within 24–36 hours** of admission for hip fracture, is associated with reduced mortality and complication rates in large registry analyses, provided the delay to optimize genuinely reversible acute medical issues (e.g., correctable coagulopathy, decompensated heart failure) is not itself prioritized over speed when the patient is otherwise fit for anesthesia.

## DAIR-Prevention Wound Care

The goal of perioperative wound management is to prevent the cascade — **hematoma → persistent drainage → superficial then deep infection → need for DAIR or two-stage revision** — rather than to treat it after the fact.

**Modifiable SSI/PJI risk factors to optimize preoperatively:** obesity (individualized BMI thresholds are used by many centers as relative contraindications to elective arthroplasty), poorly controlled diabetes (target **perioperative glucose <200mg/dL** regardless of diabetic status, since even non-diabetic hyperglycemia raises SSI risk), active smoking (cessation counseling, ideally weeks before elective surgery), malnutrition (albumin <3.5g/dL is a recognized independent risk marker), and any modifiable immunosuppression (discuss holding/adjusting disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs and biologics with rheumatology around the perioperative window per joint AAOS/ACR-style guidance).

**Intraoperative/closure technique:** meticulous layered closure, tension-free skin closure, consideration of barbed running suture for a watertight capsular/subcutaneous layer, and avoidance of excessive electrocautery near the skin edge. Anticoagulation/antiplatelet management should balance VTE risk against the bleeding/hematoma risk that directly predisposes to wound complications and secondary infection.

**Dressings:** occlusive, often silver-impregnated or waterproof dressings left undisturbed for an extended initial period (commonly 5–7 days) reduce the number of dressing changes (each an opportunity for bacterial ingress) while allowing wound inspection through a transparent window when needed.

**Incisional negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT):** reserved for **high-risk wounds** — obesity, revision surgery, prior wound complications, poor soft-tissue envelope, or high drainage anticipated — where it reduces wound complication and reoperation rates relative to standard dressings in the highest-risk subgroup, though routine use in low-risk primary arthroplasty has not shown the same benefit.

**The persistent-drainage threshold:** wound drainage persisting **beyond postoperative day 5–7** is the single most consistently cited trigger for early surgical wound review and PJI workup; addressing it proactively (rather than reassurance and waiting) is the highest-yield DAIR-prevention action available once a patient is already postoperative. When acute PJI (early postoperative, or acute hematogenous with <3 weeks of symptoms and a stable, well-fixed implant) is confirmed, **DAIR** — thorough debridement, modular component exchange, and pathogen-directed antibiotics with implant retention — is the appropriate surgical response; success rates fall sharply the longer symptom duration or biofilm maturity is allowed to progress, which is the underlying rationale for aggressive early wound surveillance rather than a "wait and see" approach.

## Rehabilitation Protocol (generic perioperative pathway)

This phased pathway applies across elective arthroplasty and operative trauma, with implant/fracture-specific weight-bearing modifications layered on top by the surgeon.

**Phase 0 — Prehabilitation (2–4 weeks before elective surgery):**
- Strength and mobility conditioning of the operative limb where time and pathology allow.
- Anemia, glycemic, and nutritional optimization (see DAIR-prevention section).
- Patient/family education on postoperative expectations, home safety modifications, and discharge-planning logistics.

**Phase 1 — Acute postoperative (Day 0–POD 1):**
- Mobilization **same day or POD 1** per ERAS principles; weight-bearing as tolerated for standard cemented fixation, per-protocol restrictions for specific uncemented/fracture constructs.
- Gentle active/active-assisted ROM, ankle pumps, and initiation of VTE mechanical/pharmacologic prophylaxis.
- Multimodal analgesia titration to enable participation in physiotherapy.

**Phase 2 — Early recovery (Week 1–2):**
- Progressive gait training with appropriate assistive device, transition off assistive device guided by strength/balance rather than a fixed calendar date.
- Wound review, suture/staple removal typically **10–14 days**.
- Discharge to home with outpatient/home physiotherapy, or short-stay rehabilitation facility for patients with inadequate home support or higher frailty burden.

**Phase 3 — Progressive rehabilitation (Week 2–6):**
- Progressive resistance training and ROM goals (e.g., functional flexion targets by 6 weeks in TKA), balance and proprioceptive work.
- Driving resumption typically **4–6 weeks** post-lower-extremity surgery once off sedating analgesics and demonstrating adequate reaction time/strength (left-sided procedures with automatic transmission may permit earlier return in some protocols).
- **Return to sedentary/desk work**: often **2–4 weeks**, individualized to commute demands and pain control.

**Phase 4 — Functional consolidation (6 weeks–3 months):**
- **Return to physically demanding/manual labor**: generally **around 3 months**, contingent on strength, ROM, and pain-control milestones rather than calendar time alone.
- **Return to low-impact recreational activity** (walking, cycling, swimming, golf): typically **3 months**.

**Phase 5 — Long-term (3–12 months):**
- **Return to higher-impact activity**, where appropriate for the implant/procedure, is individualized and typically deferred to **6–12 months**, balanced against implant-specific wear/loosening considerations in arthroplasty.
- Final functional and patient-reported outcome assessment; ongoing bone-health/secondary-fracture-prevention follow-up for fragility-fracture patients.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

*Editorial note on sourcing:* live web search was unavailable during authorship of this entry (session search budget exhausted before any query returned results), so the items below are drawn from established clinical knowledge rather than a verified, dated citation trail, and **no source URLs are included** in order to avoid fabricating links. Each item should be independently re-verified (exact publication date, journal, and current guideline status) before being treated as a citable, current-as-of-2026 reference in practice.

- A large pragmatic randomized trial in operative extremity and pelvic/acetabular fracture patients found **aspirin non-inferior to low-molecular-weight heparin** for prevention of death from any cause, with comparable VTE and bleeding rates — reinforcing aspirin as a reasonable default thromboprophylaxis agent in operative trauma, not solely in elective arthroplasty, and shaping subsequent society-level VTE prophylaxis discussion in this population.
- Continued follow-up and subgroup analyses building on the aspirin-vs-anticoagulant arthroplasty and hip-fracture trials (the EPCAT II and CRISTAL trial lineage) have kept refining which patients are truly "standard risk" enough for aspirin monotherapy versus needing LMWH/DOAC-grade prophylaxis, with an ongoing trend toward broader aspirin adoption in guideline language for average-risk primary arthroplasty.
- Growing multicenter experience with **tourniquet-less TKA in the TXA era** continues to accumulate, generally reinforcing that omitting or minimizing tourniquet time preserves early quadriceps strength and reduces postoperative pain without compromising blood loss or transfusion outcomes when TXA is used systematically.
- Formal **penicillin-allergy delabeling pathways** embedded in preoperative arthroplasty clinics (structured risk stratification followed by direct oral challenge or referral for skin testing) continue to be adopted more widely as a stewardship intervention, with reported reductions in unnecessary vancomycin/clindamycin prophylaxis and an associated signal toward improved SSI outcomes relative to unscreened "allergy label" avoidance of cefazolin.
- Orthogeriatric co-management and structured hip-fracture care bundles (expedited time-to-theatre, multimodal regional analgesia, early mobilization) continue to show reduced short-term mortality and length of stay in registry and bundled-care program comparisons, reinforcing this as an increasingly non-negotiable standard-of-care element rather than an optional service line.
- Incisional negative pressure wound therapy in high-risk (obese, revision, poor soft-tissue envelope) arthroplasty wounds continues to accumulate supportive comparative data for reduced wound complication/reoperation rates, while routine use in low-risk primary cases remains unsupported — refining rather than broadening its indicated population.
- The cardiovascular-risk caveat around high-dose intravenous TXA identified in a large noncardiac surgery trial (elevated composite vascular event risk in a broad, higher-risk noncardiac surgical population) continues to be actively discussed in the orthopedic anesthesia literature as a reason for individualized dosing/route decisions in patients with significant pre-existing cardiovascular disease, even as the orthopedic-specific safety literature at large remains reassuring.
