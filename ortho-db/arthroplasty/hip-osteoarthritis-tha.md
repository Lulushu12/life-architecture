## Quick Reference

**Core diagnoses in this monograph:** primary hip osteoarthritis (coxarthrosis), inflammatory hip arthritis (RA, spondyloarthropathy, others as a THA-indication group), and total hip arthroplasty (THA) as the definitive surgical treatment pathway.

### Classification-to-management snapshot

| Classification | What it grades | Key management pivot |
|---|---|---|
| **Kellgren-Lawrence (KL)** | Radiographic OA severity, grades 0–4 | Grade ≥3 with concordant symptoms = surgical candidate pool; grade 0–2 = non-op ladder, question the pain source |
| **Tönnis** | Hip-specific radiographic OA severity, grades 0–3 | Tönnis 2–3 correlates best with THA benefit; Tönnis 0–1 in a young dysplastic hip favors joint preservation (PAO) over THA |
| **Dorr** | Proximal femoral canal morphology/bone quality, types A/B/C | Drives cemented vs uncemented stem choice and broach/press-fit strategy |
| **Paprosky (acetabular, reference)** | Acetabular bone loss for revision planning, types I–IIIB | Determines need for augments, cages, cup-cage constructs, or triflange/custom implants |

### Compact treatment ladder

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hip OA** | Education, weight loss (if BMI elevated), structured exercise/PT, activity modification, topical/oral NSAIDs | Assistive devices (cane contralateral hand), intra-articular corticosteroid injection (short-term relief only), duloxetine/other analgesic adjuncts | THA when radiographic OA (KL/Tönnis ≥2–3) + refractory pain/function loss despite non-op care |
| **Inflammatory hip arthritis** | DMARD/biologic optimization with rheumatology, NSAIDs, PT, joint protection | Intra-articular steroid for flare, consider synovectomy in select early inflammatory disease | THA once mechanical destruction present; time surgery around immunosuppression (see Complications) |
| **THA (as procedure)** | N/A — surgical endpoint of the above pathways | N/A | Approach/implant/bearing selection individualized (below) |

### Red flags — do not treat as routine OA

- Rapidly progressive joint space loss (>2 mm/year) or destructive changes out of proportion to symptoms → rule out **rapidly destructive hip OA**, osteonecrosis, or infection.
- Fever, elevated CRP/ESR disproportionate to OA, or effusion → septic arthritis until proven otherwise.
- Groin pain in a young patient (<40) with normal/mild radiographs → screen for **femoroacetabular impingement (FAI)** and dysplasia (lateral center-edge angle <20–25°) before labeling as OA.
- Night pain, weight loss, prior malignancy → rule out metastatic disease/primary bone tumor before arthroplasty.
- New neurologic deficit or bowel/bladder change with hip/back pain → spine pathology, not hip-spine syndrome.

### Key numbers

- **Lateral center-edge angle (LCEA):** <20° dysplastic, 20–25° borderline, 25–40° normal, >40° pincer/overcoverage.
- **Tönnis angle:** normal <10°, borderline 10–15°, dysplastic >15°.
- **Alpha angle (cam FAI):** >55–60° abnormal.
- **Leg-length discrepancy tolerance post-THA:** aim within 5–10 mm; >10 mm symptomatic in a meaningful minority.
- **Cup position target ("safe zone," now individualized):** classic Lewinnek 40°±10° inclination, 15°±10° anteversion — increasingly superseded by functional/spinopelvic-adjusted targets.
- **VTE chemoprophylaxis duration:** 10–14 days minimum per CHEST; extended prophylaxis to 28–35 days favored in higher-risk patients by several national guidelines.
- **Aspirin dosing per 2022 ICM consensus:** 81 mg twice daily as an accepted option for standard-risk patients.
- **1-year mortality after elective primary THA:** <0.5%; 90-day VTE (symptomatic) with modern prophylaxis: <1%.
- **10-year implant survivorship, modern THA:** >95% in registry data for well-fixed cementless and cemented constructs alike.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Hip osteoarthritis** is a whole-joint disease of the acetabulum-femoral head articulation involving progressive articular cartilage loss, subchondral bone remodeling (sclerosis, cyst formation, osteophyte formation), synovial inflammation, and capsular fibrosis. Primary (idiopathic) OA is increasingly understood to have a substantial mechanical/structural substrate: a large proportion of "primary" hip OA in younger and middle-aged patients is secondary to unrecognized **FAI** (cam and/or pincer morphology) or **developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)**, both of which alter joint contact mechanics and accelerate cartilage failure at the acetabular rim (cam) or across a reduced weight-bearing surface (dysplasia). True idiopathic OA without an identifiable structural driver is more common in older patients and is associated with generalized OA phenotypes, obesity, and genetic predisposition.

Pathophysiologic sequence: cartilage matrix degradation (proteoglycan loss, collagen network disruption) → loss of joint space → altered load transmission → subchondral bone sclerosis and cyst formation → osteophyte formation at the margins as a stabilizing/load-redistributing response → capsular contracture (classically an anterosuperior and adduction/internal-rotation contracture pattern) → mechanical pain, stiffness, and functional loss (difficulty with shoes/socks, stairs, prolonged sitting).

**Inflammatory hip arthritis** (rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis/axial spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and less commonly other seronegative or crystal arthropathies affecting the hip) follows a different primary pathway: synovial pannus formation drives cartilage and subchondral bone destruction from the inside out, often with more concentric/global joint space loss (rather than the superior or medial predominant pattern typical of mechanical OA), earlier and more severe protrusio acetabuli, and osteopenic bone quality from disease activity and chronic corticosteroid/DMARD exposure. This has direct arthroplasty implications: higher risk of intraoperative fracture and component subsidence with poor-quality bone, higher protrusio incidence requiring medial wall grafting or reinforcement rings/cages, and a periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) risk elevated by immunosuppression.

Both pathways converge at the same endpoint — a mechanically incompetent, painful joint — but the bone quality, deformity pattern, and infection risk profile differ enough to change surgical planning, which is why both diagnoses are addressed as a single arthroplasty-bound cluster here.

### Hip OA-specific structural drivers

- **Cam-type FAI:** an aspherical femoral head-neck junction (reduced offset, "pistol grip" deformity) that causes abnormal shear/impingement of the acetabular cartilage and labrum during flexion/internal rotation, producing a characteristic anterosuperior chondrolabral lesion pattern that, left untreated in a young patient, progresses to global OA over years to decades.
- **Pincer-type FAI:** acetabular overcoverage (global, as in coxa profunda/protrusio, or focal, as in acetabular retroversion) causing early rim contact and labral crush injury; often coexists with cam morphology (mixed-type FAI, the most common presentation).
- **DDH/acetabular dysplasia:** undercoverage (LCEA <20°) concentrates load onto a reduced acetabular surface area, producing anterosuperior-predominant cartilage and labral failure at a younger age than typical primary OA; a shallow, retroverted, or dysplastic acetabulum also complicates later THA cup positioning and may require an augment, high hip center, or structural bone graft.
- **Osteonecrosis of the femoral head:** a distinct pathway (vascular insult → subchondral collapse → secondary OA) rather than primary cartilage wear; relevant here because end-stage collapse (Ficat/Steinberg advanced stages) is managed with THA using the same implant-selection principles as OA, though typically in a younger patient population, shifting bearing and fixation choices toward those favoring longevity.

### Inflammatory arthritis-specific considerations

- **Rheumatoid arthritis:** symmetric, concentric joint space loss; protrusio acetabuli is common and often more severe than in OA, requiring medial wall bone grafting or a protrusio-specific cup/cage construct at THA; osteopenic bone from disease activity and chronic corticosteroid exposure increases intraoperative fracture risk with uncemented technique.
- **Axial spondyloarthritis/ankylosing spondylitis:** hip involvement occurs in a meaningful minority of patients and can be a dominant driver of disability; concurrent spinal ankylosis is highly relevant to THA planning because a fused, immobile spine removes the pelvis's ability to compensate for cup position across postures — this population overlaps directly with the "stiff spine" spinopelvic risk group discussed under Operative treatment. Heterotopic ossification risk is also elevated in this group specifically.
- **Psoriatic arthritis and other seronegative arthropathies:** variable joint destruction patterns; periarticular soft tissue and skin considerations (psoriatic plaques near planned incision sites) require preoperative dermatologic optimization and incision-site planning to reduce infection risk.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History:** groin-predominant pain (most specific for intra-articular hip pathology) that may radiate to anterior thigh or, less commonly, buttock/lateral hip (consider greater trochanteric pain syndrome or spine pathology when pain is purely lateral or posterior). Mechanical pattern — worse with weight-bearing/activity, improved with rest — supports OA; pain at rest or night pain disproportionate to activity should raise concern for inflammatory, infectious, or neoplastic causes. Ask specifically about morning stiffness duration (>60 minutes suggests inflammatory arthritis vs <30 minutes typical of OA), other joint involvement, psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, uveitis, and family history — screening for spondyloarthropathy.

**Examination:** antalgic or Trendelenburg gait; reduced internal rotation in flexion is the earliest and most sensitive sign of hip joint pathology; positive **FADIR** (flexion-adduction-internal rotation) impingement test suggests FAI-driven pathology; assess abductor strength (single-leg stance test), leg lengths, and lumbar spine range of motion and provocative tests (straight leg raise, facet loading) to exclude referred pain. Always examine the ipsilateral knee — hip pathology commonly refers pain to the knee, particularly in children (though less relevant in the adult OA population, this remains a useful screen).

| Test/sign | What it screens for | Positive finding |
|---|---|---|
| Passive internal rotation in 90° flexion | Intra-articular hip pathology in general | Reduced range and/or reproduction of groin pain compared to contralateral side |
| FADIR (flexion-adduction-internal rotation) | Anterosuperior labral/FAI-type impingement | Reproduction of sharp groin pain |
| FABER/Patrick test | Combined hip/SI joint/iliopsoas pathology screen (nonspecific) | Groin pain (hip) vs posterior/buttock pain (SI joint) |
| Trendelenburg/single-leg stance | Abductor (gluteus medius) weakness or insufficiency | Contralateral pelvic drop or compensatory lean |
| Log roll test | Isolates the hip joint capsule specifically (minimizes soft tissue confounding) | Pain or apprehension with passive internal/external rotation of the extended leg |
| Straight leg raise / lumbar provocative tests | Referred lumbar spine pathology | Radicular pain reproduction, normal hip rotation |

**Imaging:** Standing AP pelvis plus a frog-leg or Dunn lateral of the symptomatic hip is the baseline study. Assess joint space width (normal superior joint space ≥2–3 mm), sphericity of the femoral head, acetabular coverage (LCEA, Tönnis angle), and features of FAI (cam bump, crossover sign for pincer/retroversion). **MRI** is reserved for early/equivocal OA, suspected osteonecrosis, labral pathology in a younger patient being considered for joint preservation, or when infection/tumor is on the differential. **CT** is used for detailed 3D bone stock assessment in revision planning and for surgical navigation/robotic planning in select primary cases. Labs (CRP, ESR, and joint aspiration when indicated) are mandatory before proceeding to THA in any patient with an atypical presentation, prior hip surgery, or inflammatory arthritis to exclude active infection.

**Differential diagnosis to actively exclude before committing to an OA-driven treatment plan:**

| Consideration | Distinguishing feature | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (gluteal tendinopathy/bursitis) | Lateral hip pain, point tenderness over trochanter, normal internal rotation | Targeted exam, ultrasound if diagnosis uncertain; treat independently of any coexisting mild OA |
| Lumbar spine pathology (stenosis, radiculopathy, facet arthropathy) | Pain with lumbar extension, positive straight leg raise, normal hip internal rotation | Spine imaging/exam; diagnostic hip injection can help differentiate the pain generator |
| Osteonecrosis of the femoral head | Younger patient, risk factors (steroids, alcohol, sickle cell), preserved joint space with subchondral collapse ("crescent sign") on early imaging | MRI if plain films equivocal; stage with Ficat/Steinberg systems |
| Septic arthritis | Fever, acute severe pain, disproportionate CRP/ESR elevation, effusion | Urgent aspiration; do not proceed to elective THA planning until excluded |
| Occult femoral neck stress fracture or insufficiency fracture | Acute-on-chronic pain, osteoporotic patient, endurance athlete | MRI; distinct management pathway from OA |
| Metastatic disease/primary bone tumor | Night pain, weight loss, atypical lesion morphology, prior malignancy history | Staging workup before any arthroplasty planning |

## Classification

### Kellgren-Lawrence (KL) grade — general OA radiographic severity

| Grade | Radiographic findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | No radiographic features of OA | No OA-directed treatment; investigate other pain source |
| **1** | Doubtful joint space narrowing, possible osteophytic lipping | Reassurance, activity/lifestyle counseling; not a surgical candidate on imaging alone |
| **2** | Definite osteophytes, possible joint space narrowing | Begin structured non-operative ladder |
| **3** | Multiple osteophytes, definite joint space narrowing, some sclerosis, possible bone contour deformity | Non-op ladder continues; surgical candidacy assessed on symptoms/function, not radiographs alone |
| **4** | Large osteophytes, marked joint space narrowing, severe sclerosis, definite bone contour deformity | Strong radiographic support for THA if symptoms/function refractory to non-op care |

KL was designed for the knee originally but is widely applied to the hip in general OA literature; it correlates only moderately with symptoms, so **treatment decisions are driven by pain and function, not KL grade alone** (per AAOS and NICE guidance — see below).

### Tönnis grade — hip-specific OA severity (more clinically used by hip surgeons)

| Grade | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | No signs of OA | Joint preservation candidate if structural deformity (FAI/dysplasia) present and symptomatic |
| **1** | Increased sclerosis, slight joint space narrowing, slight osteophyte formation | Joint preservation surgery (PAO, hip arthroscopy) still reasonable in the right patient — cartilage largely preserved |
| **2** | Small cysts, moderate joint space narrowing, moderate loss of femoral head sphericity | Joint preservation outcomes decline; THA increasingly favored, especially age >40 |
| **3** | Large cysts, severe/obliterated joint space, severe femoral head deformity, avascular necrosis features | THA is the treatment of choice |

Tönnis grade ≥2 is a commonly cited relative contraindication to joint-preserving procedures such as periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) or hip arthroscopy for FAI/dysplasia — cartilage damage is too advanced for preservation surgery to reliably succeed.

### Dorr classification — proximal femoral bone/canal morphology

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Type A** | "Champagne flute" — thick cortices, narrow canal, funnel-shaped metaphysis-to-diaphysis transition | Excellent uncemented press-fit fixation candidate; risk of intraoperative fracture if under-reaming/oversized broaching is used given tight, dense bone |
| **Type B** | Intermediate — moderate cortical thinning, wider canal, less pronounced funnel shape | Uncemented fixation still generally reliable; most common morphology |
| **Type C** | "Stovepipe" — thin cortices, wide canal, minimal metaphyseal-diaphyseal mismatch, low canal-flare index | Higher risk of inadequate press-fit and early subsidence with standard uncemented tapered stems; favor cemented fixation, a fully coated/cylindrical uncemented stem, or a modular/monoblock design suited to poor bone — particularly relevant in elderly, osteoporotic, or chronic steroid-exposed (inflammatory arthritis) patients |

Dorr type is assessed on the AP pelvis/hip radiograph using the canal-flare index (canal width at the lesser trochanter and 10 cm distal, relative to the isthmus) and directly informs the cemented-vs-uncemented decision layered on top of the age-based framework discussed under Operative treatment.

### Paprosky classification — acetabular bone defects (revision reference)

| Type | Bone loss pattern | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Minimal bone loss, intact rim, hemispherical cavity preserved | Standard hemispherical uncemented cup with screws |
| **IIA** | Superior bone loss but superior rim intact/supportive | Hemispherical cup with superior bone grafting; standard screw fixation usually adequate |
| **IIB** | Superior-lateral bone loss with rim deficiency | Larger hemispherical cup ± superior augment; high hip center or jumbo cup techniques considered |
| **IIC** | Medial wall (protrusio-type) defect, columns intact | Medial bone grafting; hemispherical cup often still seatable against intact columns |
| **IIIA** | Severe bone loss, 10–60% host bone contact, superior rim migration but some ischial/teardrop support remains | Porous metal augments, trabecular metal cup-cage construct, or jumbo cup with graft; anterior/posterior column integrity generally preserved |
| **IIIB** | Severe bone loss, <40% host contact, often with pelvic discontinuity | Cup-cage constructs, custom triflange components, or acetabular distraction technique; pelvic discontinuity requires posterior column plating or a construct spanning the discontinuity |

Paprosky is included here as a **reference classification** because revision THA competence requires fluency in it even in a primary-arthroplasty-focused practice; every primary THA surgeon should be able to stage a failed cup on plain films before referring or planning revision, and porous titanium augments have become the workhorse solution for IIB–IIIA defects in contemporary practice.

### Using the classifications together in clinic

In everyday decision-making these four classifications are layered, not used in isolation. A practical sequence: (1) confirm radiographic OA severity and hip-specificity with **KL/Tönnis** to establish that the joint itself — not the spine or soft tissue — is the pain generator and that cartilage loss is advanced enough that a joint-preserving option (PAO, arthroscopy) is no longer realistic (Tönnis ≥2); (2) once THA is indicated, assess **Dorr type** on the templating radiograph to set the cemented-vs-uncemented femoral strategy; (3) reserve **Paprosky** staging for the revision setting, applied to the failed cup's bone loss pattern to select the reconstruction technique. A young patient with Tönnis 0–1 changes and clear cam/pincer morphology is a joint-preservation candidate; the same patient with Tönnis 2–3 changes is not, regardless of age, and should be counseled toward arthroplasty.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

The non-operative ladder is guideline-convergent across AAOS, NICE (NG226), and OARSI, though emphasis differs slightly (AAOS is hip-OA-specific with graded strength-of-recommendation; NICE NG226 covers OA broadly across joints; OARSI produces global core/adjunct recommendation tiers).

1. **Education and self-management** — universally the foundation recommendation across all three bodies; addressing expectations, activity pacing, and the natural history of OA.
2. **Structured exercise therapy** — strong recommendation across guidelines regardless of age, BMI, or disease severity; land-based and aquatic programs both supported; the single most consistently evidence-backed intervention in the entire non-op ladder.
3. **Weight management** — strongly recommended when BMI is elevated; even modest weight loss (5–10%) improves pain and function and reduces mechanical load across the joint.
4. **Oral NSAIDs** — first-line pharmacologic option in patients without contraindication; use lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary duration given GI/renal/cardiovascular risk, particularly relevant in the older OA population.
5. **Topical NSAIDs** — more evidence-based for knee than hip (limited penetration to a deep joint) but reasonable adjunct with a favorable systemic safety profile.
6. **Assistive devices** — cane in the contralateral hand reduces joint reactive force meaningfully and is a low-cost, evidence-supported intervention.
7. **Duloxetine** — conditionally recommended by AAOS as an adjunct for patients with an inadequate response to first-line therapy, particularly with a central sensitization component.
8. **Explicitly NOT recommended by NICE NG226**: glucosamine/chondroitin, and — notably — routine intra-articular hyaluronic acid for hip OA (evidence showed no meaningful benefit and a signal of potential harm specific to the hip). AAOS 2024 similarly does not support hyaluronic acid injection for hip OA as an evidence-based intervention.

**Inflammatory hip arthritis non-operative care** is directed primarily through rheumatology: DMARD/biologic disease control is the primary lever on joint-destructive progression, with NSAIDs and PT as adjuncts; the orthopedic surgeon's non-operative role is largely limited to activity modification counseling and monitoring for the point at which mechanical destruction outpaces medical control, at which point arthroplasty planning begins (including a specific perioperative immunosuppression-hold discussion — see Complications).

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Intra-articular corticosteroid injection**: reasonable for short-term symptomatic relief (weeks to a few months), typically image-guided (fluoroscopy or ultrasound) given the deep, difficult-to-palpate hip joint — unguided landmark-based hip injection is unreliable and not recommended. Useful diagnostically (a hip that responds well to an intra-articular injection confirms the hip, not the spine, as the pain generator) and useful to bridge a patient to a planned surgical date. Repeated frequent injections should be avoided, and an injection within roughly 3 months of a planned THA is a recognized (though debated in magnitude) periprosthetic infection risk factor that most arthroplasty surgeons use as a practical cutoff before proceeding to surgery.
- **Intra-articular hyaluronic acid**: **not recommended** per NICE NG226, with evidence of no clinically meaningful benefit and a possible harm signal specific to the hip joint; AAOS 2024 concurs in not supporting routine use. This is a genuine guideline-driven "do not do" moment worth stating explicitly to patients requesting it based on knee-OA marketing.
- **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and other orthobiologics**: insufficient high-quality hip-specific evidence to support routine use outside of a research or shared-decision-making context; not part of any major guideline's core recommendations for hip OA.
- **Prolotherapy, viscosupplementation combinations**: not evidence-supported for the hip; avoid.

### Operative

**Indication for THA:** radiographically confirmed hip joint destruction (Tönnis ≥2, generally KL ≥3) concordant with clinically significant pain and functional limitation that has failed an adequate trial of the non-operative ladder above. THA is not indicated on imaging alone, and is not withheld purely for age (very elderly, appropriately optimized patients benefit substantially) — the decision is symptom- and function-driven, shared with the patient.

**Preoperative optimization:** HbA1c <8% (ideally <7%) in diabetics, smoking cessation counseling, BMI optimization (many centers use BMI 40 as a relative threshold for additional risk discussion, not an absolute cutoff), anemia correction, dental clearance if indicated, and — critically for the inflammatory arthritis population — a documented immunosuppression hold plan coordinated with rheumatology (methotrexate is now generally continued through surgery per current consensus; most biologics are held for one dosing interval preoperatively; corticosteroids are continued with stress dosing as needed rather than abruptly stopped).

#### Surgical approach

| Approach | Advantages | Disadvantages / pitfalls | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Posterior** | Extensile, familiar, excellent exposure of acetabulum and femur, works for essentially any body habitus or deformity, easiest for revision conversion | Historically higher early dislocation risk if capsule/short external rotators not repaired; risk mitigated to near-anterior levels with meticulous posterior soft tissue repair and modern bearing/component positioning | Default workhorse approach for most surgeons; obese patients, complex deformity, revision |
| **Direct anterior (DAA)** | Intermuscular/internervous (no muscle detachment), lower early dislocation risk, faster early functional recovery and less early postoperative pain in several meta-analyses | Steep learning curve with a defined complication bump early in a surgeon's experience; longer operative time (~14 minutes longer per recent meta-analysis); higher risk of lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury/paresthesia and, in some series, greater intraoperative femoral fracture and wound complication risk, especially in obese or muscular patients; femoral exposure more technically demanding without a specialized table | Thinner-build patients, surgeons with dedicated fellowship training/high volume in the approach, patients prioritizing early recovery |
| **Direct lateral (Hardinge)** | Low dislocation risk (abductors preserved anteriorly to the greater trochanter but partially detached/split), reliable exposure | Abductor weakness/limp risk if repair fails or gluteus medius is inadequately reattached; violates abductor mechanism to some degree | Patients at very high dislocation risk where a low-dislocation approach is prioritized over gait mechanics concerns; some revision scenarios |

**Comparative evidence synthesis (2024–2025 meta-analyses):** across multiple recent systematic reviews and RCT meta-analyses, DAA shows modest early advantages (lower day-1 pain scores, shorter length of stay in some series) at the cost of longer operative time; a landmark RCT with 5-year follow-up found no difference in functional, radiographic, or survivorship outcomes between DAA and posterior approach — reinforcing that mid/long-term outcomes converge regardless of approach, and approach selection should be driven primarily by surgeon experience/volume and patient-specific anatomy rather than a presumption of DAA superiority.

**Technical pearls and pitfalls by approach:**
- *Posterior:* repair the posterior capsule and short external rotators (piriformis, obturator internus/externus, gemelli) back to the greater trochanter through drill holes or suture anchors — this single technical step is the strongest modifiable determinant of early dislocation risk with this approach; avoid excessive femoral component anteversion combined with cup anteversion that together exceed a combined anteversion target of roughly 25–50° ("combined anteversion" concept, Widmer/Ranawat).
- *Direct anterior:* use a dedicated traction table or standard table with careful hip hyperextension/external rotation for femoral exposure; watch for excessive medial retractor pressure on the femoral neuromuscular bundle; convert early to an extended approach or accept a lower stem insertion trajectory rather than forcing exposure, since forced femoral exposure is the principal driver of the approach's higher intraoperative femur fracture risk; counsel patients preoperatively about expected anterolateral thigh numbness from lateral femoral cutaneous nerve traction.
- *Direct lateral:* take care to reattach the anterior one-third to one-half of gluteus medius (and minimus) securely to the greater trochanter — inadequate repair or postoperative avulsion produces a persistent abductor lurch that is the approach's principal downside relative to intermuscular approaches.
- *General:* intraoperative fluoroscopy or navigation-assisted checks of cup inclination/anteversion and leg length/offset reduce outlier positioning regardless of approach chosen, and are increasingly used even by high-volume surgeons as a final safety check rather than a substitute for technique.

#### Implant fixation: cemented vs uncemented, by age and bone quality

- **Uncemented (press-fit) fixation** has become the dominant choice internationally for primary THA in patients with adequate bone stock (Dorr A/B), reflecting registry trends toward >80–90% uncemented femoral fixation in many countries (with Sweden a notable outlier retaining high cemented rates and correspondingly excellent registry-reported outcomes). Uncemented fixation avoids cement-related cardiopulmonary events (bone cement implantation syndrome) and is generally faster.
- **Cemented femoral fixation** retains a clear, registry-supported role in **older patients (typically >70–75 years) with osteoporotic or Dorr type C bone**: American Joint Replacement Registry data demonstrate a significantly reduced risk of periprosthetic femur fracture with cemented fixation in patients ≥65 years, a difference that widens with advancing age and diminishing bone quality. Most European arthroplasty units (particularly Nordic and UK practice) cement routinely in this population; this remains a more common default in much of Europe than in the US.
- **Practical framework:** young/active patient with good bone quality (Dorr A/B) → uncemented stem and cup, biologic fixation with decades-long survivorship expectation. Elderly patient (>75), osteoporotic or Dorr C bone, or femoral neck fracture indication → cemented stem strongly favored to minimize periprosthetic fracture risk; cup is essentially always uncemented (hemispherical press-fit) in contemporary primary practice regardless of femoral-side choice. Inflammatory arthritis with poor bone quality and/or corticosteroid-related osteopenia → apply the same low bone-quality logic as the elderly osteoporotic group, independent of chronologic age.
- Acetabular component fixation is now almost universally uncemented (porous-coated hemispherical cup with screw augmentation as needed) in primary THA; cemented cups are largely reserved for specific revision or reconstruction scenarios.

**Inflammatory arthritis-specific technical considerations at THA:** anticipate protrusio acetabuli and plan for medial wall bone grafting (femoral head autograft when available) or a protrusio ring/cage if the deficiency is severe; use a cemented femoral stem preferentially given the near-universal osteopenia in this population, and lower the threshold for a collared stem design to offload axial subsidence risk; coordinate perioperative immunosuppression management explicitly with rheumatology in advance (methotrexate generally continued; most biologics held one dosing cycle; corticosteroids continued with stress-dose coverage rather than withdrawn) since both under- and over-managing immunosuppression carries risk — flare/wound healing impairment on one side, infection on the other.

#### Bearing surface selection

- **Ceramic-on-highly-crosslinked-polyethylene (CoP)**: the current default bearing for most primary THA in both US and European practice — excellent wear characteristics, no ceramic-on-ceramic squeaking risk, no fracture risk of a ceramic liner against a ceramic head under edge loading, and comparable long-term survivorship to ceramic-on-ceramic in recent meta-analyses (~96–99% at 8–10 years for both).
- **Ceramic-on-ceramic (CoC)**: excellent wear resistance and osteolysis avoidance, historically favored in younger/high-demand patients; modern series confirm comparable survivorship to CoP, though CoC carries residual risks of squeaking (patient-perceived, uncommon but bothersome) and rare ceramic component fracture — a risk that must be disclosed even though absolute rates are low with modern ceramics.
- **Metal-on-highly-crosslinked-polyethylene**: acceptable, cost-effective option, particularly in older/lower-demand patients, though wear rates are measurably higher than ceramic-headed bearings against the same polyethylene; metal ion release is a consideration, though far less relevant than with legacy metal-on-metal bearings.
- **Metal-on-metal**: essentially abandoned for primary THA given adverse local tissue reaction (ALTR/pseudotumor) risk and systemic metal ion concerns; retained only as a legacy/revision discussion point.
- **Head size**: larger femoral heads (36 mm and above where anatomy allows) increase the jump distance before dislocation and are now routine with modern crosslinked polyethylene liners that tolerate the increased contact area without excessive wear penalty.

#### Dual mobility

Dual mobility (DM) constructs — a mobile polyethylene liner articulating with a fixed metal shell around a standard femoral head — increase the effective head size and functional jump distance, substantially reducing dislocation risk. Indications have expanded well beyond the original revision-for-instability niche into **primary THA** for defined high-risk groups: patients with abductor deficiency, neuromuscular disease (e.g., Parkinson's disease, prior stroke, cognitive impairment affecting hip precautions compliance), femoral neck fracture as the surgical indication, and — increasingly — patients with adverse **spinopelvic mobility** profiles (below). Recent systematic reviews confirm low dislocation rates and robust short-to-mid-term survivorship for primary DM use in these populations, though a small, debated risk of intraprosthetic dislocation (liner disengaging from the femoral head) and increased metal-on-metal-style wear at the backside/metal shell interface are recognized long-term considerations, particularly relevant to counsel younger patients receiving DM constructs.

#### Spinopelvic considerations

A hip does not exist in isolation from the spine: pelvic tilt changes with position (standing vs sitting) via the hip-spine relationship, and a stiff or fused spine (prior fusion, severe degenerative stiffness, ankylosing spondylitis) removes the pelvis's ability to compensate, transmitting posture change directly into altered effective cup anteversion and increasing impingement/dislocation risk at the extremes of motion. The **Hip-Spine Classification** (validated multicenter series, Otto Aufranc Award-winning framework) stratifies patients by two axes: spinal deformity (normal alignment vs flatback/sagittal imbalance, PI-LL mismatch >10°) and spinal mobility (normal, >10° change in sacral slope between standing and sitting, vs stiff, <10° change). This yields four groups, with **group 2B (spinal deformity plus a stiff spine)** carrying the highest dislocation risk and warranting the most deliberate mitigation strategy.

Practical implications:
- Preoperative standing and sitting lateral radiographs (or dedicated spinopelvic imaging protocols) are increasingly incorporated for patients with known spine pathology, prior spinal fusion, or clinical stiffness on exam before primary THA.
- High-risk (stiff spine, especially with deformity) patients benefit from adjusted cup anteversion targets (typically increased anteversion to compensate for reduced posterior pelvic tilt in the seated position), larger femoral heads, and a lower threshold for **dual mobility** bearings.
- Recent cohort data demonstrate a measurable reduction in dislocation rates when high-risk (Hip-Spine 2B) patients are proactively managed with these mitigation strategies compared to historical standard-technique controls, supporting spinopelvic screening as a now-mainstream, not fringe, part of THA planning at fellowship-trained centers.
- Functional/dynamic cup positioning concepts have progressively supplanted the rigid Lewinnek "safe zone" (40°±10° inclination / 15°±10° anteversion) as the sole planning target, particularly in patients with abnormal spinopelvic mobility.

#### Robotic and navigation-assisted THA

Recent multicenter RCTs (2024–2025) consistently show robotic-assisted THA improves radiographic accuracy — better restoration of center of rotation, offset, and more precise cup inclination/anteversion relative to plan — with lower variance than manual technique. However, these same trials show **no significant difference in patient-reported functional outcomes** (Harris Hip Score, leg length correction) at short-to-mid-term follow-up, and operative time is meaningfully longer (roughly 30–35 minutes in some series). Practical takeaway: robotic assistance is a reasonable tool for improving positioning precision and is attractive in complex deformity or high-risk-instability cases where precise component targeting matters most, but it is not yet evidence-justified as a routine requirement for standard-risk primary THA, and the technology/time cost should be weighed accordingly.

#### VTE prophylaxis

International consensus remains fragmented, but the practical synthesis: **CHEST guidelines** recommend at least 10–14 days of prophylaxis with one of aspirin, adjusted-dose vitamin K antagonist, apixaban, dabigatran, fondaparinux, LMWH, low-dose unfractionated heparin, rivaroxaban, or mechanical compression. The **2022 International Consensus Meeting (ICM)** on VTE — still the most current unified position going into 2026 — formally endorsed low-dose aspirin (81 mg twice daily) over LMWH as the default for standard-risk patients following contemporary fast-track THA, a meaningful shift from historical LMWH-first practice, while reserving stronger anticoagulation (LMWH, DOACs) for patients with elevated personal VTE risk (prior VTE, active malignancy, hypercoagulable state, prolonged immobility). European fast-track THA/TKA protocols similarly favor aspirin over no prophylaxis but stop short of a blanket recommendation for or against aspirin in higher-risk procedures. Mechanical prophylaxis (intermittent pneumatic compression) is near-universally added regardless of pharmacologic choice. **Bottom line for practice:** risk-stratify; use aspirin 81 mg BID as a reasonable default for standard-risk elective primary THA per ICM 2022, escalate to LMWH/DOAC for elevated personal VTE risk, and continue prophylaxis for a minimum of 10–14 days with extension toward 28–35 days considered in higher-risk patients per several national frameworks (including NICE VTE guidance).

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Phase 1 — Acute postoperative (Day 0–2, inpatient or same-day discharge):**
- Weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) is standard for cementless and cemented primary THA through all approaches; no formal restriction unless intraoperative fracture, cortical perforation, or a specific fixation concern dictates protected weight-bearing.
- Early mobilization same day or postoperative day 1 is now standard-of-care within enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways — walking with assistive device, transfers, basic hip precautions education (posterior approach: avoid combined flexion/adduction/internal rotation in the early weeks; anterior/lateral approach precautions are typically less restrictive given the preserved posterior capsule/short external rotators).
- Multimodal analgesia (regional block/periarticular injection, acetaminophen, NSAID if not contraindicated, minimized opioid use) is standard and directly enables same-day or next-day discharge in appropriately selected patients.
- Most contemporary ERAS pathways target discharge on postoperative day 0–2.

**Phase 2 — Early rehabilitation (Weeks 1–6):**
- Outpatient or home-based PT focused on gait normalization (weaning assistive device as tolerated, typically cane by 2–4 weeks), active-assisted then active hip range of motion, and progressive isometric-to-isotonic strengthening of abductors, extensors, and core stabilizers.
- Typical PT session content in this phase: supine/side-lying hip abduction and extension isometrics progressing to gravity-resisted sets, gluteal activation drills, ankle pumps/calf activation for VTE mitigation, seated-to-standing transfer mechanics, and stair training (step-to pattern initially, reciprocal pattern as strength allows).
- Precautions (if posterior approach without robust capsular repair, or per surgeon protocol) are typically maintained through 6 weeks, after which most surgeons liberalize.
- Driving typically resumes at 2–4 weeks (left THA sooner if automatic transmission and adequate reaction time/strength; right THA and manual transmission later) per surgeon-specific criteria and local regulation.
- Wound check and radiograph typically at 2 and 6 weeks.

**Phase 3 — Progressive strengthening and functional return (Weeks 6–12):**
- Discontinue formal hip precautions (surgeon-dependent, typically 6–12 weeks) once soft tissue healing is adequate.
- Progress to resistance training, stationary cycling, pool therapy, and balance/proprioception work.
- **Return to sedentary/light-duty work:** commonly 2–6 weeks. **Return to physically demanding work** (heavy lifting, prolonged standing/walking, ladder work): typically 3–6 months, individualized to job demands and healing.
- **Return to low-impact sport** (golf, swimming, cycling, doubles tennis): generally 6–12 weeks once strength and confidence allow.
- **Return to higher-impact recreational activity** (singles tennis, hiking, skiing): typically 3–6 months, contingent on strength symmetry and surgeon clearance; high-impact/pivoting sport (running, contact sport) is generally discouraged long-term given theoretical wear and loosening concerns, though this is an individualized shared-decision discussion rather than an absolute prohibition in a well-fixed modern THA.

**Return-to-activity summary table:**

| Activity | Typical timeline | Clearance criteria |
|---|---|---|
| Sedentary/desk work | 2–4 weeks | Adequate pain control, safe transfers, sitting tolerance |
| Light-duty work (some walking/standing) | 4–6 weeks | Independent ambulation without assistive device or with cane only |
| Driving | 2–4 weeks | Off opioids, adequate reaction time and hip flexibility for pedal control, surgeon/local-regulation clearance |
| Heavy manual labor | 3–6 months | Strength within ~80–90% of contralateral side, job-specific functional testing if available |
| Swimming, stationary cycling, golf | 6–12 weeks | Wound fully healed, comfortable range of motion, surgeon clearance |
| Doubles tennis, hiking, downhill skiing | 3–6 months | Strength/balance symmetry, surgeon clearance |
| Running, singles tennis, contact/pivoting sport | Individualized; generally discouraged long-term | Shared decision-making; counsel on theoretical wear/loosening trade-off |

**Phase 4 — Long-term maintenance (3+ months onward):**
- Maintenance strengthening program indefinitely; annual or biennial clinical/radiographic surveillance is reasonable, more frequent if symptomatic or high-risk bearing/fixation construct.
- Counsel on antibiotic prophylaxis for dental/invasive procedures per current (more restrictive than historical) AAOS/ADA joint guidance — routine prophylaxis is no longer recommended for most low-risk dental procedures in patients without specific immunocompromise or prior PJI history; individualize with the treating dentist/physician.

**Rehabilitation notes specific to inflammatory arthritis patients:** coordinate the rehabilitation timeline with rheumatology, since disease flares can transiently limit PT participation; polyarticular involvement (contralateral hip, knees, shoulders) may slow the expected functional milestones above and requires individualized goal-setting rather than applying the standard-OA timeline unmodified; osteopenic bone quality reinforces the importance of adhering to any surgeon-specific loading precautions in the first 6 weeks even when WBAT is otherwise permitted.

## Complications

- **Dislocation:** historically the most common early complication, particularly with posterior approach and inadequate soft tissue repair; contemporary rates with modern technique, larger heads, and appropriate bearing/approach selection are well under 1–2% in most series. Risk factors: posterior approach without capsular repair, abductor insufficiency, cognitive impairment/non-adherence to precautions, adverse spinopelvic mobility (see above), prior lumbar fusion, and malpositioned components.
- **Periprosthetic fracture:** intraoperative (more common with uncemented press-fit stems, particularly in dense Dorr A bone or osteopenic Dorr C bone under-prepared) and postoperative (fall-related, more common with uncemented fixation in elderly/osteoporotic patients — a key driver of the cemented-fixation recommendation in this population).
- **Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI):** the most feared complication; risk elevated by inflammatory arthritis/immunosuppression, diabetes, obesity, smoking, prior hip surgery, and recent intra-articular injection close to the surgical date. Management follows a two-stage (or, in appropriately selected cases, one-stage) revision paradigm with organism-directed antibiotic therapy; a full PJI treatment algorithm is outside this monograph's scope and is addressed separately.
- **Venous thromboembolism:** addressed above under Operative treatment; symptomatic rates with modern multimodal prophylaxis and early mobilization are low (well under 1–2%).
- **Aseptic loosening / osteolysis:** long-term failure mode, substantially reduced with modern highly-crosslinked polyethylene and ceramic bearings compared to legacy conventional polyethylene; remains the leading indication for late revision when it does occur.
- **Leg length discrepancy:** common source of patient dissatisfaction even when technically within acceptable limits; meticulous preoperative templating and intraoperative length/offset checks are the primary mitigation.
- **Nerve injury:** sciatic nerve (posterior approach, more common with lengthening or retraction), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (direct anterior approach — numbness/dysesthesia over the anterolateral thigh, usually sensory-only and often improves over time but can be persistent), femoral nerve (anterior-based approaches, less common).
- **Heterotopic ossification:** more common with direct lateral and posterior approaches involving greater soft tissue trauma; risk factors include ankylosing spondylitis (relevant to the inflammatory arthritis population specifically), male sex, and prior heterotopic ossification; NSAID or selective radiotherapy prophylaxis considered in high-risk patients.
- **Limb length discrepancy and offset restoration failure:** beyond patient dissatisfaction, under-restored offset increases joint reactive force on the abductors (contributing to persistent limp) and under-restored length can predispose to instability by reducing soft tissue tension; templating with magnification-calibrated software or digital planning is standard of care.
- **Wound complications/prolonged drainage:** more common in obese patients, those on chronic corticosteroids (inflammatory arthritis population again disproportionately represented), and with direct anterior exposure in higher-BMI patients where the incision sits in a skin fold; persistent drainage beyond 5–7 days warrants a low threshold for surgical evaluation given its association with subsequent PJI.
- **Squeaking and ceramic component fracture:** specific to ceramic-on-ceramic bearings; squeaking is patient-bothersome but rarely clinically significant, while fracture, though rare with modern fourth-generation ceramics, requires immediate revision and mandates that surgeons counsel patients accordingly before choosing this bearing.
- **Adverse local tissue reaction (ALTR)/pseudotumor:** essentially a legacy metal-on-metal and, less commonly, modular femoral neck/head-taper corrosion ("trunnionosis") issue; relevant in a revision or long-term surveillance context for older implants but not a concern with contemporary ceramic or crosslinked polyethylene primary bearings.

**Longitudinal surveillance:** a reasonable practical schedule is clinical/radiographic review at 6 weeks, 1 year, and then every 2–5 years thereafter (more frequent — e.g., annually — for legacy metal-on-metal bearings, known problematic implant designs under recall/registry surveillance, or symptomatic patients at any point). New-onset pain, especially after a prior asymptomatic interval, groin or thigh pain with weight-bearing, or a new limp should prompt earlier re-evaluation for loosening, infection, or bearing-specific failure rather than being deferred to the next scheduled visit.

## Recent Evidence

- **[AAOS updates hip OA Clinical Practice Guideline (January 2024)](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-updates-clinical-practice-guideline-for-the-management-of-osteoarthritis-of-the-hip/)** — updated 14 of 23 non-operative recommendations (3 strong, 5 moderate), reaffirming exercise, weight management, and NSAIDs while explicitly not supporting hyaluronic acid injection; also newly addresses diabetes, BMI, and opioid-prescribing considerations around hip OA/THA care, giving practicing surgeons an updated, graded framework to counsel patients before offering surgery. See also the [PubMed CPG summary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38781351/).
- **[NICE NG226, "Osteoarthritis in over 16s: diagnosis and management"](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226/chapter/rationale-and-impact)** — the 2022 update (still current through 2026) that explicitly recommends **against** intra-articular hyaluronan for OA, citing no benefit and a potential harm signal specific to the hip; this remains the operative UK/European reference position against routine viscosupplementation.
- **[2025 systematic review/network meta-analysis of intra-articular treatments for knee and hip OA](https://www.esceo.org/sites/esceo/files/pdf/Expert%20consensus%20on%20hyaluronic%20acid%20injections%20for%20knee%20osteoarthritis_Fuggle%20et%20al.pdf)**-adjacent evidence base — pooling over 40 high-quality trials, found no intra-articular intervention produced a clinically meaningful pain/function improvement over placebo at 3–6 months, further reinforcing the NICE position against routine injections for hip OA specifically.
- **[Comparative efficacy of direct anterior vs. conventional approaches in THA — systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs through May 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13018-025-06227-8)** (also on [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12465139/)) — confirms DAA carries a longer operative time (mean +14.5 minutes) but lower day-1 pain scores; functional/PROM differences remain inconsistent across studies, supporting an individualized rather than uniformly DAA-preferred approach choice.
- **[Direct anterior vs posterior approach at 5-year follow-up — RCT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12631471/)** (enhanced-recovery-focused meta-analysis) — no significant difference in functional, radiographic, or survivorship outcomes at mid-term, the key evidence that approach choice should track surgeon expertise/patient anatomy rather than presumed long-term DAA superiority.
- **[Dual mobility indications for primary THA — Journal of Arthroplasty](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(24)01162-8/abstract)** and **[systematic review of DM for neurological-disorder patients (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12397136/)** — consolidate the expanding primary-THA indication set for dual mobility (neuromuscular disease, abductor deficiency, spinopelvic risk) beyond its original revision-only role, with low dislocation and strong short/mid-term survivorship.
- **[Decreased instability in high-risk Hip-Spine 2B patients with proactive mitigation — Journal of Arthroplasty (2024)](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(24)00366-8/pdf)** — cohort evidence that deliberately adjusting technique (anteversion targets, head size, dual mobility) for the highest-risk spinopelvic group meaningfully lowers dislocation compared to standard technique, cementing spinopelvic screening as mainstream preoperative practice.
- **[Cemented femoral fixation reduces periprosthetic fracture risk in patients ≥65 — American Joint Replacement Registry analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37105331/)** — the key registry evidence anchoring the age/bone-quality-based cemented-vs-uncemented framework used in this monograph.
- **[Femoral fixation for primary THA — international registry perspective (2024–2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12240149/)** — confirms wide international variation in cemented fixation rates (from single digits in the US to historically near-universal in Sweden) with excellent outcomes across strategies when matched appropriately to bone quality and age, reinforcing that fixation choice is a bone-quality decision, not a dogmatic one.
- **[Ceramic-on-ceramic vs metal-on-crosslinked polyethylene — long-term survivorship comparison (2024)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39430648/)** — 8–10-year survivorship of ~96–99% for both bearing combinations, supporting either as a reasonable modern default and de-emphasizing bearing choice as a major survivorship differentiator when both use contemporary materials.
- **[Comparative postoperative prognosis of CoC vs ceramic-on-polyethylene — updated meta-analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11438439/)** — no significant difference in dislocation, VTE, infection, osteolysis, or revision rates between the two bearing combinations, with CoC showing a modest Harris Hip Score edge; useful for framing bearing choice as a shared-decision, low-differential-risk conversation with patients.
- **[Robot-assisted vs conventional THA — multicenter open-label RCT (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40701283/)** and companion **[radiographic RCT](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11208392/)** — robotic assistance improves component-position accuracy and reduces variance but does not yet demonstrate superior clinical/functional outcomes, while adding ~30–35 minutes of operative time — the evidence base practicing surgeons should cite when counseling patients on robotic THA marketing claims.
- **[Persistent lack of international consensus on VTE prophylaxis after THA (2025)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40543826/)** — confirms continued heterogeneity across CHEST, ICM, NICE, and national society guidance in 2025, underscoring that individualized risk stratification (rather than a single universal protocol) remains the defensible practice standard going into 2026.
- **[Emerging techniques for massive acetabular bone loss in revision hip arthroplasty — AAOS Now (2025)](https://www.aaos.org/aaosnow/2025/june/clinical/clinical01/)** — summarizes the growing role of porous titanium augments and modular reconstruction for Paprosky IIB–IIIB defects, the current state-of-the-art extension of the classic Paprosky-driven revision framework.
