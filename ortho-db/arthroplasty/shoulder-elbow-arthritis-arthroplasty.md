## Quick Reference

**Diagnosis-specific treatment ladders**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glenohumeral OA (cuff intact) | Activity mod., NSAIDs, PT (ROM/deltoid-periscapular) | Corticosteroid IA injection (limit ≤2–3/year), hyaluronic acid (weak evidence) | aTSA (gold standard if concentric/mild retroversion, correctable B2/B3); rTSA if severe uncorrectable retroversion, subscapularis insufficiency, or elderly/lower-demand |
| Rotator cuff tear arthropathy (Hamada 3-5) | Analgesia, subacromial injection trial, activity modification | Not durable — arthropathy is progressive, injections buy time only | rTSA is the definitive procedure; hemiarthroplasty essentially abandoned for this indication |
| Frozen shoulder | Reassurance, home stretching, NSAIDs, structured PT | Intra-articular corticosteroid ± hydrodilatation (best in freezing/early stage) | MUA or arthroscopic capsular release if refractory after 3–6 months of non-op; UK FROST shows no clinically significant 12-month difference between MUA and ACR |
| Elbow OA | Activity mod., NSAIDs, terminal-arc stretching PT | IA corticosteroid injection (pain relief, not mechanical) | Arthroscopic/open debridement (OK procedure) if joint space preserved and active/young; TEA in low-demand elderly; interposition in young high-demand with advanced destruction |

**Walch glenoid classification (primary glenohumeral OA)**

| Type | Description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Minor central erosion, concentric | Standard aTSA, no augment |
| A2 | Major central erosion, concentric, head centered | aTSA; consider eccentric reaming or graft if deep central defect |
| B1 | Posterior joint-space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, no frank erosion | aTSA feasible; correct version by selective anterior/posterior reaming |
| B2 | Posterior glenoid erosion with biconcavity ("B-spot"), posterior humeral subluxation | Reaming (if <15° correctable) + standard aTSA, posterior augmented component, or rTSA if severe |
| B3 | Monoconcave posterior erosion, >15° retroversion or >70% posterior humeral head subluxation | Posterior augmented aTSA or rTSA (favored with subscapularis deficiency/older age) |
| C | Dysplastic glenoid, retroversion >25° regardless of wear (developmental, not erosive) | Careful version correction — often younger patients, avoid overcorrection; aTSA feasible if bone stock allows |
| D | Glenoid anteversion or anterior erosion | Anteverted reaming pattern; often prior instability-surgery shoulders |

**Hamada classification (rotator cuff tear arthropathy)**

| Grade | Radiographic finding | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Massive cuff tear, acromiohumeral distance (AHD) preserved (>6 mm) | Cuff repair, SCR, or tendon transfer possible; rTSA not required |
| 2 | AHD ≤5 mm (narrowed) | Borderline; repair/SCR vs. rTSA based on function, pain, fatty infiltration |
| 3 | Acetabularization of the acromion (concave undersurface) | Superior migration established; rTSA favored if symptomatic |
| 4A | Glenohumeral joint narrowing plus acetabularization | rTSA indicated |
| 4B | Same as 4A plus femoralization of the humeral head | rTSA indicated |
| 5 | Collapse of the humeral head | rTSA indicated; technically more demanding, plan for bone loss |

**Favard classification (glenoid erosion pattern in cuff-tear arthropathy)**

| Type | Erosion pattern | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| E0 | Concentric, no glenoid wear | Standard baseplate positioning |
| E1 | Concentric wear (centered erosion) | Central deepening reaming; standard baseplate |
| E2 | Superior glenoid erosion | Inferior tilt of baseplate to avoid notching; consider inferior augment |
| E3 | Severe superior erosion, compromised peg/screw purchase | Superior wedge augment or bone graft; longer central peg for fixation |
| E4 | Global/biconcave pattern (posterior + superior deficiency) | Combined augmentation strategy; assess both planes of deficiency |

**Samilson-Prieto classification (secondary/post-instability glenohumeral OA)**

| Grade | Inferior humeral head osteophyte | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 1–3 mm | Non-operative; activity modification, PT, monitor |
| Moderate | 3–7 mm, mild joint-space narrowing | Consider arthroscopic debridement/capsular release if young, stiffness-predominant; injections |
| Severe | >7 mm, marked joint-space narrowing/obliteration | Arthroplasty per glenoid version and cuff status; remove retained instability hardware |

**Red flags**
- Acute atraumatic shoulder pain with fever/effusion — aspirate before any injection (septic arthritis until excluded).
- Sudden loss of active elevation after minor trauma in the elderly — acute-on-chronic massive cuff tear / pseudoparalysis.
- Rapidly progressive joint destruction — exclude CPPD/Milwaukee shoulder, neuropathic (Charcot) joint, infection.
- Acute post-arthroplasty pain with instability — dislocation or infection until proven otherwise.
- Elbow OA with new ulnar nerve symptoms — osteophyte/synovitis compressing the nerve; assess before manipulation or aggressive stretching.

**Key numbers**
- Normal glenoid retroversion ≈0–10°; >15° retroversion or >70% posterior humeral subluxation defines high-risk B3-type correction.
- Critical shoulder angle (CSA) >35° associates with cuff tear/cuff-repair failure risk; CSA <30° associates with OA.
- Frozen shoulder natural history: 12–42 months untreated; up to 40% incomplete recovery, worse in diabetics.
- UK FROST: no clinically important 12-month difference between MUA and arthroscopic capsular release; early-PT arm had highest crossover rate to further treatment; MUA most cost-effective.
- TEA: target flexion contracture <30°, functional arc 30–130°; lifelong lifting restriction ~2.3–4.5 kg (5–10 lb).
- Elbow OA debridement candidacy: generally preserved joint space, impingement/osteophyte-predominant symptoms rather than diffuse chondral loss.
- Interposition arthroplasty in young patients: complication rate ≈38%, reoperation rate ≈24% (higher in post-traumatic OA than inflammatory arthritis).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Glenohumeral joint mechanics.** A ball-and-socket articulation with minimal inherent bony constraint — the glenoid covers roughly one-third of the humeral head. Stability depends on the labrum, capsuloligamentous complex, and dynamic concavity-compression from the rotator cuff balanced against deltoid pull. Loss of this balance (cuff deficiency, capsular contracture, bone loss) drives each of the pathologies in this cluster.

**Glenohumeral osteoarthritis.**
- Usually primary/idiopathic, degenerative.
- Posterior glenoid wear is the dominant pattern: posterior humeral head subluxation drives asymmetric posterior erosion (the Walch B-type spectrum).
- Secondary causes: prior instability surgery (Samilson-Prieto pattern), trauma, inflammatory arthritis, avascular necrosis, chondrolysis (historically post-thermal-capsulorrhaphy or intra-articular pain-pump related).

**Rotator cuff tear arthropathy (Neer's cuff-tear arthropathy).**
- Results from chronic massive, irreparable cuff deficiency (typically supraspinatus + infraspinatus, ± subscapularis).
- Loss of the transverse and coronal force couples lets the unopposed deltoid translate the humeral head superiorly.
- Chronic superior migration produces subacromial/coracoacromial arch impingement, "acetabularization" of the acromion undersurface, and "femoralization" of the rounded-off humeral head.
- Advanced cases lose the coracoacromial arch entirely, producing anterosuperior escape.
- Fatty infiltration of infraspinatus/teres minor (Goutallier/Fuchs grade ≥3) predicts failure of cuff repair and predicts poor active external rotation even after rTSA (positive external rotation lag sign).

**Reverse shoulder arthroplasty biomechanics.**
- Medializing and distalizing the center of rotation onto the glenoid surface increases the deltoid's effective lever arm and pre-tensions its fibers, letting the deltoid substitute for absent rotator cuff elevator function.
- Lateralization — at the glenoid (BIO-RSA, lateralized baseplate) or the humerus (onlay humeral component) — reduces scapular notching, restores an external-rotation lever arm when teres minor is intact, and reduces acromial stress-fracture risk relative to purely medialized (classic Grammont) designs, at the cost of increased shear/torque across the baseplate-bone interface.

**Elbow osteoarthritis.**
- Typically primary in laborers and athletes (throwers, weightlifters) or post-traumatic.
- Pathology centers on osteophyte formation at the coronoid/olecranon tips and the coronoid/olecranon fossae, producing mechanical impingement in terminal flexion/extension well before articular cartilage is significantly lost — this is why debridement-type procedures work even with preserved joint space.
- Ulnar neuropathy coexists in up to 20–40% of cases from osteophyte and synovitic compression at the cubital tunnel.

**Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis).**
- An inflammatory-fibrotic contracture of the glenohumeral capsule, most pronounced at the rotator interval and coracohumeral ligament.
- Progresses from synovial inflammation to capsular fibrosis (myofibroblast proliferation, type III collagen deposition) — histologically analogous to Dupuytren's disease.
- Strongly associated with diabetes mellitus (2–4x risk, more severe and treatment-resistant course), thyroid disease, and prior immobilization (post-surgical, post-fracture).
- Classic three-phase natural history: freezing (painful, 2–9 months) → frozen (stiff, 4–12 months) → thawing (resolving, 5–24 months); idiopathic disease is largely self-limited, but recovery is incomplete in up to 40% of patients, especially diabetics.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History.** Pain pattern (mechanical/activity-related in OA vs. night and rest pain in advanced arthropathy or inflammatory disease); functional loss (overhead reach, ability to reach the back pocket — a subscapularis/internal-rotation surrogate); prior surgery (instability repair or cuff repair alters version and bone stock); comorbidities (diabetes raises frozen-shoulder risk and periprosthetic infection/wound-healing risk); hand dominance; functional demand and activity level (the single most important variable in the elbow arthroplasty-vs-interposition decision).

**Physical examination.**
- Active vs. passive ROM differential: loss of both suggests a capsular/arthritic process; frozen shoulder classically shows passive ROM loss — especially external rotation at the side — out of proportion to imaging.
- Cuff-specific strength testing: belly-press and lift-off tests for subscapularis; external rotation lag sign for infraspinatus/teres minor (predicts poor postoperative external rotation after rTSA).
- Crepitus and provocative impingement signs.
- Stability testing (apprehension/relocation) when secondary post-instability OA is suspected.
- Pseudoparalysis (inability to actively elevate >90° despite preserved passive ROM) vs. pseudoparesis in cuff-tear arthropathy — distinguishes a mechanically-deficient shoulder from a painful but mechanically-intact one.
- Elbow: terminal-arc pain/block, valgus/varus stability, provocative ulnar nerve testing (Tinel's, elbow flexion test) given the high coexistence of cubital tunnel symptoms.

**Imaging.**
- Plain radiographs — true AP/Grashey, axillary lateral (mandatory for posterior subluxation/version assessment), scapular Y — as first-line for all shoulder diagnoses.
- CT with 3D reconstruction and glenoid vault modeling (planning software: Blueprint, ExactechGPS, VIP, TrueSense) is essential preoperatively for all arthroplasty candidates, particularly B2/B3 glenoids or revision cases, for accurate version/inclination measurement and implant sizing.
- MRI to assess rotator cuff integrity, tendon retraction, and Goutallier/Fuchs fatty-infiltration grading when cuff status is clinically uncertain or when planning cuff-preserving alternatives (SCR, balloon spacer).
- Elbow OA: AP/lateral (± oblique) radiographs usually suffice; CT for loose-body mapping and pre-op planning of debridement/OK procedure; nerve conduction studies when ulnar neuropathy is suspected clinically.

**Imaging findings by diagnosis**

| Diagnosis | Plain radiograph findings | CT/MRI role |
|---|---|---|
| Glenohumeral OA | Joint-space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis/cysts, inferior humeral osteophyte, posterior glenoid wear on axillary view | CT for glenoid version/vault modeling in every arthroplasty candidate |
| Cuff-tear arthropathy | Reduced acromiohumeral distance, superior humeral migration, acetabularization of acromion | MRI for cuff tendon quality/fatty infiltration if cuff-preserving options considered |
| Frozen shoulder | Usually normal (diagnosis is clinical) | MRI only to exclude other pathology if atypical presentation; not routinely needed |
| Elbow OA | Osteophytes at coronoid/olecranon tips and fossae, loose bodies, relatively preserved joint space | CT for loose-body mapping and debridement/OK-procedure planning |

## Classification

**Walch classification of primary glenohumeral OA glenoid morphology** (with the Bercik/Walch-group modification adding types C and D on 3D CT) — full type-by-type detail is tabulated in Quick Reference (A1, A2, B1, B2, B3, C, D). Clinical utility: predicts the direction and magnitude of version correction needed and stratifies the choice between simple reaming, augmented components (posterior wedge/step augments), bone grafting (BIO-RSA), and aTSA vs. rTSA. B2/B3 and C glenoids carry the highest rates of aTSA glenoid-component loosening when version is not adequately addressed at index surgery.

**Hamada classification of rotator cuff tear arthropathy** — grades 1 through 5 (grade 4 subdivided A/B), based on acromiohumeral distance and secondary degenerative change (Quick Reference table). Predicts progression to frank arthropathy and marks the threshold for converting from cuff-preserving strategies (repair, superior capsular reconstruction, subacromial balloon spacer, tendon transfer) to rTSA — Hamada ≥3 is the practical cutoff favoring rTSA when symptomatic.

**Favard classification of glenoid erosion in cuff-tear arthropathy** (E0–E4) — describes superior and central glenoid wear patterns specific to chronic cuff insufficiency, distinct from the Walch system used for cuff-intact OA (Quick Reference table). Directly guides baseplate augmentation strategy (superior wedge vs. combined posterior-superior) and inferior-tilt positioning in rTSA to minimize notching risk.

**Samilson-Prieto classification** — originally described for OA following anterior instability surgery, graded by the size of the inferior humeral head osteophyte (mild 1–3 mm, moderate 3–7 mm, severe >7 mm) with corresponding joint-space narrowing (Quick Reference table). Still used generically for grading secondary/post-instability glenohumeral OA severity and for setting the threshold between arthroscopic debridement/capsular release and arthroplasty in younger post-instability patients.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Glenohumeral OA / early cuff-tear arthropathy.**
- Activity modification, NSAIDs/analgesics.
- Structured physical therapy: ROM maintenance, periscapular and deltoid strengthening.
- APTA's 2023 CPG for glenohumeral OA endorses manual therapy plus exercise for pain and function in mild-to-moderate disease.
- These measures do not alter the structural course of OA but meaningfully delay surgery and remain appropriate first-line care per the AAOS CPG (2020, extended by 2023 AUC) for low-demand or medically unfit patients.

**Frozen shoulder — full treatment ladder.**
1. Reassurance plus a home ROM/stretching program (pendulum, table slides, wall crawls, sleeper stretch) and NSAIDs during the freezing phase.
2. Structured physiotherapy — supervised PT plus home program, the "early structured physiotherapy" arm tested in UK FROST (delivered alongside one corticosteroid injection).
3. Intra-articular corticosteroid injection, most effective when given early in the freezing phase; image guidance (ultrasound or fluoroscopy) improves accuracy.
4. Hydrodilatation/distension arthrography (large-volume saline ± steroid) — 2023 meta-analysis data (British Medical Bulletin) show at least transient superiority over steroid injection alone for disability scores and passive external rotation; benefit may not persist beyond 3–6 months versus injection alone.
- Most idiopathic cases resolve within 12–24 months without surgery; diabetics recover more slowly and less completely, with higher recurrence and contralateral involvement (20–30%).

**Elbow OA.**
- Activity modification (avoid provocative terminal-arc loading).
- NSAIDs.
- PT emphasizing gentle terminal stretching — avoid aggressive forced stretching, which can flare synovitis and worsen pain.
- Bracing/splinting for symptomatic relief, particularly in mild-to-moderate disease with preserved joint space.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

**Corticosteroid injection.** Useful diagnostically and therapeutically across glenohumeral OA, cuff-tear arthropathy (subacromial for bursal-sided symptoms), frozen shoulder, and elbow OA. Limit frequency to roughly ≤3/year per joint given theoretical chondrotoxicity, and observe a ≥3-month washout between the last IA injection and elective arthroplasty to reduce periprosthetic infection risk — a standard most arthroplasty protocols enforce.

**Hyaluronic acid (viscosupplementation).** Evidence in glenohumeral OA is weaker and more heterogeneous than in knee OA; not strongly endorsed by the AAOS CPG. May be considered as an adjunct for patients wishing to avoid or delay steroid injection or surgery; effect size is modest.

**Hydrodilatation/capsular distension.** Primarily for frozen shoulder (see treatment ladder above). Some use as an adjunct for adhesive-capsulitis-like stiffness following arthroplasty or cuff repair, though evidence there is limited.

**Superior capsular reconstruction (SCR), subacromial balloon spacer, tendon transfers (latissimus/lower trapezius).** Cuff-preserving adjuncts for irreparable rotator cuff tears without arthritis (Hamada 1) in younger patients wishing to delay or avoid rTSA. Not appropriate once glenohumeral arthritic change (Hamada ≥3) or fixed superior migration is established.

### Operative

**Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA).**
- Indicated for glenohumeral OA with an intact, functioning rotator cuff (subscapularis repairable and functional) and correctable glenoid version/bone stock (Walch A1/A2, B1, and selected B2/B3/C with augmentation).
- Approach: deltopectoral; subscapularis managed by peel, tenotomy, or lesser tuberosity osteotomy (LTO) — LTO permits radiographic union assessment but carries nonunion risk; peel/tenotomy avoids bone-healing dependency but relies entirely on soft-tissue repair integrity.
- Version-correction options: (1) eccentric/selective reaming for correctable retroversion (<15°) with adequate remaining bone; (2) posterior-augmented glenoid components (step-cut or wedge) for B2/B3, avoiding excessive reaming and preserving subchondral bone; (3) structural bone grafting for severe deficiency (used less commonly in aTSA than the equivalent BIO-RSA technique in rTSA).
- Pearls and pitfalls: avoid posterior reaming that thins the vault and risks perforation or component malposition; correct no more than necessary, since overcorrection into anteversion increases anterior instability risk; meticulous subscapularis repair is the single strongest predictor of aTSA outcome and instability risk; restore native humeral height/version during head resection — overstuffing limits ROM and accelerates glenoid wear.

**aTSA vs. rTSA decision shift.**
- aTSA was historically the "gold standard" for cuff-intact OA, but rTSA volume has grown dramatically — registry data document a roughly 150%+ increase in rTSA use over the last decade versus modest growth in aTSA.
- Drivers of the shift: expanded indications, more reliable pain relief with lower glenoid-component revision rates, and growing recognition that severe posterior glenoid wear (B2/B3), subscapularis insufficiency, older age (>65–70), and glenoid bone loss precluding durable anatomic fixation all favor rTSA even with an intact cuff.
- Recent meta-analyses (2024–2025) comparing aTSA and rTSA for B2/B3 glenoids with intact cuff show similar ROM, ASES, Constant, and VAS improvements between the two implants — there is no clear functional-outcome winner, so the decision is driven by glenoid bone stock, correctability of version, subscapularis quality, and patient age/activity rather than expected outcome superiority.
- Practical rule of thumb: younger, higher-demand patients with correctable version and an intact subscapularis still generally favor aTSA (more native biomechanics, lower dislocation risk, easier future revision); rTSA is favored at age ≥65–70, with B3/C glenoids, subscapularis insufficiency, or concern for eventual cuff failure.

**Glenoid version management — reaming, augments, and BIO-RSA.**
- Eccentric reaming: limited correction capacity; sacrifices bone; risk of exposing weak subchondral/cancellous bone if >15° correction is attempted, jeopardizing baseplate fixation.
- Metal-augmented baseplates (posterior or superior wedge/step augments): technically reproducible, avoid graft-related complications, provide immediate fixation strength, but add cost and require inventory.
- BIO-RSA (bony increased-offset RSA): autograft (typically the resected humeral head) or allograft fixed beneath the baseplate corrects version/inclination while simultaneously lateralizing the joint line, reducing scapular notching and improving stability/ROM.
- Comparative evidence (2023–2025 systematic reviews and matched cohorts): metal augments show lower short-term baseplate failure/loosening than bone graft (reported baseplate failure ≈7% in bone-graft groups vs. 0% in metal-augment groups in some series) and marginally better early pain/ASES/UCLA scores; BIO-RSA restores host bone stock — valuable in younger patients or those who may need future revision — and avoids augmented-implant cost, but graft healing/resorption remains its chief specific risk, particularly with peripheral or thin grafts.
- Choice is often surgeon/institution-dependent; regardless of augment type, central compression-screw fixation and reliable press-fit primary stability at implantation are essential.

**Reverse shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA).**
- Indicated for cuff-tear arthropathy (Hamada ≥3, or symptomatic Hamada 1–2 with pseudoparalysis), massive irreparable cuff tears with pseudoparalysis but no arthritis in older/lower-demand patients, proximal humerus fracture in the elderly, revision of failed hemiarthroplasty/aTSA, and glenohumeral OA with severe glenoid deformity or subscapularis insufficiency (above).
- Technical considerations: baseplate positioned with slight inferior tilt/inferior overhang of the glenosphere reduces inferior scapular notching (impingement of the humeral polyethylene against the inferior scapular neck); glenosphere lateralization and humeral inlay/onlay design choice both affect notching rate and external rotation; appropriate soft-tissue tensioning balances instability risk (undertensioning) against acromial/scapular-spine stress-fracture risk (overtensioning).
- Subscapularis repair when feasible reduces anterior instability risk, though its effect on functional outcome remains debated.

**Frozen shoulder — operative escalation.**
- Reserved for patients who remain functionally limited after a genuine trial (typically 3–6 months) of the non-operative ladder above, including at least one corticosteroid injection.
- Manipulation under anaesthesia (MUA): controlled, sequential release of the capsule by passive stretching under general/regional anaesthesia (forward elevation first, then external rotation, then internal rotation/extension); lowest cost and fastest to perform, but relies on capsular tissue tearing in a controlled pattern rather than a directed release, carrying a theoretical (low-incidence) risk of humeral fracture or cuff/labral injury, particularly in osteopenic or older patients.
- Arthroscopic capsular release (ACR): directed release of the rotator interval, coracohumeral ligament, and anterior/inferior/posterior capsule under direct visualization, frequently combined with MUA to complete the release; allows simultaneous inspection/treatment of intra-articular pathology (biceps, labrum, partial cuff tears) but is more costly and technically demanding.
- UK FROST directly compared these two options head-to-head with early structured physiotherapy and found no clinically important difference between MUA and ACR at 12 months, with MUA being the more cost-effective choice — this evidence supports MUA as the reasonable first operative step for most patients, reserving ACR for cases with suspected concomitant intra-articular pathology or after failed MUA.
- Diabetic patients and those with a prolonged frozen phase (>12 months) should be counseled on a higher risk of incomplete recovery and recurrence regardless of which operative technique is chosen.

**Elbow debridement / Outerbridge-Kashiwagi (OK) procedure.**
- Indicated for primary or post-traumatic elbow OA in active patients with mechanical symptoms (loss of terminal extension/flexion, locking from loose bodies, impingement pain) and reasonably preserved joint space.
- Can be performed open (classic OK — ulnohumeral arthroplasty with olecranon fossa fenestration) or arthroscopically (the "column procedure" — anterior and posterior compartment debridement, osteophyte excision, capsular release, loose-body removal).
- Frequently combined with ulnar nerve decompression/transposition when pre-op neuropathy is present or when the planned flexion gain risks traction neuropathy.
- 2024 series report significant Mayo Elbow Performance Score improvement (≈60.7 to ≈84.6) with low complication (≈2.8%) and reoperation (≈4.2%) rates; results are best when joint space is preserved and symptoms are predominantly osteophyte/impingement-driven rather than from diffuse chondral loss.

**Total elbow arthroplasty (TEA).**
- Linked/semiconstrained designs (e.g., Coonrad-Morrey) dominate for most degenerative and inflammatory indications, offering superior stability with acceptable constraint-related loosening rates.
- Unlinked designs preserve more native kinematics and bone stock but require intact collateral ligaments and are more instability-prone.
- Indicated for advanced elbow OA, inflammatory arthritis, or post-traumatic arthritis in lower-demand, typically older (>65–70) patients.
- Major limitation: a lifetime lifting restriction (generally <2.3–4.5 kg / 5–10 lb sustained load) due to polyethylene bushing wear and aseptic loosening — the central reason TEA is problematic in younger, high-demand patients.

**TEA vs. interposition arthroplasty in young patients.**
- TEA in patients under roughly 60–65 carries substantially higher long-term loosening/revision rates from activity-related mechanical stress on the linkage and bone-implant interface; it is therefore reserved for low-demand elderly patients, or for young patients with systemic inflammatory arthritis and low functional demand who accept the lifting restrictions.
- For young, high-demand patients with advanced elbow destruction unwilling to accept TEA's lifelong restrictions, interposition arthroplasty is a bone-preserving salvage alternative: fascia lata, Achilles tendon allograft, or dermal graft interposed in the ulnohumeral joint, often combined with a hinged external fixator to protect the interposed tissue and permit early motion.
- 2023 systematic review data: interposition arthroplasty achieves substantial pain and functional gains (mean MEPI pain score 11.0→26.3; functional score 41.5→74.0) but with a notable overall complication rate (≈38.4%) and reoperation rate (≈23.5%).
- Complications are markedly higher in post-traumatic OA (41.2%) than inflammatory arthritis (11.7%) — an important counseling point for young post-traumatic patients.
- Interposition preserves bone stock for eventual conversion to TEA later in life, which is its principal advantage over primary TEA in this population.

**aTSA vs. rTSA quick decision matrix**

| Factor favoring aTSA | Factor favoring rTSA |
|---|---|
| Intact, functional subscapularis | Subscapularis torn/attenuated/irreparable |
| Correctable glenoid version (<15°, Walch A/B1) | Uncorrectable retroversion or severe bone loss (B3/C) |
| Younger, higher-demand patient | Age ≥65–70 or lower functional demand |
| Concentric glenoid wear | Cuff-tear arthropathy (Hamada ≥3) or pseudoparalysis |
| Lower dislocation-risk profile desired | Prior failed cuff repair or revision setting |

**TEA implant/technique comparison**

| Option | Constraint | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| Linked/semiconstrained (e.g., Coonrad-Morrey) | Sacrifices some native kinematics for stability | Most degenerative/inflammatory arthritis in low-demand patients; forgiving of collateral ligament deficiency |
| Unlinked | Preserves native kinematics, less constraint | Requires intact collateral ligaments; more instability-prone |
| Interposition arthroplasty | No implant — soft-tissue/graft spacer | Young, high-demand patients unwilling to accept TEA's lifting restriction; bone-preserving |

**Special populations & pearls.**
- **Samilson-Prieto / post-instability OA in young patients**: retained hardware from prior stabilization (anchors, screws) should be identified pre-operatively on CT — impingement or chondral damage from prominent hardware is a treatable cause of pain distinct from the arthritis itself, and hardware removal alone can sometimes be combined with arthroscopic debridement in mild-moderate grades before committing a young patient to arthroplasty.
- **Diabetic patients**: counsel explicitly before treating frozen shoulder or offering elective shoulder/elbow arthroplasty — diabetes both slows/limits frozen shoulder recovery and elevates periprosthetic joint infection risk; optimize HbA1c (generally target <7–8%) before elective arthroplasty.
- **B2/B3 glenoid counseling**: patients should understand that neither aTSA-with-augmentation nor rTSA has been shown to be clearly superior on functional outcomes for their glenoid pattern — frame the recommendation around bone-stock preservation, revision-surgery burden, and durability rather than promising one implant is "better."
- **Elbow OA in the throwing athlete/laborer**: post-traumatic and repetitive-loading elbow OA in the young, still-active patient should be approached with debridement-type surgery first whenever joint space allows — this population is exactly where TEA's lifting restriction is least acceptable and where interposition remains a bridge, not a first-line, option.
- **Sequencing multiple joint procedures**: when both frozen shoulder and cuff pathology or early glenohumeral OA coexist, treat the capsular stiffness first (injections, PT, MUA/ACR as needed) before committing to arthroplasty decision-making, since capsular contracture can obscure true passive ROM and cuff strength assessment.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**aTSA/rTSA — general framework (adjust per subscapularis management and implant stability).**
- Weeks 0–2: sling immobilization full-time except for hygiene and elbow/wrist/hand exercises; passive/active-assisted ROM only (pendulums, supine passive forward elevation to tolerance, passive external rotation to a surgeon-specified limit — typically 0–30° if subscapularis was repaired/peeled, less restricted for rTSA without a subscapularis repair). No active internal/external rotation, no lifting.
- Weeks 2–6: continued passive/active-assisted ROM progression; begin gentle active ROM below shoulder height; sling weaning as tolerated (rTSA is often weaned faster, around 2–4 weeks, given different loading demands than subscapularis-protective aTSA protocols).
- Weeks 6–12: discontinue sling; progress to full active ROM; begin light resistance/strengthening (isometrics progressing to isotonic); subscapularis-protective restrictions typically lifted around 6–8 weeks once healing is presumed (correlate with LTO union on radiographs if osteotomy was used, generally united by 6–8 weeks).
- Months 3–6: progressive strengthening (rotator cuff-focused for aTSA, deltoid/periscapular-focused for rTSA); functional and sport-specific training as appropriate.
- Return to work: sedentary work 2–4 weeks; light-manual work 6–8 weeks; heavy manual/overhead labor 4–6 months, with permanent lifting modification often advised, particularly after rTSA.
- Return to sport: low-demand recreational activity (golf, swimming) typically 4–6 months; contact/overhead athletic activity is individualized and often discouraged long-term after rTSA given implant constraints.

**Frozen shoulder (post-MUA or arthroscopic capsular release).**
- Immediate aggressive passive ROM within 24–48 hours to prevent re-adhesion — this differs fundamentally from arthroplasty protocols, since motion (not protection) is the postoperative priority.
- Daily supervised or intensive home PT for 2–6 weeks.
- Interscalene catheter or frequent multimodal analgesia in the first days facilitates early motion tolerance.
- Return to full function typically 6–12 weeks; recurrence of stiffness is the main risk if therapy is not sustained.

**Elbow debridement/OK procedure.**
- Early motion protocol — continuous passive motion or aggressive active/active-assisted ROM starting day 1–3 postoperatively to prevent recurrent stiffness and heterotopic-ossification-related motion loss.
- Indomethacin or single-dose radiation prophylaxis considered in high-HO-risk patients (prior HO, head injury, burns).
- Strengthening deferred to 6 weeks.
- Return to manual labor/sport 3–4 months.

**Total elbow arthroplasty.**
- Splint/sling immobilization 1–2 weeks (extension-block or posterior splint per surgeon preference).
- Gentle active-assisted ROM from 2 weeks; avoid resisted extension against the triceps repair for 6–8 weeks if a triceps-splitting/reflecting approach was used.
- Lifelong lifting-restriction counseling begins at the first postoperative visit — this is a definitive restriction, not merely a rehab-phase limit.
- Return to light ADLs 6–8 weeks; permanent avoidance of repetitive lifting >2.3–4.5 kg.

## Complications

**Approximate incidence snapshot**

| Procedure | Complication | Reported incidence |
|---|---|---|
| aTSA | Subscapularis failure | 4–10% |
| aTSA | Deep infection | ≈1% |
| rTSA | Instability/dislocation | 2–8% |
| rTSA | Acromial/scapular spine stress fracture | 2–5% |
| MUA (frozen shoulder) | Humeral fracture | Rare, higher in osteopenic bone |
| Elbow debridement/OK | Reoperation | ≈4% |
| TEA | Deep infection | ≈3–5% |
| Interposition arthroplasty | Any complication / reoperation | ≈38% / ≈24% |

**aTSA.** Glenoid component loosening/failure is the leading cause of revision (higher in B2/B3 glenoids with inadequate version correction); subscapularis failure (rupture/attenuation causing anterosuperior instability, reported 4–10%); rotator cuff tear progression over time in shoulders with pre-existing degeneration; periprosthetic fracture; infection (≈1%, higher with *Cutibacterium acnes* in male patients); instability (anterior most common, related to subscapularis failure or oversized/overstuffed humeral head).

**rTSA.** Instability/dislocation (reported 2–8%, higher in revision settings and with subscapularis deficiency); scapular notching (inferior glenoid-humeral polyethylene impingement, seen in a substantial minority of medialized-design cases — clinical significance is debated, and recent literature suggests notching does not always correlate with worse clinical outcomes, though higher-grade notching does associate with eventual glenoid component loosening); acromial and scapular spine stress fractures (2–5%, related to deltoid overtensioning and osteoporotic bone, with a significant negative impact on outcome when they occur); infection; periprosthetic fracture; baseplate loosening (more common with bone-graft augmentation than metal augmentation per recent comparative data, and with severe pre-op bone loss); neurologic injury (axillary nerve traction, brachial plexopathy from arm lengthening — intraoperative nerve monitoring used selectively).

**Frozen shoulder procedures.** MUA — humeral fracture (rare but reported, particularly in osteopenic patients), rotator cuff tear, labral injury, transient neurapraxia. Arthroscopic capsular release — nerve injury (axillary nerve at risk during inferior capsular release), incomplete release/recurrent stiffness, instability from over-release (rare). UK FROST found arthroscopic capsular release carries higher cost and complication burden without a meaningfully better 12-month outcome than MUA.

**Elbow debridement/OK.** Heterotopic ossification; incomplete relief if diffuse chondral loss is unaddressed; transient ulnar neuropathy; recurrent stiffness/loose body formation over time; a not-insignificant reoperation rate (≈4%).

**TEA.** Aseptic loosening — the dominant long-term failure mode, accelerated by activity/loading and the central reason for lifting restrictions and relative contraindication in young/high-demand patients; infection (higher baseline risk than lower-extremity arthroplasty given the thin soft-tissue envelope, reported ≈3–5%); triceps insufficiency/rupture; ulnar neuropathy; periprosthetic fracture; bushing/polyethylene wear requiring revision.

**Interposition arthroplasty.** Substantial complication rate (≈38%) and reoperation rate (≈24%) as above, driven by graft attenuation/failure, recurrent stiffness, instability, and heterotopic ossification; failure rates are notably higher in post-traumatic OA than in inflammatory arthritis.

## Recent Evidence

- [AAOS Appropriate Use Criteria — Humeral Component Design in Primary aTSA and Treatment of Shoulder OA with Severe Glenoid Retroversion (April 2023)](https://www.aaos.org/aaos-home/newsroom/press-releases/aaos-releases-new-appropriate-use-criteria/) — new AUC tools operationalize the 2020 AAOS glenohumeral OA CPG specifically for B2/B3-type severe retroversion decision-making and humeral stem/resurfacing design choice, reinforcing that severe retroversion is a decision point favoring augmentation or rTSA.
- [APTA Clinical Practice Guideline: Physical Therapist Management of Glenohumeral Joint Osteoarthritis (2023, Physical Therapy journal)](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article/103/6/pzad041/7146561) — codifies PT-directed management (manual therapy plus exercise) as evidence-supported first-line care for mild-to-moderate glenohumeral OA, complementing the AAOS surgical CPG.
- [Outcomes of anatomic versus reverse shoulder arthroplasty for B2 & B3 glenoids with intact rotator cuff — updated systematic review and proportional meta-analysis (2024–2025)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12274211/) — finds comparable ROM/ASES/Constant/VAS improvements between aTSA and rTSA for posteriorly-worn glenoids with intact cuff, reinforcing that the aTSA-vs-rTSA choice in B2/B3 should be driven by bone stock/version-correctability/age rather than expected functional superiority of either implant.
- [Reverse versus anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty for glenohumeral osteoarthritis with intact cuff: meta-analysis of clinical outcomes (2024, Journal of Shoulder and Elbow Surgery)](https://www.jshoulderelbow.org/article/S1058-2746(24)00558-5/abstract) — a further 2024 confirmatory meta-analysis supporting an equivalent clinical-outcome profile, legitimizing the ongoing shift toward broader rTSA use even in cuff-intact OA.
- [Bony increased offset reverse shoulder arthroplasty (BIO-RSA) vs. metal augments — systematic review (2025, Archives of Orthopaedic and Trauma Surgery)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00402-025-05897-7) — pooled ≈2,364 shoulders; metal-augmented baseplates showed lower short-term baseplate failure rates and modestly better pain/functional scores versus BIO-RSA, informing augment-type selection when correcting glenoid version/bone loss.
- [Functional and Radiographic Outcomes of Bone Grafting for Severe Glenoid Defects in Reverse Shoulder Arthroplasty (2024, Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery)](https://doi.org/10.2106/JBJS.24.01052) — dedicated outcomes data for BIO-RSA-type bone grafting in the most severe glenoid deficiency cases, clarifying which defect patterns still benefit from graft over metal augmentation.
- [Short-Term Radiographic Outcomes of Bone versus Metallic Augmented Baseplate in RSA — matched case-control study (2024, PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11155345/) — matched-cohort confirmation that metal augments trend toward better short-term radiographic stability than bone graft in central-screw baseplates.
- [Instability after reverse shoulder arthroplasty — systematic review of interventions and clinical outcomes (2025, Journal of Shoulder and Elbow Surgery)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1058274625003210) — consolidates management pathways (closed reduction, revision to constrained/lateralized components, subscapularis repair) for post-rTSA instability, the most common early revision indication.
- [Long-Term Outcomes Following Reverse Total Shoulder Arthroplasty — systematic review, minimum 10-year follow-up (2025, JBJS Open Access)](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jbjsoa/fulltext/10.2106/jbjs.oa.25.00025~long-term-outcomes-following-reverse-total-shoulder) — first large systematic synthesis of true long-term (≥10-year) rTSA data, providing durability benchmarks now that indications have expanded to younger patients.
- [Do outcomes of interposition arthroplasty in young patients differ based on indication? — systematic review (2023, Journal of Shoulder and Elbow Surgery)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37423462/) — quantifies the indication-dependent complication gradient (post-traumatic OA 41.2% vs. inflammatory arthritis 11.7% complication rate) that should directly inform counseling of young post-traumatic elbow OA patients considering interposition versus TEA.
- [Elbow Interpositional Arthroplasty using Achilles Tendon Allograft — case report (2025, Journal of Orthopaedic Case Reports)](https://jocr.co.in/wp/2025/08/elbow-interpositional-arthroplasty-using-an-achilles-tendon-allograft-a-case-report/) — recent technique documentation reflecting continued refinement of graft choice (allograft vs. autograft fascia lata/dermal) for interposition in bone-preserving young-patient salvage surgery.
- [Interpositional Arthroplasty with Dermal Grafting and Hinged External Fixator for Elbow Osteoarthrosis with Stiffness in Young Adults (2024–2025, PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12050123/) — describes the modern combined technique (dermal interposition plus hinged fixator protecting the graft while permitting early motion), the current preferred construct for this indication.
- [Efficacy of hydrodilatation in frozen shoulder — systematic review and meta-analysis (2023, British Medical Bulletin)](https://academic.oup.com/bmb/article/147/1/121/7231525) — confirms hydrodilatation produces at least transient superior improvement in disability and passive external rotation compared with corticosteroid injection alone, supporting its place in the treatment ladder for refractory freezing-phase frozen shoulder before proceeding to MUA/capsular release.
- [Ultrasound-guided hydrodilatation versus corticosteroid injection for adhesive capsulitis — pilot randomized comparative study (2025, Cureus)](https://www.cureus.com/articles/389435-ultrasound-guided-hydrodilatation-versus-corticosteroid-injection-for-adhesive-capsulitis-a-pilot-randomized-comparative-study.pdf) — recent 2025 RCT-level data continuing to refine the comparative-effectiveness picture between the two most common frozen shoulder injection strategies.
- [UK FROST — United Kingdom Frozen Shoulder Trial, multi-centre RCT of early structured physiotherapy vs. MUA vs. arthroscopic capsular release](https://www.york.ac.uk/health-sciences/research/trials/ytutrialsandstudies/archive/ukfrost/) — the largest RCT in the field (503 patients) remains the practice-defining reference: MUA with steroid injection is the most cost-effective strategy; arthroscopic capsular release carries higher cost/complication burden without a clinically important 12-month outcome advantage over MUA; early physiotherapy alone (with one steroid injection) is accessible earlier but has a higher rate of needing further treatment — this hierarchy continues to be cited and reaffirmed in 2023–2025 follow-up analyses and guideline discussions.
- [Elbow arthroscopic debridement (arthroscopic Outerbridge-Kashiwagi and osteocapsular arthroplasty) — comparative outcomes with endoscopic/open cubital tunnel release (2024, Fortune Journals)](https://www.fortunejournals.com/articles/arthroscopic-outerbridgekashiwagi-procedure-with-endoscopic-cubital-tunnel-release-comparison-with-arthroscopic-osteocapsular-arth.html) — 2024 comparative series (53 cases) reaffirms low complication/reoperation rates and significant MEPS improvement for arthroscopic debridement-type elbow OA surgery, supporting its continued first-line surgical status in active patients with preserved joint space.
