## Quick Reference

**Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) treatment ladder**

| Line | Precollapse (ARCO 0–IIC) | Postcollapse (ARCO IIIA–IV) |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | Protected weight-bearing + risk-factor modification; consider core decompression if symptomatic and lesion <30% head volume | Activity modification, analgesia, walking aids if elderly/low-demand or asymptomatic incidental finding |
| 2nd | Core decompression ± biologic augmentation (BMAC/MSC, ± demineralized bone matrix); pharmacologic disease modification (bisphosphonate, denosumab) investigational | Head-preserving osteotomy (rotational/Sugioka, or angular) in selected young patients with small, non-weight-bearing-zone lesions |
| Surgical (definitive) | Vascularized fibular graft or free/pedicled vascularized bone graft in large lesions of young patients wanting joint preservation | Total hip arthroplasty — the default once >2 mm collapse or joint-space narrowing present, especially symptomatic |

**FAI / labral pathology treatment ladder**

| Line | Intervention |
|---|---|
| 1st | Structured physiotherapy (activity modification, hip-and-core strengthening, gait/movement retraining) for 3 months minimum |
| 2nd | Image-guided intra-articular corticosteroid/local anesthetic injection — diagnostic + short-term therapeutic |
| Surgical | Hip arthroscopy (labral repair/selective debridement + femoral osteochondroplasty ± acetabuloplasty) for structural FAI syndrome refractory to nonoperative care, Tönnis ≤1, minimal joint space loss |

**Adult hip dysplasia treatment ladder**

| Line | Intervention |
|---|---|
| 1st | Activity modification, hip abductor/core strengthening, weight optimization |
| 2nd | Intra-articular injection for symptom clarification/temporizing relief |
| Surgical | Periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) in skeletally mature patients, LCEA <20–25°, Tönnis grade 0–1, congruent joint, open triradiate closed; THA once secondary OA established (Tönnis ≥2) or in Crowe III–IV/Hartofilakidis high dislocation with degenerative change |

**Gluteal tendinopathy / GTPS treatment ladder**

| Line | Intervention |
|---|---|
| 1st | Load management education, avoid adduction/compressive positions, progressive abductor loading program (isometric → isotonic → energy storage) 8–12 weeks |
| 2nd | Ultrasound-guided peritrochanteric corticosteroid injection (short-term benefit, ≤8–12 weeks superiority over exercise, no long-term advantage) or PRP (evidence mixed, possibly durable at 1 year) |
| Surgical | Endoscopic/open gluteus medius/minimus repair for confirmed full-thickness tear refractory to ≥6 months conservative care; ITB bursectomy/release rarely as isolated procedure |

**Red flags (any diagnosis in this cluster):**
- Sudden atraumatic hip pain in a patient on high-dose corticosteroids, post-transplant, or with sickle cell disease → **assume ONFH until proven otherwise**, MRI within days, not weeks.
- Fever, elevated inflammatory markers, or effusion out of proportion → septic arthritis until excluded (aspirate).
- Acute-on-chronic collapse with new limp/shortening in known ONFH → urgent re-imaging, likely conversion to THA.
- Groin pain with mechanical locking/catching plus a positive anterior impingement test in a patient <40 → structural FAI/labral tear, not "hip flexor strain."
- Lateral hip pain that fails 3 months of loading rehab plus a positive resisted abduction/single-leg stance test → image for gluteal tear before repeated steroid injections (injection risk of tendon attritional weakening with repeated dosing).
- Hip pain in a young female with a history of childhood DDH/breech/family history, positive Trendelenburg, and lateral center-edge angle <20° → dysplasia work-up, do not treat as isolated labral pathology alone.

**Key numbers to remember**

| Parameter | Value | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| Lateral center-edge angle (LCEA), normal | 25–39° | <20° = dysplastic (PAO candidate range 15–20° borderline); >40° = pincer/overcoverage |
| Tönnis angle (acetabular index), normal | 0–10° | >10° suggests dysplasia/instability |
| Alpha angle (cam), normal | <50–55° | Higher = cam morphology; surgical resection target restores <50° |
| Femoral head collapse threshold (ARCO IIIA vs IIIB) | 2 mm | >2 mm depression = worse prognosis, narrows joint-preservation window |
| Necrotic lesion size (combined necrotic angle / Kerboul angle) | <200° good prognosis; >200° poor with core decompression alone | Determines candidacy for isolated core decompression vs vascularized graft |
| Crowe classification threshold for subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy | Crowe III–IV (>50–75% subluxation) | Predicts need for femoral shortening at THA |
| Corticosteroid dose associated with ONFH risk | >2 g cumulative prednisone-equivalent, or >20 mg/day for >1 month | Threshold used for surveillance MRI decisions in transplant/rheumatologic patients |
| Time from symptom onset to MRI diagnosis (ideal) | Within 2 weeks | MRI is >99% sensitive/specific; plain films are normal in early ARCO 0–I |
| PAO correction target | LCEA 25–35°, acetabular version corrected, medialization ≤10 mm | Overcorrection risks iatrogenic pincer impingement |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**Femoral head vascular anatomy.** The femoral head receives blood almost exclusively via the **medial femoral circumflex artery (MFCA)**, through its deep branch running along the posterosuperior neck within the retinacular (Weitbrecht) fibers, terminating as the lateral epiphyseal (superior retinacular) vessels — the dominant supply to the weight-bearing dome. The lateral femoral circumflex artery contributes anteriorly and inferiorly (inferior retinacular/metaphyseal vessels), and the artery of the ligamentum teres (a branch of the obturator or posterior division of the MFCA) is a minor, often absent-in-adults contributor. This watershed anatomy explains why the anterosuperior/superolateral quadrant — territory of the terminal MFCA branches — is the most frequent and most consequential necrosis site: it lies in the primary weight-bearing zone.

**Osteonecrosis pathophysiology.** A final common pathway of intraosseous vascular interruption (arterial occlusion, venous outflow obstruction/intraosseous hypertension, or a combination) leads to osteocyte and marrow cell death within 12–48 hours of critical ischemia. Trabecular bone remains structurally intact for weeks despite cell death (radiographically silent stage). Repair attempts at the necrotic-viable interface (creeping substitution) generate a reactive interface — the origin of the crescent sign and sclerotic rim — but this repair front is mechanically weaker than native subchondral bone; under cyclic load it fails, producing subchondral fracture (crescent sign) and eventually segmental collapse. Once the subchondral plate fails, articular cartilage — nourished by synovial fluid and initially spared — loses its structural support and secondary osteoarthritis follows.

Risk factors cluster into **traumatic** (femoral neck fracture — up to 15–35% AVN depending on displacement/fixation quality/timing; hip dislocation) and **atraumatic**: corticosteroid exposure (dose- and duration-dependent, commonly cited threshold >2 g cumulative prednisone-equivalent), alcohol excess, sickle cell disease/hemoglobinopathies, coagulopathies (thrombophilia/hypofibrinolysis), Gaucher disease, SLE, HIV/antiretroviral therapy, decompression sickness (dysbaric), radiation, and idiopathic (up to 20–40% of atraumatic cases). Bilateral involvement is present in 50–80% of atraumatic ONFH at diagnosis or on subsequent screening, mandating contralateral hip MRI at presentation.

**FAI morphology.** **Cam** morphology is a loss of the normal femoral head-neck offset (aspherical head, alpha angle elevation), typically anterosuperior, causing shear injury to the chondrolabral junction with axial loading/flexion-internal rotation. **Pincer** morphology is focal (acetabular retroversion, prominent anterior wall) or global (coxa profunda/protrusio) acetabular overcoverage causing labral crush injury and a contrecoup posteroinferior cartilage lesion. Mixed morphology is most common clinically. Repetitive impingement produces chondrolabral junction delamination, labral tearing, and — over years — secondary osteoarthritis, which is the principal rationale for early surgical correction in symptomatic structural FAI.

**Dysplasia.** Developmental undercoverage of the acetabulum (shallow, often anteriorly and laterally deficient, with excessive anteversion) shifts contact stresses to a smaller area of anterosuperior cartilage/labrum, producing chronic overload, labral hypertrophy and tearing, and accelerated osteoarthritis, typically in the third to fifth decade. The labrum in dysplastic hips is frequently hypertrophic and takes on a secondary weight-bearing/stabilizing role — a mechanism that explains why isolated arthroscopic labral treatment without addressing the underlying bony undercoverage tends to fail or accelerate deterioration ("microinstability").

**Gluteal tendinopathy / GTPS.** The gluteus medius and minimus insert onto the greater trochanter (medius onto the posterosuperior and lateral facets, minimus onto the anterior facet), functioning analogously to the rotator cuff of the hip. Tendinopathy arises from a combination of tensile overload and compressive loading against the greater trochanter (adduction, cross-leg sitting, standing with hip hitched), producing an insertional/enthesopathic degeneration spectrum from reactive tendinopathy through partial- to full-thickness tearing, often accompanied by trochanteric bursitis (which is frequently secondary/reactive rather than the primary pathology — hence the shift in terminology from "trochanteric bursitis" to GTPS/gluteal tendinopathy).

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History.** Establish onset character (atraumatic groin pain suggests ONFH or intra-articular pathology; lateral hip pain worse lying on the side and with stairs/single-leg stance suggests GTPS), corticosteroid/alcohol/sickle cell/transplant history, mechanical symptoms (catching, locking, giving way — labral/FAI), and functional limitation (squatting, pivoting, prolonged sitting typical of FAI; night pain and inability to lie on the affected side typical of GTPS).

**Examination.**
- **Anterior impingement test (FADIR — flexion, adduction, internal rotation)**: positive in FAI/labral pathology; sensitivity high (~90%) but poor specificity — a positive test is a screening, not confirmatory, finding.
- **FABER (Patrick) test**: distance from lateral knee to table; asymmetry and reproduction of groin pain suggest intra-articular pathology, posterior buttock pain can suggest SI joint.
- **Log roll**: pain with passive internal/external rotation with the leg extended is the most specific test for intra-articular hip pathology (low false-positive rate).
- **Trendelenburg test**: positive (pelvic drop) suggests abductor (gluteus medius/minimus) dysfunction/tear or hip instability from dysplasia.
- **Resisted abduction in side-lying / single-leg stance for 30 seconds**: pain reproduction is the most useful clinical test cluster for gluteal tendinopathy; palpation of the greater trochanter alone is nonspecific (high false-positive rate in asymptomatic people).
- **Range of motion**: reduced internal rotation in flexion is characteristic of cam FAI and early osteoarthritis alike.

**Imaging.**
- **Plain radiographs (AP pelvis, frog-leg/Dunn lateral, false-profile view)**: first-line for all five diagnoses. AP pelvis for LCEA, Tönnis angle/grade, crossover sign (acetabular retroversion), Shenton's line (dysplasia/subluxation); frog/Dunn lateral for alpha angle (cam); false-profile view for anterior coverage (anterior center-edge angle).
- **MRI (non-contrast, dedicated hip protocol)**: gold standard for early ONFH — the double-line sign on T2 is pathognomonic; also stages lesion size (Kerboul/combined necrotic angle) and detects bone marrow edema predicting imminent collapse. Also the primary modality for labral tears (MR arthrography increases sensitivity to ~90%+ for labral and chondral lesions vs ~70–80% for non-arthrographic MRI) and for gluteal tendon tears (partial vs full-thickness, degree of retraction, muscle atrophy/fatty infiltration — Goutallier-type grading is used analogously to the shoulder).
- **CT**: valuable for 3D version assessment (femoral and acetabular), PAO/osteotomy preoperative planning, and precise Crowe/Hartofilakidis classification measurement in complex dysplasia.
- **Ultrasound**: office-based screening for gluteal tendon tears/bursal effusion and for image-guided injection; operator-dependent, lower sensitivity than MRI for partial tears.
- **Bone scan**: largely historical for ONFH screening, superseded by MRI; retains a niche in multifocal osteonecrosis screening when MRI is contraindicated.

Diagnostic hip injection (intra-articular local anesthetic ± steroid under fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance) remains the most reliable way to confirm an intra-articular pain generator (FAI/labral tear/early OA) before committing to hip arthroscopy, particularly in patients with concurrent spine or peritrochanteric pathology.

### Differential diagnosis pearls

Because groin/hip pain in this age group is frequently multifactorial, deliberately separate intra-articular from extra-articular and referred pain generators before committing to a treatment pathway:

- **Lumbar spine (L1-L3 radiculopathy or referred pain)**: normal log-roll test, positive straight-leg raise/femoral stretch, dermatomal distribution — obtain lumbar imaging if the log roll is negative and hip imaging is unremarkable.
- **Inguinal/sports hernia and athletic pubalgia**: pain with resisted sit-up/adductor testing, tenderness at the pubic symphysis/conjoint tendon, often coexists with cam FAI in athletes ("hip-groin syndrome") and must be assessed separately since isolated hip surgery will not resolve it.
- **Adductor-related groin pain**: tenderness and pain on resisted adduction at the adductor origin, common in field-sport athletes and easily confused with intra-articular pathology on history alone.
- **Iliopsoas tendinopathy/impingement (including post-THA psoas impingement)**: anterior groin pain with resisted hip flexion from a flexed/externally rotated position; can coexist with or mimic anterior labral pathology and, after arthroplasty, with acetabular component overhang.
- **Inflammatory arthritis / spondyloarthropathy**: consider in bilateral, insidious-onset hip pain with morning stiffness >30-60 minutes, especially in younger patients, before attributing symptoms solely to structural FAI or dysplasia; screen with inflammatory markers and HLA-B27 when the pattern fits.
- **Septic arthritis and occult fracture (femoral neck stress fracture, especially in athletes/osteoporotic patients)**: always excluded urgently when pain is acute, disproportionate to exam findings, or accompanied by systemic symptoms — a missed femoral neck stress fracture can displace and itself become a cause of secondary osteonecrosis.

## Classification

### ARCO 2019 (Association Research Circulation Osseous) — osteonecrosis of the femoral head

The 2019 revision (Yoon et al., 4-round international Delphi consensus, 29 experts) simplified the historical 1993 six-stage system by removing the quantitative lesion-size substaging in favor of a more reproducible qualitative framework, and split collapse into early/late based on a 2 mm depression threshold.

| Stage | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Normal or nonspecific radiograph/MRI; histology only (rarely used clinically) | Observation, risk-factor modification |
| **I** | Normal radiograph; **MRI or bone scan positive** (double-line sign); subclassified Ia/Ib/Ic by extent (medial/central/lateral on coronal MRI, correlating with Kerboul angle) | Precollapse — candidate for core decompression ± biologics if symptomatic; extent (lateral involvement) predicts higher collapse risk |
| **II** | Radiographic abnormality (sclerosis, cysts, osteopenia) **without** collapse or subchondral fracture; subclassified IIa/IIb/IIc by extent as above | Precollapse — still within the joint-preservation window; core decompression most effective here |
| **IIIA** | **Subchondral fracture (crescent sign) or early collapse ≤2 mm** | Transition zone — joint preservation possible in small/medial lesions but success rates fall sharply; vascularized bone grafting considered in young patients |
| **IIIB** | **Collapse >2 mm** | Joint preservation largely unsuccessful except highly selected small lesions/rotational osteotomy candidates; THA increasingly favored |
| **IV** | Osteoarthritic changes: joint-space narrowing, acetabular involvement, femoral head flattening/destruction | THA is the standard of care |

### Ficat-Arlet (classic, still widely used clinically)

| Stage | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Asymptomatic, normal imaging, MRI may already be positive | Surveillance in at-risk patients |
| I | Symptomatic, normal or near-normal radiograph, positive MRI/bone scan | Core decompression candidate |
| II | Sclerosis/cysts on radiograph, femoral head sphericity preserved (IIA no crescent, IIB crescent sign present) | Core decompression ± biologics best results; IIB approaching transition to collapse |
| III | Subchondral collapse, "crescent sign," flattening, sequestrum, head remains congruent with acetabulum | Head-preserving osteotomy or vascularized graft in select young patients; otherwise THA |
| IV | Joint-space narrowing, acetabular cartilage loss, osteoarthritic changes | THA |

### Steinberg (University of Pennsylvania) — quantitative, most useful for research/lesion-size documentation

| Stage | Findings | % head involvement subclassification |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Normal/nondiagnostic radiograph, bone scan, MRI | — |
| I | Normal radiograph, abnormal bone scan and/or MRI | A <15%, B 15–30%, C >30% of head involved |
| II | Cystic/sclerotic changes on radiograph | A <15%, B 15–30%, C >30% |
| III | Subchondral collapse (crescent sign) without flattening | A <15%, B 15–30%, C >30% of articular surface |
| IV | Flattening of femoral head | A <15% surface/<2 mm depression, B 15–30%/2–4 mm, C >30%/>4 mm |
| V | Joint-space narrowing and/or acetabular changes | A/B/C mild/moderate/severe |
| VI | Advanced degenerative changes | — |

Management implication: the A/B/C quantitative substaging at each stage is what drives joint-preservation decision-making — lesions <15% (A) rarely progress to collapse and can often be observed or treated with decompression alone; lesions >30% (C), especially with lateral/weight-bearing involvement, have high collapse risk and are relatively poor candidates for isolated core decompression, favoring vascularized grafting or earlier arthroplasty counseling.

### Tönnis classification (acetabular dysplasia — grading of secondary osteoarthritis)

| Grade | Radiographic findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No signs of osteoarthritis | PAO candidate (ideal) |
| 1 | Increased sclerosis, mild joint-space narrowing, mild osteophyte formation | PAO still favorable if congruent joint and preserved cartilage on MRI/intraop |
| 2 | Small cysts, moderate joint-space narrowing, moderate loss of femoral head sphericity | PAO borderline — relative contraindication; individualize by age/cartilage status |
| 3 | Large cysts, severe/obliterated joint space, avascular necrosis, severe femoral head deformity | PAO contraindicated — THA is the procedure of choice |

*(Note: the Tönnis acetabular index/angle — the angle between Hilgenreiner's line and a line to the lateral acetabular sourcil — is a distinct measurement used mainly in pediatric DDH assessment; normal adult value approximately 0–10°, and is different from this Tönnis OA grading scale, though both originate from the same author and are frequently conflated.)*

### Crowe classification — proximal femoral migration in dysplastic/dislocated hips

| Type | Proximal migration of femoral head (as % of femoral head diameter, or Hartofilakidis-style measurement of pelvic height) | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | <50% (<10 mm) | Standard THA technique, low risk of needing shortening |
| II | 50–75% (10–15 mm) | Anatomic or near-anatomic cup placement usually achievable; consider structural graft for cup coverage |
| III | 75–100% (15–20 mm) | High risk of sciatic/femoral nerve traction injury with acute lengthening; consider subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy |
| IV | >100% (>20 mm), high dislocation | Subtrochanteric shortening derotational osteotomy nearly always required; small femoral components, cup placed at true acetabulum with possible medialization/structural graft |

### Hartofilakidis classification — an alternative, arguably more surgically relevant, 3-tier system for dysplastic/dislocated hips

| Type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| A — Dysplasia | Femoral head remains contained within a true, albeit shallow/deformed, acetabulum | THA with standard-size cup at or near anatomic hip center; PAO an alternative in the precartilage-loss stage |
| B — Low (partial) dislocation | Femoral head articulates with a false acetabulum that partially overlaps the true acetabulum | Cup placed at true acetabulum, often with superolateral structural autograft for coverage; femoral shortening frequently needed |
| C — High (complete) dislocation | Femoral head has migrated superiorly/posteriorly with no contact with the true acetabulum; false acetabulum entirely separate | Cup at true acetabulum, subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy essentially mandatory, small components, high nerve-injury risk — most technically demanding THA subgroup |

### Warwick Agreement (2016 international consensus statement) — FAI syndrome definition and management framework

The Warwick Agreement (Griffin et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2016) did not create a numeric grading system but established consensus terminology and diagnostic/management criteria, agreed by 22 panelists across 5 specialties. Key consensus points with direct management implications:

| Consensus domain | Statement | Practical implication |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis (triad) | **FAI syndrome** requires the coexistence of (1) symptoms, (2) clinical signs (e.g., positive FADIR), and (3) imaging findings (cam and/or pincer morphology) — morphology alone on imaging without symptoms is **not** a diagnosis | Avoid operating on incidental cam/pincer morphology found on imaging in asymptomatic patients |
| First-line treatment | Conservative care (activity modification, physiotherapy, education) should be tried before surgery in most patients | Structures the treatment ladder — surgery reserved for refractory symptomatic structural FAI |
| Surgical indication | Surgery (open or arthroscopic) is indicated for symptomatic FAI syndrome that has failed conservative treatment, aiming to correct morphology and treat labral/chondral damage | Confirms arthroscopy is not first-line even when morphology is present |
| Outcome measurement | Patient-reported outcome measures (iHOT-33/12, HOS, NAHS) should be used to assess results, alongside standardized imaging measurements (alpha angle, LCEA) | Basis for structured PROM-driven follow-up in hip-preservation practice |
| Terminology | Adopted "FAI syndrome" (rather than "FAI") to emphasize it is a clinical syndrome, not a radiographic finding | Changed literature/coding nomenclature post-2016 |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**ONFH.** Precollapse asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic small lesions (Steinberg A, ARCO I–II with limited extent) can be observed with serial imaging every 3–6 months and risk-factor modification (steroid minimization where medically feasible, alcohol cessation, anticoagulation/lipid management if a coagulopathy or dyslipidemia is implicated). Protected weight-bearing (crutches) for 6–12 weeks has weak evidence for altering natural history but is commonly recommended pragmatically during the diagnostic/decision window. In postcollapse or elderly/low-demand patients unfit for surgery, activity modification and analgesia are appropriate pending eventual arthroplasty.

**FAI/labral tear.** The Warwick Agreement and subsequent RCT evidence (FASHIoN, FAIR trials — see Recent Evidence) support structured physiotherapy as first-line: hip/core strengthening (particularly gluteal and deep rotator control), activity/movement pattern modification (avoiding deep flexion-internal rotation loading), and manual therapy/education, generally trialed for a minimum of 8–12 weeks, often 3–6 months, before considering surgery, except when mechanical locking/catching or a large unstable labral flap dominates the picture.

**Dysplasia.** Symptomatic but low-grade dysplasia, particularly borderline dysplasia (LCEA 18–25°), may respond to abductor/core strengthening programs analogous to instability rehabilitation, weight optimization, and activity modification; nonoperative treatment does not address the underlying structural undercoverage and is a bridge/adjunct rather than a durable solution once symptoms are significant.

**GTPS/gluteal tendinopathy.** First-line and, per current systematic review evidence, arguably as effective in the medium term as injection: a progressive loading program (isometric holds progressing to isotonic and then energy-storage/plyometric loading), avoidance of compressive positions (standing with hip hitched/adducted, cross-leg sitting, side-lying directly on the trochanter without a pillow), weight management, and correction of contributing biomechanical factors (ITB tightness, contralateral pelvic drop). A 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis (Fearon and colleagues' line of work; Scientific Reports 2024) found exercise-based interventions superior to minimal intervention and comparable to corticosteroid injection at medium-term follow-up, with better durability than injection alone.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **ONFH**: no injection has an established disease-modifying role; intra-articular steroid is avoided given theoretical concerns about accelerating osteonecrosis/collapse and because pain in early ONFH is typically intraosseous rather than synovial.
- **FAI/labral tear**: fluoroscopic or ultrasound-guided intra-articular local anesthetic ± corticosteroid injection serves a dual diagnostic (confirming an intra-articular source of pain, strong predictor of a positive surgical response if arthroscopy is subsequently pursued) and short-term therapeutic role; repeated injections are discouraged given theoretical chondrotoxicity and the risk of masking progressive labral/chondral damage.
- **Dysplasia**: same diagnostic/temporizing injection role as FAI; particularly useful to differentiate intra-articular pain from extra-articular abductor overload that commonly coexists in dysplastic hips (compensatory gluteal overuse).
- **GTPS/gluteal tendinopathy**: ultrasound-guided peritrochanteric corticosteroid injection provides superior short-term (4–8 week) pain relief versus exercise alone or "wait and see," but this advantage is not sustained beyond approximately 8–12 weeks, and multiple RCTs (including the 2018 LEAP trial and subsequent reviews) show exercise therapy achieves equal or superior outcomes at 8–15 months with a more favorable risk profile (repeated corticosteroid exposure raises theoretical concern for tendon attritional weakening/progression to full-thickness tear). **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)** has emerging but heterogeneous evidence — some trials show benefit over corticosteroid at 12 months, but a 2025 systematic review (Bremer et al.) concluded evidence quality remains low-to-moderate and protocols are non-standardized. **Barbotage/needle tenotomy** and **shockwave therapy** are adjuncts with modest supporting evidence, generally reserved for refractory reactive tendinopathy without frank tearing.
- **Core decompression with biologic augmentation** (technically an operative-adjacent procedure, discussed further under Operative): bone marrow aspirate concentrate (BMAC), concentrated bone marrow mononuclear cells, and platelet-rich plasma are injected into the decompression tract; multiple 2024 meta-analyses (see Recent Evidence) show improved clinical scores and reduced collapse/conversion-to-THA rates versus core decompression alone in early-stage (ARCO I–II) disease, though heterogeneity in cell preparation and lesion size limits strength of recommendation.

### Operative

**Osteonecrosis of the femoral head**

- **Core decompression**: the mainstay joint-preserving procedure for precollapse (ARCO 0–IIC/Ficat-Arlet I–II) symptomatic disease. Technique: one or multiple small-diameter drill tracts (multiple small-diameter "multiple drilling" technique) or a single larger-diameter (8–10 mm) core tract under fluoroscopic guidance, entering below the greater trochanter and directed into the necrotic lesion, stopping just short of the subchondral plate to avoid iatrogenic fracture. Pearls: enter lateral cortex low enough to avoid a stress riser at the subtrochanteric region (fracture risk with high entry points and large-diameter cores); confirm needle-tip position in two fluoroscopic planes before decompression; avoid breaching the articular surface. Evidence favors better outcomes in small-to-medium lesions (Steinberg <30%, medial/central location) and worse outcomes in large lateral lesions — the single strongest predictor of core decompression failure is precollapse lesion size and, once present, degree of collapse.
- **Core decompression + biologic augmentation** (BMAC, concentrated bone marrow mono/mesenchymal cells, ± demineralized bone matrix or structural graft through the tract): increasingly the default technique at hip-preservation centers for ARCO I–II disease given consistent 2023–2024 meta-analytic signal of improved pain scores and reduced collapse rates versus decompression alone (see Recent Evidence).
- **Vascularized fibular grafting (free vascularized fibular graft, "trap-door" technique) or vascularized iliac crest graft**: reserved for larger precollapse or early postcollapse (ARCO IIIA) lesions in young, active patients strongly motivated to avoid/delay arthroplasty; technically demanding (microsurgical anastomosis), donor-site morbidity (fibular graft: risk of ankle instability, EHL weakness, sensory deficit), but historically the best reported joint-preservation results for large lesions in expert hands (reported survivorship 70–80% at 5–10 years in selected series), largely superseded in general practice by core decompression + biologics given the morbidity/complexity trade-off, but retained as an option at specialized centers.
- **Rotational osteotomy (Sugioka transtrochanteric anterior/posterior rotational osteotomy) or angular intertrochanteric osteotomy**: rotates the necrotic segment out of the primary weight-bearing zone into an area of intact cartilage; technically demanding, largely practiced in Asian hip-preservation centers with the most published experience; candidacy requires a small-to-moderate lesion, an intact posteroinferior (or anteroinferior) viable segment to rotate into the weight-bearing dome, and open triradiate-independent skeletal maturity; converts to THA if failed, though prior osteotomy increases THA technical complexity (altered proximal femoral anatomy/hardware).
- **Total hip arthroplasty**: the definitive procedure for postcollapse disease (ARCO IIIB–IV) or failed joint preservation, and increasingly considered earlier in older/lower-demand patients even at ARCO IIIA given predictable, durable outcomes with modern bearings. Technical considerations specific to ONFH: younger average patient age than typical THA populations (often <50, particularly steroid- and alcohol-related and sickle cell disease) drives preference for highly cross-linked polyethylene or ceramic-on-ceramic bearings to minimize wear-related revision risk over a longer expected implant lifespan; underlying etiology matters — sickle cell disease patients have elevated perioperative complication rates (infection, delayed wound healing, thromboembolism) requiring hematology co-management and preoperative exchange transfusion protocols in some centers; corticosteroid-associated ONFH patients often have osteopenic bone affecting fixation choice (cementless fixation remains standard but component sizing/press-fit stability should be confirmed intraoperatively).

**Femoroacetabular impingement / labral tear**

- **Hip arthroscopy** is the standard surgical approach for symptomatic structural FAI syndrome refractory to conservative care, with the goal of (1) correcting cam and/or pincer morphology (femoral osteochondroplasty, acetabular rim trimming), (2) treating the labrum (repair preferred over debridement when tissue quality permits — see Recent Evidence for comparative data), and (3) capsular management (closure/plication increasingly favored over unrepaired capsulotomy, given biomechanical and clinical data on postoperative microinstability). Patient selection is critical: best outcomes in Tönnis grade 0–1, joint space ≥2 mm, age <40 (though good results reported to 50s in appropriately selected patients), absence of significant dysplasia (LCEA <20° is a relative contraindication to isolated arthroscopic rim trimming, since further bony resection in an already undercovered hip risks iatrogenic instability).
- **Labral repair vs. debridement vs. reconstruction**: repair is favored when labral tissue is of adequate quality and length, with systematic review/registry data (German Cartilage Registry, 2024) showing both repair and selective debridement achieve >90% THA-free survival at 5 years, but repair trends toward superior patient-reported outcome scores where head-to-head data exist. Labral reconstruction (autograft — iliotibial band or capsule; or allograft) is reserved for irreparable tears, prior failed repair, or hypoplastic/absent labral tissue, with durable outcomes reported at minimum 5-year follow-up in recent series.
- **Open surgical hip dislocation (Ganz approach)**: reserved for complex or combined cam/pincer deformity not amenable to arthroscopic correction, extensive posterior/global overcoverage, or combined deformity requiring simultaneous femoral and acetabular correction with direct visualization; higher morbidity (trochanteric osteotomy, longer rehabilitation) but unmatched exposure.
- **Periacetabular osteotomy in borderline dysplasia with FAI-type symptoms**: an important and actively studied gray zone (LCEA 18–25°) — recent 2024–2025 systematic reviews/meta-analyses comparing PAO versus arthroscopy in borderline dysplasia show both achieve clinically meaningful improvement, with a trend toward the literature favoring PAO (alone or combined with arthroscopy) when instability features (positive anterior/posterior apprehension, acetabular version abnormality) dominate, and isolated arthroscopy favored when impingement features dominate without coverage-based instability.

**Adult hip dysplasia**

- **Periacetabular osteotomy (Bernese/Ganz PAO)**: the procedure of choice for symptomatic dysplasia in skeletally mature patients (closed triradiate cartilage) with a congruent joint and preserved cartilage (Tönnis 0–1, occasionally selected Tönnis 2). Technique: peri-acetabular cuts (ischial, superior pubic ramus, iliac, and posterior column osteotomies) allow free rotation and reorientation of an acetabular fragment with intact posterior column and blood supply, correcting lateral (LCEA), anterior (anterior center-edge angle on false-profile), and version deformities simultaneously, followed by internal fixation with screws. Correction targets: LCEA 25–35° (undercorrection risks persistent instability; overcorrection >40° risks iatrogenic pincer impingement and is a recognized cause of PAO failure), acetabular version normalized, medialization of the hip center ≤10 mm. Concomitant hip arthroscopy or arthrotomy for labral/chondral treatment and femoral osteochondroplasty (combined "FAO," femoral-acetabular osteotomy) is increasingly performed when cam morphology or labral pathology coexists, which is common in dysplastic hips. Complication profile: nerve injury (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve most common, sciatic/femoral rare but serious), nonunion (low, <5% in experienced hands), heterotopic ossification, and a long learning curve — outcomes are volume-dependent and PAO is best performed at high-volume hip-preservation centers. Reported 10–20 year THA-free survivorship in appropriately selected patients (Tönnis 0 preoperatively) exceeds 60–90% in mature cohort series.
- **Total hip arthroplasty for dysplasia**: indicated once secondary osteoarthritis is established (Tönnis ≥2) or in older patients, and is technically demanding in direct proportion to Crowe/Hartofilakidis severity. Key technical issues: acetabular component sizing and coverage in a shallow, often retroverted/anteverted socket (small cups, medialization to the true floor, occasional structural autograft for superolateral coverage in Crowe II–III); leg-length management (limb lengthening beyond 3–4 cm risks sciatic nerve palsy, mandating subtrochanteric shortening derotational osteotomy in Crowe III–IV/Hartofilakidis B–C); femoral deformity (excessive anteversion, narrow diametaphyseal canal) often requires modular or small-stature stems; abductor deficiency and altered hip biomechanics from chronic subluxation affect postoperative limp risk even with technically sound reconstruction.

**Gluteal tendinopathy / GTPS**

- **Endoscopic (or open) gluteus medius/minimus repair**: indicated for confirmed partial- or full-thickness tears on MRI with a positive clinical correlate (weakness, Trendelenburg, refractory pain) after ≥6 months of failed conservative management. Technique parallels rotator cuff repair principles — debridement of degenerative tendon, anchor-based transosseous-equivalent repair to the trochanteric facets, with endoscopic approach favored for lower morbidity and direct bursal visualization; open repair (occasionally with local tissue augmentation or synthetic/biologic patch for severe retraction/fatty infiltration) reserved for large retracted tears or revision settings. Outcomes are good-to-excellent in properly selected patients (partial tears, minimal fatty infiltration) but deteriorate with chronic full-thickness tears and significant muscle atrophy (Goutallier grade ≥3), where repair integrity and functional recovery are compromised.
- **Iliotibial band release/bursectomy**: rarely performed as an isolated procedure now that GTPS is understood as primarily a tendinopathy; may be added at the time of gluteal repair for coexisting external coxa saltans (snapping hip) or a thickened, symptomatic ITB.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Core decompression (ONFH)**

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Toe-touch to partial (protected) weight-bearing on crutches | Pain control, gentle hip ROM, avoid high-impact/pivoting activity |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to full weight-bearing as tolerated if imaging/symptoms allow | Stationary bike, pool therapy, progressive strengthening |
| III | 3–6 months | Full weight-bearing | Return to low-impact recreational activity; repeat MRI at 3 and 6 months to confirm no interval collapse before clearing higher-impact activity |
| IV | 6–12 months | Full | Return to unrestricted activity if imaging stable and asymptomatic; ongoing surveillance imaging at 6–12 month intervals given risk of late collapse/contralateral disease |

**Hip arthroscopy (FAI/labral repair)**

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing / ROM | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I — Protection | Weeks 0–2 | 20 lb flat-foot/partial weight-bearing with crutches (protocols vary; labral repair generally more restrictive than isolated debridement); hip flexion limited to <90°, avoid extremes of external rotation to protect capsular repair | Circumduction/passive ROM, quadriceps/gluteal activation, cryotherapy, CPM or stationary bike (no resistance) in many protocols |
| II — Early strengthening | Weeks 2–6 | Progress to full weight-bearing off crutches by ~2–4 weeks (per surgeon protocol and intraoperative findings — microfracture or extensive osteoplasty may extend protection) | Progressive ROM to full, closed-chain strengthening (bridges, mini-squats), gait normalization, aquatic therapy |
| III — Strength/neuromuscular | Weeks 6–12 | Full weight-bearing, full ROM | Hip/core/pelvic strengthening progression, single-leg stability work, stationary bike/elliptical, begin light jogging around 8–12 weeks if strength and gait criteria met |
| IV — Return to sport | 3–6 months | — | Sport-specific drills, plyometrics, cutting/pivoting progression; return-to-sport criteria: symmetric strength (≥90% of contralateral limb on isokinetic/functional testing), full pain-free ROM, successful completion of sport-specific functional testing, typically 4–6 months for pivoting/contact sport, earlier (~3–4 months) for running-based sport |

Return-to-work timing after hip arthroscopy: sedentary work 1–2 weeks, physically demanding/manual labor 8–12 weeks depending on procedure extent (isolated labral repair faster than combined extensive osteochondroplasty).

**PAO**

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Touch-down/partial weight-bearing (foot-flat, ~20 lb) on crutches; hip precautions to protect osteotomy fixation | Pain control, gentle passive/active-assisted ROM, isometric activation, DVT prophylaxis |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to weight-bearing as tolerated once radiographic union of osteotomy sites confirmed (typically by 6–8 weeks) | Stationary bike, pool therapy, progressive closed-chain strengthening, gait retraining off crutches |
| III | 3–6 months | Full weight-bearing | Progressive strengthening (abductors, core), balance/proprioception, low-impact conditioning |
| IV — Return to sport/high demand activity | 6–12 months | — | Full radiographic healing, symmetric strength, sport-specific progression; impact/pivoting sport typically not cleared before 6–9 months; heavy manual labor return around 4–6 months, sedentary work 6–8 weeks |

**Gluteal tendon repair**

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | Milestones |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Weeks 0–6 | Protected weight-bearing (crutches, brace in abduction/neutral rotation per surgeon preference) to protect repair | Passive ROM only, avoid active abduction, cryotherapy |
| II | Weeks 6–12 | Progress to full weight-bearing, wean brace/crutches | Begin isometric then gentle active-assisted abductor activation, gait retraining (avoid Trendelenburg compensation pattern) |
| III | 3–6 months | Full | Progressive isotonic strengthening, closed-chain single-leg work, return to low-impact activity |
| IV | 6–9 months | — | Return to running/impact activity once strength ≥85–90% of contralateral side and pain-free single-leg stance/step-down testing achieved |

**Non-operative GTPS/tendinopathy loading program**: isometric abductor holds (pain-relieving, low compressive load) for the first 1–2 weeks in irritable presentations, progressing over 8–12 weeks to isotonic strengthening (side-lying and standing abduction, step-downs) and finally energy-storage/functional loading (single-leg squats, lateral lunges) for higher-demand patients, always avoiding end-range hip adduction/compression positions throughout.

## Complications

**ONFH-specific**
- Core decompression: subtrochanteric stress fracture (particularly with large-diameter cores or low medial entry points) — incidence low (<5%) but avoidable with technique attention; heterotopic bone formation in the tract; failure to prevent collapse in large/lateral lesions (up to 30–50% failure at 5 years for large lesions).
- Vascularized grafting: donor-site morbidity (fibular graft — ankle instability, sensory deficit, rare compartment syndrome), nonunion of the graft, technical failure of microvascular anastomosis.
- THA in ONFH population: higher revision risk historically reported versus THA for primary osteoarthritis (younger patients, higher activity demand, some etiologies — sickle cell, chronic steroid use — carry elevated infection and periprosthetic fracture risk); bilateral disease means contralateral surveillance and counseling are essential.

**FAI/hip arthroscopy-specific**
- Iatrogenic instability from over-resection of acetabular rim (especially in undiagnosed borderline dysplasia) — one of the most consequential and preventable complications; underscores the importance of pre-operative LCEA assessment.
- Under-resection of cam lesion — the most common reason for revision arthroscopy/persistent symptoms.
- Femoral neck fracture from aggressive osteochondroplasty weakening the neck (rare, catastrophic).
- Heterotopic ossification, transient traction-related neurapraxia (pudendal, sciatic, femoral — usually resolves), adhesions/capsular scarring in a minority.
- Progression to osteoarthritis/THA conversion in a meaningful minority over 10+ years even after successful arthroscopy, particularly with pre-existing joint space narrowing or advanced chondral damage at index surgery (see Recent Evidence — conversion rates up to ~24% at 11–12 years in cohorts with baseline OA).

**PAO-specific**
- Nonunion of osteotomy sites (<5% experienced centers), lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury (common, usually transient), sciatic/femoral nerve injury (rare, <1–2%, more common with concurrent correction of severe deformity), intra-articular penetration of osteotomy cuts, over/under-correction, heterotopic ossification, and — the long-term concern — conversion to THA in a minority, technically more complex after PAO due to altered version/anatomy and retained hardware.

**Dysplasia/complex THA-specific**
- Nerve palsy (sciatic most common) from acute limb lengthening beyond safe threshold (~3–4 cm) — mitigated by subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy in Crowe III–IV.
- Nonunion at the shortening osteotomy site, component malposition due to distorted anatomy, higher dislocation risk from soft-tissue laxity/abductor deficiency.

**GTPS/gluteal repair-specific**
- Persistent abductor weakness/Trendelenburg gait if repair fails or muscle quality was poor preoperatively (fatty infiltration is the strongest negative predictor).
- Retear, particularly in large chronic tears with retraction.
- Corticosteroid injection risk: theoretical acceleration of tendon degeneration/attritional tearing with repeated dosing — a rationale for limiting injections to 1–2 per year and favoring exercise-first strategies.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[The 2019 Revised Version of ARCO Staging System](https://www.arthroplastyjournal.org/article/S0883-5403(19)31101-5/abstract)** and subsequent reliability studies, including a **[2023 interobserver reliability study of the 2021 ARCO classification](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10249300/)** reporting a kappa of 0.652 and 90.25% agreement — confirms the revised (2 mm collapse threshold, IIIA/IIIB split) staging is reproducible enough for routine clinical decision-making and remains the reference staging system referenced in this monograph.
- **[Core Decompression With Bone Marrow Aspirate Concentrate for Avascular Necrosis of the Femoral Head (2025, PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11869797/)** — adds to the growing body of evidence that BMAC augmentation of core decompression improves clinical outcomes in early-stage disease; strengthens the case for biologic augmentation as an increasingly standard adjunct rather than an experimental add-on.
- **[Systematic review and meta-analysis of core decompression + regenerative therapy vs. decompression alone, 954 subjects (2024/2025, PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11747542/)** — pooled analysis through July 2024 found regenerative augmentation (PRP/BMAC/MSC) associated with meaningful short-to-mid-term functional and symptomatic improvement over decompression alone, reinforcing the treatment-ladder positioning of biologics in this monograph.
- **[Core decompression combined with bone marrow mononuclear cells: systematic review and meta-analysis, International Journal of Surgery, October 2024](https://journals.lww.com/international-journal-of-surgery/fulltext/2024/10000/core_decompression_combined_with_bone_marrow.69.aspx)** — found superior VAS pain outcomes with cell augmentation versus decompression alone, further consolidating the evidence base for biologic-augmented core decompression as first-line surgical treatment for precollapse ONFH.
- **[Effectiveness of Different Types of Core Decompression in Early-Stage ONFH: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, MDPI 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3271/13/4/258)** — compares multiple small-diameter drilling versus large-diameter single-core techniques, informing technique selection discussed under Operative Treatment.
- **[The efficacy of Denosumab in the treatment of femoral head osteonecrosis: retrospective comparative study, Scientific Reports 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54685-7)** — reported femoral head collapse in 24.7% of denosumab-treated ARCO I–II patients versus 38.1% of controls, the strongest signal to date for a disease-modifying pharmacologic option in early-stage ONFH; still investigational/off-label and not yet incorporated into formal society guidelines, but relevant for shared decision-making discussions in steroid-induced disease.
- **[Modern Hip Arthroscopy for FAIS May Delay the Natural History of Osteoarthritis in 25% of Patients: A 12-Year Follow-up Analysis, AJSM 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38459690/)** — matched-pair analysis showing a 42% relative risk reduction in OA progression at 12 years in operated versus non-operated contralateral hips, among the longest follow-up data supporting a disease-modifying (not just symptomatic) benefit of arthroscopic correction of FAI morphology in appropriately selected patients.
- **[Ten-year outcomes of hip arthroscopy for FAI with osteoarthritis, PMC 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12205409/)** and **[High Survival Rate and Satisfaction at >11-Year Follow-Up, Arthroscopy 2025](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2024.08.012)** — both report sustained functional benefit but a substantial (up to ~24%) conversion-to-THA rate at 10+ years in cohorts with baseline Tönnis 1–2 changes, refining patient counseling: arthroscopy delays but does not reliably prevent arthroplasty once baseline degenerative change is present at index surgery.
- **[Mid- to long-term outcomes of capsular management in hip arthroscopy for FAIS: multilevel meta-analysis, PMC 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12850580/)** — comparing capsule preserved/repaired/unrepaired strategies through studies to May 2025; supports the current trend toward routine capsular closure/plication over leaving the capsulotomy unrepaired, cited under Operative Treatment.
- **[Both Labral Debridement and Labral Repair Result in >90% THA-free Survival at 5-Year Follow-Up: German Cartilage Registry (KnorpelRegister DGOU), Arthroscopy 2024](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2023.04.014)** — large registry data confirming both techniques are durable at 5 years from a THA-avoidance standpoint, though PROM data elsewhere continue to favor repair when tissue quality allows (see Operative Treatment).
- **[Patient-Reported Outcomes After PAO Versus Hip Arthroscopy for Borderline Acetabular Dysplasia Are Both Favorable: Systematic Review, Arthroscopy 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749806324010223)** and the **[2025 systematic review and multi-level meta-analysis, Wiley/PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12221243/)** — the most current comparative evidence in the actively contested borderline-dysplasia (LCEA 18–25°) treatment gray zone; both interventions show clinically meaningful improvement without a clear across-the-board winner, reinforcing the individualized instability-versus-impingement-dominant framework used in this monograph's Operative Treatment section.
- **[Patients Undergoing Hip Arthroscopy With Concomitant PAO Demonstrate Clinically Meaningful Improvement at 2 Years, Arthroscopy 2025](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2024.08.044)** — supports the growing practice of combined arthroscopic and osteotomy correction in dysplastic hips with coexisting FAI/labral pathology, discussed under Operative Treatment (combined "FAO").
- **[The efficacy of gluteal tendinopathy treatments: A systematic review, Bremer, Nicklen, Fearon, Morrissey, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02692155251327298)** — a comprehensive contemporary review (search to August 2024) across exercise, injection (corticosteroid and PRP), and surgical management; reinforces exercise as the preferred first-line intervention with the most favorable long-term risk-benefit profile among conservative options.
- **[Exercise compared to control or other conservative treatments in GTPS: systematic review and meta-analysis, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38295551/)** and the companion **[exercise-based interventions meta-analysis, Scientific Reports 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-53283-x)** — together provide the strongest current quantitative support for structured loading programs over corticosteroid injection at medium-to-long-term follow-up, directly informing the treatment-ladder ranking in this monograph's Quick Reference.
- **[What factors influence pain scores following corticosteroid injection in GTPS? A systematic review, PMC 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10874005/)** — clarifies that injection response is heterogeneous and predictors are not yet reliable, supporting a cautious, time-limited role for corticosteroid injection rather than a default second-line therapy.
