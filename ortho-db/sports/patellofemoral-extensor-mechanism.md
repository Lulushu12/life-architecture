## Quick Reference

**Red flags / do-not-miss**
- **Extensor lag / inability to straight-leg raise after trauma** → assume complete quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture until proven otherwise; missed diagnosis rate remains 10–50% at first presentation (delayed repair worsens outcome).
- **Palpable patellar/quadriceps tendon defect + hemarthrosis + high-riding or low-riding patella on lateral X-ray** → acute rupture, surgical urgency (ideally <2–3 weeks).
- **First-time dislocation with large osteochondral fragment or persistent hemarthrosis** → MRI mandatory, consider urgent arthroscopy/fixation.
- **Bilateral atraumatic quadriceps tendon rupture** → screen for chronic kidney disease, hyperparathyroidism, gout, fluoroquinolone use, diabetes, SLE, or steroid/statin exposure.
- **Skeletally immature patient with instability** → check physeal status before MPFL femoral tunnel planning (physeal-sparing techniques required).

**Key numbers**
| Parameter | Normal | Pathologic threshold | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| TT-TG (CT/MRI) | <15 mm | **>20 mm** = classic surgical indication for TTO | 15–20 mm = grey zone, weigh with other risk factors |
| TT-TG (MRI, cartilage-based) | — | often 3–5 mm less than CT-bone-based | use consistent modality when trending |
| Caton-Deschamps index | 0.6–1.2 (mean ~1.0) | >1.2 = patella alta; >1.3 severe | alta increases instability & anterior knee pain risk |
| Sulcus angle | ~138° | >145° = dysplastic | part of Dejour classification |
| Insall-Salvati | 0.8–1.2 | supportive but less reliable post-op | use CD index as primary height index |
| First-time dislocation recurrence (conservative) | — | **≈25–35%** | up to 50% with additional persistent symptoms |
| Recurrence after isolated MPFL-R | — | **~3–8%** | lower with concomitant bony correction when indicated |
| Patellar tendon repair union/return to sport | — | **>90%** return to sport; RTS ~5–9 months | strength deficits can persist >1 year |
| Quadriceps tendon re-rupture | — | **2–6%** | higher in delayed repair, comorbid disease |

**Treatment ladder (compact)**

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patellofemoral pain syndrome | Activity modification, hip/quad strengthening (VMO-biased + proximal hip), patellar taping trial | Foot orthoses if pes planus, short course NSAIDs | Rarely indicated; only after failed 6–12 mo comprehensive rehab and clear structural cause |
| First-time patellar dislocation, low risk (no loose body, TT-TG <20, low Dejour grade) | Brief immobilization, early ROM, quad/VMO rehab | Bracing (patellofemoral brace) during return to sport | MPFL-R if recurrence or persistent apprehension |
| First-time dislocation, high risk (osteochondral fragment, high-grade dysplasia, patella alta, TT-TG >20) | — | — | Early MPFL-R ± à-la-carte bony procedure (TTO, trochleoplasty) |
| Recurrent instability | — | — | MPFL-R ± TTO ± trochleoplasty per à-la-carte algorithm |
| Patellar tendinopathy | Relative load management, isometrics for pain, education | Heavy slow resistance (HSR) or eccentric decline squat program 12 wk; consider shockwave; PRP investigational | Surgical debridement/tendon scraping only after ≥6 months failed comprehensive loading program |
| Patellar tendon rupture | — | — | Primary repair (transosseous tunnel or suture anchor) within 2–3 wk, augmentation if needed |
| Quadriceps tendon rupture | — | — | Primary repair (suture anchor or transosseous), earlier the better; augment/allograft if chronic/retracted |

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The **extensor mechanism** spans quadriceps muscle → quadriceps tendon (trilaminar: rectus femoris superficial, vasti intermediate/deep) → patella → patellar tendon → tibial tubercle. The patella is the largest sesamoid bone, functioning to increase the quadriceps lever arm (~30–50% torque gain) and centralize forces from the four quadriceps components onto the patellar tendon.

**Patellofemoral stability** is a composite of:
- **Static soft-tissue restraints**: the **medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL)** is the primary passive restraint to lateral translation, providing 50–60% of restraining force in the first 20–30° of flexion (before the trochlear groove takes over as bony constraint). Originates near the adductor tubercle/medial epicondyle, inserts on the proximal-medial patella. Medial patellotibial ligament and medial patellomeniscal ligament are secondary restraints.
- **Bony geometry**: trochlear depth/morphology (dysplasia), patellar height (alta reduces bony engagement in early flexion), and tibial tubercle position relative to the trochlear groove (TT-TG, rotational alignment).
- **Dynamic restraints**: VMO (vastus medialis obliquus) timing/strength, hip external rotator/abductor control of femoral internal rotation (a major contributor to dynamic valgus and lateral patellar tracking).
- **Torsional/coronal alignment**: increased femoral anteversion, external tibial torsion, and genu valgum each increase the effective Q-angle and lateral vector on the patella.

**Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS)** is a diagnosis of exclusion representing anterior knee pain from altered patellofemoral joint loading/tracking without frank instability or structural lesion — multifactorial: quadriceps/hip weakness, movement pattern (dynamic knee valgus), training-load errors, and occasionally mild maltracking. It is NOT synonymous with chondromalacia patellae (a structural cartilage diagnosis) though the terms are historically conflated.

**Patellar tendinopathy ("jumper's knee")** is a failed-healing response at the proximal patellar tendon-bone interface (deep posterior fibers of the proximal pole, in >80% of cases) from repetitive tensile/compressive overload in jump-and-land sports (volleyball, basketball). Histology shows tendinosis (mucoid degeneration, neovascularization, hypercellularity) rather than classic inflammation — the rationale for "tendinopathy" nomenclature over "tendinitis."

**Tendon ruptures**: quadriceps tendon ruptures typically occur in patients >40 years (often with degenerative/metabolic tendon disease — CKD on dialysis, diabetes, gout, hyperparathyroidism, SLE, fluoroquinolone or statin exposure) via eccentric quadriceps contraction against a flexed knee (e.g., stumbling). Patellar tendon ruptures occur in a younger population (<40 years), often with pre-existing tendinopathy or after patellar tendon graft harvest, steroid injection, or in weightlifters — likewise via forceful eccentric loading. The relative rarity of patellar tendon rupture versus quadriceps rupture in older patients reflects the patellar tendon's larger cross-sectional area and lower baseline degenerative burden.

**Per-diagnosis pathophysiology notes**

- **Patellar instability/dislocation**: nearly always a lateral dislocation (medial patellar dislocation is a rare, usually iatrogenic entity after over-aggressive lateral release). The MPFL tears in >90% of first-time dislocations — most commonly at the femoral origin (~50-60%), followed by mid-substance and patellar-sided avulsion (~15-30%, historically associated with higher failure if only repaired rather than reconstructed). Concurrent osteochondral shear injury to the medial patellar facet and/or lateral femoral condyle occurs in a substantial minority (up to ~40-50% in some MRI series) and is the main driver of long-term post-traumatic patellofemoral arthrosis risk if missed.
- **Trochlear dysplasia**: a developmental failure of the trochlear groove to deepen normally during growth, believed related to abnormal patellofemoral joint reaction forces in utero/early childhood (patella not centered → groove doesn't form). It is present in the majority of patients with recurrent instability (studies report dysplasia in >85% of recurrent dislocators) and is the single strongest anatomic risk factor for redislocation.
- **PFPS**: increasingly understood as a load-capacity mismatch — the patellofemoral joint's tolerance to compressive/shear load is exceeded by training demands, compounded by proximal (hip) kinetic chain deficits driving dynamic knee valgus and lateral patellar overload, rather than a single anatomic "maltracking" lesion in most patients.
- **Patellar tendinopathy**: mechanical model centers on the proximal-pole "compressive-tensile" zone where the tendon wraps the inferior pole of the patella during deep flexion/loaded jump-landing, combined with tensile overload during rapid force generation (jumping, cutting) — explains the strong association with volleyball/basketball ("jumper's knee") and why proximal-pole-specific loading (decline squat) targets the pathological zone.
- **Tendon rupture**: both quadriceps and patellar tendon ruptures typically occur through a pre-degenerated tendon (even in "acute" presentations), which is why histologic examination of ruptured tendon almost always shows chronic tendinosis change — true rupture of a fully healthy tendon under physiologic load is exceedingly rare and should prompt a search for a systemic/metabolic contributor.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (twisting/pivoting with valgus-external rotation for dislocation vs. eccentric quad contraction for rupture), sensation of the kneecap "going out," prior dislocation episodes, sporting demands, anterior knee pain pattern (activity-related, worse with stairs/squatting/prolonged sitting = "theater sign" for PFPS), and localized proximal patellar tendon pain with jumping (tendinopathy).

**Examination**
- **Instability**: apprehension test (lateral patellar glide at 20–30° flexion reproducing apprehension), patellar glide quadrant test, J-sign (lateral patellar shift in terminal extension indicating maltracking), assess for generalized ligamentous laxity (Beighton score), Q-angle, femoral anteversion (trochanter prominence angle test), tibial torsion, foot posture.
- **PFPS**: pain with resisted quad extension/squatting, patellar compression (grind) test, assess hip abductor/external rotator strength (single-leg squat for dynamic valgus), VMO bulk/timing.
- **Tendinopathy**: focal tenderness at proximal patellar pole (knee extended, quad relaxed), pain reproduced with single-leg decline squat.
- **Rupture**: palpable suprapatellar (quad) or infrapatellar (patellar tendon) gap, effusion/hemarthrosis, **inability to perform or maintain a straight-leg raise** against gravity (extensor lag) is the cardinal sign — always test even when radiographs look unremarkable, since partial ruptures with intact retinacula may still permit weak SLR.

**Imaging**
- **Radiographs**: AP, lateral (true lateral with femoral condyles overlapped — for Caton-Deschamps and Dejour crossing sign/double contour), and **Merchant/skyline (axial) view** at 30–45° flexion for sulcus angle, subluxation, trochlear dysplasia signs. Lateral view also identifies patella alta/baja after rupture (patella baja with quad rupture due to unopposed patellar tendon pull; patella alta with patellar tendon rupture).
- **MRI**: gold standard for MPFL tear location/pattern (femoral, mid-substance, or patellar avulsion — patellar-sided avulsions have higher failure rate with repair alone), osteochondral lesions of the medial patellar facet/lateral femoral condyle (typical after dislocation), trochlear morphology, patellar tendon/quadriceps tendon integrity and retraction gap, and confirms partial vs. complete tears.
- **CT**: preferred for precise TT-TG measurement (though MRI increasingly used to avoid radiation, with lower absolute values), rotational profile (femoral anteversion, tibial torsion) when planning derotational osteotomy.
- **Ultrasound**: useful bedside/dynamic tool for tendinopathy (hypoechoic thickening, neovascularity on Doppler) and for confirming tendon rupture gap when MRI unavailable/contraindicated.

**Key special tests at a glance**

| Test | Technique | Positive finding suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Apprehension (Fairbank) | Lateral glide of patella at 20-30° flexion | Patellar instability / MPFL insufficiency |
| Patellar glide (quadrant) test | Medial/lateral translation in quadrants of patellar width at 20-30° flexion | >2 quadrants lateral glide = excessive laxity |
| J-sign | Observe patella during active extension from flexion | Lateral maltracking near terminal extension, often with alta |
| Patellar tilt test | Attempt to lift lateral facet with knee extended | Tight lateral retinaculum |
| Clarke's / patellar grind test | Compress patella against trochlea during quad contraction | Nonspecific for patellofemoral chondral pathology/PFPS (poor specificity, use cautiously) |
| Decline single-leg squat | 25° decline board, single-leg squat | Reproduces proximal patellar tendon pain in tendinopathy |
| Active/passive SLR | Attempt straight-leg raise against gravity | Extensor lag = complete or significant partial extensor tendon rupture |
| Beighton score | Generalized joint hypermobility screen | Score ≥4-6/9 correlates with higher instability recurrence risk |

**Adolescent-specific differentials**: in skeletally immature patients with anterior knee pain, always consider **Osgood-Schlatter disease** (tibial tubercle apophysitis, tender/prominent tubercle, self-limiting) and **Sinding-Larsen-Johansson syndrome** (inferior patellar pole apophysitis, analogous traction apophysitis at the proximal patellar tendon origin) before defaulting to a PFPS or tendinopathy label — both are managed with activity modification and quadriceps/patellar tendon loading tolerance rather than the adult tendinopathy loading protocols, and both resolve with physeal closure.

**Differential diagnosis of anterior knee pain** (relevant when PFPS is suspected but exam/imaging is atypical): patellar tendinopathy (focal proximal-pole tenderness, not diffuse), fat pad impingement (Hoffa's, tender inferomedial/inferolateral to patella, worse with hyperextension), plica syndrome (palpable medial plica band, snapping), symptomatic bipartite patella (superolateral fragment tenderness, characteristic radiographic fragment), osteochondritis dissecans of the lateral femoral condyle or patella, prepatellar bursitis (superficial, fluctuant swelling, occupational "housemaid's knee"), referred hip pathology (always screen hip ROM/impingement signs in atypical or refractory "PFPS," especially in adolescents — slipped capital femoral epiphysis presents as knee pain), and early patellofemoral osteoarthritis in older patients (joint space narrowing/osteophytes on skyline view).

## Classification

### Dejour Classification (Trochlear Dysplasia)

Based on axial CT/MRI and lateral radiograph findings (crossing sign, supratrochlear spur, double contour).

| Type | Radiographic/imaging features | Severity | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Type A** | Crossing sign present; trochlea shallow but symmetric, sulcus angle >145° | Mild | Usually MPFL-R alone sufficient if instability present |
| **Type B** | Crossing sign + trochlear spur (flat or convex trochlea in axial images) | Moderate | Consider combined MPFL-R + flattening/deepening trochleoplasty if spur >7 mm or convex |
| **Type C** | Crossing sign + double contour (medial hypoplastic facet, lateral facet convex, asymmetric facets) | Severe | High redislocation risk with MPFL-R alone; strongly consider trochleoplasty |
| **Type D** | Crossing sign + supratrochlear spur + double contour ("cliff pattern"/vertical medial facet) | Most severe | Trochleoplasty (deepening, e.g., Bereiter/Dejour technique) usually indicated in symptomatic recurrent instability |

**Practical point**: Types A/B are often managed with soft-tissue (MPFL-R) ± tibial tubercle correction; Types C/D increasingly favor combined trochleoplasty given persistently elevated redislocation rates with isolated MPFL-R in high-grade dysplasia.

### Caton-Deschamps Index (Patellar Height)

Ratio on true lateral radiograph (knee flexed 30°): **CD = (distance from inferior patellar articular margin to anterosuperior tibial plateau) / (patellar articular surface length)**.

| CD Index | Interpretation | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| <0.6 | Patella baja | Consider in post-traumatic/post-surgical stiffness workup; rarely relevant to instability |
| 0.6–1.2 | Normal | No height-related intervention needed |
| 1.2–1.3 | Patella alta | Risk factor for instability; consider distalizing TTO if combined with other risk factors and symptomatic instability |
| >1.3 | Severe patella alta | Strong indication to include tibial tubercle distalization in surgical plan (à-la-carte) |

### TT-TG Distance Thresholds

Measured on axial CT or MRI as the distance between a line through the deepest point of the trochlear groove and a line through the center of the tibial tubercle, both perpendicular to the posterior condylar axis.

| TT-TG | Interpretation | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| <15 mm | Normal | Not a target for bony correction |
| 15–20 mm | Grey zone | Correlate with other risk factors (dysplasia grade, alta, rotational malalignment); isolated MPFL-R often sufficient |
| **>20 mm** | Classic pathologic threshold (Dejour) | Indication to add **tibial tubercle osteotomy (medialization ± distalization)** to MPFL-R |

**Nuance (2023–2024 literature)**: the 20 mm cutoff is increasingly recognized as imperfect, particularly in high-grade trochlear dysplasia where the "trochlear groove" reference point is itself abnormal/absent, inflating or invalidating the measurement; excess TT-TG driven by **tibial internal rotation** (rather than true tubercle lateralization) predicts worse outcomes from TTO and may be better addressed by derotational correction. Alternative indices (e.g., TT-PCL, TT–posterior intercondylar eminence) are proposed as more robust in dysplastic trochleae — know these exist but TT-TG >20 mm remains the operative anchor point in most European and US practice.

### Blazina Classification (Jumper's Knee / Patellar Tendinopathy)

Based on pain pattern relative to activity.

| Stage | Clinical description | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Pain only after activity/sport, no functional impairment | Relative rest, load monitoring, begin isometric/eccentric-isotonic program; usually continues sport with modification |
| **II** | Pain during and after activity, but still able to perform at reasonable level | Structured HSR/eccentric loading program (12 weeks minimum), address training load errors |
| **III** | Pain during and after activity, with increasing difficulty performing at desired level (prolonged pain, some intra-activity limitation) | Intensify loading program, consider adjuncts (shockwave), extend timeline 3–6 months; surgery only if refractory ≥6 months |
| **IV** | Complete tendon rupture | Surgical repair (this stage exits the tendinopathy pathway into acute rupture management) |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

**Patellofemoral pain syndrome**: mainstay is **exercise therapy** — combined hip and knee strengthening (proximal hip abductor/external rotator strengthening plus quadriceps strengthening) outperforms knee-only strengthening; the 2018 5th International Patellofemoral Pain Research Retreat (Gold Coast) consensus in BJSM remains the anchor guideline and continues to be reaffirmed by subsequent systematic reviews through 2024, recommending: exercise therapy (strong evidence) as first line; foot orthoses may help in the short term especially in those with more pronated feet; patellar taping/bracing may reduce pain in the short term as an adjunct to exercise (not standalone); combined interventions (exercise + taping/orthoses) show greater effect than exercise alone in short term. Course: minimum 6 weeks, typically 3 months, before reassessing; most improve substantially by 8–12 weeks. Avoid open-chain resisted terminal extension in symptomatic phase (high patellofemoral joint reaction force); favor closed-chain, limited-arc work initially, progressing arc and load as tolerated.

**First-time/low-risk patellar dislocation**: brief symptomatic management (ice, short period of immobilization for comfort only, typically <1–2 weeks) followed by early ROM and progressive quadriceps/VMO strengthening, hip strengthening, and neuromuscular/proprioceptive training. A **patellofemoral stabilizing brace** may be used during return to sport/high-risk activity, though evidence for long-term benefit is weak (ESSKA 2024 consensus notes bracing offers no clear long-term benefit but physiotherapy is essential regardless of pathway chosen).

**Patellar tendinopathy**: **progressive tendon loading** is central.
- **Isometric loading** (e.g., Spanish squat or leg extension holds, 5 × 45 sec at ~70% MVC) is used acutely for same-session analgesia and to allow continued training/competition during in-season management — evidence for isometrics' analgesic effect is well established though the magnitude of superiority over isotonic loading for pain relief has been questioned by newer trials.
- **Heavy slow resistance (HSR)** training (e.g., leg press, squat, hack squat — 3-4 sets, 6-15 RM tempo ~3 sec concentric/3 sec eccentric, 3×/week, progressive load) over 12 weeks: comparable or superior VISA-P outcomes to eccentric decline squat protocols with better patient adherence/tolerance, and mixed-treatment meta-analyses (2024) rank HSR and moderate slow resistance training highly for VISA-P improvement, generally favoring it over eccentric-only protocols for long-term functional gains.
- **Eccentric decline squat program** (Alfredson-type/Silbernagel protocol, 25° decline board, 3×15 reps twice daily, 12 weeks) remains an effective, well-validated alternative, particularly historically dominant before HSR data matured; expect discomfort during early sessions (acceptable pain, typically ≤5/10 that settles).
- Combine loading with **addressing kinetic chain contributors** (ankle dorsiflexion, quadriceps/hip strength, landing mechanics) and load management (jump volume monitoring).
- Timeline: minimum 12 weeks of a structured, supervised loading program before considering it a "failed" conservative trial; many athletes need 3-6 months for full return to high-level jumping sport.

**Quadriceps/patellar tendon rupture**: non-operative management is reserved for **partial tears with intact extensor mechanism function** (preserved active SLR, <50% tendon width torn on MRI) — managed with hinged knee brace locked in extension, progressive flexion allowed per protocol, and quad strengthening; low threshold to convert to surgery if extensor lag develops or on serial exam. Complete ruptures are a surgical diagnosis.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Corticosteroid injection** into or around the patellar tendon is **not recommended** — associated with increased risk of subsequent tendon rupture; avoid peritendinous steroid in patellar tendinopathy.
- **PRP (platelet-rich plasma)** for patellar tendinopathy: mixed-quality evidence, some trials show VISA-P improvement over placebo/eccentric exercise alone at 6-12 months, but heterogeneous preparations and outcomes — considered adjunctive/investigational for refractory Blazina stage II-III cases rather than first-line; several 2023-2024 network meta-analyses continue to rank exercise-based interventions above injectable therapies.
- **Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT)**: reasonable adjunct for refractory tendinopathy (stage II-III) after failed initial loading program, moderate evidence for pain reduction, low morbidity.
- **Dry needling / percutaneous tenotomy**: adjunct with modest evidence, typically combined with structured loading rather than standalone.
- **Hyaluronic acid injection**: not specifically indicated for patellofemoral pain/tendinopathy management; more relevant to broader knee OA context and not a core recommendation here.
- **Intra-articular hyaluronic acid or PRP for PFPS**: not supported as primary treatment — exercise therapy remains the evidence base; injections are not part of core PFPS consensus recommendations.

### Operative

**Patellar instability — the "à-la-carte" algorithm**

Modern management, formalized in the **ESSKA 2024 formal consensus on first-time patellar dislocation (Parts 1 and 2)**, is a risk-factor–driven, individualized ("à-la-carte") approach rather than a one-size-fits-all procedure:

1. **MPFL reconstruction (MPFL-R)** is the cornerstone soft-tissue procedure for essentially all surgical candidates — superior to MPFL repair, proximal realignment alone, or conservative management in reducing redislocation and improving Kujala scores per multiple meta-analyses/network meta-analyses. Graft options: gracilis or semitendinosus autograft (most common), allograft, or quadriceps tendon (partial-thickness) autograft. Femoral tunnel position is critical — malpositioning (too anterior/proximal) is the leading technical cause of failure/overconstraint; fluoroscopic confirmation of the Schöttle point is standard. Fixation with interference screw, suture anchor, or cortical button on the patellar side; patellar-sided avulsion MPFL tears have higher failure with repair-only strategies and generally favor reconstruction.
2. **Indications to add bony procedures** (assessed via the classic "menu" of risk factors — dysplasia grade, patellar height, TT-TG, rotational malalignment, patient age/growth remaining):
   - **Tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO)**: medialization for TT-TG >20 mm (or 15-20mm combined with other risk factors); add distalization when Caton-Deschamps >1.2-1.3 (patella alta). Techniques: Elmslie-Trillat (medialization), Fulkerson (anteromedialization, favors concurrent chondral offloading), Roux-Goldthwait (pediatric, tendon transfer without physeal violation). Fixation with 1-2 screws; weight-bearing typically restricted 4-6 weeks pending union.
   - **Trochleoplasty**: reserved for high-grade dysplasia (Dejour B-D, particularly C/D) with a supratrochlear spur or convex/absent trochlear groove, where MPFL-R alone carries persistently elevated redislocation risk. Deepening trochleoplasty (Bereiter or Dejour technique) is most widely used and shows the lowest recurrence in meta-analysis (~0.3-2.4% redislocation across techniques in a pooled analysis of ~1000 procedures); technically demanding with risk of chondral injury, arthrofibrosis (reported in a meaningful minority, especially pediatric series), and patellofemoral overcorrection/stiffness — best performed in high-volume centers. Can be performed close to skeletal maturity in adolescents per ESSKA consensus.
   - **Derotational (femoral and/or tibial) osteotomy**: for significant torsional malalignment (increased femoral anteversion, external tibial torsion) contributing to a functionally elevated TT-TG or patellar maltracking independent of true anatomic lateralization — increasingly recognized as an under-utilized correction, particularly when TT-TG elevation is driven by tibial internal rotation rather than tubercle position.
   - **Distal femoral varus osteotomy**: for genu valgum contributing to instability (mechanical axis correction), can be combined with soft-tissue/bony patellofemoral procedures.
   - **Osteochondral fragment management**: fragments ≥1 cm² in the PF contact zone should be repaired (refixation preferred over excision) when anatomically feasible; loose fragments <1 cm² or non-viable fragments are excised.
3. **Skeletally immature patients**: physeal-sparing MPFL reconstruction techniques (e.g., using the adductor tubercle/distal femoral physis-respecting tunnel trajectories, or soft-tissue-only fixation), guided growth (hemiepiphysiodesis) for coronal malalignment, and delaying formal trochleoplasty/TTO until near skeletal maturity when feasible.

**First-time dislocation — the evidence shift**: historically, first-time dislocations were managed conservatively with surgery reserved for recurrence. Accumulating evidence (multiple meta-analyses 2022-2025, ESSKA 2024 consensus) now supports that **conservative management carries a substantial recurrence rate (≥25%, up to 50% with residual symptoms)**, and that **primary MPFL-R in first-time dislocators with identifiable risk factors** (high-grade dysplasia, patella alta, elevated TT-TG, young age, contralateral dislocation history, osteochondral injury) yields meaningfully lower redislocation rates (pooled ~3-8% vs ~25-30% conservative) and higher Kujala scores. Practical approach: **low-risk first-timers** (no major anatomic risk factors, no loose body) reasonably start conservative; **high-risk first-timers** (multiple risk factors present) are increasingly offered early surgical stabilization rather than "waiting for a second dislocation." This represents a genuine practice shift from the older "two-strikes" paradigm.

**Patellar tendon rupture — repair**
- Timing: repair ideally within **2-3 weeks** of injury (acute); delayed repair (>3-6 weeks) requires additional techniques for retraction (V-Y quadricepsplasty, semitendinosus/allograft augmentation, or Codivilla-type turndown flaps) and carries worse outcomes.
- Technique: **transosseous tunnel repair** (non-absorbable suture through longitudinal patellar tunnels, tied over the proximal pole) remains the traditional gold standard; **suture anchor repair** is an increasingly favored modern alternative — biomechanically comparable-to-superior failure loads and less patellar bone disruption (avoids stress risers/longitudinal fracture risk from tunnel drilling), with recent systematic reviews (2024-2025) showing similar re-rupture rates and PROMs between the two techniques, so selection is largely surgeon preference/cost-driven rather than a clear outcome-based mandate.
- **Suture tape (internal brace) augmentation** is increasingly used to protect the primary repair and enable earlier, more aggressive rehabilitation/motion — biomechanical and early clinical data (2024-2025) support decreased gap formation without adverse effect on posterior tendon tension/contact mechanics, though mid/long-term comparative outcome data are still maturing.
- Partial-thickness tears in the tendon substance are re-approximated primarily; peripatellar retinacular tears repaired concurrently.

**Quadriceps tendon rupture — repair**
- Technique: **suture anchor repair** and **transosseous tunnel repair** are both well-supported; a 2025 systematic review found **similar re-rupture rates and PROMs** between the two, though suture anchor technique is less invasive (smaller incision, no patellar tunnel drilling) and has biomechanically more consistent failure loads — many high-volume centers have shifted toward suture anchors as the preferred modern technique for primary repair, while acknowledging transosseous remains a valid, lower-cost option with equivalent clinical outcomes.
- For chronic/retracted ruptures (>4-6 weeks, quadriceps retraction precluding tension-free primary repair): augmentation options include semitendinosus/gracilis autograft, Achilles allograft, or synthetic mesh, sometimes combined with V-Y quadricepsplasty for length.
- Address underlying metabolic/systemic risk factors (CKD, hyperparathyroidism, gout) post-repair — contralateral rupture risk is elevated in these populations and warrants counseling/surveillance.

**Technical pearls and pitfalls (operative summary)**

| Procedure | Pearl | Common pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| MPFL-R | Confirm femoral tunnel with fluoroscopic Schöttle point before final fixation; tension graft in ~30-45° flexion, not full extension | Anterior/proximal femoral tunnel malposition → overconstraint, loss of flexion, medial-sided pain/early arthrosis |
| Tibial tubercle osteotomy | Preserve a distal bony hinge/soft-tissue sleeve for stability; plan medialization magnitude from measured TT-TG, not by feel | Excessive medialization overloads medial facet; inadequate fixation risks fracture through the osteotomy |
| Trochleoplasty | Preserve cartilage-subchondral bone continuity during groove deepening; combine with MPFL-R when residual laxity remains after bony correction | Overzealous deepening risking chondral necrosis/fracture of the osteochondral flap; underappreciating concurrent patella alta |
| Patellar/quad tendon repair | Restore correct patellar height (compare to contralateral CD index intraoperatively/fluoroscopically) before final fixation tensioning | Overtensioning → patella baja (painful, restricted flexion); undertensioning → alta and extensor weakness |
| Suture anchor repair | Distribute anchors across the tendon footprint for even load-sharing | Anchor pull-out in osteoporotic bone; consider transosseous augmentation in this setting |

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Patellar instability — post-MPFL-R (± bony procedure)**
- **Weeks 0-2**: hinged brace locked in extension for ambulation (unlock for supervised ROM exercises), weight-bearing as tolerated with crutches (WBAT if isolated MPFL-R; touch-down to protected WB for ~4-6 weeks if concurrent TTO pending union) — quad sets, straight-leg raises, patellar mobilization, ROM 0-90° by end of week 2.
- **Weeks 2-6**: progress ROM to full flexion, wean brace/crutches as quad control returns (typically able to perform SLR without lag before weaning brace), begin closed-chain strengthening (mini-squats, leg press limited arc), stationary bike once ROM >100°.
- **Weeks 6-12**: full ROM expected, progressive strengthening (closed then open chain as tolerated), proprioceptive/balance training, begin light jogging around week 8-12 if quad strength ≥80% contralateral and no effusion.
- **Months 3-6**: sport-specific agility, plyometrics, cutting/pivoting drills introduced progressively from month 4.
- **Return to sport**: typically **6-9 months**, criteria-based (not purely time-based) — quadriceps strength ≥90% contralateral limb, single-leg hop test symmetry ≥90%, no apprehension on exam, completion of sport-specific agility progression without symptoms. Bony procedures (TTO) require radiographic union (typically by 8-12 weeks) before impact/pivoting sport.

**PFPS — rehabilitation**
- No fixed phase timeline; program is criteria- and symptom-based over a typical **6-12 week** structured course.
- Core content: combined **hip (abductor/external rotator) and knee (quadriceps) strengthening**, closed-chain progression, gait/movement retraining for dynamic valgus, addressing training load errors, foot orthoses trial if pronated foot type, patellar taping/bracing as short-term adjunct to enable pain-free exercise participation.
- Return to sport: graduated return once pain <2-3/10 with sport-specific loading, symmetric strength, and no recurrence with progressive loading — no universally validated single RTS test exists; rely on symptom-guided progressive loading.

**Patellar tendinopathy — loading progression**
- **Weeks 0-2 (isometric phase)**: isometric holds for analgesia (e.g., 5×45 sec, ~70% MVC), activity modification (reduce but don't fully cease loading if tolerable), address training load.
- **Weeks 2-12 (isotonic/HSR or eccentric phase)**: progressive HSR (leg press/squat, 3-4 sets 6-15RM, 3×/week) or eccentric decline squat program (3×15, 2×/day) — expect gradual VISA-P improvement over the full 12 weeks; acceptable pain during loading (≤3-5/10 that settles within 24h) is not a reason to stop.
- **Months 3-6 (energy storage/plyometric phase)**: introduce jump/landing mechanics retraining, plyometric progression once pain-free with heavy loading, gradual return to full training volume.
- **Return to sport**: criteria-based — VISA-P score improvement plateaued or near-normal, pain ≤2-3/10 with full training load, symmetric single-leg hop/landing mechanics; may take 3-6 months for Blazina II-III, occasionally longer for elite jumping-sport athletes.

**Extensor tendon repair (patellar or quadriceps) — early rehabilitation**
- **Weeks 0-2**: hinged brace **locked in full extension** for ambulation and at rest; isometric quad sets and ankle pumps immediately; protect the repair — no active resisted extension.
- **Weeks 2-6**: begin protected, gradually increasing passive/active-assisted ROM per surgeon protocol (commonly progressing ~0-30° at week 2, advancing ~30°/2 weeks toward full flexion by week 6-8) — internal brace/augmented repairs often permit earlier, faster ROM progression than isolated suture repair.
- **Weeks 6-12**: brace unlocked for ambulation once active SLR without lag is demonstrated (often ~week 6), progressive closed-chain strengthening, stationary bike, full weight-bearing without brace by ~week 8-12 if quad control adequate.
- **Months 3-6**: progressive resistance strengthening, proprioception, light jogging permitted around **16-24 weeks** once quadriceps strength deficit <30% and cleared by surgeon.
- **Return to sport/heavy labor**: typically **6-9 months**, with strength deficits (quadriceps torque) frequently persisting beyond 12 months even in good outcomes — counsel patients accordingly, particularly manual laborers and jumping-sport athletes.
- **Return to work**: sedentary work often at 2-4 weeks (brace-dependent mobility); physical/manual labor requiring kneeling, squatting, or ladder work typically delayed to 4-6 months pending strength and functional milestones.

**Milestone summary table (extensor tendon repair)**

| Milestone | Typical timing | Criteria to progress |
|---|---|---|
| Brace unlocked for ambulation | ~Week 6 | Active SLR without lag |
| Full weight-bearing without brace | Week 8-12 | Adequate quad control on exam |
| Light jogging | Week 16-24 | Quad strength deficit <30%, surgeon clearance |
| Return to sport/heavy labor | Month 6-9 | Quad strength ≥85-90% contralateral, functional hop/agility testing, no effusion/pain with loading |
| Full strength recovery | Often >12 months, sometimes incomplete | Persistent deficits are common even in "good" outcomes — set expectations early |

## Complications

**Overview table**

| Procedure/pathway | Complication | Approx. incidence / notes |
|---|---|---|
| MPFL-R | Recurrent instability | ~3-8% pooled; higher if bony risk factors unaddressed |
| MPFL-R | Overconstraint (femoral tunnel malposition) | Leading technical cause of revision surgery |
| TTO | Nonunion/delayed union | Uncommon; protect WB until radiographic union (~8-12 wk) |
| TTO | Compartment syndrome | Rare but a recognized surgical emergency from anterior compartment bleeding |
| Trochleoplasty | Arthrofibrosis | Reported up to ~13% in pediatric series |
| Trochleoplasty | Chondral injury during deepening | Technique-dependent; higher risk in less experienced hands |
| Patellar/quad tendon repair | Re-rupture | 2-6%, higher with delayed repair or metabolic comorbidity |
| Patellar/quad tendon repair | Persistent extensor weakness | Can persist >12 months even in successful repairs |
| Conservative PFPS/tendinopathy pathway | Chronicity / non-response | A meaningful minority remain symptomatic at 1 year |

- **MPFL reconstruction**: recurrent instability (~3-8% pooled, higher if bony risk factors under-addressed), overconstraint/iatrogenic medial patellar overload from malpositioned femoral tunnel (leading cause of revision), patellar fracture (graft tunnel stress riser, rare), stiffness/arthrofibrosis, persistent anterior knee pain, saphenous nerve injury (hamstring graft harvest).
- **TTO**: nonunion/delayed union (uncommon but serious — protect WB until radiographic union), tibial fracture (screw stress riser or early aggressive loading), compartment syndrome (rare, from bleeding into anterior compartment — a recognized emergency), hardware irritation requiring removal, overcorrection (medial patellofemoral overload).
- **Trochleoplasty**: chondral injury during groove deepening, arthrofibrosis/stiffness (a meaningfully reported complication, particularly pediatric series ~13%), avascular change to trochlear fragment (rare), persistent pain despite stable patella.
- **Patellar/quadriceps tendon repair**: re-rupture (2-6%, higher with delayed repair or comorbid tendon disease), persistent extensor lag/quadriceps weakness (can be long-term even with technically sound repair), patella baja/alta from malpositioned repair tension, infection, arthrofibrosis from prolonged immobilization (mitigated by internal brace/augmentation enabling earlier motion), suture/anchor irritation.
- **PFPS/tendinopathy conservative pathway**: chronicity/failure to respond (a meaningful minority remain symptomatic at 1 year even with structured rehab — set expectations), progression of tendinopathy to partial or complete rupture if loading errors persist unaddressed (Blazina IV).
- **General patellofemoral surgery**: complex regional pain syndrome (uncommon but recognized after knee surgery in predisposed patients), deep venous thrombosis (standard VTE risk stratification applies).

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **[Management of first-time patellar dislocation: The ESSKA 2024 formal consensus — Part 1](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39976176/)** (2025, KSSTA) — 55-expert European Level I consensus establishing MRI as mandatory for symptomatic first-time dislocators to assess dysplasia, patella height, and osteochondral injury; formalizes the risk-factor-driven evaluation framework underlying the à-la-carte surgical algorithm.
- **[Management of first-time patellar dislocation: The ESSKA 2024 formal consensus — Part 2](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40053919/)** (2025, KSSTA) — companion consensus on treatment: confirms MPFL-R as preferred surgical stabilization over repair/proximal realignment, endorses combined bony procedures for complex anatomic risk factors, states conservative recurrence rate is ≥25% with ~50% having persistent symptoms, notes physical therapy is essential regardless of pathway while bracing lacks proven long-term benefit — directly informs the practice shift toward earlier surgical stabilization in high-risk first-time dislocators.
- **[MPFL reconstruction vs. repair/realignment/conservative management — network meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10545352/)** (PMC, cites 2023 analysis) — confirms MPFL-R as the superior technique across comparators for primary dislocation, reinforcing it as first-line surgical stabilization when surgery is indicated.
- **[Increased TT-TG from tibiofemoral rotation predicts poor TTO outcomes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37789216/)** (2023) — shows that TT-TG elevation driven by tibial internal rotation (rather than true tubercle lateralization) predicts worse results from isolated TTO, supporting consideration of derotational osteotomy when rotational malalignment is the dominant driver rather than defaulting to TTO for any TT-TG >20 mm.
- **[TT-PCL / TT-posterior intercondylar eminence distance as alternative to TT-TG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11509304/)** (2024) — proposes more reliable surgical-planning metrics than TT-TG specifically in high-grade dysplasia, where the trochlear groove landmark itself is distorted, refining but not replacing the classic 20 mm TT-TG operative threshold.
- **[Trochleoplasty in pediatric patients — systematic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39811171/)** (2025) — confirms trochleoplasty can be performed safely near skeletal maturity with low recurrent instability (~1%) but notable arthrofibrosis rate (~13%), directly informing timing decisions in adolescent high-grade dysplasia.
- **[Mixed comparison of eccentric, isometric, and HSR training for patellar tendinopathy — network meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39559237/)** (2024, Heliyon) — ranks moderate/heavy slow resistance training favorably for VISA-P outcomes relative to eccentric training alone, supporting HSR as an equal-or-preferred first-line loading strategy alongside/instead of the traditional eccentric decline squat protocol.
- **[Effectiveness of strengthening exercise vs. other conservative treatments for PFPS — systematic review with meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12533450/)** (2024) — reaffirms strengthening exercise as the most effective conservative intervention for PFPS through mid-2024 literature, consistent with and extending the 2018 BJSM consensus rather than superseding it.
- **[Transosseous tunnel vs. suture anchor repair for quadriceps tendon rupture — systematic review](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2025.04.059)** (2025, Arthroscopy) — finds similar re-rupture rates and PROMs between the two fixation strategies, supporting either as an acceptable primary repair technique with selection driven by surgeon preference/biomechanical considerations rather than outcome superiority.
- **[Suture anchor vs. transosseous patellar suture fixation for quadriceps tendon rupture](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39902339/)** (2025) — corroborates comparable ROM, strength, and complication rates between techniques at short-term follow-up, further supporting flexible technique selection.
- **[Medial patellofemoral ligament fixation with suture tape augmentation — biomechanical study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/390848020_Medial_patellofemoral_ligament_fixation_with_suture_tape_augmentation_decreases_lateral_patellar_motion_without_changing_contact_pressure)** (2025) — shows suture tape (internal brace) augmentation of MPFL fixation decreases lateral patellar translation without adversely altering patellofemoral contact pressure, supporting its growing use to protect repairs/reconstructions and enable earlier rehabilitation.
- **[MPFL repair with suture tape augmentation — midterm outcomes](https://doi.org/10.3390/biomimetics10010065)** (2025) — midterm (~4.5 year) follow-up showing good clinical outcomes regardless of skeletal maturity or baseline joint laxity, extending prior short-term internal-brace MPFL data into the midterm range.
- **[Prognostic factors favoring surgical intervention for first-time athletic patellar dislocation — systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11968062/)** (2024) — synthesizes risk factors (osteochondral injury, high-grade dysplasia, patella alta, elevated TT-TG, younger age) that should tilt first-time dislocators toward early surgical stabilization, operationalizing the ESSKA consensus's risk-stratified approach for everyday case selection.
