## Quick Reference

**Core decision drivers**: direction of instability, glenoid bone loss %, Hill-Sachs on-/off-track status, age/sex/sport (contact vs overhead vs collision), number of prior dislocations, capsular laxity (MDI vs traumatic).

### Treatment ladder by diagnosis

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior instability, first-time, low-risk** (>25 y, non-contact) | Sling 1–2 wk comfort + early ROM/PT | Structured RTP rehab | Arthroscopic Bankart if recurrent or persistent apprehension |
| **Anterior instability, first-time, high-risk** (<25 y, male, contact/collision, ISIS ≥4, off-track) | Shared decision — most guidelines favor early stabilization | — | Primary arthroscopic Bankart (± remplissage) within weeks of injury |
| **Recurrent anterior, subcritical bone loss (13.5–20%), on-track** | — | Structured PT/activity modification if surgery declined | Arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage |
| **Recurrent anterior, off-track or ≥20–25% glenoid loss** | — | — | Latarjet (open or arthroscopic) ± Hill-Sachs management |
| **Bony Bankart (acute, displaced fragment)** | — | — | Arthroscopic fragment fixation (suture/screw) if fragment reducible and >~10–15% glenoid arc; excise + capsulolabral repair ± bone graft if comminuted |
| **HAGL lesion** | — | Trial of PT rarely successful once diagnosed | Open or arthroscopic HAGL repair (humeral-sided capsule reattachment) |
| **Posterior instability** | Activity mod + posterior capsule/rotator cuff strengthening (scapular control, ER/IR balance), 3–6 mo trial | Activity-specific bracing (lineman stance avoidance) | Arthroscopic posterior labral repair ± posterior bone augmentation if reverse bone loss |
| **MDI** | Rehab-first: 3–6 (up to 6–12) months structured scapular/rotator-cuff/proprioceptive program | Continued rehab, psychological/behavioral factors addressed | Inferior capsular shift / arthroscopic plication only after failed structured rehab |

### Key numbers to memorize

| Parameter | Value | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Subcritical glenoid bone loss | 13.5–20% | Zone of individualized decision-making; on-track/off-track and sport risk tip the balance |
| "Critical" glenoid bone loss | ≥20–25% | Bone block (Latarjet) generally indicated regardless of track status |
| Glenoid track width | ~83% of glenoid width (D = 0.83 × d − HS interval) | Determines on-track vs off-track Hill-Sachs |
| ISIS score cutoff (classic) | ≥6/10 → Latarjet primary | Some 2024 data suggest lower cutoffs (≥4) better predict recurrence in mixed populations |
| Recurrence after isolated arthroscopic Bankart (unselected) | 10–20% (up to 67% in high-risk young contact athletes) | Drives patient selection algorithms |
| Recurrence, Bankart + remplissage (RCT, medium-term) | 10% vs 30% Bankart alone | Landmark Woodmass et al. 2024 RCT |
| Latarjet recurrent instability rate | 2–8% | Lower than isolated Bankart in bone-loss/high-risk cohorts |
| Latarjet overall complication rate | ~15–30% (mostly minor); screw-related 0–16% | Arthroscopic technique has similar or slightly higher screw-removal rate than open |
| First dislocation recurrence risk, age <20 | 60–90% | Strongest driver toward early stabilization discussion |
| MDI rehab success rate | ~80% avoid surgery with structured program | Rehab-first is guideline consensus |

### Red flags

- **Axillary nerve injury** after dislocation — test deltoid sensation/contraction before and after reduction.
- **Irreducible dislocation** or fracture-dislocation — urgent reduction under sedation/anesthesia, re-image.
- **Posterior dislocation missed on AP film** — "lightbulb sign," rim sign, trough sign; low index of suspicion after seizure/electrocution/high-energy trauma.
- **HAGL** in a patient with recurrent instability and a *normal-appearing* labrum on arthroscopy — check the axillary pouch.
- **Engaging Hill-Sachs / off-track lesion** in a contact athlete — high failure risk with isolated Bankart.
- **Rotator cuff tear in instability patient >40 y** ("terrible triad" concept) — always assess cuff integrity, not just labrum.
- **Rapid glenohumeral arthropathy after stabilization surgery** (especially with prominent hardware/staples) — think Samilson-Prieto progression.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

The glenohumeral joint sacrifices bony constraint for range of motion; stability depends on a balance of **static** (glenoid version/depth, labrum, capsuloligamentous complex — superior, middle, and inferior glenohumeral ligaments, negative intra-articular pressure) and **dynamic** (rotator cuff, scapulothoracic control, long head of biceps) restraints.

- **Anterior instability** typically follows a traumatic abduction-external rotation force, producing a **Bankart lesion** (anteroinferior labral tear off the glenoid rim, 2–7 o'clock right shoulder) and/or **ALPSA** (anterior labroligamentous periosteal sleeve avulsion, medialized healed labrum). Concomitant **Hill-Sachs lesion** (posterosuperior humeral head impaction fracture from impact against the anterior glenoid rim) is present in 70–90% of first-time traumatic dislocations and nearly universal in recurrent instability.
- **Bony Bankart** = a fracture of the anteroinferior glenoid rim rather than a purely soft-tissue avulsion; converts a labral problem into a bone-loss problem and materially changes fixation strategy and recurrence risk.
- **HAGL lesion**: avulsion of the inferior glenohumeral ligament (anterior band) from its **humeral** insertion rather than the glenoid; occurs in 4–9% of instability cases, more common after low-energy/subluxation mechanisms in older or hyperlaxity-adjacent patients, and easily missed because the glenoid-side labrum looks normal.
- **Posterior instability** results from posteriorly directed forces (axial load on a flexed, adducted, internally rotated arm — classic in offensive linemen, powerlifters (bench press), and after seizure/electrical injury). Pathology: posterior labral tear (reverse Bankart), posterior capsular redundancy, reverse Hill-Sachs (anteromedial humeral head impaction), and occasionally posterior glenoid bone loss/dysplasia.
- **Multidirectional instability (MDI)** is a distinct entity: **atraumatic or minimally traumatic**, symptomatic instability in ≥2 directions (classically inferior plus anterior and/or posterior), driven by **capsular redundancy/generalized ligamentous laxity** rather than a discrete labral tear, often bilateral, and frequently associated with connective tissue hyperlaxity (Beighton score) and abnormal scapular/rotator-cuff neuromuscular control.
- **Glenoid bone loss** progresses with recurrent dislocation events ("bone loss begets bone loss" — each event erodes more anteroinferior glenoid and enlarges the Hill-Sachs), which is the biomechanical argument for earlier surgical stabilization in high-risk patients.

**Capsuloligamentous mechanics**: the **inferior glenohumeral ligament complex (IGHLC)** is the primary static restraint to anterior/posterior translation in the abducted, externally or internally rotated arm (the position of apprehension) and is the structure torn (glenoid-side, Bankart) or avulsed (humeral-side, HAGL) in traumatic instability. The **rotator interval** (superior glenohumeral ligament + coracohumeral ligament) restrains inferior and posteroinferior translation with the arm at the side and is the key structure addressed by rotator interval closure in MDI surgery. Negative intra-articular pressure (the "vacuum effect" of an intact, closed capsule) is a secondary stabilizer that is lost with any capsular tear or portal violation, which is part of the rationale for meticulous capsular closure/plication in MDI procedures.

**Associated soft-tissue lesions to actively search for at arthroscopy**: SLAP tears (concomitant biceps anchor involvement, more common in overhead athletes), **GLAD lesion** (glenolabral articular disruption — chondral injury without capsular disruption, seen after forced adduction/lower-energy events), and combined anterior-inferior-posterior labral tears in high-energy or multidirectional presentations. Failure to address a concomitant posterior extension of an anterior labral tear is a recognized cause of persistent instability after an isolated anterior Bankart repair.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: mechanism (traumatic vs atraumatic/positional), direction and number of episodes, ease of dislocation/subluxation (more episodes with less force over time = bone loss progression), voluntary/muscular control (exclude voluntary/psychogenic instability from true MDI), sport/occupation demands, hyperlaxity symptoms (other joints, family history), prior surgery.

**Exam**:
- **Apprehension/relocation (Fowler) test** — anterior instability; relief with posterior humeral head pressure supports anterior labral pathology.
- **Load-and-shift, sulcus sign** (graded 1–3, and check for **sulcus sign that does not reduce with external rotation** — a hallmark of rotator interval laxity in MDI).
- **Jerk test, Kim test** — posterior instability.
- **Beighton score** ≥4–6/9 supports generalized hyperlaxity relevant to MDI.
- **HAGL suspicion**: instability with a relatively normal glenoid-side exam and MRI; confirm on axillary pouch imaging.
- Always document **neurovascular status**, especially axillary nerve, before and after any reduction maneuver.

**Imaging**:
- Plain radiographs: **AP, true AP (Grashey), axillary lateral, scapular Y**; **West Point view** for anteroinferior glenoid rim; **Stryker notch view** for Hill-Sachs.
- **CT with 3D reconstruction and digital humeral subtraction** — gold standard for quantifying glenoid bone loss (best-fit circle/PICO method) and Hill-Sachs size/location; essential when bone loss is suspected or before any bone-block procedure.
- **MRA (MR arthrogram)** — labral/capsular detail, HAGL detection (J-sign or drooping/absent IGHL on axial images), rotator cuff/biceps assessment; superior to non-contrast MRI for labral pathology.
- **Glenoid track calculation** requires both glenoid width (CT) and Hill-Sachs interval/width (CT or MRA) — see Classification below.

**Differential diagnosis and special populations**:
- **Overhead/throwing athletes**: distinguish true anterior structural instability from **internal impingement / posterosuperior labral fraying with anterior microinstability** related to GIRD (glenohumeral internal rotation deficit) and repetitive microtrauma — management philosophy differs substantially (stretching/scapular program and throwing mechanics correction first; surgery is a last resort in throwers given documented lower return-to-prior-level rates after anterior stabilization in this group).
- **Voluntary/psychogenic instability**: patients who can demonstrate subluxation at will without pain, often with underlying psychological factors — screen for this before committing to a Stanmore Type III rehabilitation pathway or any surgery, since surgical outcomes in voluntary instability are poor.
- **Epileptic/seizure-related and electrical-injury dislocations**: high index of suspicion for **bilateral posterior fracture-dislocation** — obtain axillary views bilaterally even if only one side is symptomatic.
- **Adolescent/open physis patients**: instability surgery is not contraindicated by open physes, but anchor/implant placement should respect the physis, and family counseling should address the very high recurrence risk of nonoperative management in this age group.

## Classification

### Stanmore Triangle (Classification of Instability)

A conceptual, non-numerical classification describing instability as a spectrum across three "polar" types, useful for framing treatment philosophy (structural vs muscle-patterning problem) rather than as a scoring tool.

| Polar Type | Description | Management Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Type I — Traumatic structural** | Classic traumatic unidirectional instability with a structural (Bankart) lesion, normal muscle patterning | Structural/surgical repair (Bankart, ± bone augmentation) is primary treatment |
| **Type II — Atraumatic structural** | Structural abnormality (capsular redundancy, labral pathology) in the absence of a single traumatic event, normal muscle patterning | May need structural correction (capsular plication) but usually after rehab trial |
| **Type III — Muscle patterning (non-structural)** | Instability driven by abnormal muscle recruitment/patterning (overactive prime movers, underactive stabilizers) with no true structural lesion; classically atraumatic, often multidirectional/bilateral | Physiotherapy-led rehabilitation is first-line and often sufficient; surgery is high-risk for failure and reserved for refractory cases after confirming no missed structural lesion |

Patients can migrate along the triangle (e.g., a Type III muscle-patterning shoulder that develops a structural lesion after a traumatic episode becomes a polar/intermediate Type I–III), which is the classification's key clinical utility — it forces re-evaluation rather than a one-time label.

### ISIS Score (Instability Severity Index Score, Balg & Boileau)

Six preoperative clinical/radiographic variables, each scored 0 or 1–2, total 0–10, developed to predict recurrence risk after **isolated arthroscopic Bankart repair** and to flag patients who should have a bone-block procedure instead.

| Variable | Points |
|---|---|
| Age at surgery ≤20 years | 2 |
| Degree of sports participation: competitive | 2 |
| Type of sport: contact/forced overhead | 1 |
| Shoulder hyperlaxity (clinical) | 1 |
| Hill-Sachs lesion visible on AP radiograph in external rotation | 2 |
| Glenoid loss of contour (bone loss) on AP radiograph | 2 |

**Management implication**: classically, **ISIS ≤3 → isolated arthroscopic Bankart repair** (low recurrence, ~5–10%); **ISIS ≥6 → primary Latarjet/bone-block recommended** (isolated Bankart recurrence rises to >70% in this group); **ISIS 4–5** is a gray zone requiring individualized judgment (consider remplissage augmentation). A 2024 multicenter validation (Blommestein et al., *KSSTA*) found ISIS remains predictive (OR 1.55 per point) but suggested a **lower cutoff (~≥4)** may better flag recurrence risk in contemporary, heterogeneous populations — reinforcing that ISIS is a risk-stratification aid, not a rigid rule, and should be interpreted alongside direct bone-loss measurement and track status.

### Glenoid Track / On-Track–Off-Track Concept (Yamamoto/Di Giacomo)

Integrates glenoid bone loss AND Hill-Sachs size/location into a single bipolar-lesion model to predict whether the Hill-Sachs will "engage" the glenoid rim in functional positions.

- **Glenoid track (GT) width** = 0.83 × d (d = intact glenoid diameter, measured by best-fit circle) minus the glenoid bone defect width.
- **Hill-Sachs interval (HSI)** = distance from the medial margin of the rotator cuff footprint to the lateral edge of the Hill-Sachs lesion, measured on axial CT/MRI.
- **On-track**: HSI < GT width → the Hill-Sachs stays within the glenoid track through the arc of motion and does not engage → lower re-dislocation risk with soft-tissue repair alone.
- **Off-track**: HSI ≥ GT width → the Hill-Sachs lesion extends medial to the glenoid track and will engage the anterior glenoid rim during abduction-external rotation → high recurrence risk if treated with isolated Bankart repair.

| Category | Definition | Management Implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **On-track, glenoid loss <13.5%** | Small/moderate Hill-Sachs, minimal glenoid loss | Isolated arthroscopic Bankart repair; low recurrence |
| **On-track, glenoid loss 13.5–20% (subcritical)** | Borderline glenoid loss but Hill-Sachs still on-track | Individualize: Bankart alone acceptable in low-demand patients; add remplissage in contact/overhead athletes to reduce recurrence margin |
| **Off-track, glenoid loss <20–25%** | Hill-Sachs engages despite modest glenoid loss | Bankart + remplissage (fills/tenodeses the Hill-Sachs into the infraspinatus, converting it extra-articular) is the standard arthroscopic solution |
| **Off-track or glenoid loss ≥20–25%** | Combined bipolar bone loss exceeding soft-tissue repair capacity | Bone-block procedure (Latarjet, iliac crest/Eden-Hybinette) is generally indicated; remplissage alone insufficient |

Note: the 13.5% and 20–25% figures are the two thresholds most cited in the 2024–2025 algorithmic literature (see Recent Evidence); some contemporary algorithms use a single "subcritical zone" of 13.5–20% requiring individualization, escalating to bone block above ~20%.

### Samilson-Prieto Classification (Glenohumeral Arthropathy after Instability)

Radiographic grading of degenerative changes ("dislocation arthropathy") that develop after recurrent instability or instability surgery, based on humeral head/glenoid osteophyte size on AP radiograph.

| Grade | Radiographic Finding | Management Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild (I)** | Inferior humeral head osteophyte ≤3 mm | Observation; optimize activity, NSAIDs; continue instability surveillance |
| **Moderate (II)** | Osteophyte 3–7 mm, with or without mild joint space narrowing | Symptom-directed conservative care; consider arthroscopic debridement/capsular release for mechanical symptoms; counsel on arthroplasty timeline |
| **Severe (III)** | Osteophyte >7 mm with glenohumeral joint space narrowing/irregularity | Consider arthroplasty (TSA if glenoid bone stock and cuff intact, or reverse TSA if cuff-deficient/glenoid-deficient/younger-patient bone-block hardware complicates anatomy); avoid further soft-tissue-only stabilization surgery |

Clinically relevant because **instability surgery itself is a risk factor** for this arthropathy (historically higher after non-anatomic procedures like Putti-Platt/Magnuson-Stack, and after intra-articular hardware prominence); anatomic, extra-articular hardware placement (as taught in modern Latarjet technique) is intended to mitigate this risk.

## Treatment

### First-Time Dislocator: Decision Framework by Age & Sport

The single highest-leverage decision in the entire cluster is what to do after a **first-time traumatic anterior dislocation**, because recurrence risk (and therefore the future bone-loss trajectory) is overwhelmingly predicted by age, sex, and sport/contact demand rather than by the index injury severity itself.

| Patient profile | Recurrence risk if treated nonoperatively | Recommended pathway |
|---|---|---|
| Age <20, male, contact/collision or overhead athlete | 60–90% | Discuss **early arthroscopic stabilization** (within weeks, often before return to the same season) as a legitimate first-line option, not merely a fallback after recurrence; supported by 2025 AAC position statement and ISAKOS current-concepts review |
| Age 20–29, recreational contact sport | ~40–60% | Individualize: shared decision-making incorporating ISIS score, imaging (bone loss/track), and season timing; structured rehab trial acceptable if patient prefers, with a low threshold to proceed to surgery after a second event |
| Age >30, non-contact/sedentary | 10–25% | Structured nonoperative rehab first; surgery reserved for recurrence or persistent functional apprehension |
| Age >40, first dislocation | Lower recurrence but higher associated rotator cuff tear risk | Assess cuff integrity (MRI/exam) before attributing symptoms to instability alone; "terrible triad" (instability + cuff tear + nerve injury) requires cuff-directed as well as instability-directed treatment |
| In-season competitive athlete, any age | Variable | Season-timing discussion: bracing/taping and activity modification to complete the season is reasonable for lower-grade/subluxation events; a frank dislocation with apprehension in a contact athlete more often warrants surgery before return rather than after a second event |

**Practical rule of thumb**: the traditional "treat first-time dislocations nonoperatively, operate after the second" paradigm is being progressively replaced — for the specific subgroup of young male contact/collision or overhead athletes — by a risk-stratified approach that offers early stabilization as first-line, because each recurrent event both increases bone loss and delays return to competition more than an early, well-indicated primary repair would.

### Non-operative

- **First-time, low-risk dislocator** (age >25–30, non-contact/non-overhead sport or sedentary, no significant bone loss): brief immobilization for comfort (**internal rotation sling 1–3 weeks is traditional**; evidence on **external rotation bracing** shows theoretically better labral apposition but no consistent reduction in recurrence and poor compliance — not mandated by any major guideline), followed by early progressive ROM and rotator cuff/scapular strengthening. Return to sport once full painless ROM and strength are regained and apprehension has resolved, typically 3–6 weeks for non-contact activity.
- **MDI — rehab-first is the guideline-consistent standard of care.** Structured program (Rockwood/Watson-style protocol) for a minimum **3–6 months** (many protocols extend to 6–12 months before considering surgery) focused on: scapular stabilizer strengthening (serratus anterior, lower trapezius), rotator cuff strengthening with emphasis on dynamic humeral head centering, proprioceptive/neuromuscular re-education, and correction of maladaptive muscle patterning (Stanmore Type III concept). Reported success (avoiding surgery) in ~80% of well-selected patients; poor response should prompt re-evaluation for a missed structural (Type I/II) component or psychological/voluntary instability.
- **Posterior instability**, especially atraumatic/microtraumatic or in-season athletes: posterior capsule and cuff strengthening, activity/technique modification (e.g., lineman stance, bench-press grip/depth) for **3–6 months minimum** before considering surgery, since many respond well to rehabilitation alone.
- Nonoperative management is **not** first-line for high-risk first-time dislocators (young, male, contact/collision or overhead athlete, off-track lesion, or bony Bankart) given recurrence rates of 60–90% reported in this subgroup — shared decision-making should present early stabilization as an evidence-supported alternative to prolonged conservative care (see Recent Evidence).

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- Intra-articular corticosteroid injections have a limited, symptom-modulating role and are **not disease-modifying** in instability; consider only for pain control in patients pursuing prolonged conservative management or as a diagnostic adjunct, not as definitive treatment.
- **PRP/biologics**: no high-quality evidence supports biologic augmentation for labral healing in shoulder instability repair; not part of any current guideline recommendation.
- Adjunctive **fluoroscopic/US-guided diagnostic injection** can help differentiate intra-articular instability pain from extra-articular (AC joint, biceps) pain generators in atypical presentations.

### Operative

**Arthroscopic Bankart repair (anteroinferior labral repair)**
- Indication: recurrent anterior instability, ISIS ≤3–4, on-track lesion, glenoid bone loss <13.5% (or acceptable in <20% in lower-demand patients).
- Technique: 3–4 suture anchors placed low on the glenoid face (5–7 o'clock start), capsulolabral tissue advanced superiorly and laterally to restore the labral bumper and capsular tension; address a concomitant SLAP or posterior labral extension if present.
- Pitfalls: anchor placement too far medial on the glenoid neck under-restores the bumper effect; missing an ALPSA (medialized, healed-down labrum) leads to inadequate mobilization; failing to recognize off-track status pre-operatively is the single largest driver of "unexplained" recurrence.

**Remplissage (posterior capsulodesis/infraspinatus tenodesis into the Hill-Sachs defect)**
- Indication: off-track Hill-Sachs with subcritical or acceptable glenoid bone loss; used as an adjunct to Bankart repair, not a stand-alone procedure.
- Technique: arthroscopic placement of 1–2 anchors within the Hill-Sachs defect, sutures passed through infraspinatus tendon and posterior capsule, tied to fill the defect and render it extra-articular, preventing engagement.
- Evidence: landmark double-blind RCT (Woodmass et al., *AJSM* 2024) — recurrent instability at medium-term follow-up 10% (remplissage) vs 30% (Bankart alone); meta-analyses show a small, generally non-significant loss of external rotation (a few degrees) that does not appear to compromise overall shoulder function or return-to-sport rates at 5-year follow-up.
- Pitfall: does not correct glenoid-side bone loss — inappropriate as sole augmentation when glenoid loss approaches/exceeds ~20–25%.

**Bony Bankart fixation**
- Indication: acute or chronic displaced anteroinferior glenoid rim fracture large enough to be biomechanically relevant (generally >10–15% of glenoid articular arc) and reducible to anatomic position.
- Technique: arthroscopic reduction and fixation with suture-anchor capsulolabral repair incorporating the bone fragment, or (for larger/comminuted fragments) suture-button or screw fixation; if the fragment is too comminuted or resorbed to fix, treat as a bone-loss problem (Latarjet/bone graft) rather than attempting fixation of non-viable bone.
- Pearl: CT is mandatory to assess fragment size, comminution, and healing potential before deciding fixation vs. bone-block conversion.

**Latarjet procedure (open or arthroscopic coracoid transfer)**
- Indication: glenoid bone loss ≥20–25%, off-track lesions with bone loss beyond remplissage's reliable range, revision instability surgery, and increasingly as a primary option in high-risk contact/collision athletes even with subcritical loss (given lower recurrence in this subgroup — see Recent Evidence).
- Mechanism of stabilization ("triple effect"): bony increase of the glenoid arc, a dynamic sling effect from the conjoint tendon (through split subscapularis), and capsular reinforcement/repair.
- Technique: coracoid osteotomy, transfer through (or below, "congruent-arc" variant) a split in the subscapularis, fixation flush with the glenoid face at the 3–5 o'clock position (right shoulder) with 2 screws (or cortical button constructs), care to avoid lateral overhang (>2–3 mm associated with arthropathy) or medialization (under-corrects instability).
- Arthroscopic Latarjet: technically demanding, steep learning curve (~definable improvement after 40–70 cases in published series); comparable complication/recurrence rates to open in experienced hands, with possibly higher screw-related complication rates reported in some systematic reviews — surgeon experience is the dominant variable.
- Complications: nonunion/fibrous union (~5–10%), graft osteolysis, screw-related pain/loosening/removal (0–18%, higher after arthroscopic technique in some series), recurrent instability (2–8%), subscapularis dysfunction, and rare neurovascular injury (musculocutaneous/axillary nerve). Suprascapular and axillary nerve are at risk during coracoid mobilization and inferior screw placement.
- Revision Latarjet after failed arthroscopic Bankart: outcomes comparable to primary Latarjet with low recurrence, supporting its role as the workhorse revision procedure.

**Alternative bone-block options (Eden-Hybinette, distal tibial/iliac crest allograft)**
- Indication: revision setting after failed Latarjet, coracoid unsuitable for transfer (prior fracture, insufficient size), or surgeon/patient preference to avoid a second dynamic-sling mechanism at revision.
- Technique: free bone graft (iliac crest autograft classically, or distal tibial/glenoid allograft increasingly favored for articular cartilage congruency) fixed to the anteroinferior glenoid to restore the arc.
- Pearl: distal tibial allograft's native cartilage cap can restore a more anatomic, congruent articular surface than iliac crest and is gaining favor in revision algorithms, though iliac crest remains more widely available and familiar.

**HAGL repair**
- Indication: confirmed HAGL lesion (glenoid-side pathology may coexist — "floating IGHL").
- Technique: open (deltopectoral, subscapularis split or peel) or arthroscopic repair reattaching the avulsed IGHL to the anatomic humeral neck footprint with suture anchors; open approach remains more common given exposure demands, though arthroscopic HAGL repair is increasingly described by experienced arthroscopists.
- Pearl: always inspect the axillary pouch during diagnostic arthroscopy for instability, especially when the labrum appears intact/near-normal despite a convincing instability history — HAGL is the classic "missed lesion."

**Posterior labral repair**
- Indication: recurrent/persistent posterior instability failing 3–6 months structured rehab, especially in collision athletes (American football linemen) and overhead/throwing athletes with symptomatic posterior labral tears.
- Technique: arthroscopic posterior capsulolabral repair with suture anchors (increasingly all-suture anchors for low-profile fixation in a tight posterior working space), plication of redundant posterior capsule; address reverse Hill-Sachs or posterior glenoid bone loss/retroversion with bone augmentation (posterior bone block/allograft, or glenoid osteotomy for pathologic retroversion) only in bone-deficient or revision cases.
- Outcomes: recent systematic reviews/meta-analyses report **~90–92% return to sport/work** and favorable patient-reported outcomes at short-to-midterm follow-up in athletes, including collision-sport cohorts; minimum-10-year data show durable outcomes for isolated posterior capsulolabral repair.

**Inferior capsular shift / arthroscopic capsular plication (MDI surgery)**
- Indication: reserved for MDI that has **failed a genuine structured rehabilitation trial** (per Stanmore/consensus philosophy) — surgery-first approaches to MDI have historically underperformed.
- Technique: open inferior capsular shift (historical gold standard, durable but more morbid) vs arthroscopic multi-anchor capsular plication (rotator interval closure plus circumferential plication) — modern practice favors arthroscopic plication with comparable outcomes and lower morbidity in experienced hands, reserving open shift for revision or severe global laxity.
- Pearl: address rotator interval laxity (positive sulcus sign not reducing with external rotation) explicitly — isolated anteroinferior plication without interval closure risks residual inferior/multidirectional symptoms.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Arthroscopic Bankart repair ± remplissage**
- **Weeks 0–4**: sling immobilization (adjust duration/position per surgeon preference, typically neutral to slight internal rotation); passive/active-assisted ROM starting within the first 1–2 weeks (pendulum, passive elevation to tolerance, external rotation limited to 0–20° to protect the repair); no active internal rotation loading, no lifting.
- **Weeks 4–6**: discontinue sling; progress passive/active ROM toward full elevation and external rotation as tolerated; begin isometric rotator cuff/scapular stabilizer activation; avoid combined abduction-external rotation ("apprehension position") loading.
- **Weeks 6–12**: progressive resistance strengthening (rotator cuff, scapular stabilizers, deltoid), restore full ROM, closed-chain stability drills, begin sport-specific proprioceptive work around week 8–10.
- **Weeks 12–16**: sport-specific/return-to-throw or contact-preparation progressions; plyometric and eccentric loading for overhead athletes.
- **Return to non-contact sport**: ~4–5 months; **return to contact/collision sport**: typically **5–6 months**, contingent on full painless ROM, ≥90% strength symmetry on isokinetic testing, negative apprehension, and successful completion of sport-specific functional testing — not solely time-based.
- Remplissage does not meaningfully alter this timeline in most protocols, though some surgeons extend early external rotation restriction slightly given the infraspinatus tenodesis component.

**Latarjet (open or arthroscopic)**
- **Weeks 0–2**: sling for comfort, pendulum and passive ROM within a protected arc; avoid resisted internal/external rotation.
- **Weeks 2–6**: progressive passive-to-active ROM; subscapularis-protective protocol (limit early resisted internal rotation and forced external rotation stretching to protect the subscapularis split/repair) — this is a key technical difference from isolated Bankart rehab.
- **Weeks 6–12**: active strengthening once radiographic evidence of early graft healing (or per surgeon protocol, clinical criteria) supports loading; scapular and rotator cuff strengthening progression.
- **3 months**: confirm bony union/incorporation before full-contact clearance is considered; light sport-specific training may begin with surgeon clearance.
- **Return to contact/collision sport**: typically **4–6 months**, gated by radiographic graft healing (union rates generally >90–95% in modern series), full strength recovery, and functional testing; earlier than historical open-era timelines in some accelerated arthroscopic protocols, but union confirmation remains the primary gate.

**Posterior labral repair**
- **Weeks 0–4**: sling with abduction pillow avoided (neutral position), passive ROM with internal rotation and cross-body adduction restricted (protects posterior repair) while elevation/external rotation are generally allowed earlier than in anterior repair rehab (mirror-image logic).
- **Weeks 4–8**: progressive active ROM, begin scapular/rotator cuff isometrics.
- **Weeks 8–16**: progressive resistance strengthening, closed-chain and posterior chain (posterior deltoid, cuff external rotators) emphasis.
- **Return to collision sport (e.g., football linemen)**: typically **5–7 months**, later than anterior stabilization given higher posterior-chain loading demands of the position and slower confidence in posterior capsule strength.

**MDI structured rehabilitation (non-operative primary pathway)**
- **Phase 1 (0–6 weeks)**: pain control, scapular positioning awareness, isometric rotator cuff and periscapular activation avoiding provocative positions.
- **Phase 2 (6–12 weeks)**: progressive dynamic strengthening of scapular stabilizers (serratus anterior, lower/middle trapezius) and rotator cuff, closed-chain proprioceptive drills.
- **Phase 3 (3–6 months)**: sport-specific/functional progression, open-chain strengthening, plyometrics for overhead athletes; reassess for surgical candidacy only if plateaued or worsening despite documented compliance at this stage.
- Minimum trial before considering surgery: **3–6 months** per most consensus recommendations; some protocols extend to 6–12 months given the generally favorable natural history with adherence.

**Return-to-work** (non-athletic, desk/light-duty): typically 1–2 weeks post-op with sling; **manual labor/overhead occupational demands**: mirror sport timelines above (4–6 months for Latarjet/heavy labor).

**Return-to-sport criteria (applies across procedures, not purely time-based)**: full painless active and passive ROM within 5–10° of the contralateral side; rotator cuff and periscapular strength ≥90% of contralateral limb on isokinetic or handheld dynamometry; negative apprehension/relocation testing; successful completion of a sport-specific functional progression (closed-chain loading, contact simulation drills where applicable); for bone-block procedures, radiographic confirmation of graft union/incorporation before clearance to full-contact collision sport. Clearing an athlete on time alone, without meeting strength and union criteria, is a recognized modifiable risk factor for early recurrence.

## Complications

- **Recurrent instability** — the dominant complication across all procedures; rates driven primarily by inadequate bone-loss assessment/track classification at index surgery rather than technical failure alone.
- **Stiffness/loss of external rotation** — more common after remplissage (usually modest, 5–10°, rarely functionally limiting) and after overly aggressive capsular plication in MDI surgery.
- **Nerve injury** — axillary nerve (traction during open procedures, inferior anchor/screw placement), musculocutaneous nerve (medial coracoid dissection in Latarjet), suprascapular nerve (posterior work, aggressive medial anchor placement).
- **Hardware complications (Latarjet)** — screw loosening/prominence/breakage, graft nonunion or resorption, graft fracture; screw removal required in a meaningful minority (higher after arthroscopic technique in some series).
- **Subscapularis dysfunction** — from open subscapularis takedown/repair (largely mitigated by the split technique in modern Latarjet) or from arthroscopic subscapularis-splitting portals if not carefully managed.
- **Glenohumeral arthropathy (Samilson-Prieto progression)** — long-term risk after any instability surgery, higher with intra-articular hardware prominence, non-anatomic historical procedures, and repeated dislocation events pre-surgery; underscores the rationale for earlier, well-selected stabilization.
- **Missed concomitant lesion** — unrecognized HAGL, posterior labral extension, or SLAP lesion is a common and preventable cause of "failed" anterior stabilization; systematic circumferential arthroscopic evaluation (including the axillary pouch) at index surgery reduces this risk.
- **Infection** — low overall (<1–2%) but relevant given implant use in bone-block procedures.

### Approximate complication rates by procedure

| Procedure | Recurrent instability | Reoperation | Notable specific complication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arthroscopic Bankart alone | 10–20% (up to 67% high-risk) | 5–15% | Stiffness (rare) |
| Arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage | ~10% | 5–10% | Modest ER loss (5–10°) |
| Latarjet (open or arthroscopic) | 2–8% | 5–15% (screw removal a major driver) | Screw-related complications 0–18%; nonunion 5–10% |
| HAGL repair | Low if isolated lesion correctly identified | Low | Axillary nerve proximity (open approach) |
| Posterior labral repair | 5–15% | 5–10% | Slightly slower return-to-sport timeline than anterior repair |
| Inferior capsular shift/plication (MDI) | 10–20% if surgery undertaken without adequate rehab trial | Variable | Overtightening → loss of external rotation/function |

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **2024 — Woodmass et al., *AJSM*, multicenter double-blind RCT**: arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage vs. Bankart alone showed medium-term (3–9 yr) recurrent instability of **10% vs 30%**, confirming remplissage's protective effect persists well beyond the initial 2-year RCT report and should be considered standard adjunct for off-track lesions. [Woodmass et al., AJSM 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/03635465241254063)
- **2025 — Arthroscopy Association of Canada position statement (Abdel Khalik et al.)**: systematic review/meta-analysis and formal position statement on first-time anterior shoulder dislocation management, supporting individualized early-stabilization discussion in high-risk (young, male, contact/collision) patients rather than routine nonoperative-first management. [AAC Position Statement, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/23259671251316893)
- **2025 — Journal of ISAKOS review**: "Approach to Treating Patients after First-time Anterior Shoulder Instability" synthesizes evidence that individualized, risk-stratified decision-making (age, sport, bone loss, track status) should replace uniform nonoperative-first protocols for first-time dislocators. [PMC, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12446161/)
- **2025 — Matched cohort, French Arthroscopic Society**: Latarjet vs Bankart + remplissage in minimal glenoid bone loss showed comparable outcomes, supporting remplissage-augmented Bankart as a viable bone-preserving alternative even in some Latarjet-eligible patients. [PMC, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965702/)
- **2025 — Martial arts/contact athlete cohort study**: in bone loss <20%, Latarjet showed markedly lower recurrence (6% vs 25%) and reoperation (6% vs 18%) versus Bankart without remplissage, reinforcing that sport-specific contact demands (not bone loss percentage alone) should influence the Bankart-vs-Latarjet threshold in subcritical bone loss. [PubMed, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40749860/)
- **2024 — Subcritical bone loss cohort (JSES/AJSM-adjacent)**: arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage yielded outcomes similar to open Latarjet for both primary and revision stabilization in subcritical glenoid bone loss, supporting remplissage-augmented repair as a lower-morbidity option in appropriately selected subcritical-loss patients. [PubMed, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38945290/)
- **2025 — 10–20% glenoid bone loss cohort**: primary Latarjet showed lower redislocation but higher revision rates versus primary arthroscopic Bankart at minimum 2-year follow-up, highlighting that "lower recurrence" and "fewer reoperations" are not always the same outcome and both must be weighed in shared decision-making. [PubMed, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39894338/)
- **2025 — Systematic review/meta-analysis, ScienceDirect**: across a wide range of glenoid bone loss, arthroscopic Bankart + remplissage showed similar clinical outcomes but a significantly **lower complication rate** than open or arthroscopic Latarjet, an important counterweight to Latarjet's lower recurrence advantage when counseling patients on procedure choice. [ScienceDirect, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666639125001397)
- **2025 — "Beyond Bankart" algorithmic review**: proposes a contemporary decision algorithm anchored on the **13.5% subcritical** and **~20–25% critical** glenoid bone loss thresholds combined with on-/off-track status, formalizing the tiered approach reflected in this monograph's Quick Reference and Classification tables. [ScienceDirect, 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2773157X25002942)
- **2024 — Reliability study of 2D on-track/off-track measurement**: confirms measurement variability exists in glenoid track calculation depending on technique, arguing for standardized measurement protocols (best-fit circle method) before using track status as a hard surgical cutoff. [PMC, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11909657/)
- **2024 — Blommestein et al., *KSSTA***: multicenter validation of ISIS as a predictor of recurrence after arthroscopic Bankart repair (OR 1.545 per point); proposed cutoff analysis (0–3 / 4–6 / 7–10) suggests a **lower threshold (~≥4)** may better identify at-risk patients in contemporary practice than the classic ≥6 cutoff, prompting reconsideration of historical ISIS thresholds. [Wiley/KSSTA, 2024](https://esskajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ksa.12235)
- **2024 — EFORT Open Reviews, novel MDI classification (AB classification)**: systematic review highlights persistent lack of a unified MDI definition (46% of studies include trauma, 34% exclude it) and proposes a traumatic-etiology/hyperlaxity-based subclassification, underscoring that MDI remains definitionally heterogeneous even as rehab-first management is broadly agreed upon. [PMC/EFORT Open Reviews, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11044084/)
- **2025 — Meta-analysis, posterior instability arthroscopic repair**: pooled return-to-sport/work of 91.7% (all patients) and 90.9% (athletes) after primary arthroscopic posterior labral repair, consolidating posterior stabilization as a reliably effective procedure once nonoperative measures fail. [Arthroscopy, Sports Medicine & Rehabilitation, 2025](https://www.arthroscopysportsmedicineandrehabilitation.org/article/S2666-061X(24)00175-5/fulltext)
- **2025 — Collision-athlete posterior Bankart cohort**: favorable outcomes after arthroscopic posterior Bankart repair specifically in collision-sport athletes (e.g., American football), supporting extension of posterior stabilization indications into higher-demand contact populations previously considered higher-risk for posterior surgery. [PMC, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12800795/)
- **2025 — All-suture anchor posterior labral repair in football players**: demonstrates low-profile all-suture anchor fixation as an effective technical evolution for the tight posterior glenohumeral working space, with good outcomes reported in this cohort. [AJSM, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671251343646)
- **2023–2024 — Screw-related complication systematic review (arthroscopic vs open Latarjet)**: arthroscopic Latarjet may carry a higher screw-related complication/screw-removal rate than open technique in some series (up to 18% vs 7.3%), a technical consideration for surgeons transitioning to the arthroscopic approach. [Wiley/ASMR, 2023](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.asmr.2023.04.010)
- **2024 — Arthroscopic Latarjet learning curve study**: quantifies a substantive learning curve (measurable improvement in operative time/complications across the first ~171 cases), relevant when counseling patients on surgeon-specific risk and when deciding whether to refer for arthroscopic vs open technique. [PubMed, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38173830/)
