## Quick Reference

**Weight-bearing radiographs are mandatory for every diagnosis in this cluster** — non-WB films systematically understate deformity and can misguide osteotomy selection.

### Hallux valgus severity → management (at a glance)

| Severity | HVA | IMA | Typical DMAA | First-line surgical strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mild | 15–20° | <11° | normal/mild ↑ | Distal osteotomy (chevron) or MIS distal chevron/Akin |
| Moderate | 20–40° | 11–16° | often ↑ | Diaphyseal osteotomy (scarf) ± Akin; distal chevron if DMAA-driven and reducible |
| Severe | >40° | >16° | variable | Proximal osteotomy or Lapidus (1st TMT arthrodesis), especially with 1st ray hypermobility/instability |

Normal values: HVA <15°, IMA <9°, DMAA <10–15° (some references to 15°), hallux interphalangeal angle (HVI) <10°.

### Treatment ladders — major diagnoses

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hallux valgus | Wide toe-box shoes, spacers, orthotics | — (bracing/night splints have no proven deformity-modifying effect) | Osteotomy (chevron/scarf/proximal) ± Akin, or Lapidus by severity/instability |
| Hallux rigidus | NSAIDs, stiff-soled/rocker-bottom shoe, dorsiflexion-blocking orthotic | Intra-articular corticosteroid injection (temporizing) | Cheilectomy (± Moberg) grade 1–2/select 3; arthrodesis or synthetic cartilage implant grade 3–4 |
| Lesser toe deformity | Toe spacers/silicone sleeves, wide/deep toe-box, taping | Flexor tenotomy (flexible) | PIP arthroplasty/arthrodesis, extensor tenotomy/capsulotomy, Weil osteotomy, plantar plate repair |
| Morton neuroma | Metatarsal pad, wide shoe, activity mod | Corticosteroid injection (1–3×); alcohol sclerosing series | Neurectomy (dorsal or plantar approach) |
| Metatarsalgia | Metatarsal pad/bar, rocker sole, orthotic offloading | Address underlying cause (plantar plate brace/strap) | Weil osteotomy ± plantar plate repair |
| Freiberg disease | Activity modification, stiff-soled shoe, offloading orthotic | Immobilization (boot) 4–6 wk in early stage | Dorsiflexion closing-wedge (Gauthier) osteotomy stage II–IV; debridement/arthroplasty stage V |
| Sesamoid pathology | Rigid orthotic with sesamoid cutout/carbon plate, activity mod | CAM boot immobilization 4–6 wk | Partial (not complete) sesamoidectomy; ORIF/bone graft for nonunion |
| Bunionette | Wide shoe, padding | — | Lateral condylectomy (type 1) or distal/diaphyseal/proximal osteotomy by type |

### Red flags

- **Acute, severe forefoot pain with erythema/warmth** → rule out gout, septic arthritis, Charcot neuroarthropathy (especially diabetic neuropathy) before assuming mechanical metatarsalgia.
- **Rapidly progressive multi-toe clawing** in a patient without known neuromuscular disease → screen for Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease or other peripheral neuropathy (check foot arch shape, cavus, hindfoot varus).
- **Bilateral, symmetric forefoot pain with morning stiffness elsewhere** → consider inflammatory arthropathy (RA, psoriatic arthritis) driving metatarsalgia/lesser toe deformity; check inflammatory markers.
- **Point tenderness over metatarsal neck with recent activity increase** → stress fracture until proven otherwise, not simple metatarsalgia.
- **Sudden loss of sensation in the diabetic foot with forefoot collapse** → Charcot process; immediate offloading (total contact cast), not elective forefoot surgery.

### Differential diagnosis pearls

| Presentation | Consider besides the obvious mechanical diagnosis |
|---|---|
| Forefoot pain + web-space numbness/click | Morton neuroma vs stress fracture vs plantar plate tear — exam + ultrasound differentiate |
| 2nd MTP pain with swelling, no trauma | Plantar plate tear/synovitis vs early crossover toe vs Freiberg disease (younger patients) vs stress fracture |
| Multi-toe clawing, high arch | Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease or other peripheral neuropathy until excluded |
| Bilateral symmetric forefoot pain, morning stiffness | Rheumatoid/psoriatic arthritis driving metatarsalgia and lesser toe deformity |
| Painful 1st MTP after minor trauma, effusion | Turf toe (plantar plate/capsule sprain) vs early hallux rigidus flare vs gout |
| Great toe pain, tophaceous swelling | Gout — check serum urate, consider joint aspiration before assuming hallux rigidus flare |

### Key numbers

- HVA >40°, IMA >16–18°, or a hypermobile/arthritic 1st TMT joint → favor **Lapidus** over distal/diaphyseal osteotomy.
- Coughlin-Shurnas grade ≥3 with pain **throughout** midrange dorsiflexion → cheilectomy alone unreliable; consider fusion or implant.
- 1st MTP arthrodesis position: **~15–20° dorsiflexion relative to the floor** (10–15° relative to the 1st metatarsal shaft), 5–15° valgus, neutral rotation.
- Bipartite tibial sesamoid prevalence in the general population: **~10–33%**, often bilateral — do not mistake for an acute fracture.
- Morton neuroma diagnostic ultrasound threshold: lesions **>5 mm** correlate more strongly with symptoms.
- Cartiva/synthetic cartilage implant literature: reoperation/revision rates in the **~8–14%** range at mid-term follow-up (higher than fusion, which has union rates of 90–97%).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

**First ray complex.** The 1st MTP joint is stabilized medially by the medial collateral ligament and abductor hallucis, laterally by the adductor hallucis (transverse and oblique heads) and lateral collateral ligament, and plantarly by the plantar plate and the sesamoid apparatus. The tibial and fibular sesamoids sit within the flexor hallucis brevis tendon, articulate with the crista (plantar keel) of the 1st metatarsal head, and translate with pronation/valgus drift of the hallux. Hallux valgus develops through an interplay of medial capsular attenuation, lateral soft-tissue contracture (adductor hallucis, lateral MTP capsule, transverse metatarsal ligament), progressive metatarsus primus varus (medial deviation of the 1st metatarsal), and pronation of the hallux with sesamoid subluxation beneath the intermetatarsal space. Contributing factors: pes planovalgus, ligamentous laxity (generalized hypermobility, Ehlers-Danlos), first-ray hypermobility at the tarsometatarsal (TMT, Lisfranc) joint, inflammatory arthropathy, neuromuscular disease, and constrictive/pointed-toe footwear (a strong epidemiologic association though not a sole cause).

**Hallux rigidus** is degenerative osteoarthritis of the 1st MTP joint, typically starting dorsally (where impingement occurs at terminal dorsiflexion during gait) with a dorsal osteophyte ("dorsal bunion") that mechanically blocks dorsiflexion before global joint-space narrowing occurs. Etiology is multifactorial: chronic microtrauma/hallux valgus interphalangeus, elongated or dorsiflexed 1st metatarsal, flat or chevron-shaped metatarsal head, prior trauma (turf toe, osteochondral injury), inflammatory arthropathy, and a strong familial/bilateral tendency (~70–80% report contralateral involvement).

**Lesser toe deformities** arise from an imbalance between the intrinsic muscles (lumbricals, interossei — normally flex the MTP and extend the IP joints) and the extrinsic long flexors/extensors, aggravated by MTP joint synovitis/plantar plate attrition (especially 2nd MTP, which lacks the protection of an adjacent toe and bears disproportionate load with an Index-minus or long 2nd metatarsal). Chronic MTP synovitis progressively attenuates the plantar plate, permitting dorsal subluxation and eventual crossover deformity.

**Morton (interdigital) neuroma** is not a true neuroma but a perineural fibrosis of the common digital nerve, most often in the 3rd webspace where branches from the medial and lateral plantar nerves converge (a thicker, less mobile nerve), followed by the 2nd webspace. Repetitive nerve entrapment beneath the transverse intermetatarsal ligament, tight/narrow toe-box footwear, and forefoot pronation/hypermobility are contributory.

**Metatarsalgia** is a symptom complex, not a diagnosis — pain under the metatarsal heads from primary (structural: long/plantarflexed metatarsal, cavus foot, iatrogenic after a first-ray procedure that transfers load laterally), secondary (systemic: RA, gout, diabetic fat-pad atrophy), or neuroma-related mechanisms. Plantar plate insufficiency of the 2nd (occasionally 3rd) MTP joint is a very common structural driver in isolated 2nd-ray overload.

**Freiberg disease** is osteonecrosis of a lesser metatarsal head, overwhelmingly the 2nd (also 3rd), classically in adolescent/young adult females, attributed to repetitive microtrauma to a vulnerable epiphyseal blood supply during rapid growth combined with mechanical overload of a relatively long 2nd metatarsal.

**Sesamoid pathology** encompasses sesamoiditis (chronic overload/inflammation), acute and stress fracture, osteonecrosis, and chronic diastasis of a bipartite sesamoid; the tibial sesamoid is far more frequently symptomatic given greater axial load transmission during push-off.

**Bunionette (tailor's bunion)** is a prominence/deformity of the 5th metatarsal head analogous to hallux valgus at the opposite border of the foot, from lateral metatarsal head hypertrophy, shaft bowing, or an increased 4th–5th intermetatarsal angle.

**Load transfer across the forefoot.** During normal push-off the 1st ray bears roughly twice the load of each lesser ray individually, transmitted substantially through the sesamoid apparatus. Any procedure that shortens, elevates, or destabilizes the 1st ray (aggressive Lapidus correction, over-resected cheilectomy, complete sesamoidectomy) redistributes load laterally and is a recognized mechanism for iatrogenic transfer metatarsalgia under the lesser metatarsal heads — this is why the Treatment and Complications sections below repeatedly flag lateral-ray overload as a risk of 1st-ray procedures, and why isolated lesser-metatarsal osteotomy (Weil) must respect the overall metatarsal parabola rather than being planned in isolation.

**Guideline landscape.** Unlike hip/knee arthroplasty or spine, there is no single unifying AAOS or NICE clinical practice guideline covering the whole forefoot cluster; the evidence base is instead built from society position statements, industry-sponsored pivotal trials (notably the Cartiva MOTION trial, which underpinned both FDA approval and subsequent NICE medical-technology guidance review in the UK), and specialty consensus documents from bodies such as the American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (AOFAS), the British Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society (BOFAS), and European groups including EFAS (European Foot and Ankle Society) and ESSKA-AFAS. When counseling patients, it is more defensible in this subspecialty to cite the specific pivotal trial or comparative series (e.g., "MOTION trial," a named MIS-versus-open RCT) than a single named blanket guideline, and this monograph does so throughout the Treatment and Recent Evidence sections.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

- **Standing (weight-bearing) AP, lateral, and oblique/sesamoid axial radiographs of the foot** are the baseline study for essentially every diagnosis in this cluster. Non-weight-bearing films underestimate hallux valgus and metatarsalgia-related deformity.
- **Hallux valgus:** measure HVA (angle between 1st metatarsal and proximal phalanx long axes), IMA (between 1st and 2nd metatarsal long axes), DMAA (distal metatarsal articular angle — orientation of the metatarsal head articular surface relative to the shaft axis; elevated DMAA suggests a congruent-joint deformity requiring an intra-articular/biplanar correction rather than a simple shaft osteotomy), hallux valgus interphalangeus (HVI), and tibial sesamoid position (Hardy & Clapham grading, 0–7; position ≥4–5 suggests significant sesamoid subluxation warranting lateral release/derotation). Assess 1st TMT joint congruency and obliquity on the AP view, and clinically stress the 1st ray in the sagittal plane for hypermobility (>8–10 mm of dorsal-plantar excursion relative to the 2nd ray is generally considered abnormal, though the test has only moderate reliability).
- **Hallux rigidus:** lateral view for dorsal osteophyte size and joint-space narrowing pattern (dorsal vs global); clinically measure passive 1st MTP dorsiflexion (normal ~65–75°) and note whether pain occurs at end-range only versus throughout the arc (this distinction drives grade 3 vs 4 treatment selection).
- **Lesser toe deformities:** clinical classification by which joint is deformed and whether the deformity is passively correctable (flexible) or fixed (rigid); perform the **MTP dorsal drawer/Lachman test** for plantar plate instability (>50% subluxation or frank dislocatability = grade 2–3 tear) and assess for a positive "V-sign" (gap between toes) suggesting crossover deformity. Weight-bearing AP looks for MTP subluxation/dislocation and metatarsal parabola (relative metatarsal protrusion, Maestro method).
- **Morton neuroma:** clinical Mulder click (palpable/audible click with lateral forefoot compression + web-space palpation) has good specificity; **ultrasound** is the preferred first-line imaging (dynamic, no contraindications, size >5 mm correlates with symptoms) with sensitivity/specificity generally >90% in experienced hands; **MRI** reserved for equivocal cases, revision planning, or when webspace injection has been performed (post-injection fibrosis clouds ultrasound).
- **Metatarsalgia:** weight-bearing AP for metatarsal parabola/relative length, lateral for metatarsal declination; ultrasound or MRI for plantar plate tear grading (Coughlin/Nery classification, grade 0 normal to grade IV frank dislocation).
- **Freiberg disease:** AP/lateral foot radiographs stage the disease (Smillie); MRI helpful early (bone marrow edema, subchondral collapse before radiographic changes are visible) and to assess viable articular cartilage for joint-preserving osteotomy planning.
- **Sesamoid pathology:** AP, lateral, and a dedicated **sesamoid axial (skyline) view**; bipartite sesamoids typically have smooth, rounded, sclerotic margins versus the sharp/jagged, non-sclerotic margins of an acute fracture. **Bone scan or MRI** distinguishes acute fracture/stress reaction/osteonecrosis (marrow edema) from an asymptomatic bipartite variant when radiographs are equivocal — this distinction matters because ~10–33% of the population has a bipartite tibial sesamoid, frequently bilateral, and comparison views of the contralateral foot are useful.
- **Bunionette:** weight-bearing AP measures the 4th–5th intermetatarsal angle (normal <8°) and the 5th MTP lateral deviation angle, which together define Coughlin type.

## Classification

### Hallux valgus severity (HVA/IMA)

| Grade | HVA | IMA | Notes / management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | <15° | <9° | No treatment |
| Mild | 15–20° | <11° | Distal soft-tissue procedure + distal metaphyseal osteotomy (chevron); MIS distal chevron/Akin is a reasonable alternative |
| Moderate | 20–40° | 11–16° | Diaphyseal osteotomy (scarf) usually required for adequate translation; distal chevron only if congruent joint/high DMAA and reducible |
| Severe | >40° | >16° | Proximal osteotomy (crescentic/opening wedge) or 1st TMT arthrodesis (Lapidus), particularly if 1st ray hypermobile/unstable, arthritic, or deformity recurrent |

DMAA and sesamoid position modify this framework: an elevated DMAA (congruent-joint hallux valgus, common in juveniles) needs an intra-articular or biplanar correction (e.g., biplanar chevron or double/triple osteotomy) rather than a shaft translation alone, or the deformity recurs. Marked sesamoid subluxation (Hardy & Clapham ≥4–5) mandates adequate lateral soft-tissue release/derotation regardless of the bony procedure chosen.

### Coughlin-Shurnas classification — hallux rigidus

| Grade | Dorsiflexion (passive) | Radiographic findings | Clinical | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 40–60° (or 10–20° less than normal side) | Normal | Stiffness only, no pain | Observation, activity modification |
| 1 | 30–40° | Dorsal osteophyte, minimal joint-space narrowing | Mild pain at extremes of dorsiflexion | NSAIDs, shoe modification/stiff sole, orthotic; cheilectomy if refractory |
| 2 | 10–30° | Dorsal/lateral/medial osteophytes, mild–moderate joint-space narrowing, flattening of metatarsal head | Moderate–severe pain, more constant, may have periarticular pain | Cheilectomy ± Moberg (dorsiflexion) proximal phalanx osteotomy |
| 3 | ≤10° | Severe joint-space narrowing, substantial osteophyte formation, possible subchondral cysts, sesamoid involvement | Pain at extremes of ROM (near-constant), **but a pain-free midrange arc** | Cheilectomy may still succeed if a pain-free midrange arc is preserved; otherwise arthrodesis or synthetic cartilage implant |
| 4 | Same as Grade 3 | Same as Grade 3 | Pain **throughout** the midrange arc of motion, not just at extremes | Cheilectomy unreliable; arthrodesis (gold standard) or synthetic cartilage implant in selected patients |

The Grade 3/4 distinction — a pain-free versus pain-throughout midrange arc — is the single most important clinical discriminator for whether a motion-preserving procedure (cheilectomy) can still be offered.

### Hammer vs claw vs mallet toe

| Deformity | MTP joint | PIP joint | DIP joint | Typical association |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hammer toe** | Neutral or mild extension | Flexed | Extended or neutral | Isolated, most often 2nd toe, often idiopathic or from a tight/short shoe |
| **Claw toe** | Extended (hyperextension/dorsal subluxation) | Flexed | Flexed | Usually multiple toes; strongly associated with neuromuscular disease (Charcot-Marie-Tooth, diabetic neuropathy) or cavus foot |
| **Mallet toe** | Neutral | Neutral | Flexed | Isolated DIP-level deformity, often from a long toe or tight distal shoe box |

Management implication: **flexible** deformities (passively correctable) respond to soft-tissue procedures — flexor tenotomy or flexor-to-extensor tendon transfer (Girdlestone-Taylor) for hammer toe, extensor tenotomy/dorsal MTP capsulotomy for claw toe. **Rigid** deformities require bony correction — PIP resection arthroplasty or arthrodesis (K-wire, intramedullary implant, or peg-in-hole fusion) ± extensor tenotomy/capsulotomy, with a Weil osteotomy added when there is concurrent MTP subluxation/dislocation to decompress the joint and allow soft-tissue balancing.

### Coughlin classification — bunionette

| Type | Deformity | 4th–5th IMA | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Enlarged/laterally deviated 5th metatarsal head (widened head), normal shaft alignment | Normal | Lateral condylectomy ± distal osteotomy |
| 2 | Lateral bowing of the 5th metatarsal shaft | Increased | Distal chevron osteotomy or diaphyseal oblique osteotomy |
| 3 | Widened 4th–5th intermetatarsal angle from divergence ("splayfoot") | Increased | Diaphyseal or proximal osteotomy for adequate translation; isolated distal osteotomy under-corrects |

### Smillie staging — Freiberg disease (referenced under Treatment/Freiberg subsection)

Stage I (subchondral fracture, no collapse) through Stage V (flattened, arthritic articular collapse) — full detail with corresponding management is given in the Freiberg subsection of Treatment to keep staging and management adjacent.

### Sesamoid pathology — diagnostic categories and imaging distinction

| Entity | Radiographic clue | Confirmatory imaging | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bipartite sesamoid (asymptomatic anatomic variant) | Smooth, rounded, sclerotic (corticated) margins; often bilateral | Comparison contralateral-foot views; no marrow edema on MRI | No treatment; do not mistake for fracture |
| Acute sesamoid fracture | Sharp, jagged, non-sclerotic fracture line; single event/trauma history | MRI/bone scan shows acute marrow edema | Immobilization first; ORIF/bone graft only for symptomatic nonunion |
| Sesamoiditis / stress reaction | Often normal or subtle radiographs | MRI marrow edema without discrete fracture line | Offloading orthotic, activity modification, boot trial |
| Sesamoid osteonecrosis (AVN) | Increased density/fragmentation over time | MRI low signal, patchy or diffuse marrow change; bone scan may show decreased or increased uptake depending on stage | Prolonged offloading; partial sesamoidectomy if refractory |

### Plantar plate tear grading (Coughlin/Nery) — referenced under Metatarsalgia and Lesser Toe Deformities

| Grade | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Normal plantar plate, synovitis only | Non-operative: taping, offloading |
| I | Attenuation/thinning, no tear | Non-operative first-line; consider debridement if refractory |
| II | Partial tear (<50% width) or one-sided attachment tear | Direct repair if instability/symptoms persist despite conservative care |
| III | Tear >50% width, MTP subluxable/dislocatable | Direct plantar plate repair strongly favored, usually combined with Weil osteotomy |
| IV | Complete tear with fixed MTP dislocation | Repair with Weil osteotomy; salvage arthroplasty if joint destroyed |

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Hallux valgus:** wide/soft toe-box shoes, toe spacers, bunion pads, and prefabricated or custom orthotics reduce symptoms but **do not reverse or reliably slow radiographic progression**; counsel patients accordingly before offering these as a durable alternative to surgery. Night splints/bracing have not been shown to produce lasting deformity correction in adults.
- **Hallux rigidus:** activity/shoe modification (rocker-bottom sole, stiff/carbon-fiber shank, Morton's extension to limit terminal dorsiflexion), NSAIDs, and a dorsiflexion-blocking orthotic form first-line management for Coughlin-Shurnas grade 0–2; most grade 0–1 patients are managed successfully nonoperatively long-term.
- **Lesser toe deformities:** wide/deep toe-box footwear, silicone toe sleeves/spacers, and taping/strapping (dynamic splinting) are effective for flexible deformities and mild symptoms; largely palliative for fixed/rigid deformity.
- **Morton neuroma:** metatarsal pad positioned just proximal to the neuroma, wide/low-heel shoes, and activity modification are appropriate first-line measures and produce meaningful symptomatic improvement in a substantial minority of patients, more so with smaller lesions and shorter symptom duration.
- **Metatarsalgia:** metatarsal pad or bar, rocker-sole shoe, custom orthotic with a metatarsal offloading dome, and treatment of the underlying driver (e.g., a plantar-plate strapping/taping program for 2nd MTP instability) are first-line and often sufficient in mechanical (non-structural) cases.
- **Freiberg disease:** early-stage (Smillie I–II) disease is managed with activity modification, a stiff-soled or rocker-bottom shoe, and a period of offloading (CAM boot 4–6 weeks) to allow the fracture/collapse to stabilize.
- **Sesamoid pathology:** activity modification, a rigid/carbon-fiber orthotic with a sesamoid cutout to offload the affected sesamoid, and — for acute fracture/stress reaction — CAM boot immobilization for 4–6 weeks are first-line; most sesamoiditis and many stress reactions resolve without surgery.
- **Bunionette:** wide toe-box shoes and lateral forefoot padding are first-line and often sufficient given the lower biomechanical demand on the 5th ray compared with the 1st.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Hallux rigidus:** intra-articular corticosteroid injection provides temporary symptomatic relief and can help confirm the 1st MTP as the pain source; effect duration is typically weeks to a few months and diminishes with repeated use — use as a bridge, not a substitute for definitive treatment in higher-grade disease. Viscosupplementation has weaker evidence in the 1st MTP than in the knee and is not a standard recommendation.
- **Morton neuroma:** corticosteroid injection (typically 1–3 injections, dorsal or plantar approach, ideally ultrasound-guided) gives short-to-medium-term relief in a majority of patients but symptom recurrence is common, and repeated injections carry a risk of plantar fat-pad atrophy and, rarely, tendon/plantar plate weakening if injected too plantarly or too frequently. **Alcohol sclerosing injection** (dilute, typically ~4% dehydrated alcohol in local anesthetic, administered as a series of 4–7 ultrasound- or palpation-guided injections roughly 1–2 weeks apart) is a well-described intermediate step before surgery, with reported success (meaningful symptom relief, procedure avoidance) in roughly two-thirds to just under 90% of patients across published series, though study quality is heterogeneous and reflects mostly single-center case series rather than large RCTs. Radiofrequency ablation and cryoablation are described alternatives with a smaller evidence base.
- **Metatarsalgia / plantar plate disease:** corticosteroid injection into an inflamed MTP joint can help acutely but should be used cautiously (and generally avoided as a repeated strategy) when plantar plate pathology is suspected, given a plausible (though not definitively proven) risk of accelerating plate attenuation/rupture.

### Operative

**Hallux valgus — osteotomy selection by severity**

- **Distal chevron osteotomy:** workhorse for mild–moderate deformity (HVA <30–40°, IMA <13–15°). V-shaped osteotomy through the metatarsal neck, lateral translation of the capital fragment (typically up to ~40–50% of shaft width), screw or headless-compression fixation. A **biplanar chevron** adds a rotational cut to correct an elevated DMAA. Technical pearls: preserve the plantar/lateral soft-tissue hinge to protect the blood supply to the capital fragment; avoid combining an aggressive lateral release with a distal osteotomy in a single stage when perfusion is a concern (risk factor for metatarsal head AVN).
- **Scarf (diaphyseal Z-osteotomy):** versatile for moderate–severe deformity; permits translation, shortening/lengthening, plantar/dorsal displacement, and rotation through a long diaphyseal Z-cut, giving more powerful IMA correction than a distal osteotomy while retaining a lower nonunion risk than proximal osteotomies. Technically demanding (risk of "troughing"/vertical displacement if the cuts are not parallel) and requires meticulous fixation (typically two screws).
- **Proximal osteotomy (crescentic, opening/closing wedge) or Lapidus (1st TMT arthrodesis):** reserved for severe deformity (HVA >40°, IMA >16–18°), a hypermobile or arthritic 1st TMT joint, ligamentous laxity syndromes, juvenile/adolescent hallux valgus, and recurrent deformity after a prior distal/diaphyseal procedure. Lapidus fuses the 1st TMT joint (typically plate ± compression screw construct), directly addressing 1st-ray instability, but carries a nonunion risk (~3–6% in contemporary series) and a longer initial protected weight-bearing period than a distal osteotomy.
- **Akin (proximal phalanx medial closing-wedge) osteotomy:** adjunct, not a stand-alone procedure, for residual hallux valgus interphalangeus after the metatarsal deformity is corrected.
- **MIS (percutaneous) bunion surgery — 3rd generation:** contemporary MIS technique (e.g., percutaneous chevron/Akin — "PECA" — with dedicated burr instrumentation and formal screw fixation) differs materially from first-generation percutaneous techniques (which lacked internal fixation and had higher malunion/recurrence rates). Comparative series and randomized trials against open chevron/scarf report **broadly equivalent radiographic correction (HVA/IMA) and patient-reported outcomes at 1–2 years**, with the MIS group showing less early soft-tissue swelling/pain and a faster early return to shoes, at the cost of a **steep learning curve**, a recognized risk of thermal osteonecrosis if burring technique is poor, and similar rates of recurrence and revision to open techniques in experienced hands. MIS is best regarded as a technique choice with an outcomes profile comparable to open surgery in mild–moderate deformity when performed by a surgeon past the learning curve, rather than as an independently superior procedure; evidence for severe deformity and for MIS-Lapidus equivalents is less mature.
- **Lateral soft-tissue release/derotation of the sesamoid apparatus** is performed as needed (via the 1st webspace or as part of the distal osteotomy exposure) whenever sesamoid subluxation is significant, regardless of which osteotomy is chosen.

**Hallux rigidus**

- **Cheilectomy:** removal of the dorsal ~25–30% of the metatarsal head osteophyte/exostosis and any impinging dorsal phalangeal osteophyte, preserving the plantar articular cartilage; indicated for Coughlin-Shurnas grade 1–2 and select grade 3 with a preserved pain-free midrange arc. Reported success (satisfactory pain relief, procedure durability) in roughly 80–90% of appropriately selected patients at intermediate-to-long-term follow-up; a **Moberg (dorsiflexion) proximal phalanx osteotomy** is a useful adjunct in borderline grade 2/3 cases to functionally increase dorsiflexion after cheilectomy.
- **Arthrodesis:** the reference-standard, most predictable procedure for grade 3–4 (and revision) hallux rigidus. Fusion position ~15–20° dorsiflexion relative to the floor (≈10–15° relative to the metatarsal shaft), 5–15° valgus, neutral rotation; contemporary fixation (crossed screws, or plate + compression screw) achieves union rates of **90–97%**. Trade-off: permanent loss of 1st MTP motion, which alters push-off mechanics and shoe-wear preference (limits high-heel use) but is generally well tolerated functionally.
- **Synthetic cartilage implant (polyvinyl alcohol hydrogel, Cartiva):** a motion-preserving alternative to fusion for grade 3 (and selected grade 4) disease, press-fit into the metatarsal head after minimal bone resection. The pivotal MOTION randomized trial (Baumhauer et al., JBJS 2016) found the implant **non-inferior to arthrodesis** for pain and function at 2 years while preserving MTP motion. Subsequent mid-to-long-term series report generally durable pain relief but a **secondary/revision surgery rate meaningfully higher than fusion** (commonly cited in the roughly 8–14% range at 4–7 years across published cohorts, with implant subsidence and progressive metatarsal-side degeneration as recognized failure modes) — counsel patients that the implant trades a small-but-real revision-risk premium for motion preservation, and that revision to fusion after a failed implant remains a reliable salvage. Appropriate candidates are grade 3 patients who prioritize retained motion and accept a somewhat higher reoperation likelihood; arthrodesis remains preferable for grade 4, high-demand laborers, or patients who prioritize the single most durable outcome.
- **Resection (Keller) arthroplasty:** reserved for low-demand, typically elderly patients; relieves pain reliably but sacrifices intrinsic muscle balance and push-off strength, with a recognized risk of transfer metatarsalgia and cock-up deformity — not first-line in an active patient.

**Lesser toe deformities**

- **Flexible hammer toe:** flexor-to-extensor tendon transfer (Girdlestone-Taylor) or simple flexor tenotomy.
- **Flexible claw toe:** extensor tenotomy/dorsal MTP capsulotomy ± flexor tenotomy, addressing the underlying MTP hyperextension.
- **Rigid hammer/claw/mallet toe:** PIP joint resection arthroplasty (removal of the femoral head/condyles of the proximal phalanx) or formal PIP arthrodesis, fixed with a K-wire, intramedullary implant, or peg-in-hole technique; arthrodesis generally gives a more durable, straighter, more stable correction than resection arthroplasty at the cost of a stiffer toe.
- **Concurrent MTP subluxation/dislocation (crossover toe):** add a **Weil osteotomy** (oblique intra-articular shortening/elevating osteotomy of the metatarsal neck) to decompress the joint and permit reduction, with **direct plantar plate repair** (via a dorsal MTP approach, typically with an implant fixed with anchor or transosseous suture) for grade II–III plantar plate tears (Coughlin/Nery classification); simple soft-tissue balancing without addressing plate incompetence has a high recurrence rate for higher-grade tears.

**Morton neuroma**

- **Neurectomy (surgical excision):** indicated after failure of activity modification, footwear change, and at least one round of injections (corticosteroid and/or alcohol sclerosing); performed via a **dorsal approach** (less wound-healing morbidity, standard first choice) or a **plantar approach** (better direct visualization/access, some surgeons prefer for revision cases, at the cost of a weight-bearing scar). Reported success in the range of **80–90%**, with a recognized risk of a symptomatic **stump (amputation) neuroma** — reported incidence spans a wide range across series (roughly 2–30% depending on definition/follow-up) — and permanent numbness of the involved web space, which patients must be counseled about preoperatively as an expected trade-off, not a complication per se.

**Metatarsalgia**

- **Weil osteotomy:** oblique intra-articular shortening ± elevating osteotomy of the metatarsal neck for isolated or multi-ray overload; effective for decompressing an overloaded MTP joint but carries a well-recognized risk of **floating toe deformity** (loss of plantar flexion power/MTP subluxation) if over-shortened or if the plantar plate/flexor balance is not respected.
- **DMMO (distal metatarsal minimally invasive osteotomy):** percutaneous extra-articular alternative to open Weil, avoiding formal fixation in most techniques (relies on callus formation with the metatarsal head "floating" into a corrected position); growing evidence base with outcomes broadly comparable to open Weil in appropriately selected multi-ray overload, with less soft-tissue morbidity but less precise control of final position.
- **Plantar plate repair:** for grade II–III (Coughlin/Nery) tears with instability, as above under lesser toe deformities.

**Freiberg disease**

| Smillie stage | Findings | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Subchondral fracture of the metatarsal head, no collapse | Offloading/immobilization; usually resolves |
| II | Central depression of the articular surface | Continued conservative management, or joint debridement if refractory |
| III | Further central collapse with a viable dorsal or plantar hinge of articular cartilage | **Dorsiflexion closing-wedge osteotomy (Gauthier/Kinnard technique)** — rotates viable plantar articular cartilage dorsally into the load-bearing zone; joint-preserving procedure of choice at this stage |
| IV | Loose intra-articular body/fragmentation | Debridement, loose body excision ± osteotomy |
| V | Flattened, arthritic collapse of the metatarsal head | Joint debridement, interpositional arthroplasty, or implant/resection arthroplasty as salvage |

**Sesamoid pathology**

- **Partial sesamoidectomy** is strongly preferred over complete excision when surgery is needed for refractory sesamoiditis, osteonecrosis, or nonunion — **complete tibial sesamoidectomy** materially increases the risk of iatrogenic **hallux valgus** (loss of the medial static/dynamic restraint), and **complete fibular sesamoidectomy** increases the risk of iatrogenic **hallux varus**; both are avoided when a partial excision (removing only the fractured/necrotic pole) can achieve symptom relief while preserving the FHB sling.
- **ORIF/bone grafting with screw or tension-band fixation** is reserved for a symptomatic fracture nonunion in a young, active patient who wishes to avoid sesamoidectomy.

**Bunionette**

- **Lateral condylectomy** alone for Coughlin type 1 with prominent lateral head but normal shaft/IMA alignment.
- **Distal chevron osteotomy** for type 1–2 with mild shaft-driven deformity, mirroring the medial-side chevron principle.
- **Diaphyseal oblique (Weil-type) or proximal osteotomy** for type 2–3 with an increased 4th–5th intermetatarsal angle, since a distal-only procedure under-corrects a shaft- or angle-driven deformity.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Hallux valgus (distal/diaphyseal osteotomy, e.g., chevron/scarf, screw-fixed)**

- Weeks 0–2: postoperative shoe or boot, weight-bear as tolerated on the heel/lateral border in most rigid-fixation constructs (surgeon- and construct-dependent); elevation and ice to control swelling; dressing changes, wound check at 10–14 days.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive weight-bearing in a stiff-soled postoperative/surgical shoe; begin gentle active/active-assisted 1st MTP ROM once the wound is healed (~2–3 weeks) to limit stiffness; avoid running/impact.
- Weeks 6–8: radiographic union check; transition to a wide, supportive regular shoe as swelling and comfort allow; progress ROM and strengthening (towel scrunches, intrinsic strengthening, gait retraining).
- Weeks 8–12: return to low-impact exercise (cycling, swimming) as comfort allows; return to running/impact sport typically **10–12+ weeks**, guided by radiographic union and symptom resolution.
- Return to sedentary work: often as early as 1–2 weeks in a postoperative shoe; **return to standing/manual labor: 6–10 weeks**, dependent on osteotomy type and fixation stability.

**Hallux valgus — Lapidus (1st TMT arthrodesis)**

- Weeks 0–2: non-weight-bearing or protected touch-down weight-bearing in a splint/boot per fixation construct (rigid plate constructs increasingly allow earlier protected weight-bearing).
- Weeks 2–6: progressive weight-bearing in a CAM boot as radiographs allow.
- Weeks 6–8: radiographic fusion check; if consolidating, transition out of the boot into supportive shoes.
- Weeks 8–12+: continue progressive loading and return to impact activity only after confirmed union (typically **10–12 weeks**, sometimes longer); nonunion risk (~3–6%) means union should be radiographically confirmed, not assumed, before full unrestricted return to sport.

**Hallux rigidus**

- Cheilectomy: weight-bear as tolerated in a postoperative shoe immediately; early active ROM started within the first 1–2 weeks is important to prevent recurrent dorsal impingement/stiffness; return to regular shoes by 4–6 weeks; return to sport/impact activity typically 6–8 weeks.
- Arthrodesis: non-weight-bearing or heel-weight-bearing in a boot for the first 2 weeks, then progressive weight-bearing in a boot to ~6 weeks, transitioning to a supportive shoe once union is confirmed (typically 8–10 weeks radiographically, with clinical union often trailing slightly); return to low-impact activity ~10–12 weeks, high-impact/running only after solid union, often 3–4 months.
- Synthetic cartilage implant: similar early protected weight-bearing to cheilectomy (postoperative shoe, weight-bear as tolerated), with earlier initiation of ROM given the motion-preserving intent; return to activity generally parallels cheilectomy timelines (6–8 weeks for most activity).

**Lesser toe / Weil osteotomy / plantar plate repair**

- Weeks 0–2: postoperative shoe, K-wire (if used for PIP fixation) protected until removal typically at **3–4 weeks** in clinic; weight-bear as tolerated with attention to keeping load off the operated ray where a Weil osteotomy has been performed.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive weight-bearing, buddy-taping of the toe once K-wire is removed to maintain alignment during early healing; gentle ROM exercises.
- Weeks 6–8: transition to regular shoes as swelling allows; strengthening and gait normalization.
- Return to sport: typically **8–12 weeks**, longer if plantar plate repair was performed given the need for soft-tissue healing under tension.

**Morton neuroma excision**

- Weeks 0–2: postoperative shoe, weight-bear as tolerated, wound care.
- Weeks 2–4: transition to regular shoes as comfort allows; gradual return to activity.
- Return to sport: typically **4–6 weeks**; counsel on expected permanent web-space numbness distal to the excision site.

**Freiberg disease (Gauthier osteotomy)**

- Weeks 0–4: protected weight-bearing in a postoperative shoe/boot per fixation stability.
- Weeks 4–8: progressive weight-bearing and shoe transition once early union/consolidation is evident radiographically.
- Return to activity: typically **8–12 weeks**, guided by radiographic healing.

**Sesamoidectomy**

- Weeks 0–2: protected weight-bearing in a stiff-soled postoperative shoe/boot, off-loading the forefoot.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive weight-bearing, transition toward regular supportive/stiff-soled shoes.
- Return to running/impact/push-off-intensive sport: often **8–12 weeks**, since the FHB sling and 1st MTP push-off mechanics need to accommodate the altered sesamoid anatomy.

**Return-to-sport criteria (applies across procedures in this cluster):** radiographic union/consolidation confirmed where an osteotomy or arthrodesis was performed; near-full, pain-free active ROM of the operated joint(s) compared with the contralateral side; ability to perform a single-leg heel-rise and single-leg hop without pain; resolution of swelling to the point of fitting normal athletic footwear; and a graduated return through walking, brisk walking, jogging, running, and sport-specific cutting/pivoting drills, with no step advanced while symptomatic. Contact/pivoting sport is generally the last stage cleared, typically not before 12–16 weeks for osteotomy/fusion procedures and 6–8 weeks for soft-tissue-only procedures (neurectomy, cheilectomy).

**General physiotherapy content across this cluster:** early edema control (elevation, compression, cryotherapy), progressive active and passive ROM of the MTP/IP joints once wound-healed, intrinsic foot muscle strengthening (towel scrunches, marble pickup, short-foot exercises), gait retraining emphasizing normal heel-to-toe rollover and push-off, and scar mobilization once incisions are sealed. Impact/running return should generally be staged (walk → brisk walk → jog → run → sport-specific drills) and gated by radiographic union where an osteotomy/arthrodesis was performed, not by time alone.

## Complications

- **Hallux valgus surgery:** recurrence (~10–16% across series, higher with undercorrected IMA, unaddressed elevated DMAA, or unaddressed 1st-ray hypermobility), **iatrogenic hallux varus** (overcorrection of IMA, excessive lateral release/fibular sesamoidectomy, or medial capsule overplication — often more disabling than the original deformity), transfer metatarsalgia (from excessive 1st-ray shortening/elevation), **1st metatarsal head avascular necrosis** (risk increases with combined aggressive lateral soft-tissue release and a distal osteotomy that compromises the remaining blood supply), Lapidus nonunion (~3–6%), malunion, hardware irritation, infection, and residual/recurrent bunion pain from inadequate deformity correction for the severity grade.
- **Hallux rigidus surgery:** recurrent dorsal impingement/incomplete relief after cheilectomy (undersized resection or progression of underlying arthritis), arthrodesis malunion (excess dorsiflexion causes IP joint/plate-level overload and shoe-wear difficulty; insufficient dorsiflexion or plantarflexion causes painful push-off and transfer metatarsalgia to the lesser toes), nonunion (~3–10% depending on series and fixation), and — for synthetic cartilage implant — subsidence, progressive metatarsal-sided arthritis, and revision to arthrodesis.
- **Lesser toe surgery:** recurrent deformity (undercorrection or failure to address MTP-level pathology), floating toe (from Weil over-shortening/plantar plate imbalance), swan-neck deformity, transfer metatarsalgia to adjacent rays, pin-tract infection with K-wire fixation, and vascular compromise of the toe from overly aggressive soft-tissue release in a toe with borderline perfusion.
- **Morton neuroma excision:** stump (amputation) neuroma (reported incidence highly variable across series, roughly 2–30%), permanent web-space numbness (expected, not a true complication, but must be consented for), wound complications, and persistent/recurrent pain from missed multiple neuromas or an incorrect index level.
- **Metatarsalgia/Weil osteotomy:** floating toe/MTP instability, transfer metatarsalgia to adjacent (usually more lateral) rays if a single ray is overcorrected relative to its neighbors, malunion, and recurrence if the underlying cavus/plantar plate driver is not addressed.
- **Freiberg disease surgery:** avascular progression despite osteotomy, hardware prominence, and residual stiffness; salvage arthroplasty/implant carries the same general lesser-MTP arthroplasty complication profile (instability, transfer metatarsalgia).
- **Sesamoid surgery:** iatrogenic hallux valgus (complete tibial sesamoidectomy) or hallux varus (complete fibular sesamoidectomy), weakness of push-off, and persistent pain if the wrong (or both) sesamoid(s) are excised without indication.
- **Bunionette surgery:** transfer lesser metatarsalgia, recurrence if the wrong osteotomy type is chosen for the Coughlin type present, and lateral wound complications given thinner soft-tissue coverage on the lateral forefoot.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **MIS (percutaneous) bunion surgery — 2025 meta-analysis confirms equivalence to open chevron:** a systematic review/meta-analysis (search through June 2025; 6 studies, 3 RCTs, 342 patients/352 feet) comparing MIS vs open chevron osteotomy found comparable AOFAS scores, VAS pain, and radiographic correction (HVA/IMA/DMAA) between groups — see [ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S126877312500267X). An earlier meta-analysis in *PMC* (2022) reached the same equivalence conclusion — [Minimally Invasive vs. Open Surgery for Hallux Valgus: A Meta-Analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8978717/).
- **MIS proximal vs distal chevron-Akin for severe deformity (2024 RCT):** a randomized prospective study found both proximal and distal MIS chevron-Akin osteotomies effective for severe hallux valgus, but the **distal** variant had a notably higher rate of unsatisfactory correction at final follow-up than the proximal variant — [PubMed 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38705891/).
- **Cartiva synthetic cartilage implant — mid-term data now published (2024):** a mid-term outcomes study (*Bone & Joint Open*, 2024) reports the longest independent follow-up in the literature to date, describing "reassuring mid-term efficacy... with better-than-expected survival" — [Bone & Joint 2024](https://boneandjoint.org.uk/Article/10.1302/2633-1462.59.BJO-2024-0031.R1) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39314066/)). Reported figures vary by series: one study found a 5-year **revision** rate of only 5% with no implant loosening, while a separate shorter-follow-up cohort reported an 18.2% **total reoperation** rate (including revisions to arthrodesis and manipulations under anesthesia) and roughly a third of patients developing postoperative stiffness requiring MUA — [Surgical Outcomes of Synthetic Cartilage Implant Hemiarthroplasty, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10728759/). This confirms the monograph's general "8–14% reoperation range, higher than fusion" framing is directionally right but the true rate is series-dependent and warrants individualized counseling rather than a single number.
- **Morton neuroma corticosteroid injection — durability lower than previously assumed:** a study found 51% of patients receiving a single ultrasound-guided corticosteroid injection required further treatment within 2 years (32% went on to surgical excision, 19% to a repeat injection), and at a mean 4.8-year follow-up the original injection remained effective in only 36% of patients — [Efficacy of Ultrasound-Guided Steroid Injections, PMC 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12681235/). Minor local complications (skin discoloration) occurred in 25% of injected patients.
- **Alcohol sclerosing injection for Morton neuroma — 2024/2025 systematic reviews support the monograph's staged approach:** a systematic review (12 studies, 6 RCTs, 1,438 patients) and a related PRISMA-registered single-arm meta-analysis both report alcohol injections are well tolerated with no serious adverse events and a favorable safety profile versus corticosteroid (no reported fat-pad atrophy or skin hypopigmentation), supporting its role as an effective intermediate step before neurectomy — [Infiltrative Treatment of Morton's Neuroma: A Systematic Review, ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1524904224001905); [Corticosteroid- and Alcohol-Based Injections, ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S3050717025000061).
- **DMMO vs Weil osteotomy — 2024 meta-analysis finds equal efficacy, different complication profiles:** a 2024 meta-analysis (4 studies, 211 patients) found Weil's osteotomy and DMMO equally safe and effective for metatarsalgia (no significant AOFAS/VAS difference), but time to bony healing was significantly **longer for DMMO**, while Weil osteotomy had more wound problems and MTP stiffness — [PubMed 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38821005/) ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0958259224000348)).
- **Weight-bearing CT (WBCT) — first metatarsal pronation confirmed as a distinct, measurable driver of hallux valgus:** 2023–2026 WBCT literature confirms hallux valgus is part of a global first-ray malalignment involving first metatarsal pronation, medial column instability, and progressive sesamoid subluxation, with pronation recognized as a prognostic factor for recurrence; WBCT gives more accurate/reproducible pronation and sesamoid-alignment measurement than weight-bearing radiographs, though clinical impact of correcting pronation specifically remains uncertain and unstandardized — [Skeletal Radiology, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00256-026-05300-5); [Frontiers in Surgery 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/surgery/articles/10.3389/fsurg.2025.1682172/full); [Foot & Ankle International 2025, First Metatarsal Pronation Correction After 4th-Generation Percutaneous Osteotomy](https://doi.org/10.1177/10711007251344273).
- **Bunionette MIS techniques — 2024 systematic review moves this from emerging toward established:** a 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of 18 studies (580 patients, 714 feet) through December 2023 found percutaneous/MIS fifth-metatarsal osteotomy techniques for bunionette to be clinically safe with meaningful, statistically significant improvements in pain and AOFAS function scores — [Lewis et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/24730114241263095). A Johns Hopkins retrospective series (111 patients/132 feet) of percutaneous osteotomy **without internal fixation or postoperative immobilization** reported a 1.5% complication rate — [SAGE Journals 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/10711007231205289).
- **Plantar plate repair — largest long-term series to date (2013–2023 cohort):** a retrospective review of 196 primary second plantar plate repairs (mean follow-up 48 months) supports the direct plantar-approach repair as durable, though the authors note the plantar approach remains underutilized due to concerns about scar-related pain — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1067251626000098). A separate 2024 study of 31 patients undergoing plantar-approach repair after shortening osteotomy for subluxated/dislocated 2nd–3rd MTP joints confirmed significant radiologic improvement (p<0.001) — [ECIOS/PMC 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11604560/).

**Please independently verify each of the above before relying on it for a specific clinical or medico-legal decision** — this section reflects targeted searches run in August 2026 and is not a substitute for a full systematic review.
