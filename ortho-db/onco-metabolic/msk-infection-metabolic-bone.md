## Quick Reference

### Red flags — do not miss

- **Necrotizing fasciitis**: pain out of proportion to exam, pain crossing fascial planes beyond visible erythema, skin anesthesia, hemorrhagic bullae, crepitus, rapid progression over hours, systemic toxicity disproportionate to skin findings. Treat the diagnosis as a **surgical emergency confirmed only in the OR** — do not wait for imaging or a LRINEC cut-off.
- **Septic arthritis of a native joint**: any acutely hot, swollen, painful joint with restricted range is septic until aspirated and proven otherwise, especially in a prosthetic-adjacent native joint, an immunosuppressed host, or IV drug use.
- **Atypical femoral fracture**: prodromal thigh/groin ache in a patient on ≥3–5 years of bisphosphonate or ≥1 year of denosumab — image the *contralateral* full femur; ~40% are bilateral or have a contralateral incomplete fracture at presentation.
- **CRPS**: disproportionate pain plus autonomic/trophic change persisting beyond expected fracture-healing timeline — early recognition inside the first 8–12 weeks materially changes trajectory.
- **Paget disease**: new, localized, worsening pain in a previously stable pagetic segment — think **sarcomatous transformation** (<1% but aggressive) until excluded.
- **Osteomalacia**: bilateral, symmetric pseudofractures (Looser zones) at femoral neck, pubic rami, or scapula in a patient with vague, migratory bone pain and proximal myopathy — do not fix these surgically until the mineralization defect is corrected.

### Compact treatment ladder

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical / invasive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adult osteomyelitis | Pathogen-directed antibiotics after deep culture; optimize host (glycemic control, smoking cessation, nutrition) | Prolonged suppressive antibiotics if host/anatomy prohibits cure (Cierny-Mader C host) | Staged debridement to bleeding bone, dead-space management (local antibiotic carrier ± free flap), stabilization if type IV |
| Fracture-related infection (FRI) | Confirm via FRI consensus criteria → targeted systemic antibiotics after deep sampling | Biofilm-active agents (rifampin combo for staphylococci) once implant retained and stable | DAIR (debridement, antibiotics, implant retention) if early/stable; staged exchange if late/loose |
| Native septic arthritis | Emergent joint decompression + empiric then culture-directed IV antibiotics | Serial aspiration only for small/superficial joints with rapid response | Arthroscopic or open washout — mandatory for hip/shoulder, failed aspiration, prosthetic-adjacent, or delayed presentation |
| Necrotizing fasciitis | Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (incl. clindamycin for toxin suppression) as a bridge, never as definitive therapy | IVIG in streptococcal toxic-shock-associated cases (evidence mixed) | Emergent radical debridement within hours of suspicion; planned re-look every 24–48h until clean |
| TB & atypical MSK infection | RIPE regimen (rifampin/isoniazid/pyrazinamide/ethambutol) for confirmed TB; targeted therapy for NTM/Brucella/fungal | Extend continuation phase (to 9–12 months) for spinal/extensive disease | Decompression/debridement for neurologic deficit, instability, large abscess, or medical failure |
| Osteoporosis / fragility fracture | Calcium + vitamin D repletion, weight-bearing exercise, fall-risk reduction, oral bisphosphonate if moderate risk | Anabolic-first (teriparatide/romosozumab) if very-high risk, then mandatory antiresorptive consolidation | Fixation augmentation (cement, locking constructs) for fragility fractures; FLS enrollment for all ≥50y fragility fractures |
| Atypical femoral fracture | Stop antiresorptive; vitamin D/calcium correction; consider teriparatide for incomplete AFF | Protected weight-bearing trial for incomplete, non-displaced lesions | Prophylactic IM nailing for symptomatic incomplete AFF or lateral cortex radiolucent line; nail both femurs if bilateral risk |
| Paget disease | Zoledronic acid 5 mg single infusion (treatment of choice) if symptomatic/active/pre-surgical | Repeat course if biochemical relapse; calcitonin only if bisphosphonate-intolerant | Osteotomy for deformity, arthroplasty for secondary arthritis, fixation for pathologic fracture |
| Osteomalacia / vit D deficiency | Vitamin D loading (e.g., 50,000 IU/wk × 6–8 wks) + calcium; treat underlying cause | Phosphate + calcitriol / burosumab for renal phosphate-wasting disorders | Avoid elective fixation/fusion until biochemically corrected; fix only true fractures with delayed WB |
| Hyperparathyroid bone disease | Correct vitamin D, hydration, address secondary causes (CKD) | Cinacalcet if surgery not feasible or parathyroid carcinoma | Parathyroidectomy — definitive for primary HPT meeting international workshop criteria |
| CRPS | Early active mobilization, graded motor imagery/mirror therapy, vitamin C prophylaxis post-fracture/surgery | Sympathetic block (diagnostic/therapeutic), multidisciplinary pain program, ketamine infusion | Spinal cord stimulation for refractory CRPS after ≥6 months failed conservative care |

### Key numbers

- **LRINEC**: score ≥8 high risk (historic PPV ~93%), ≥6 intermediate — but sensitivity only ~70–80%; a low score **does not rule out** necrotizing fasciitis.
- **NF debridement timing**: door-to-OR <12h associated with markedly lower mortality; overall mortality ~20–30%, rising toward ~70% with delay >24h.
- **Septic arthritis synovial WBC**: >50,000/µL with >90% PMNs strongly suggestive (native joint); lower thresholds (>25,000–30,000) apply in immunosuppressed/early presentations.
- **FRI**: ≥1 confirmatory criterion is diagnostic; obtain ≥3–5 deep tissue samples, hold cultures 10–14 days for low-virulence organisms (*Cutibacterium acnes*).
- **DEXA T-score**: normal ≥ −1.0; osteopenia −1.0 to −2.5; osteoporosis ≤ −2.5; severe osteoporosis = ≤ −2.5 + fragility fracture.
- **FRAX intervention thresholds** (commonly used): treat if 10-yr hip fracture probability ≥3% or major osteoporotic fracture probability ≥20% (NOF-style absolute thresholds), or per NOGG age-specific curves.
- **ASBMR AFF**: ≥4 of 5 major features required, location femoral diaphysis (subtrochanteric to supracondylar), atraumatic or low-energy.
- **Anabolic duration caps**: teriparatide/abaloparatide lifetime max 24 months; romosozumab max 12 months (contraindicated if MI/stroke within past year).
- **Denosumab discontinuation**: mandatory bisphosphonate bridge (typically zoledronic acid) 6–9 months after last dose to prevent rebound vertebral fractures — never simply stop.
- **Bisphosphonate drug holiday**: consider after 3–5 years (oral) or after the 3-year HORIZON extension window (IV zoledronic acid) in low-moderate risk patients; continue to 6–10 years in high-risk (prior vertebral fracture, T-score still ≤−2.5).
- **Budapest clinical criteria**: ≥1 reported symptom in ≥3 of 4 categories + ≥1 observed sign in ≥2 of 4 categories, sensitivity ~0.85/specificity ~0.69.
- **Vitamin C for CRPS prophylaxis**: 500 mg/day for 50 days after distal radius fracture/foot-ankle surgery — low-cost, low-risk intervention with supportive (though heterogeneous) trial evidence.

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Bone infection — general principles

Bone is normally resistant to infection due to its vascularity and immune surveillance; infection establishes via **hematogenous seeding** (metaphyseal sinusoidal slow-flow vessels in adults less prominent than in children, but still a route via vertebral Batson's plexus), **contiguous spread** (from adjacent soft tissue infection, ulcer, or joint), or **direct inoculation** (open fracture, surgery, implant). Once established, bacteria — classically *Staphylococcus aureus* — form **biofilm**: a glycocalyx matrix embedding sessile bacterial communities on necrotic bone (sequestrum) or implant surfaces, dramatically raising the minimum biofilm eradication concentration relative to planktonic MIC and rendering antibiotics alone curative only in the earliest, non-biofilm stage. Devitalized, infected bone becomes a **sequestrum**; the host lays down reactive new bone around it (**involucrum**), which may itself contain draining sinus tracts (**cloacae**). Chronicity is defined by the presence of sequestrum, sinus tract, or a history of infection persisting/recurring beyond several weeks despite treatment.

### Fracture-related infection

FRI shares osteomyelitis biology but is defined by the presence of an **orthopedic implant**, which independently promotes biofilm formation (foreign-body effect lowers the inoculum needed for infection by several orders of magnitude) and complicates eradication because implant removal versus retention becomes a central strategic decision tied to fracture stability, time since index surgery, and biofilm maturity (biofilm matures over the initial 2–4 weeks post-implantation — the biological basis for DAIR's time-window logic).

### Native septic arthritis

Synovium lacks a limiting basement membrane, allowing rapid bacterial and leukocyte influx into the joint. Bacterial and host proteolytic enzymes (matrix metalloproteinases, cytokine-driven chondrocyte catabolism) degrade articular cartilage within **as little as 8 hours** of untreated infection — the physiologic basis for treating septic arthritis as a surgical emergency. Intra-articular pressure rise can also compromise the epiphyseal/femoral head blood supply, particularly in the hip.

### Necrotizing fasciitis

Infection spreads along the **superficial fascial planes** (Scarpa's/Camper's fascia in the trunk, deep fascia investing the limb), with relatively sparse local blood supply to fascia enabling rapid, silent proximal-distal spread often well beyond the visible skin changes — explaining why skin findings dramatically underestimate the extent of necrosis. Thrombosis of perforating vessels causes overlying skin ischemia (hence late bullae/anesthesia), and bacterial exotoxins (streptococcal superantigens in Type II disease) drive toxic shock physiology.

### Tuberculous & atypical MSK infection

Skeletal TB is virtually always secondary to hematogenous dissemination from a (often clinically silent) primary pulmonary focus, seeding the vertebral body (anterior, subchondral, richly vascular region) most commonly — **Pott's disease** — producing progressive anterior vertebral body destruction, disc space narrowing (a key differentiator from metastatic disease, which usually spares the disc), paravertebral "cold" abscess formation, and kyphotic collapse (gibbus). Peripheral joint TB is typically monoarticular (hip, knee), indolent, granulomatous with caseating necrosis and pannus formation eroding cartilage from the periphery inward. Atypical infections in the European context include nontuberculous mycobacteria (*M. marinum* — fish tank/aquatic inoculation hand infection; *M. avium* complex in prosthetic joint infection), and **Brucella** MSK involvement (sacroiliitis, spondylitis) which remains endemic in parts of the Balkans and Mediterranean basin — relevant to a Romanian practice.

### Osteoporosis & bone remodeling coupling

Bone remodeling couples osteoclastic resorption to osteoblastic formation via the **RANK/RANKL/OPG axis** (RANKL drives osteoclastogenesis; OPG is its decoy receptor) and the **Wnt/β-catenin–sclerostin axis** (sclerostin, secreted by osteocytes, inhibits osteoblastic bone formation; anti-sclerostin therapy is the mechanistic basis of romosozumab). Estrogen deficiency (menopause) uncouples remodeling toward resorption by increasing RANKL and pro-osteoclastogenic cytokines; age-related osteoporosis adds impaired osteoblast recruitment/function. The trabecular-rich vertebral body and femoral neck are disproportionately affected.

### Atypical femoral fracture

AFF is a **stress/insufficiency fracture** occurring on the tension (lateral) side of a femur whose remodeling has been chronically suppressed by long-term antiresorptive therapy, impairing microdamage repair and allowing accumulation of unrepaired microcracks that propagate transversely from the lateral cortex — biomechanically distinct from the typical spiral/oblique osteoporotic femoral neck or intertrochanteric fracture pattern.

### Paget disease

Disordered, focal, markedly accelerated bone turnover driven by abnormally large, hyperactive osteoclasts (possible paramyxoviral trigger, historically debated) in a genetically susceptible host (**SQSTM1/p62** mutations affecting NF-κB signaling account for a substantial minority of cases, more so in familial disease). Disease evolves through **lytic** (osteoclast-dominant), **mixed**, and **sclerotic** (osteoblast-dominant, disorganized woven bone) phases, producing enlarged, structurally weak, hypervascular bone prone to deformity, degenerative arthritis of adjacent joints, and pathologic fracture.

### Osteomalacia & hyperparathyroid bone disease

Osteomalacia is a defect of **osteoid mineralization** (as opposed to osteoporosis, a deficit of bone quantity with normally mineralized matrix), most commonly from vitamin D deficiency/malabsorption, renal phosphate wasting (X-linked hypophosphatemia, tumor-induced osteomalacia via FGF23 overproduction), or renal tubular acidosis. Primary hyperparathyroidism produces catabolic, PTH-driven bone loss preferentially at **cortical** sites (subperiosteal resorption, distal radius) with relative trabecular sparing — mechanistically opposite to postmenopausal osteoporosis, which is more trabecular-dominant — culminating in severe, now rare, **osteitis fibrosa cystica** (brown tumors, bone cysts).

### CRPS

CRPS pathophysiology integrates **neurogenic inflammation** (exaggerated local release of substance P and CGRP producing vasodilation, edema, and sensitization), **sympathetic-afferent coupling**, **central sensitization** (cortical reorganization of somatosensory maps demonstrable on functional imaging), and an **autoimmune component** (autoantibodies against autonomic receptors described in subsets). Type I occurs without a definable nerve lesion; Type II (causalgia) follows a defined peripheral nerve injury — the clinical (Budapest) criteria are otherwise identical.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

### Adult osteomyelitis / FRI

History of prior surgery/implant, wound history, sinus tract, and the **probe-to-bone test** (positive test raises post-test probability meaningfully in a diabetic foot ulcer, but sensitivity/specificity are only moderate — it is a rule-in, not a rule-out, tool). Inflammatory markers: CRP is more useful than ESR for trend-following through treatment (shorter half-life, faster normalization with successful source control); procalcitonin adds little discriminatory value in bone infection specifically. **MRI** is the imaging modality of choice for marrow edema, sequestrum, abscess extent, and soft-tissue involvement (limited by metalwork artifact); **CT** better delineates sequestrum and cortical destruction and the implant-bone interface when metalwork is present. Nuclear medicine (labeled leukocyte scan, FDG-PET/CT) is reserved for equivocal cases, metalwork-heavy anatomy, or when MRI is contraindicated — FDG-PET/CT has particularly useful application in suspected vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis and in differentiating infection from Charcot arthropathy in the diabetic foot.

### Native septic arthritis

Arthrocentesis is mandatory before starting antibiotics whenever feasible — delaying aspiration to "see the effect of antibiotics first" is a common and consequential error, since it both worsens diagnostic yield and delays source control. Send synovial fluid for cell count with differential, Gram stain (~50–75% sensitive), culture (~70–90% sensitive, higher with adequate volume and rapid transport), and crystal analysis (crystal arthropathy is the principal mimic and can coexist with infection — a positive crystal result does not exclude septic arthritis). Serum CRP/ESR/WBC are adjuncts, not substitutes for joint fluid analysis. Ultrasound guides aspiration of deep joints (hip, shoulder) and confirms effusion when clinical exam is equivocal.

### Necrotizing fasciitis

Primarily a **clinical** diagnosis. Cross-sectional imaging (CT showing fascial gas or fluid tracking, non-enhancing fascia, or MRI with fascial hyperintensity) may support the diagnosis but must never delay operative exploration in a patient with a compatible clinical picture. The definitive test is surgical: lack of resistance to blunt finger dissection along the fascial plane (the "finger test"), dishwater-grey exudate, thrombosed subcutaneous vessels, and lack of bleeding from the tissue at exploration.

### TB & atypical MSK infection

Interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA, preferred over tuberculin skin testing in BCG-vaccinated populations, relevant in Romania) or PPD; CT/MRI for spinal disease (paravertebral or psoas abscess, disc/endplate involvement with relative **disc preservation early** — a key differentiator from pyogenic spondylodiscitis, where disc destruction is earlier and more severe, and from metastatic disease, which typically spares the disc entirely); image-guided or open biopsy for histopathology (caseating granuloma) and microbiology; **GeneXpert MTB/RIF** for rapid molecular diagnosis and rifampin-resistance screening (results in hours versus weeks); mycobacterial culture remains the diagnostic gold standard but is slow (up to 6 weeks on solid media, faster on liquid culture systems). For suspected Brucella, serology (agglutination titers) plus blood/tissue culture; for NTM, prolonged culture at multiple temperatures and species identification are needed to select the correct regimen.

### Osteoporosis & fragility fracture pathway

DEXA at femoral neck, total hip, and lumbar spine (the lowest T-score among sites governs classification), vertebral fracture assessment (VFA) on the same DEXA visit to detect radiographically silent vertebral compression fractures (a substantial proportion of vertebral fractures are clinically unrecognized until specifically looked for), FRAX calculation incorporating clinical risk factors, and secondary-cause labs (calcium, phosphate, PTH, 25-OH vitamin D, TSH, serum protein electrophoresis/free light chains if the presentation is atypical for age or severity, testosterone in men) — a fragility fracture in a man or a premenopausal woman should prompt a more thorough secondary-cause work-up than a typical postmenopausal presentation.

### Atypical femoral fracture

Full-length femur radiographs of **both** femurs (lateral cortex focus) at presentation — bilaterality or a contralateral incomplete lesion is common enough that unilateral imaging alone is inadequate. MRI detects early marrow and periosteal edema at the lateral cortex before a radiographically visible lucent line develops, and is the investigation of choice for a patient with prodromal thigh pain on long-term antiresorptive therapy and normal plain films.

### Paget disease

Serum alkaline phosphatase (bone-specific ALP if concurrent liver disease confounds total ALP) is the primary activity marker and the standard parameter for monitoring treatment response. Plain radiographs show cortical thickening, coarsened trabeculae, mixed lytic/sclerotic change, "cotton wool" skull, "picture-frame" vertebral body, and diaphyseal bowing (classically anterior/lateral bowing of the tibia — "sabre shin"). Technetium-99m bone scan at diagnosis maps the full polyostotic extent of disease, since clinical and plain-film assessment alone underestimates the number of involved sites.

### Osteomalacia & vitamin D deficiency

25-OH vitamin D, PTH (secondary elevation), calcium (typically low-normal), phosphate (low), and ALP (elevated in most causes — paradoxically **low or normal** in hypophosphatasia, an important mimicker that changes management, since bisphosphonates are contraindicated in that condition). Radiographs show **Looser zones/pseudofractures** (bilateral, symmetric, at the femoral neck, pubic rami, or scapula), which are pathognomonic when present and should prompt a metabolic work-up before any consideration of prophylactic fixation.

### Hyperparathyroid bone disease

Serum calcium, intact PTH, phosphate (low), and 24-hour urine calcium (to exclude familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia, a benign mimicker of primary HPT that does not warrant parathyroidectomy). DEXA should specifically include the **distal 1/3 radius**, the most cortical-bone-sensitive site and therefore the most sensitive for detecting PTH-driven bone loss, which can be under-recognized if only hip/spine (more trabecular) sites are scanned. Imaging for subperiosteal resorption (radial aspect of the middle phalanges), "salt and pepper" skull, tapering distal clavicles, and brown tumors is reserved for more advanced/longstanding disease, now uncommon in regions with routine biochemical screening.

### CRPS

Primarily a clinical diagnosis applying the Budapest criteria (below). Triple-phase bone scan (delayed-phase periarticular uptake) is supportive in equivocal early presentations. Plain radiographs may show patchy periarticular osteopenia (Sudeck's atrophy) later in the disease course but are neither sensitive nor specific enough to be diagnostic, and normal imaging does not exclude CRPS.

## Classification

### Cierny-Mader classification (chronic osteomyelitis)

| Anatomic type | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I — Medullary | Infection confined to the medullary canal/endosteal surface (e.g., hematogenous, infected IM nail) | Intramedullary reaming/unreaming often sufficient; implant removal if present |
| II — Superficial | Infection limited to exposed bone surface, contiguous soft-tissue source, cortex only | Surgical debridement of exposed cortex + soft-tissue coverage; no deep excision needed |
| III — Localized | Well-marginated, full-thickness cortical sequestrum; segment remains mechanically stable after debridement | Radical debridement to bleeding bone possible without destabilizing the bone; dead-space management |
| IV — Diffuse | Circumferential/permeative involvement; bone unstable before or rendered unstable by debridement (e.g., infected nonunion, infected segmental defect) | Requires debridement **and** skeletal stabilization/reconstruction (external fixation, staged bone transport, induced-membrane/Masquelet, vascularized bone graft) |

| Physiologic host | Definition | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| A host | Normal immune/metabolic/vascular status | Standard curative strategy pursued |
| B host (Bs = systemic; Bl = local) | Compromised: diabetes, malnutrition, renal/hepatic failure, immunosuppression, smoking, PAD (Bs); lymphedema, venous stasis, radiation, scarring, major-vessel injury (Bl) | Optimize modifiable factors pre-operatively; higher failure/complication rate anticipated |
| C host | Treatment morbidity would exceed that of the disease itself | Suppressive antibiotics, amputation, or palliation may be more appropriate than attempted cure |

Clinical stage = anatomic type + host class (e.g., "type IIIBs") — this composite is what should appear in the operative note and drives the debridement-versus-suppression decision.

### FRI consensus definition (confirmatory vs. suggestive criteria)

| Confirmatory criteria (any one is diagnostic) | Suggestive criteria (raise suspicion, prompt further workup) |
|---|---|
| Fistula, sinus tract, or wound breakdown communicating with the bone/implant | New-onset joint effusion, localized erythema, warmth, swelling, or increasing pain |
| Purulent drainage from the wound or purulence around the implant intra-operatively | Persistent, increasing, or new wound drainage |
| Microorganisms isolated by culture from ≥2 separate deep tissue/implant samples (phenotypically indistinguishable), OR a single deep sample growing a highly virulent pathogen | Radiological signs (periosteal reaction, implant loosening not explained by mechanical failure, involucrum/sequestrum) |
| Histopathological confirmation of microorganisms in deep tissue with special stains | Unexplained persistent fever, systemic inflammatory markers elevated without another source |

**Workflow implication**: obtain ≥3–5 deep tissue samples with dedicated instruments per site (never a superficial swab), send removed implants for **sonication** (dramatically increases culture yield over tissue culture alone), and hold cultures 10–14 days when low-virulence organisms (*Cutibacterium acnes*, coagulase-negative staphylococci) are suspected — a single positive low-virulence culture is suggestive, not confirmatory, unless it recurs in a second sample.

### LRINEC score (limits explicit)

| Variable | Value | Points |
|---|---|---|
| CRP (mg/L) | ≥150 | 4 |
| WBC (×10⁹/L) | <15 / 15–25 / >25 | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Hemoglobin (g/dL) | >13.5 / 11–13.5 / <11 | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| Sodium (mmol/L) | <135 | 2 |
| Creatinine (µmol/L) | >141 | 2 |
| Glucose (mmol/L) | >10 | 1 |

Total score 0–13. **≥8** = high risk (originally reported PPV ~93%); **6–7** = intermediate risk; **<6** = low risk. **Limits (critical)**: sensitivity in external validation cohorts is only ~70–80%; up to 10–20% of surgically confirmed necrotizing fasciitis present with LRINEC <6. It was derived retrospectively and was never intended as a stand-alone rule-out screen. **A low LRINEC must never override clinical suspicion or delay surgical exploration** — the score supports triage, not exclusion.

### Gustilo-Anderson classification / Gaenslen approach — infection-risk context

Gustilo-Anderson grades **open fractures**, and is included here because grade drives FRI risk stratification and prophylactic antibiotic strategy, not because it is itself an infection classification.

| Grade | Definition | Approximate infection risk |
|---|---|---|
| I | Wound <1 cm, clean, minimal soft-tissue damage, simple fracture pattern | ~0–2% |
| II | Wound 1–10 cm, moderate soft-tissue damage, no extensive flaps | ~2–7% |
| IIIA | Extensive soft-tissue laceration/flap but adequate bone coverage; high-energy mechanism or segmental fracture regardless of wound size | ~7% |
| IIIB | Extensive soft-tissue loss with periosteal stripping and bone exposure requiring flap coverage; heavy contamination | ~10–25% |
| IIIC | Any open fracture with an arterial injury requiring repair | ~25–50% |

**Gaenslen's (split-heel) approach**: a longitudinal, posterior heel-splitting incision originally described for drainage of calcaneal osteomyelitis, still referenced as an extensile posterior exposure when deep calcaneal infection or an infected calcaneal nonunion needs direct posterior access — noted here as the surgical-approach counterpart to Gustilo-Anderson's injury-severity context, since both bear directly on infection risk and wound-management strategy in the foot/ankle and long bones.

### FRAX & DEXA T-score

| DEXA category | T-score | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | ≥ −1.0 | Reassess risk factors; lifestyle counseling |
| Osteopenia (low bone mass) | −1.0 to −2.5 | FRAX calculation to decide on treatment; repeat DEXA interval per baseline risk |
| Osteoporosis | ≤ −2.5 | Pharmacologic treatment indicated regardless of FRAX |
| Severe/established osteoporosis | ≤ −2.5 + fragility fracture | High-risk category — consider anabolic-first strategy |

**FRAX** estimates 10-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture (MOF: hip, clinical spine, wrist, proximal humerus) and hip fracture specifically from age, sex, BMI, prior fragility fracture, parental hip fracture, current smoking, long-term glucocorticoid use, rheumatoid arthritis, secondary osteoporosis causes, alcohol ≥3 units/day, and femoral neck BMD. **Intervention thresholds** vary by health system: US-style absolute thresholds treat at hip probability ≥3% or MOF probability ≥20%; UK NOGG/NICE use age-specific threshold curves rather than fixed cut-offs. A prior hip or vertebral fragility fracture is itself an automatic treatment indication independent of FRAX or T-score.

### ASBMR atypical femoral fracture (AFF) criteria

Location: femoral diaphysis, from just distal to the lesser trochanter to just proximal to the supracondylar flare. Fracture must be atraumatic or associated with minimal trauma (fall from standing height or less) and non-pathologic (tumor, Paget disease, and other focal bone lesions must be excluded).

| Major features (≥4 of 5 required for diagnosis) | Minor features (supportive, not required) |
|---|---|
| Fracture line originates at the lateral cortex and is essentially transverse in orientation | Generalized increase in cortical thickness of the femoral diaphyses |
| Minimal or no comminution | Unilateral or bilateral prodromal dull, aching thigh/groin pain |
| Complete fractures extend through both cortices (may have a medial spike); incomplete fractures involve only the lateral cortex | Bilateral incomplete or complete femoral diaphysis fractures |
| Fracture is non-comminuted or minimally comminuted | Delayed fracture healing |
| Localized periosteal or endosteal thickening at the fracture site ("beaking"/"flaring") | — |

**Management implication**: meeting criteria mandates stopping antiresorptive therapy, imaging the contralateral femur (bilaterality/contralateral incomplete fracture common), and considering prophylactic fixation of a symptomatic incomplete lesion before it completes.

### Budapest criteria (CRPS)

| Category | Symptom (patient-reported, need ≥1 in ≥3 of 4 categories) | Sign (examiner-observed, need ≥1 in ≥2 of 4 categories) |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory | Hyperesthesia and/or allodynia | Hyperalgesia (pinprick) and/or allodynia (light touch, temperature, deep pressure, joint movement) |
| Vasomotor | Temperature asymmetry, skin color change/asymmetry | Temperature asymmetry (>1°C), skin color change/asymmetry |
| Sudomotor/edema | Edema, sweating change/asymmetry | Edema, sweating change/asymmetry |
| Motor/trophic | Decreased range of motion, motor dysfunction (weakness, tremor, dystonia), trophic changes (hair, nail, skin) | Decreased range of motion, motor dysfunction, trophic changes |

Diagnosis additionally requires **continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event** and **no other diagnosis that better explains** the presentation. Clinical diagnostic criteria: sensitivity ~0.85, specificity ~0.69. Stricter **research criteria** (requiring a sign in all four categories) trade sensitivity for higher specificity (~0.68 with fewer false-positive inclusions) and are used for trial enrollment, not routine clinical diagnosis. CRPS Type I = no definable nerve lesion; Type II (causalgia) = definable peripheral nerve injury — the Budapest criteria apply identically to both.

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Osteomyelitis/FRI**: pathogen-directed systemic antibiotics only *after* adequate deep-tissue sampling (empiric therapy before sampling is discouraged outside sepsis). Classic duration was 4–6 weeks of IV therapy; large randomized non-inferiority evidence for bone/joint infection (oral versus IV therapy once the patient is clinically stabilized and the organism/susceptibility is known) supports early oral step-down in appropriately selected patients, reducing line-related complications and hospital stay without compromising cure rates — the duration should be individualized to clinical/biochemical response and surgical source control rather than fixed to a single universal number. Treat modifiable host factors in parallel (glycemic control, HbA1c target <8%; smoking cessation; nutritional optimization via albumin/prealbumin trending), since Cierny-Mader B-host status is often at least partially reversible and materially affects cure probability.
- **Native septic arthritis**: never treat with antibiotics alone if the joint can be decompressed — non-operative aspiration-only management is reserved for small/superficial joints (wrist, ankle) with rapid clinical response to a single or limited series of aspirations.
- **Necrotizing fasciitis**: antibiotics are always adjunctive to surgery, never a substitute; empiric broad-spectrum coverage (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam or carbapenem + clindamycin ± vancomycin/linezolid for MRSA coverage) with clindamycin included specifically for its **toxin-suppressive** effect via protein synthesis inhibition in streptococcal/staphylococcal toxin-mediated disease.
- **TB/atypical infection**: standard drug-susceptible skeletal TB — 2-month intensive phase (rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) followed by a continuation phase; many bone/joint TB experts extend continuation to **9–12 months total** (versus the 6-month pulmonary-disease standard) for spinal disease or extensive bone involvement, given the difficulty of confirming sterilization in avascular, abscess-laden tissue. Brucellar spondylitis: combination doxycycline + rifampin (± aminoglycoside induction) for a minimum of 6–8 weeks, often longer for spinal involvement.
- **Osteoporosis pathway**: calcium (dietary preferred, supplement to reach ~1000–1200 mg/day total intake) and vitamin D (target 25-OH vitamin D generally ≥20–30 ng/mL) repletion, progressive weight-bearing/resistance exercise, fall-risk assessment and home-hazard modification, minimization of sedative/hypotensive medications.
- **AFF**: discontinue the causative antiresorptive; correct vitamin D/calcium; protected weight-bearing trial (often with a period of non- or partial weight-bearing) is reasonable for an incomplete, non-displaced lateral cortex lesion without a full radiolucent line, with close radiographic follow-up — but the threshold for prophylactic fixation is low given the high rate of progression to complete fracture.
- **Paget disease**: indications to treat include bone pain attributable to active Paget, hypercalcemia, high-output cardiac failure, neurologic compression from an expanding pagetic segment, and **pre-operative** normalization before elective surgery on pagetic bone (reduces intra-operative bleeding and improves bone quality for fixation).
- **Osteomalacia**: correct the underlying cause — vitamin D/calcium repletion for nutritional/malabsorptive disease; phosphate + active vitamin D analog (calcitriol) or **burosumab** (anti-FGF23 monoclonal antibody) for X-linked hypophosphatemia and tumor-induced osteomalacia (with tumor localization/resection where feasible).
- **Hyperparathyroid bone disease**: hydration and correction of vitamin D deficiency (with monitoring, as repletion can occasionally unmask/worsen hypercalcemia in primary HPT); cinacalcet for patients unfit for or declining parathyroidectomy.
- **CRPS**: early active mobilization and desensitization-focused physiotherapy, graded motor imagery and mirror therapy, and vitamin C prophylaxis after high-risk fracture/surgery.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Local antibiotic carriers** (osteomyelitis/FRI dead-space management): non-absorbable PMMA beads/spacers deliver very high local antibiotic concentrations but require a second procedure for removal; **absorbable calcium sulfate (± hydroxyapatite composite)** carriers avoid a second surgery, allow bone-void filling, and have become the more commonly favored contemporary option, though evidence of superiority over PMMA on hard infection-eradication endpoints remains limited to comparative cohort data rather than definitive RCTs — the selection is often pragmatic (dead-space geometry, need for staged reconstruction, cost).
- **Septic arthritis**: serial closed-needle aspiration is an adjunct/alternative only for select superficial joints with rapid resolution; it is not a substitute for surgical washout when criteria for operative management are met (see Operative).
- **Osteoporosis**: subcutaneous denosumab (RANKL inhibitor, 6-monthly), subcutaneous teriparatide/abaloparatide (daily anabolic), subcutaneous romosozumab (monthly anabolic, sclerostin inhibitor) — all delivered by injection and central to the modern treatment ladder (see Operative/systemic-therapy discussion below for sequencing).
- **Paget disease**: single IV zoledronic acid 5 mg infusion is now the treatment of choice — superior and more durable biochemical (ALP) normalization compared with oral bisphosphonates in head-to-head trials; subcutaneous calcitonin retained only as a rarely used alternative in bisphosphonate-intolerant patients.
- **Hyperparathyroid bone disease**: cinacalcet (oral calcimimetic) lowers PTH/calcium medically when surgery is deferred.
- **CRPS**: stellate ganglion block (upper limb) or lumbar sympathetic block (lower limb) — diagnostic and potentially therapeutic in sympathetically-maintained pain phenotypes; intravenous ketamine infusion for refractory central sensitization; intra-articular/peri-neural corticosteroid has a limited, adjunct role only.

### Operative

- **Chronic osteomyelitis (Cierny-Mader-guided)**: radical debridement to punctate ("paprika sign") bleeding bone is the single most important determinant of cure — inadequate debridement is the leading cause of recurrence. Type III lesions tolerate debridement without destabilization; **type IV lesions require concurrent stabilization** — options include external fixation (circular frame for staged bone transport across segmental defects), the induced-membrane (Masquelet) two-stage technique, vascularized fibular or iliac crest transfer for large defects, or acute shortening with subsequent lengthening. Dead space is managed with a local antibiotic carrier, muscle flap, or both; soft-tissue coverage (local or free flap) is planned concurrently with bony reconstruction, not as an afterthought.
- **Fracture-related infection — DAIR vs. staged exchange**: **debridement, antibiotics, and implant retention (DAIR)** is appropriate for early infection (generally within the first weeks post-fixation, before biofilm maturation, i.e., "early" per the FRI consensus timeline), a stable implant, and healthy soft tissues — success depends on aggressive debridement, exchange of modular components, and biofilm-active antibiotic combinations (e.g., rifampin combination therapy for staphylococcal infection once source control is achieved). Late infection, a loose implant, or an unhealed fracture generally mandates **staged removal/exchange**: implant removal, radical debridement, temporary antibiotic-impregnated spacer or external fixation for stability, culture-directed systemic therapy, then definitive re-fixation once infection is controlled and (ideally) the fracture has healed enough to tolerate implant removal, or reconstruction proceeds concurrently if bone stability cannot otherwise be maintained.
- **Native septic arthritis — washout strategy**: emergent joint decompression is indicated for any confirmed or strongly suspected native septic arthritis. **Arthroscopic washout** is favored for the knee and increasingly the shoulder and hip in centers with arthroscopic expertise — comparative series show similar reoperation and functional outcomes to open arthrotomy with less morbidity and shorter hospital stay, though loculated/thick purulent collections may still require open debridement for adequate access. **Open arthrotomy remains preferred/necessary** for the hip in many practices (deep joint, difficult arthroscopic access to the entire capsule, risk of femoral head vascular compromise from a tense effusion demanding truly rapid decompression) and for deep, multi-compartment, or heavily loculated disease anywhere. Repeat washout is required in roughly 10–30% of cases depending on organism virulence (higher for *S. aureus*) — a low threshold for re-look at 48–72h if clinical/biochemical improvement stalls is appropriate. Absolute indications for surgical (rather than aspiration-only) management: hip or shoulder involvement, failure to improve after 1–2 aspirations, loculated fluid on imaging, prosthetic-adjacent native joint, immunocompromised host, or presentation delayed beyond 3–5 days.
- **Necrotizing fasciitis — time-critical operative pathway**: once suspected, the pathway is **emergent operative exploration**, not further diagnostic testing — imaging or scoring systems must not delay the trip to the OR. Radical debridement of all non-viable fascia and overlying skin/subcutaneous tissue to a margin of visibly healthy, bleeding, adherent fascia; amputation may be the only source-control option for a rapidly progressive limb-threatening extremity infection. A **mandatory planned re-look at 24–48h** (often more than once) is standard, continuing until the wound bed is clean and progression has stopped; delayed reconstruction (skin grafting, flap coverage) follows only after source control is achieved. Time-to-debridement is the dominant modifiable predictor of mortality.
- **TB & atypical MSK infection**: surgery is reserved for neurologic deficit from spinal cord/cauda equina compression, spinal instability or significant/progressive kyphotic deformity, large abscess not responding to needle drainage, and failure of medical therapy; anterior debridement with strut grafting and posterior instrumentation is the classic approach for Pott's disease with significant destruction, though many cases of skeletal TB without instability or neurologic compromise are managed medically alone.
- **Osteoporosis/fragility fracture fixation**: augmentation strategies for osteoporotic bone include cement (PMMA) augmentation of screws in periarticular fractures, locking-plate constructs to improve fixation in poor-quality metaphyseal/diaphyseal bone, and increased attention to construct length/fixation-point number rather than relying on a single fixation-point failure-prone strategy; arthroplasty is favored over fixation for many displaced femoral neck fractures in elderly, low-demand patients given nonunion/AVN risk of fixation in this population.
- **AFF**: **prophylactic intramedullary nailing** is indicated for a symptomatic incomplete AFF or any complete radiolucent lateral cortex line, given high progression risk to complete, often displaced fracture; both femurs should be assessed and nailed if bilateral disease is present, since contralateral progression during unilateral treatment is common. Complete AFF is treated with IM nailing (full-length, given the diaphyseal/subtrochanteric-to-supracondylar span), accepting a higher delayed-union/nonunion rate than typical femoral shaft fractures due to the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology — some surgeons add teriparatide as an adjunct to promote healing, though this is an off-label, biologically-rationalized rather than guideline-mandated practice.
- **Paget disease**: osteotomy for significant angular deformity impairing function or predisposing to fracture; total hip/knee arthroplasty for secondary degenerative arthritis in pagetic bone — technically demanding due to bone hypervascularity (higher blood loss), deformity requiring corrective osteotomy or custom/modular implants, and altered bone quality affecting fixation; fixation of pathologic fractures through pagetic bone; urgent biopsy and oncologic referral for suspected sarcomatous transformation (new rapidly enlarging mass, lytic destruction, or disproportionate new pain in a previously stable segment).
- **Hyperparathyroid bone disease**: **parathyroidectomy** is the definitive treatment for primary HPT meeting international workshop criteria (serum calcium >1 mg/dL above the upper limit of normal, DEXA T-score ≤−2.5 at any site or a prior fragility fracture, eGFR <60 mL/min, age <50 years, or nephrolithiasis/nephrocalcinosis) — bone density improves substantially post-operatively, especially at cortical sites.
- **CRPS**: **spinal cord stimulation** is considered for refractory CRPS after ≥6 months of failed comprehensive conservative management (physiotherapy, sympathetic blocks, pharmacotherapy); long-term follow-up data show early pain-relief benefit that attenuates over years, so patient selection and expectation-setting are essential; peripheral nerve stimulation is an emerging, less invasive alternative in some centers.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

**Infection (osteomyelitis / FRI / septic arthritis) — post-debridement/fixation**
- Weeks 0–2: protected/non-weight-bearing per fixation stability and soft-tissue status; wound and flap surveillance; early passive/active-assisted ROM of adjacent joints unless a re-look procedure is imminent.
- Weeks 2–6: progressive weight-bearing as fixation and infection control allow; continue systemic antibiotics per culture-directed duration (weeks, guided by clinical/biochemical response rather than a fixed universal number of days); physiotherapy to restore joint ROM and prevent contracture, particularly after septic arthritis given the rapid cartilage injury described above.
- Beyond 6 weeks: strengthening progresses once bony union/soft-tissue healing is secure; return to work is individualized to job physical demand and wound/bone status, often 3–6 months for physically demanding roles after major reconstruction; return-to-sport only after radiographic union and infection-marker normalization sustained off antibiotics.

**Fragility fracture / osteoporosis pathway (hip fracture as the prototype)**
- Day 0–2: early mobilization is the single strongest modifiable driver of outcome — weight-bearing as tolerated the day after fixation/arthroplasty in the overwhelming majority of fragility hip fractures; delirium prevention, VTE prophylaxis, and same-admission orthogeriatric co-management.
- Weeks 1–6: progressive gait training with appropriate assistive device, home safety assessment, initiation/continuation of anti-osteoporosis pharmacotherapy (ideally started during the index admission — evidence supports in-hospital initiation improving adherence), and **enrollment in a Fracture Liaison Service** before discharge.
- Months 2–6: strength and balance training targeting fall-risk reduction; DEXA if not already performed; ongoing FLS-coordinated follow-up (medication adherence checks, repeat DEXA at ~1–2 years or per pharmacotherapy protocol).
- Return to pre-fracture function is the realistic goal in the majority of community-dwelling patients within 6–12 months, though a meaningful proportion do not regain full pre-fracture mobility — early mobilization and FLS engagement are the two levers most strongly associated with better functional recovery.

**AFF (post prophylactic or completed-fracture nailing)**
- Weeks 0–6: protected weight-bearing per fixation and fracture pattern; given the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology, expect a slower biological healing course than a typical traumatic femoral shaft fracture — radiographic surveillance is more frequent.
- Weeks 6–12+: progressive weight-bearing as callus/bridging is demonstrated; contralateral femur surveillance continues throughout.
- Delayed union is more common than in standard femoral shaft fractures; do not resume high-impact loading or unrestricted return to sport until solid radiographic union is confirmed, generally not before 4–6 months and often longer.

**Necrotizing fasciitis / major soft-tissue reconstruction**
- Days 0–14 (acute/source-control phase): dressing changes or negative-pressure wound therapy between staged debridements; joint positioning/splinting to prevent contracture even while systemically unwell; nutritional support is a rehabilitation priority given the catabolic burden of extensive tissue loss and repeated operative trips.
- Weeks 2–8: once the wound bed is clean and reconstruction (graft/flap) has been performed, progressive mobilization per flap-protection protocol (typically a period of immobilization for free-flap take, then graded activity); scar and contracture management begins as soon as the reconstruction is stable.
- Months 3–12: long-term functional and cosmetic rehabilitation, often with plastic-surgery-led scar therapy, compression garments, and staged secondary reconstructive procedures; return to work is highly variable (weeks for a limited-debridement survivor to many months for extensive limb reconstruction or after amputation, where prosthetic rehabilitation becomes the primary pathway).

**TB & atypical MSK infection**
- First 2 months (intensive medical phase): activity is guided by pain and, for spinal disease, by stability — bracing may be used for spinal TB with significant vertebral destruction pending healing; peripheral joint TB is mobilized as tolerated once acute synovitis settles.
- Months 2–9+ (continuation phase): progressive return to functional activity as imaging and inflammatory markers trend toward normal; for spinal TB, serial radiographic monitoring of kyphosis progression continues through this window since deformity can progress even on effective medical therapy.
- Post-surgical (when decompression/fusion was required): rehabilitation follows standard spinal fusion or joint arthrodesis/arthroplasty protocols, layered on top of continued anti-tuberculous therapy for the full planned duration.

**Paget disease / osteomalacia / hyperparathyroid bone disease (post-fixation or post-arthroplasty)**
- Weight-bearing and rehabilitation timelines follow the specific procedure performed (osteotomy, arthroplasty, or fracture fixation), but progression should be **more conservative** than in a metabolically normal patient until biochemical activity (ALP for Paget; 25-OH vitamin D/phosphate/PTH for osteomalacia and hyperparathyroid bone disease) has been confirmed to be trending toward normal, since healing capacity is directly tied to correction of the underlying metabolic abnormality.
- Post-parathyroidectomy patients should have staged, monitored calcium repletion integrated into their early rehabilitation plan given the risk of hungry bone syndrome limiting early mobilization if hypocalcemia is not anticipated and treated.

**CRPS**
- Weeks 0–4 (prevention/early phase): early active (not passive-forced) mobilization within the pain-tolerable range, graded motor imagery, mirror therapy, edema control, and desensitization techniques — the earlier this begins relative to symptom onset, the better the trajectory.
- Weeks 4–12: progressive functional loading, occupational therapy for task-specific retraining, escalation to sympathetic block or pharmacotherapy if plateaued.
- Beyond 3 months (established/chronic CRPS): multidisciplinary pain program combining physiotherapy, psychological support (pain-coping strategies, addressing kinesiophobia), and consideration of neuromodulation for refractory cases; return-to-work is often graded/part-time initially given fluctuating function, with vocational rehabilitation input for prolonged cases.

## Complications

- **Chronic osteomyelitis**: recurrence after "definitive" treatment is reported in a substantial minority (roughly one-fifth to one-third in several series) even with modern staged debridement protocols, materially higher in B/C hosts and with inadequate initial debridement; amputation may ultimately be required for uncontrolled type IV disease in a severely compromised host, and should be discussed as a legitimate, sometimes superior, option rather than a last resort only after repeated failed limb-salvage attempts.
- **FRI**: nonunion and persistent infection when implant retention is attempted outside appropriate indications (late presentation, loose implant, unhealthy soft tissue); selection and emergence of resistant organisms with repeated, poorly-targeted antibiotic courses — reinforcing the importance of getting deep cultures right the first time.
- **Native septic arthritis**: irreversible articular cartilage destruction and post-septic degenerative arthritis if decompression is delayed beyond roughly the first 24–48 hours; osteonecrosis of the femoral head in hip septic arthritis from tamponade effect of a tense effusion if not urgently decompressed — a time-sensitive complication analogous to (though pathophysiologically distinct from) SCFE/traumatic hip dislocation vascular risk.
- **Necrotizing fasciitis**: mortality roughly 20–30% overall, rising toward ~70% with delayed source control beyond 24 hours from presentation; limb loss, multi-organ failure from toxic shock, and major functional/cosmetic morbidity from radical debridement requiring extensive later reconstruction (skin grafting, flap coverage, sometimes multiple staged procedures).
- **TB/atypical infection**: **Pott's paraplegia** from cord compression (historically reported in a meaningful minority of untreated/late-presenting spinal TB), progressive kyphotic deformity, chronic discharging sinus formation, and — for NTM/fungal disease in immunocompromised hosts — high relapse rates requiring very prolonged (often many months to over a year) combination therapy.
- **Osteoporosis pharmacotherapy**: osteonecrosis of the jaw and atypical femoral fracture with long-term antiresorptive use (both rare — AFF incidence rises with cumulative bisphosphonate exposure, particularly beyond 5 years — but the biological rationale for periodic drug-holiday reassessment); **rebound vertebral fractures with denosumab discontinuation** if not bridged with a bisphosphonate, including reported clusters of multiple simultaneous vertebral fractures; hypocalcemia with potent antiresorptives in vitamin D-deficient or renally-impaired patients if not corrected before initiation.
- **AFF**: contralateral progression to a complete fracture during unilateral treatment if the other femur is not surveilled and treated; delayed union/nonunion after nailing occurs more frequently than in typical traumatic femoral shaft fractures given the underlying suppressed-remodeling biology.
- **Paget disease**: pathologic fracture through weakened pagetic bone, secondary degenerative arthritis of adjacent joints, high-output cardiac failure in extensive polyostotic disease, cranial nerve compression (hearing loss with skull-base involvement), and — rare (well under 1% of patients) but critical — **sarcomatous transformation**, which carries a poor prognosis and is the reason any new, disproportionate, or rapidly worsening pain in a known pagetic segment must be investigated promptly.
- **Osteomalacia/hyperparathyroid bone disease**: nonunion/failed fixation if surgery proceeds before biochemical correction of the mineralization defect; pathologic fracture through brown tumors; post-parathyroidectomy "hungry bone syndrome" (severe, sometimes prolonged transient hypocalcemia) in patients with severe pre-operative bone disease and high pre-operative ALP, requiring planned peri-operative calcium/calcitriol supplementation.
- **CRPS**: chronic pain and disability, joint contracture, disuse osteopenia, and, in a subset of patients, treatment-refractory disease despite comprehensive multidisciplinary care, with meaningful long-term impact on limb function and quality of life.

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

- **FRI confirmatory criteria validated in large cohorts**: an international retrospective cohort study testing the 2018/2020 FRI confirmatory-criteria framework against cases lacking classic confirmatory findings reported the presence of ≥1 confirmatory criterion carrying sensitivity ~97.5% and specificity ~100% for true infection, reinforcing the framework as the operational diagnostic standard rather than a construct needing revision — [Diagnosis of fracture-related infection in patients without clinical confirmatory criteria: an international retrospective cohort study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10134751/). A 2025 perspectives paper from the International Society of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy reaffirms standardized deep-tissue sampling, implant sonication, and extended culture incubation as current practice — [Fracture-Related Infections: Current Status and Perspectives from ISAC](https://www.mdpi.com/2079-6382/14/11/1095).
- **Local antibiotic carriers — calcium sulfate remains at least comparable to PMMA, without superiority proven**: 2025 reviews continue to describe absorbable calcium sulfate (± hydroxyapatite) carriers as achieving infection-control outcomes broadly comparable to PMMA beads while avoiding a second removal procedure, with the evidence base still resting on comparative cohorts/older small RCTs rather than definitive head-to-head trials — [Local Antibiotic Delivery Systems and Their Applications in Orthopaedic Surgery, JBJS OA 2025](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/jbjsoa/fulltext/10.2106/jbjs.oa.25.00157~local-antibiotic-delivery-systems-and-their-applications-in); [Efficacy and Safety of Antibiotic-Loaded Calcium Sulfate in the Treatment of Osteomyelitis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, 2025](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9416548/v1).
- **Septic arthritis — arthroscopic washout confirmed at least equivalent to open for the knee**: a 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis of RCTs and observational studies found arthroscopic washout gave comparable length of stay, complication, reoperation, and readmission rates versus open arthrotomy for native knee septic arthritis, with better patient-reported outcomes — [Does arthroscopic or open washout in native knee septic arthritis result in superior post-operative function?, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11010436/); a second 2024 systematic review reached the same conclusion — [Arthroscopic Treatment Is a Safe and Effective Alternative to Open Treatment for Acute Septic Arthritis of the Native Knee, Arthroscopy 2024](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1016/j.arthro.2023.05.038).
- **Septic arthritis — arthroscopic approach extended to the shoulder**: a 2025 systematic review reported arthroscopic treatment as noninferior to open treatment for native glenohumeral septic arthritis, extending the arthroscopic-first trend beyond the knee — [Arthroscopic Treatment Shows Noninferior Outcomes to Open Treatment for Native Glenohumeral Septic Arthritis, 2025](https://arthroscopyjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/arj.70173).
- **Osteoporosis — anabolic-first sequencing gains further guideline traction, but no single mandated sequence**: the updated 2024 UK NOGG guideline confirms all three anabolics (teriparatide, abaloparatide, romosozumab) may be used at different points in a patient's lifetime course, notes growing evidence that anabolic-first sequencing outperforms the older antiresorptive-then-anabolic order, but stops short of endorsing one fixed sequence, deferring to specialist-guided individualization — [The 2024 UK clinical guideline for the prevention and treatment of osteoporosis, Archives of Osteoporosis 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11657-025-01588-3); [NOGG 2024 Guideline (PDF)](https://www.nogg.org.uk/sites/nogg/download/NOGG-Guideline-2024.pdf?v5=). AACE has likewise updated its recommendations to help guide anabolic-versus-antiresorptive first-line therapy selection — [New osteoporosis recommendations from AACE help therapy selection](https://www.mdedge.com/rheumatology/article/226010/osteoporosis/new-osteoporosis-recommendations-aace-help-therapy).
- **Romosozumab cardiovascular signal — real-world data remain mixed, caution persists**: a 2024 real-world cohort analysis found first-year MACE incidence with romosozumab (27.2/1000 person-years) numerically lower than with teriparatide (34.0/1000 person-years) — [Cardiovascular outcomes of romosozumab treatment — real-world data analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11647516/) — while a propensity-matched JCEM cohort study and a 2024 sclerostin/cardiovascular-risk review describe the evidence as still unsettled, with disproportionality signals in some databases not replicated in others — [Cardiovascular Safety of Romosozumab vs PTH Analogues, JCEM](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/110/3/e861/7628384); [Sclerostin and Cardiovascular Risk: Evaluating the Cardiovascular Safety of Romosozumab, 2024](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9059/12/12/2880). This supports retaining the existing contraindication in recent MI/stroke rather than loosening it.
- **Denosumab discontinuation — bridging remains mandatory but imperfect, especially after long-term use**: a 2025 review confirms that stopping denosumab without relay therapy causes rapid rebound bone turnover and, in up to ~10% of unbridged patients, multiple vertebral fractures within 6–12 months; the European Calcified Tissue Society continues to recommend zoledronic acid (or extended oral bisphosphonate/denosumab continuation as alternatives) as bridging therapy, though bisphosphonate bridging shows only mixed success after medium-to-long-term (>2.5–3 years) denosumab exposure — [Denosumab discontinuation in the clinic: implications of rebound bone turnover and emerging strategies, JBMR 2025](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/40/9/1017/8064740). A 2025/2026 case series reports romosozumab as a rescue option after rebound vertebral fractures have already occurred — [Romosozumab after rebound vertebral fractures induced by denosumab discontinuation: a case series](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12790271/).
- **Fracture Liaison Service outcomes — mortality and refracture benefit increasingly quantified**: a 2025 Korean coordinator-based FLS study reported in-hospital mortality of 1.0% vs 4.2%, 1-year mortality 8.6% vs 12.5%, and 1-year refracture 5.7% vs 10.7% versus controls — [Clinical Outcomes of a Coordinator-Based Fracture Liaison Service for Hip Fractures in Korea](https://jkms.org/DOIx.php?id=10.3346%2Fjkms.2026.41.e26); a Hong Kong pre-post study found FLS associated with lower mortality (OR 0.65) and refracture falling from 5.6% to 0% — [Clinical effectiveness of a fracture liaison service for geriatric hip fracture patients in Hong Kong, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/22104917241284643); a 2025 systematic review/meta-analysis found FLS participation associated with a 46% relative reduction in subsequent fracture risk — [The impact of a fracture liaison service for patients with femoral fractures on subsequent fractures and mortality, Archives of Osteoporosis 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11657-025-01505-8); a five-year prospective study confirms reduced short-term mortality and second fractures — [Real-life effectiveness of a Fracture Liaison Service in reducing short-term mortality and second fractures after hip fracture](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40987981/).
- **Burosumab indication broadening confirmed for both XLH and tumor-induced osteomalacia**: UK NICE guidance for adult X-linked hypophosphatemia was updated in August 2024 (pediatric guidance revised April 2025), and burosumab gained tumor-induced-osteomalacia approval in France in 2024, extending on its earlier US/Japan/Korea TIO approvals — [Burosumab for X-linked Hypophosphataemia and Tumour-induced Osteomalacia in Adults](https://touchendocrinology.com/endocrine-oncology/journal-articles/burosumab-for-x-linked-hypophosphataemia-and-tumour-induced-osteomalacia-in-adults/); a 2025 early-access-program case series supports real-world efficacy/safety in TIO — [Real-world efficacy and safety of burosumab in tumor-induced osteomalacia](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12050030/).
- **CRPS — new 2025 guidance targets the chronic phase specifically**: the American Society of Interventional Pain Physicians published 2025 guidelines that build on (not replace) the Budapest criteria, adding a time-dependent framework requiring criteria to persist ≥12 months before a case is classified as chronic CRPS, alongside reaffirmed multimodal, patient-specific management — [Chronic CRPS Diagnosis and Treatment: 2025 ASIPP Guidelines](https://painmed.org/diagnosing-treating-chronic-crps/).
- **Vitamin C for CRPS prophylaxis — evidence is more conflicting than previously stated, not just heterogeneous**: a 2023 systematic review/meta-analysis found ≥42 days of vitamin C prophylaxis reduced CRPS-I after wrist, ankle, and foot fractures regardless of dose — [Effect of Perioperative Vitamin C on the Incidence of CRPS: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis, 2023](https://boneandjoint.org.uk/Article/10.1302/1358-992X.2023.3.034) — but a subsequent 2024 meta-analysis of 8 studies found the pooled effect size was **not statistically significant**, materially softening the earlier positive signal — [Efficacy of vitamin C supplementation in preventing and treating CRPS-I in orthopedic patients: a systematic review and meta-analysis, 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39447383/). Given this conflict, vitamin C prophylaxis should be framed as low-risk/low-cost and reasonable to offer rather than as evidence-proven effective.
- **Necrotizing fasciitis — new international consensus published, IVIG still unresolved**: a 2025 Chinese Burn Association consensus on adult necrotizing fasciitis diagnosis and treatment (literature reviewed through January 2025) continues to list IVIG only as a possible adjunct (e.g., for cervical necrotizing fasciitis) rather than a mandated therapy — [Consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of adult necrotizing fasciitis (2025 edition)](https://academic.oup.com/burnstrauma/article/doi/10.1093/burnst/tkaf031/8160908). Source control via early radical debridement remains the only intervention with unambiguous mortality benefit.
