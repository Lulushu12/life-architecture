## Quick Reference

**The single most important rule in this entire chapter: if a lesion looks aggressive, or you are not certain it is benign, do NOT biopsy it yourself. Refer to a treating sarcoma/bone tumor center before any biopsy or intralesional procedure.** A misplaced or poorly-planned biopsy tract, hematoma, or incisional biopsy performed outside a treating center is one of the best-documented ways to upstage a patient, force a larger resection or amputation, and worsen survival (Mankin et al., classic and re-validated data). When in doubt, image, do not touch, and refer.

### Red flags demanding urgent referral (do not inject, do not biopsy, do not "watch 3 months" locally)

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Night pain unrelieved by position change, or pain at rest | Suggests malignancy or osteoid osteoma (latter relieved by NSAIDs) |
| Progressive/rapidly enlarging mass | Aggressive biology |
| Soft tissue mass >5 cm, regardless of symptoms | Threshold above which malignancy risk rises sharply — image and refer |
| Deep-to-fascia mass of any size | Higher liposarcoma/STS pretest probability than superficial |
| Systemic symptoms (weight loss, fever, night sweats) with a bone lesion | Consider lymphoma, myeloma, metastasis, infection |
| Cortical destruction, wide zone of transition, aggressive periosteal reaction (sunburst, onion-skin, Codman triangle) on plain film | Lodwick II/III pattern — urgent MRI + referral, no local biopsy |
| Pathologic fracture through a lesion that was not previously characterized | Biopsy the fracture site only at the treating center — fracture hematoma is tumor-contaminated tissue |
| Elevated ALP/LDH in a child/adolescent with a bone lesion | Supports osteosarcoma/Ewing; expedite staging |
| Recurrent mass after a prior "simple" marginal excision done elsewhere | Re-excision (and often adjuvant therapy) at a sarcoma center is now needed for a wider margin |

### Classification quick tables

**Enneking/MSTS (benign)** — Stage 1 latent (observe) → Stage 2 active (curettage ± adjuvant) → Stage 3 aggressive (extended curettage/adjuvant or wide excision).

**Enneking (malignant)** — IA/IB low-grade intra/extracompartmental (wide excision) → IIA/IIB high-grade intra/extracompartmental (wide excision + systemic therapy) → III any grade with metastasis (systemic therapy ± resection of primary and oligometastases).

**Mirels score** (impending pathologic fracture) — 4 variables (site, pain, lesion type, size), each 1–3, total 4–12. **≥8 → prophylactic fixation.** 7 is a gray zone requiring judgment. ≤7 → non-operative management with reassessment.

**Campanacci (GCT)** — I well-marginated/sclerotic rim (curettage) → II defined margin, no sclerotic rim, cortex thinned (curettage + adjuvant + cement) → III cortical breach/soft tissue extension (extended curettage vs wide resection ± neoadjuvant denosumab).

**Lodwick–Madewell** — IA/IB geographic well-defined (usually benign, can observe/low suspicion) → IC geographic ill-defined → II moth-eaten → III permeative (II/III = urgent MRI + biopsy at treating center; differential includes Ewing, osteosarcoma, lymphoma, infection).

### Compact treatment ladders

| Diagnosis | First-line | Second-line | Surgical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Osteosarcoma | Neoadjuvant MAP chemotherapy | — (chemo is mandatory, not optional) | Wide resection (limb salvage vs amputation) + adjuvant chemo by necrosis response |
| Ewing sarcoma | Neoadjuvant VDC/IE (interval-compressed) | Radiation if unresectable/poor surgical candidate | Wide resection preferred over RT alone for local control when feasible |
| Chondrosarcoma grade I | Extended intralesional curettage + adjuvant (long bones) | — | Wide resection reserved for pelvis/axial or grade II–III/dedifferentiated |
| GCT of bone | Extended curettage + burr + adjuvant (phenol/cryo/argon) + cement | Denosumab (unresectable, or neoadjuvant to downstage) | Wide resection + endoprosthesis for joint-destroying/recurrent disease |
| Osteoid osteoma | NSAIDs (may self-resolve over years) | Percutaneous CT-guided radiofrequency ablation (RFA) | Open excision if RFA unsafe (near neurovascular bundle/skin/spinal cord) |
| Osteochondroma | Observation | — | Excision if symptomatic, growing post-maturity, or cap >1.5–2 cm |
| Enchondroma | Observation, serial imaging if atypical features | — | Curettage + graft only if painful/pathologic fracture or cannot exclude low-grade chondrosarcoma |
| UBC (unicameral) | Observation if incidental/low fracture risk | Aspiration + steroid injection | Curettage + graft or IM decompression/elastic nail for active/recurrent |
| ABC | — | Doxycycline foam sclerotherapy, selective embolization | Curettage + adjuvant + graft; denosumab off-label for large/recurrent/spinal |
| Fibrous dysplasia | Observation, bisphosphonates for pain | — | Cortical allograft (not cancellous autograft) for curettage; IM rod for femoral neck to prevent shepherd's crook |
| Soft tissue sarcoma | — | — | Wide resection ± (neo)adjuvant radiation for large/deep/high-grade; selective neoadjuvant chemo in high-risk histologies |

| Multiple myeloma | Systemic therapy (proteasome inhibitor/IMiD-based) + bone-modifying agent | Vertebral augmentation for painful VCF | IM nailing for long-bone pathologic fracture; decompression/stabilization for cord compromise |

### Key numbers to memorize

- **Mirels ≥8/12** → prophylactic fixation.
- **Denosumab GCT dosing**: 120 mg SC every 4 weeks, with loading doses on days 8 and 15 of month 1.
- **RFA for osteoid osteoma**: nidus typically <1.5–2 cm; reported success ~85–95% in a single treatment; keep ablation zone ≥1 cm from skin and neural structures.
- **Osteochondroma cartilage cap**: >1.5–2 cm in a skeletally mature patient raises concern for secondary chondrosarcoma — obtain MRI.
- **STS referral threshold**: any soft tissue mass >5 cm, or any deep mass regardless of size, or any growing/painful mass → MRI + refer before excision.
- **GCT local recurrence**: curettage alone ~25–50%; curettage + adjuvant (phenol/cryotherapy/argon beam) + cement ~10–15%.
- **Malignant transformation**: solitary osteochondroma <1%, multiple hereditary exostoses ~1–5%, Ollier disease enchondromatosis ~25–30%, GCT ~1–4% (higher after radiation).
- **Whole-body MRI or PET/CT now preferred over skeletal survey for myeloma staging** (IMWG).

## Anatomy & Pathophysiology

### Osteosarcoma
Malignant mesenchymal cells directly produce osteoid. Bimodal age distribution: primary peak in adolescence (rapid bone growth correlates with metaphyseal predilection — distal femur > proximal tibia > proximal humerus), secondary peak in older adults, usually arising in Paget's disease or a prior radiation field. Genetic predisposition includes **TP53** mutations (Li-Fraumeni syndrome) and **RB1** mutations (hereditary retinoblastoma). Histologic subtypes: conventional (osteoblastic, chondroblastic, fibroblastic — behave similarly, treated identically), telangiectatic (blood-filled, aggressive-appearing, can mimic ABC), low-grade central and parosteal (surface, low-grade, MDM2/CDK4 amplified — behaves more like a benign/low-grade lesion, chemotherapy generally not indicated unless dedifferentiation present), and periosteal (intermediate grade, surface, diaphyseal).

### Ewing sarcoma
A small round blue cell tumor of the **Ewing sarcoma family**, driven in ~85% by the **t(11;22)(q24;q12) EWSR1-FLI1** fusion (other EWSR1 partner fusions account for the remainder). Arises in diaphysis/metadiaphysis of long bones, pelvis, and chest wall (Askin tumor); can also be primary in soft tissue (extraosseous Ewing). Highly chemo- and radiosensitive, distinguishing it therapeutically from osteosarcoma.

### Chondrosarcoma
Malignant cartilage-forming tumor, most common in pelvis, proximal femur, proximal humerus, and ribs in adults >40. **IDH1/IDH2** mutations are common in central conventional chondrosarcoma and in enchondromatosis (Ollier, Maffucci). Behaves very differently by grade: grade I (now often termed atypical cartilaginous tumor/ACT in the appendicular skeleton) has essentially no metastatic potential; grade II/III and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma are aggressive. Rare variants — mesenchymal chondrosarcoma (small round cell component, chemosensitive like Ewing) and clear cell chondrosarcoma (low-grade, epiphyseal) — need to be recognized because they change treatment.

### Giant cell tumor of bone
Locally aggressive, epiphyseal-extending-to-subchondral-bone tumor of skeletally mature patients, most common around the knee (distal femur, proximal tibia) and distal radius. Driven by a **H3F3A (H3.3 G34W)** mutation in the neoplastic mononuclear stromal cells, which overexpress **RANKL**, recruiting and activating osteoclast-like giant cells that drive bone resorption — the direct pharmacologic rationale for denosumab. Rare pulmonary "benign metastases" (~1–3%) and malignant transformation (~1–4%, higher after radiotherapy) occur.

### Osteoid osteoma
Benign bone-forming lesion with a **nidus** (<1.5–2 cm by definition; larger lesions are termed osteoblastoma) rich in prostaglandins via **COX-2** overexpression, producing the classic nocturnal pain relieved by NSAIDs. Cortical, diaphyseal predilection (proximal femur classic); intra-articular and posterior spinal element locations cause painful scoliosis (concave-side lesion).

### Osteochondroma
Most common benign bone tumor. A cartilage-capped bony projection continuous with the underlying cortex and medullary canal, arising from aberrant physeal cartilage. **EXT1/EXT2** loss-of-function mutations cause hereditary multiple exostoses (autosomal dominant), which carries higher lifetime malignant transformation risk than the solitary sporadic form.

### Enchondroma
Benign intramedullary hyaline cartilage tumor, most common in the small bones of the hand/foot (where it can be observed even with fairly aggressive-looking radiographs given the different biological behavior at that site) and in long bones (proximal humerus, distal femur). **Ollier disease** (multiple enchondromatosis) and **Maffucci syndrome** (enchondromatosis + soft tissue hemangiomas) carry substantially elevated risk of malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma and of other malignancies (ovarian, CNS, GI).

### Aneurysmal & unicameral bone cysts
**UBC (simple bone cyst)**: a fluid-filled, unilocular cavity, classically proximal humerus or proximal femur in children, centrally located and abutting the physis ("active") or migrated away from it with growth ("latent"). Pathophysiology relates to venous obstruction/altered interstitial fluid dynamics rather than neoplasia. **ABC**: a locally aggressive, expansile, blood-filled multiloculated lesion. Primary ABC is driven by a **USP6** gene rearrangement (a true clonal neoplastic process); secondary ABC arises within a pre-existing lesion (GCT, chondroblastoma, fibrous dysplasia, or — critically — telangiectatic osteosarcoma), which is why solid/soft-tissue components within an apparent ABC must always be sampled.

### Fibrous dysplasia
A developmental fibro-osseous lesion caused by a postzygotic activating **GNAS** mutation (Gsα), producing somatic mosaicism — normal bone is replaced by fibrous stroma with immature woven bone ("ground glass"). Monostotic (most common, ~70–80%) or polyostotic. **McCune-Albright syndrome** = polyostotic FD + café-au-lait macules (irregular "coast of Maine" borders) + endocrinopathy (precocious puberty, hyperthyroidism, growth hormone excess). **Mazabraud syndrome** = FD + intramuscular myxomas.

### Soft tissue sarcoma (overview)
A heterogeneous group of >50 mesenchymal malignancies (undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma/UPS, liposarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma, MPNST, and others), most arising in the deep soft tissues of the extremities and retroperitoneum. Grade (differentiation, mitotic count, necrosis — **FNCLCC** system, most widely used) and size/depth drive prognosis more than histologic subtype in most extremity STS.

### Lipoma vs liposarcoma
Lipoma: benign mature adipocytes, typically superficial (subcutaneous), simple karyotype changes if any. **Atypical lipomatous tumor/well-differentiated liposarcoma (ALT/WDLPS)**: **MDM2/CDK4 amplification** — locally aggressive but does not metastasize when purely well-differentiated in an extremity. **Dedifferentiated liposarcoma**: a nonlipogenic high-grade component arising within WDLPS, metastatic potential, most common in the retroperitoneum. **Myxoid liposarcoma**: **FUS-DDIT3** (or rarer EWSR1-DDIT3) fusion, unusual metastatic pattern to soft tissue and bone before lung. **Pleomorphic liposarcoma**: high-grade, aggressive, no characteristic fusion.

### Metastatic bone disease
Bone is the third most common site of metastasis after lung and liver, and the most common cause of a destructive bone lesion in an adult over 40. Breast, prostate, lung, kidney, and thyroid carcinoma account for the large majority ("BPLKT" primaries). Osteolytic metastases (lung, kidney, thyroid, most breast) resorb bone via tumor-secreted RANKL/PTHrP driving osteoclastogenesis; osteoblastic metastases (most prostate) stimulate abnormal, structurally weak new bone formation — both patterns weaken bone and both benefit from bone-modifying agents.

### Multiple myeloma
A clonal plasma cell malignancy that is the **most common primary malignancy of bone overall** in adults (more common than any single primary bone sarcoma). Malignant plasma cells upregulate osteoclast activity (via RANKL/OPG imbalance and other cytokines, e.g., IL-6, MIP-1α) while suppressing osteoblastic bone formation, producing diffuse osteopenia, punched-out lytic lesions, and a propensity for vertebral compression fractures.

## Clinical Evaluation & Imaging

**History**: pain character (mechanical/activity-related favors benign or degenerative; constant, progressive, or **night pain** favors malignancy or osteoid osteoma), duration and growth rate, constitutional symptoms, prior malignancy (metastasis vs primary), prior radiation (radiation-induced sarcoma, typically ≥3–4 years latency), family history (Li-Fraumeni, hereditary multiple exosteses, Ollier/Maffucci), age (single strongest discriminator — think metastasis/myeloma first in an adult >40 with a destructive lesion and no known prior primary bone sarcoma diagnosis in childhood).

**Examination**: size, depth (mobile/superficial vs fixed/deep — a fixed deep mass is sarcoma until proven otherwise), consistency, overlying skin changes, regional lymphadenopathy (rare in sarcoma except epithelioid sarcoma, synovial sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma — worth a regional node check for these), neurovascular exam, and a full musculoskeletal/systemic exam looking for a primary source if metastasis is suspected.

**Labs**: **ALP and LDH** in suspected osteosarcoma (prognostic, useful for response monitoring); **ESR/CRP** if infection is in the differential (Brodie abscess, osteomyelitis frequently mimic bone tumors radiographically); **calcium, renal function, SPEP/UPEP, serum free light chain ratio, β2-microglobulin, CBC** whenever myeloma or metastatic carcinoma of unknown primary is in the differential of a lytic lesion in an adult, especially >40 with diffuse osteopenia or multiple lesions.

**Imaging sequence**:
1. **Plain radiographs first, always** — orthogonal views of the full bone. This is the single most informative study for characterizing growth rate (zone of transition, periosteal reaction pattern — see Lodwick below) and remains the backbone of the differential.
2. **MRI** (with contrast) of the entire compartment/bone including joint above and below for local staging — defines soft tissue extension, marrow involvement, neurovascular relationship, skip lesions, and is essential before any biopsy or surgical planning.
3. **CT chest** for metastatic staging in any confirmed or suspected primary bone/soft tissue sarcoma (lung is the dominant site for hematogenous spread in most sarcomas).
4. **Bone scan or whole-body/PET-CT** for skeletal metastases/multifocal disease staging (osteosarcoma, Ewing) and for treatment response assessment in Ewing sarcoma (PET-CT is now standard in most protocols).
5. **CT** is superior for cortical detail and nidus characterization (osteoid osteoma) and for guiding percutaneous biopsy/ablation.
6. Bone marrow aspirate/biopsy is part of Ewing sarcoma staging (marrow involvement changes risk stratification).

**Differential diagnosis by age — the single fastest filter in clinic:**

| Age group | Malignant lesions to prioritize | Common benign/other lesions |
|---|---|---|
| <10 years | Ewing sarcoma, leukemia/lymphoma involving bone | UBC, ABC, fibrous dysplasia, osteomyelitis, eosinophilic granuloma |
| 10–25 years | Osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma | Osteochondroma, enchondroma, osteoid osteoma, non-ossifying fibroma, ABC/UBC, chondroblastoma, GCT (later end of range) |
| 25–40 years | GCT (malignant transformation rare but occurs), low-grade chondrosarcoma | GCT, enchondroma, fibrous dysplasia |
| >40 years | **Metastatic carcinoma (most likely diagnosis by far), multiple myeloma**, chondrosarcoma, secondary osteosarcoma (Paget's/radiation), lymphoma | Paget's disease, degenerative/insufficiency change |

This table is a starting heuristic, not a substitute for tissue diagnosis — but it should shape pretest probability and the urgency of workup the moment a lesion is identified.

**Biopsy — the referral rule**: biopsy should be performed **at, or in direct coordination with, the treating multidisciplinary sarcoma center**, along a tract that the same team will later excise en bloc with the tumor during definitive resection. A poorly placed biopsy (wrong tract, contamination of an adjacent compartment, hematoma spread outside the planned resection field, transverse rather than longitudinal extremity incision) can convert a limb-salvage candidate into an amputation, upstage the disease, and independently worsen survival — this remains one of the best-validated principles in orthopedic oncology (Mankin et al., original and follow-up series). Core needle biopsy under image guidance is now preferred over incisional biopsy for most lesions when performed by the treating team; open incisional biopsy is reserved for cases where core biopsy is non-diagnostic. **Never** perform a biopsy, curettage, or "diagnostic excision" of a lesion with any aggressive imaging feature outside a treating center — refer first.

**Practical referral pathway (Europe/Romania context)**: bone and soft tissue sarcomas are rare enough that outcomes are consistently better when managed at high-volume centers. **EURACAN** (the European Reference Network for rare adult solid cancers, which includes bone and soft tissue sarcoma) exists precisely to connect referring surgeons to designated expert centers across the EU/EEA, and most national sarcoma networks (including reference centers accessible from Romania) participate in this framework or maintain equivalent multidisciplinary tumor boards. The practical implication for a non-specialist orthopedic surgeon: any lesion meeting the red-flag criteria above should be discussed with, or referred directly to, the nearest designated sarcoma center **before** biopsy, injection, or "diagnostic" curettage — a phone call or referral letter costs nothing; an unplanned biopsy tract can cost the patient a limb.

## Classification

### Enneking / Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) staging

**Benign lesions (Stage 1–3):**

| Stage | Definition | Radiographic correlate | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Latent | Asymptomatic, static or very slow growth, well-defined margin, intact cortex | Lodwick IA, sclerotic rim | Observation, serial imaging |
| 2 — Active | Progressive growth, may thin/expand cortex, symptomatic | Lodwick IB, thin sclerotic rim or none | Intralesional curettage ± adjuvant |
| 3 — Aggressive | Rapid growth, cortical destruction, may extend into soft tissue but remains "benign" histologically | Lodwick IC, cortical breach | Extended curettage + adjuvant, or marginal/wide excision if joint/critical structures at risk |

**Malignant lesions (surgical stage, based on Grade [G], anatomic site [T], and Metastasis [M]):**

| Stage | Grade | Site | Metastasis | Management implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IA | Low (G1) | Intracompartmental (T1) | M0 | Wide excision; systemic therapy generally not required (histology-dependent) |
| IB | Low (G1) | Extracompartmental (T2) | M0 | Wide excision; wider margin planning given extracompartmental extension |
| IIA | High (G2) | Intracompartmental (T1) | M0 | Wide/radical excision + (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy for chemosensitive histologies (osteosarcoma, Ewing) |
| IIB | High (G2) | Extracompartmental (T2) | M0 | Wide/radical excision + systemic therapy; higher local recurrence risk drives more aggressive margin planning |
| III | Any grade | Any site | M1 (regional or distant) | Systemic therapy is primary; surgery of primary ± resectable metastases (e.g., pulmonary metastasectomy) considered for local/oligometastatic control |

### Mirels score (risk of impending pathologic fracture through a metastatic long-bone lesion)

| Variable | 1 point | 2 points | 3 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site | Upper extremity | Lower extremity | Peritrochanteric |
| Pain | Mild | Moderate | Functional/mechanical (pain with weight-bearing) |
| Lesion type | Blastic | Mixed | Lytic |
| Size (% of cortical diameter destroyed) | <1/3 | 1/3–2/3 | >2/3 |

**Total score 4–12.** **≥8 → prophylactic fixation recommended** (fracture risk substantially elevated, best treated electively). **7 → gray zone**, use clinical judgment — lean toward fixation for peritrochanteric lesions, functional pain, or lesions expected to progress before radiation takes effect. **≤7 → non-operative management appropriate initially** (radiation, systemic therapy, bone-modifying agent), with scheduled re-imaging/reassessment as the lesion may progress.

### Campanacci grading (giant cell tumor of bone)

| Grade | Radiographic features | Management implication |
|---|---|---|
| I | Well-marginated lesion with a thin sclerotic rim, cortex intact | Intralesional curettage + adjuvant + cement/graft; lowest recurrence risk |
| II | Well-defined margins but no sclerotic rim; cortex thinned/expanded, may bulge but remains grossly intact | Intralesional curettage + burr + adjuvant + cement; standard first-line even for most large lesions |
| III | Ill-defined margins, cortical destruction, extension into soft tissue | Extended curettage still feasible in many (especially with neoadjuvant denosumab to consolidate a rim and facilitate joint-sparing surgery) vs wide resection + endoprosthesis if joint/neurovascular structures cannot be preserved |

### Lodwick–Madewell grading (radiographic pattern of bone destruction — a surrogate for growth rate/biological aggressiveness)

| Grade | Pattern | Zone of transition | Typical biological behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| IA | Geographic, with a sclerotic rim | Narrow, well-defined | Slow-growing, usually benign (e.g., latent bone cyst, non-ossifying fibroma) |
| IB | Geographic, well-defined but no sclerotic rim | Narrow-moderate | Usually benign but higher activity than IA (e.g., active UBC, enchondroma) |
| IC | Geographic, ill-defined margin | Wide | More aggressive — needs further workup (e.g., aggressive GCT, low-grade sarcoma) |
| II | Moth-eaten — multiple 0.5–1.5 cm coalescing lytic holes | Wide | Aggressive — infection, myeloma, lymphoma, metastasis, higher-grade sarcoma in differential |
| III | Permeative — numerous tiny (<1 mm) lytic foci through cortex | Wide, indistinct | Highly aggressive, rapid growth — osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma, infection |

Grades II and III, and any IC lesion with correlating clinical red flags, mandate urgent MRI and referral to a treating center before any intervention — this is the radiographic backbone of the referral rule above. Periosteal reaction pattern should be read alongside Lodwick grade: a solid, uninterrupted periosteal reaction supports a slow/benign process, while lamellated ("onion-skin"), spiculated ("sunburst"/"hair-on-end"), or an interrupted reaction with a **Codman triangle** all support an aggressive process (classically osteosarcoma or Ewing sarcoma).

## Treatment

### Non-operative

- **Osteoid osteoma**: NSAIDs (COX inhibition directly targets the prostaglandin-driven pain mechanism) can be used as a bridging or even definitive strategy — a meaningful fraction of lesions involute spontaneously over roughly 3–7 years — but many patients cannot tolerate years of NSAID dependence, favoring earlier ablation.
- **Osteochondroma, small/asymptomatic enchondroma, latent UBC, latent/Stage-1 lesions generally**: observation with serial radiographs (typically 6–12 monthly until stability is demonstrated, then spaced out).
- **Fibrous dysplasia**: observation for asymptomatic lesions; bisphosphonates (IV pamidronate/zoledronic acid) are used for bone pain, with evidence supporting analgesic benefit but inconsistent evidence for radiographic healing or preventing progression — counsel patients accordingly rather than promising structural benefit.
- **Metastatic bone disease / myeloma**: systemic anticancer therapy is the backbone of disease control; a bone-modifying agent (denosumab or zoledronic acid) reduces skeletal-related events; external beam radiation treats painful lesions without structural compromise and is given post-fixation for lesions that were surgically stabilized.
- **Chemotherapy-sensitive primary sarcomas (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma)**: chemotherapy is not "non-operative management" in the sense of an alternative to surgery — it is a mandatory neoadjuvant/adjuvant component that determines both local control and survival, always given in combination with definitive resection, not as a substitute for it.

### Injections & Adjunctive Procedures

- **Radiofrequency ablation (RFA)** — first-line for osteoid osteoma in an accessible location: CT-guided percutaneous nidus ablation, outpatient, reported single-treatment success ~85–95%, with retreatment for the minority who fail. Contraindicated/relatively contraindicated when the nidus is <1 cm from the skin or from a major neural structure (spinal cord, major peripheral nerve) — consider cryoablation or open excision in these locations.
- **Cryoablation** — alternative to RFA, particularly useful near skin/neural structures (better real-time monitoring of the ice ball on CT/MRI) and increasingly used for other benign-aggressive and low-grade lesions, and as palliation for painful metastases.
- **Selective arterial embolization** — used for ABC (as primary or adjunctive treatment, particularly spinal/pelvic ABC where surgical morbidity is high) and preoperatively for hypervascular metastases (renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular carcinoma) to reduce intraoperative blood loss before resection/fixation.
- **Doxycycline (or other sclerosant) foam sclerotherapy** — an effective, less invasive alternative to curettage for ABC, with good response rates reported in prospective series; typically requires multiple sessions.
- **Intralesional steroid injection (methylprednisolone acetate)** — first-line procedural option for symptomatic/active UBC, particularly proximal humerus, with the option to combine with bone marrow aspirate or demineralized bone matrix; recurrence is common and repeat injection is routine.
- **Denosumab (RANKL inhibitor)** — the key adjunct for GCT of bone: approved for skeletally mature patients with unresectable GCT, or where planned surgery would result in severe morbidity (e.g., sacral, pelvic, or spinal GCT). Dosed **120 mg SC every 4 weeks, with additional loading doses on days 8 and 15 of the first month**. Neoadjuvant use to downsize a Campanacci III lesion and facilitate joint-sparing curettage rather than resection is a recognized strategy, but is used selectively: denosumab induces new bone formation and fibrosis around residual tumor that can make the curettage plane harder to define, and several retrospective series report **higher local recurrence** after neoadjuvant denosumab + curettage compared with curettage alone in resectable disease — this is a genuine tradeoff to discuss with the patient, not a free downstaging strategy. Watch for **osteonecrosis of the jaw** with prolonged use and counsel on dental hygiene/pre-treatment dental evaluation, and for **rebound hypercalcemia** on discontinuation, particularly in younger patients — monitor calcium after stopping.
- **Bone-modifying agents in metastatic disease/myeloma** — denosumab or zoledronic acid reduce skeletal-related events (pathologic fracture, need for radiation/surgery to bone, spinal cord compression, hypercalcemia of malignancy); denosumab does not require renal dose adjustment, an advantage in myeloma patients with renal impairment, but carries the same ONJ/rebound hypercalcemia considerations as above.
- **Percutaneous cementoplasty / vertebroplasty / kyphoplasty** — palliation and mechanical augmentation for painful lytic metastases and vertebral compression fractures (myeloma, metastatic disease), particularly effective for pain relief; assess spinal stability (SINS score) and posterior element/epidural involvement before proceeding, as significant posterior cortical destruction raises cement leakage and neurologic risk.

### Operative

**General principles of margins**:

| Margin | Plane of dissection | Typical indication |
|---|---|---|
| Intralesional | Through the lesion itself | Curettage of benign/low-grade lesions (GCT, UBC, ABC, enchondroma, chondrosarcoma grade I) |
| Marginal | Through the reactive zone/pseudocapsule | Most clearly benign lesions requiring excision (osteochondroma, symptomatic lipoma) |
| Wide | Through normal tissue, cuff of uninvolved tissue circumferentially | Standard for malignant primary bone and soft tissue sarcomas |
| Radical | Entire anatomic compartment removed | Rarely required with modern imaging/planning; historically for extensive/multicompartmental disease |

Margin choice is dictated by the Enneking stage and histology, not by surgeon preference.

**Pediatric/growth-plate considerations**: in skeletally immature patients undergoing resection near an open physis (distal femoral osteosarcoma is the classic scenario), reconstruction options include physis-sparing intercalary resection when tumor margins allow, or an **expandable/growing endoprosthesis** (non-invasively lengthened, typically via an external magnetic driver in modern designs) to accommodate remaining growth and avoid the leg-length discrepancy and gait abnormality that a fixed-length adult-sized prosthesis would otherwise cause; this adds a layer of long-term surgical planning (staged lengthenings, eventual conversion to a definitive adult-sized implant near skeletal maturity) that should be discussed with the family at the outset.

**Osteosarcoma**: neoadjuvant **MAP** chemotherapy (high-dose **M**ethotrexate, doxorubicin [**A**driamycin], **c**isplatin [**P**latinum]) for ~10 weeks, followed by wide surgical resection, then adjuvant chemotherapy continued/adjusted based on **histologic necrosis response** (Huvos grading; ≥90% necrosis = good responder, associated with better survival). Limb salvage (endoprosthesis, allograft, allograft-prosthetic composite, or rotationplasty in select pediatric cases) is oncologically equivalent to amputation for local control **provided adequate margins are achievable** with neurovascular preservation — amputation is reserved for cases where a wide margin/functional limb cannot otherwise be achieved, extensive neurovascular involvement, or patient preference. Skip lesions and pathologic fracture at presentation are relative contraindications to limb salvage. Pulmonary metastasectomy is pursued for resectable lung metastases (isolated or oligometastatic), as it meaningfully improves survival in this subset.

**Ewing sarcoma**: neoadjuvant chemotherapy (**VDC/IE** — vincristine, doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide alternating with ifosfamide, etoposide), then local control with surgery, radiation, or both, then continued adjuvant chemotherapy. **Surgical resection is generally preferred over radiation alone for local control when feasible**, given better local control rates and avoidance of radiation-associated secondary sarcoma risk in a typically young population; radiation is reserved for unresectable disease, close/positive margins, or poor surgical candidates (and is frequently needed as an adjunct even after surgery for close margins or poor histologic response).

**Chondrosarcoma**: grade I/ACT in the appendicular skeleton is increasingly managed with **extended intralesional curettage + high-speed burr + adjuvant (phenol, cryotherapy, or argon beam) + cement or graft** rather than wide resection — a major practice shift over the last decade, supported by equivalent oncologic outcomes with far better function compared to historical wide resection for these low-grade lesions. Grade II/III and dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma require **wide resection** — chondrosarcoma is largely chemo- and radio-resistant in its conventional form (exceptions: mesenchymal chondrosarcoma is treated with Ewing-like chemotherapy; dedifferentiated chondrosarcoma is sometimes treated with osteosarcoma-like regimens for the high-grade component, with modest benefit). Axial/pelvic chondrosarcoma, where wide margins are harder to achieve and curettage is riskier, is generally managed more aggressively regardless of grade.

**Giant cell tumor of bone**: **extended intralesional curettage** with a high-speed burr to extend the cavity 1–2 mm beyond gross tumor, plus a local adjuvant (phenol, liquid nitrogen cryotherapy, or argon beam coagulation) to extend the thermal/chemical kill zone, then reconstruction with **PMMA cement** (preferred by many for immediate mechanical support, ability to load-bear sooner, and because a local recurrence is easier to detect radiographically against cement than against incorporated bone graft) or bone graft. Wide resection with endoprosthetic or allograft reconstruction is reserved for Campanacci III lesions with joint destruction not amenable to joint-sparing curettage, or for recurrent disease after failed curettage. Sacral and spinal GCT are managed with denosumab ± embolization ± surgery given the morbidity of direct resection.

**Osteoid osteoma**: RFA first-line where anatomically safe (see above); open surgical excision (with or without intraoperative navigation/fluoroscopic or CT guidance to localize the nidus) reserved for lesions abutting the spinal cord, major nerves, or where percutaneous access is not feasible.

**Osteochondroma**: marginal excision at the base including the cartilage cap and a thin rim of normal cortex, indicated for mechanical symptoms (nerve/vessel impingement, bursitis, limitation of motion), fracture through the stalk, continued growth after skeletal maturity, or a thickened cap (>1.5–2 cm) raising concern for secondary chondrosarcoma.

**Enchondroma**: observation for typical, asymptomatic lesions; curettage + bone graft/substitute for painful lesions, pathologic fracture, or when imaging cannot confidently exclude low-grade chondrosarcoma (endosteal scalloping involving more than two-thirds of cortical thickness, cortical breach, associated soft tissue mass, and progressive growth on serial imaging are the key discriminating features that should prompt biopsy rather than presumptive curettage).

**UBC / ABC**: UBC managed with steroid injection first-line for active/symptomatic lesions in growing children, or curettage + graft/bone substitute ± intramedullary decompression (cannulated screws or an elastic stable intramedullary nail placed across the cyst to provide durable decompression and mechanical support) for recurrent or high fracture-risk lesions (proximal femur especially, given fracture morbidity at that site). ABC managed with curettage + adjuvant + graft (recurrence historically ~10–20% even with adjuvant use), doxycycline sclerotherapy, or selective embolization, particularly for lesions in surgically morbid locations (spine, pelvis); always biopsy/sample solid components first to exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma or secondary ABC arising within another lesion.

**Fibrous dysplasia**: curettage + **cortical allograft** is preferred over cancellous autograft for reconstruction, because autograft placed into dysplastic bone tends to be resorbed and remodeled into further dysplastic bone, giving poor durability; **intramedullary fixation (rod)** of the proximal femur is used both to prevent and to treat the progressive varus "shepherd's crook" deformity in polyostotic/McCune-Albright disease, and corrective osteotomy is reserved for established deformity.

**Soft tissue sarcoma**: **wide resection with a negative margin** is the cornerstone; (neo)adjuvant radiotherapy is added for large (>5 cm), deep, high-grade tumors to reduce local recurrence — the classic randomized comparison of preoperative vs postoperative radiation (O'Sullivan et al.) found equivalent local control and survival but a higher acute wound-complication rate with preoperative RT (~35% vs ~17%) and more late fibrosis/joint stiffness/lymphedema with postoperative RT, informing a case-by-case choice; chemotherapy is used selectively — neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy is considered for high-risk histology/grade extremity STS (informed by trials such as ISG-STS 1001), while low-grade or small superficial STS is generally managed with surgery alone.

**Lipoma vs liposarcoma**: simple marginal excision is appropriate and curative for a typical lipoma once the diagnosis is confident (no red-flag features, or MRI-confirmed pure fat signal without atypical components). Any lesion with MRI features atypical for a simple lipoma (thick/nodular septations >2 mm, non-fatty enhancing components, size >10 cm) requires **core needle biopsy and referral to a sarcoma center** rather than local excision — an "excisional biopsy" of a suspected liposarcoma performed outside a treating center risks the same referral-rule consequences described above (contaminated planes, need for a larger re-resection).

**Metastatic bone disease**: **prophylactic fixation** for Mirels ≥8 lesions or impending fracture; **intramedullary nailing** is generally preferred for diaphyseal long-bone lesions because it is load-sharing, allows immediate weight-bearing, and prophylactically protects the entire bone length against subsequent lesions; **endoprosthetic replacement** (e.g., proximal femoral replacement) is favored over fixation for extensively destroyed peri-articular bone (especially proximal femur/femoral neck) where fixation constructs would have insufficient purchase or unacceptable failure risk; plate-and-screw constructs with cement augmentation are used for peri-articular and upper-extremity lesions not amenable to nailing or replacement. Post-fixation radiation (typically initiated ~2–3 weeks after surgery once wound healing allows) reduces local tumor progression and construct failure. Acetabular metastatic disease is assessed by the **Harrington classification** to guide reconstruction complexity (curettage + cement + standard cup vs cage/triflange or custom reconstruction for major column deficiency).

**Multiple myeloma**: same biomechanical principles as metastatic disease apply to long-bone pathologic fractures (IM nailing preferred for diaphyseal lesions); **vertebral augmentation** (vertebroplasty/kyphoplasty) is highly effective for painful compression fractures; spinal instability (assessed with the **SINS** score) or neurologic compromise from epidural disease warrants decompression and instrumented stabilization, generally combined with radiation and systemic therapy.

**Technical pearls and pitfalls**:

- **Curettage cavities (GCT/ABC/UBC/chondrosarcoma grade I)**: use a headlight and a small window with a large curette/burr to access the full cavity — an inadequately sized cortical window is the most common reason for incomplete curettage and early recurrence. Extend the burr 1–2 mm into grossly normal-appearing bone circumferentially before applying the chemical/thermal adjuvant.
- **Cement vs graft in GCT**: cement generates measurable exothermic thermal necrosis that contributes to the adjuvant effect, but do not rely on it as the sole adjuvant — mechanical curettage technique remains the dominant determinant of local control.
- **Endoprosthetic reconstruction**: meticulous soft-tissue reconstruction (extensor mechanism reattachment at the proximal tibia, abductor reattachment at the proximal femur) is as important to functional outcome as the implant itself, and under-tensioning either repair is a common, avoidable cause of extensor lag or Trendelenburg gait.
- **IM nailing for metastatic disease**: nail the entire bone (not just the lesion) to protect against occult or future skeletal metastases along the same segment — a short segmental nail that leaves unprotected bone proximal or distal to the lesion is a recognized cause of subsequent fracture at the nail end.
- **RFA near neurovascular structures**: use CT-guided temperature monitoring and consider hydro- or gas-dissection to displace an adjacent nerve or vessel away from the ablation zone rather than abandoning an otherwise favorable percutaneous approach.
- **Biopsy tract placement**: always plan the tract as if the diagnosis will be malignant, oriented longitudinally along the line of the eventual resection incision, avoiding crossing anatomic compartments or neurovascular structures — this is true even when the lesion looks benign, because frozen-section/final pathology can surprise.

## Rehabilitation Protocol

Rehabilitation is dictated by the reconstruction, not the diagnosis — the phases below apply generically, with diagnosis-specific notes following the table.

| Phase | Timeline | Weight-bearing | ROM | Key content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Protection | 0–2 weeks | Curettage + cement: protected partial WB from day 1–2. Grafted cavity/large defect: non-WB. Endoprosthesis: non-WB to touch-down WB (protect extensor/abductor repair). IM nail (metastatic): WB as tolerated (load-sharing construct) | Passive ROM of adjacent joints as construct allows | Wound care, drain management, extended DVT prophylaxis, pain control, chemo-cycle coordination if applicable |
| 2 — Early mobilization | 2–6 weeks | Curettage/cement: progress to full WB if radiographically stable. Graft-only: remains protected. Endoprosthesis: progress toward partial WB by 6 weeks | Active-assisted → active ROM; gentle isometrics | Neutropenia checks before advancing high-fall-risk activity in chemo patients; early gait training |
| 3 — Progressive loading | 6–12 weeks | Graft/curettage cavities: full WB once incorporation confirmed. Endoprosthesis: partial → full WB (proximal femoral replacements progress slower given abductor vulnerability) | Full active ROM goal | Closed-chain strengthening initiated, gait normalization, home exercise program |
| 4 — Strengthening & return to function | 3–6 months | Full WB established | Full ROM maintenance | Proprioceptive/balance training, progressive resistance, functional/vocational retraining; for metastatic disease, goals are individualized around prognosis and systemic therapy tolerance rather than a fixed timeline |

**Diagnosis-specific notes**: chemotherapy-receiving patients (osteosarcoma, Ewing) need rehabilitation explicitly coordinated around chemotherapy cycles — expect slower, non-linear progression, monitor counts before advancing fall-risk activity, and plan for substantial cardiopulmonary and general deconditioning requiring dedicated conditioning work alongside limb-specific therapy. Endoprosthetic reconstructions (proximal tibial replacement especially) require protection of the extensor mechanism repair with bracing in extension for the first 6 weeks before progressive flexion is allowed. Metastatic bone disease patients prioritize immediate mobilization and pain control over strict phase adherence, given competing survival and quality-of-life considerations.

**Return to work**: sedentary work typically 6–12 weeks after limb-salvage reconstruction or IM nailing once wound-healed and pain-controlled; physical labor delayed until adequate strength/endurance is demonstrated, often 4–6 months or longer after major reconstruction, and may never be fully regained after proximal femoral/pelvic endoprosthetic reconstruction — set expectations early.

**Return to sport/high-impact activity**: generally discouraged or significantly restricted after endoprosthetic reconstruction (implant longevity concerns, particularly in younger patients who will need revision surgery over their lifetime); low-impact activity (swimming, cycling) is typically permitted once healed (~4–6 months), with high-impact/contact sport counseled against for most megaprosthesis reconstructions given mechanical failure risk. For benign lesions treated with curettage alone (small/moderate cavity, good bone quality restored), return to full activity including sport is reasonable by 3–6 months once radiographic healing/incorporation is confirmed and the bone is judged to have regained adequate structural strength.

## Complications

- **Local recurrence**: the dominant complication across benign-aggressive and low-grade lesions — curettage alone for GCT ~25–50% vs ~10–15% with adjuvant + cement; ABC ~10–20% even with adjuvant curettage; chondrosarcoma grade I after extended curettage with adjuvant has recurrence rates comparable to historical wide resection series in modern cohorts, but higher than for a truly wide margin.
- **Infection**: the leading complication of endoprosthetic limb-salvage reconstruction, with reported rates roughly 10–40% depending on anatomic site (higher for proximal tibia, given thin soft tissue coverage, than distal femur), reconstruction extent, and prior/adjuvant chemoradiation — often requiring staged revision, and a major driver of secondary amputation in this population.
- **Nonunion / graft or allograft failure**: allograft-related complications (fracture, nonunion at the host-graft junction, infection) remain a persistent problem with structural allograft reconstructions, motivating the shift toward endoprosthetic or allograft-prosthetic composite options at many centers for large-segment defects.
- **Malignant transformation**: GCT (~1–4%, higher post-radiation), Ollier disease enchondromas (~25–30% lifetime risk to chondrosarcoma), multiple hereditary exostoses (~1–5%), fibrous dysplasia (<1%, higher with McCune-Albright or prior radiation) — the rationale for continued surveillance imaging in these conditions rather than one-time clearance.
- **Denosumab-specific**: osteonecrosis of the jaw (dose/duration-related — pretreatment dental evaluation recommended for planned prolonged courses), atypical femoral fracture with long-term use, and **rebound hypercalcemia and accelerated bone turnover on discontinuation**, which can be marked, particularly in children/adolescents — plan a monitored taper or transition rather than abrupt cessation where feasible.
- **RFA-specific**: skin burn, nerve injury (especially superficial peroneal/saphenous nerve territory lesions, or spinal locations), and rarely, fracture through the ablation cavity in weight-bearing bone if the ablation zone is oversized relative to remaining cortical bone stock.
- **Pathologic fracture through a treated site**: risk of nonunion is elevated compared with a fracture through normal bone, particularly if radiation has been given to the site — prophylactic fixation thresholds (Mirels) exist specifically to avoid the far worse morbidity, delayed union, and technical difficulty of fixing a completed pathologic fracture versus stabilizing prophylactically.
- **Chemotherapy toxicity** (osteosarcoma/Ewing regimens): doxorubicin cardiotoxicity (cumulative dose-dependent, requires baseline and surveillance echocardiography/MUGA), cisplatin ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity, ifosfamide nephrotoxicity and encephalopathy, and secondary/treatment-related malignancy (leukemia) as a late effect — long-term survivorship surveillance is a lifelong responsibility handed off from pediatric/medical oncology, and orthopedic surgeons managing these patients into adulthood should remain aware of it.
- **Radiation-induced sarcoma**: a recognized late complication (typically ≥3–4 years after radiation, often a decade or more), relevant when a new aggressive-appearing lesion arises within a prior radiation field — treat with the same urgency and referral-rule discipline as any new sarcoma.

### Post-treatment surveillance

| Diagnosis | Local surveillance | Systemic/metastatic surveillance | Typical duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Osteosarcoma / Ewing sarcoma | Local imaging (MRI or radiographs of reconstruction) every 3 months years 1–2, then spacing out | CT chest every 3 months years 1–2, then every 4–6 months years 3–5, then annually | Minimum 5–10 years given late-relapse potential |
| Chondrosarcoma (high-grade) | Local imaging every 3–6 months for 2 years, then annually | CT chest every 6 months for 2 years, then annually | 5–10 years |
| Chondrosarcoma grade I (curettage), GCT | Radiographs every 3–6 months year 1 (recurrence detection), then annually | Chest imaging generally not required for grade I; consider periodic chest imaging for GCT given rare benign pulmonary implants | 2–5 years minimum, longer for GCT given late recurrence reports |
| Soft tissue sarcoma (high-grade) | Physical exam ± local MRI every 3–4 months years 1–3, then every 6 months years 4–5 | CT chest every 3–4 months years 1–3, spacing out thereafter | 5–10 years |
| Benign latent/active lesions (osteochondroma, small enchondroma, healed UBC) | Radiographs at 6–12 month intervals until stability demonstrated | Not indicated | Until skeletal maturity or 2–3 documented stable studies |

## Recent Evidence & Guidelines (2023–2026)

A note on sourcing: the web search tool available for this session reached its query budget before any of the searches planned for this monograph could be executed, so the items below are drawn from established, high-confidence knowledge of major trials, consensus updates, and guideline bodies in orthopedic oncology rather than from freshly retrieved, dated source links. No URLs are included in this section for that reason — do not treat the absence of hyperlinks as an oversight; it reflects the tooling constraint, not a lower confidence in the underlying facts. This section should be revisited with live search capacity to attach verifiable links and to check for anything published after this note that is not reflected here.

- **NCCN Bone Cancer Guidelines** (rolling annual updates) continue to anchor MAP chemotherapy as the neoadjuvant/adjuvant standard for osteosarcoma and interval-compressed VDC/IE for Ewing sarcoma, with response-adapted adjuvant therapy for osteosarcoma based on histologic necrosis — no fundamental change to this backbone in the current cycle, reaffirming rather than revising prior practice.
- **NCCN and ESMO-EURACAN-GENTURIS Soft Tissue Sarcoma guidelines** continue to support risk-stratified use of neoadjuvant anthracycline/ifosfamide-based chemotherapy in high-risk extremity/trunk STS, informed by the ISG-STS 1001 trial framework, with ongoing refinement of which histologic subtypes derive the most benefit (myxoid liposarcoma with round-cell component and leiomyosarcoma are among the more chemosensitive subtypes typically prioritized).
- **rEECur trial** (relapsed/refractory Ewing sarcoma, international randomized comparison of four regimens — topotecan/cyclophosphamide, irinotecan/temozolomide, gemcitabine/docetaxel, and high-dose ifosfamide) has continued to mature and inform relapse-setting regimen selection, with high-dose ifosfamide emerging as a preferred option in patients able to tolerate it in updated analyses — changing practice away from older empirical relapse regimen choices toward evidence-based selection.
- **Denosumab in GCT of bone** — accumulating longer-term and multi-institutional retrospective data continue to refine the neoadjuvant-use controversy, reinforcing that neoadjuvant denosumab facilitates joint-sparing surgery in borderline-resectable/Campanacci III disease but is associated with a higher local recurrence rate than curettage without denosumab in resectable disease — this has sharpened patient selection (reserving neoadjuvant denosumab for genuinely unresectable or high-morbidity-resection cases rather than using it routinely to "shrink" resectable tumors) rather than changing the drug's fundamental approved indication.
- **Chondrosarcoma grade I / ACT management** — the shift toward extended intralesional curettage with adjuvant over wide resection for appendicular grade I disease has continued to be reinforced by additional multi-center retrospective cohorts reporting comparable oncologic safety with superior function, solidifying what began as an emerging practice change into a widely accepted standard at sarcoma centers.
- **IMWG (International Myeloma Working Group) imaging guidance** continues to support whole-body low-dose CT, whole-body MRI, or PET-CT over conventional skeletal survey for initial staging and detection of myeloma-defining bone disease, given superior sensitivity for early lytic/marrow lesions — orthopedic surgeons should expect myeloma patients to arrive with cross-sectional whole-body imaging rather than a plain-film skeletal survey at most referring centers now.
- **Denosumab vs zoledronic acid for skeletal-related event prevention** in bone metastases and myeloma — the established comparative trial evidence (denosumab superior to zoledronic acid for time-to-first SRE in breast and castration-resistant prostate cancer, non-inferior in other solid tumors, non-inferior and preferred in renal-impairment myeloma populations given no need for renal dose adjustment) continues to underpin near-universal preference for denosumab as first-line bone-modifying agent where cost/access allow, across current ASCO and ESMO supportive-care guidance.
- **Preoperative vs postoperative radiotherapy in extremity STS** — the long-standing O'Sullivan-era trade-off (higher acute wound complications with preoperative RT vs higher late fibrosis/lymphedema with postoperative RT) remains the operative framework guiding sequencing decisions at sarcoma centers, with ongoing refinement of dose/fractionation and increasing interest in hypofractionated preoperative regimens to reduce the wound-complication burden while preserving the local-control and late-toxicity advantages of preoperative treatment.
